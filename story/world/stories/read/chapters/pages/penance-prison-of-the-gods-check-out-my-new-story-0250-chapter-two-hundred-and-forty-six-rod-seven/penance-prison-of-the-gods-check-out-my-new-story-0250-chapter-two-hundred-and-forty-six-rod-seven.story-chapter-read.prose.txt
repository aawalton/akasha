I didn’t even realize I was walking until the rubble gave way beneath my boots. The mimic’s crumbled chamber fell behind like the last page of a book I never wanted to read again. My legs moved on instinct, one step, then another, as if forward was the only thing left to do. The others were gathering themselves behind me, groaning, whispering, bleeding, just like me. But something pulled me on.

The air changed first, bringing pressure rather than a breeze, subtle at first, the kind that settles before a storm. Light followed, cool and unnatural, gleaming off the stone like moonlight caught in a blade. It wasn’t coming from torches or crystals. It was… leaking, somehow, from the space ahead, trickling like water through cracks in the dungeon’s skin. The ground underfoot shimmered faintly, like heat haze, but colder.

I slowed, every muscle tensed, my fingers itching near the staff. “Hessa,” I called back, voice rough. “Do you feel that?”

She didn’t answer right away, but I heard her footsteps falter before she spoke, her breath catching. “…Yes. It’s like the air’s watching us.”

Behind her, Halver muttered, “Fantastic. First the mimic, now the walls have eyes.”

“Keep it down,” Syla snapped. “Rod, what do you see?”

Without warning, the wall in front of me folded, peeling back on itself like a mirror, silent and seamless. A passage appeared, smooth and narrow, impossibly clean. I caught a glimpse of my own reflection staring back: bloodied, scorched, eyes far too old. That image splintered into six, then twelve, until I couldn’t tell which one was me anymore.

The dungeon was changing again.Of course it was.

I glanced back as Halver stepped into the glow, his outline swallowed by light and glass. “Halver, wait,” I started, but Syla’s voice cut through sharper: “Rod—move.” A faint click revealed the seam was glass, not metal. The door vanished, and the mirrors began to shift.

The world twisted sideways. My own reflection blurred and fractured, warping into a dozen jagged shards as the corridor folded in on itself. I took a single step back on instinct and nearly fell flat as the floor beneath my boots changed. What was once stone was now polished mirror, so clean I could see every scab on my face and every bloodstain clinging to my robes. Behind me, Syla staggered in, blades drawn, as the last seam of the entrance snapped shut behind her.

Light reflected from every angle: cold, perfect, unsettling. I turned in a slow circle, the air suddenly tighter than it had any right to be. The walls weren’t walls anymore but endless silver, warped in subtle ways, too tall and too narrow. The reflections moved unnaturally; my head tilted slower than I did, and my eyes lagged.

“This isn’t real,” I muttered.

“It’s real enough,” Syla said behind me, her voice low as her eyes scanned the shifting angles. “And we’re alone.”

I looked over my shoulder. Her hood was down now, cheeks cut, dark hair clinging to one temple. She held her blades at a downward angle, her stance reflexive and dangerous. “Where are the others?” I asked, a knot tightening in my stomach. “How could they just vanish?”

Syla shook her head. “They’re gone. Or trapped. This place split us the moment you walked in.”

I clenched my jaw. “Should’ve figured the mirrors would pull something.”

“Mirrors lie,” she said. “That’s what they do.”

The floor shimmered again as new corridors bloomed like veins off the edges of our vision. With every blink, the layout changed, passages expanding and narrowing before reshaping entirely. The light didn’t come from torches or orbs; its source was the mirrors themselves, glowing as if each one contained its own tiny sun. I took a cautious step forward. My reflection didn’t.

I froze.

Syla noticed it too. “...Don’t trust what you see.”

“No shit,” I said, but my voice was tight. That thing in the glass had my eyes and my face, yet something was wrong. The jaw clenched too hard. The posture was too straight. The mouth didn’t move when I did. It smiled. I turned away, forcing myself to look for a path. One hallway loomed ahead, distinct for being narrower than the rest, low and coiled like a snake. Its lack of an obvious trap made it more suspicious.

Syla nodded toward it. “Only way forward.”

I glanced at her, my mind racing. It could also be the only way down. “You always this calm in mirror mazes?”

Her mouth twitched, humorless. “You haven’t seen me not calm yet.” I adjusted my grip on the staff and stepped toward the passage. “Let’s see what the maze thinks of us.”

We stepped into the glass, and the maze blinked shut behind us.

The corridor narrowed almost immediately. The floor crunched beneath our boots with faint, delicate cracks, like stepping across frozen glass that didn’t want to stay frozen. Beneath the flawless surface, something brittle waited to break. The walls leaned in, angling toward us just enough to make me feel like I was shrinking, our reflections flickering and distorting with every step.

“You feel that?” I whispered.

“Watched,” Syla said, barely moving her lips.

The ceiling shimmered above us, a floating pane of thick mirror suspended like a guillotine blade. It reflected us perfectly, but with a jarring half-second delay, as if it was thinking about whether to mimic us. I took another step forward. A faint click echoed, a sound I recognized not as metal, but as the brittle stress of glass. I froze, and Syla did too, her breath stilling. A long groan echoed from above as the mirrored ceiling trembled, fractures spiderwebbing outward from its center.

The entire ceiling was descending.

“Trap,” I muttered, stepping backward, only to hear the same groan behind us. I spun to see the hall we’d entered was gone, replaced by another reflection. “I hate this place.”

Syla was already moving, her eyes scanning the uniform glass. “Look for the odd one out,” she said, voice clipped. “Always one real mirror in the funhouse.”

I swept my staff across the floor, tapping each reflective panel and listening for a difference in pitch. Tap. Tap. Tap. The sounds were identical until my staff struck a panel near the left wall. Clink. That was it. A thin line ran along the panel, a hair off-center. I stepped closer, staff raised, but Syla beat me to it. She hurled a dagger, end over end, straight into the false reflection. It struck, fractured, and then the entire left wall imploded, folding open like an origami door as the roar of splintering glass filled the corridor. The ceiling jerked, picking up speed.

“Move!” I shouted.

We bolted through the gap, our boots skidding on scattered shards. My sleeve caught on a jagged edge, tearing open and spattering blood on the mirrored floor. Behind us, the ceiling slammed down with the force of a hammer, shattering every panel with a shriek like dying metal.

Silence descended as we collapsed beyond the threshold.

Shattered reflections lay around us like broken eyes. “That was too close,” I said, catching my breath.

Syla wiped blood from her cheek with the back of her glove. “Next time, you throw the knife.”

The maze was quiet now, the only sound our own shallow, ragged breaths echoing too loudly against the glass. I pressed a palm to my ribs, wincing. The fight with the mimic had left bruises blooming like rot beneath the skin, and my torn sleeve was sticking to my arm where blood had dried.

“Sit,” Syla said flatly, already kneeling.

“I’m fine,” I lied.

“You’re limping.”

“I’m always limping.”

She didn’t smile or argue, simply opened her belt pouch and pulled out a strip of pale cloth and a thin glass vial. She poured the contents—a thin, blue liquid smelling of mint and rot—into her palm, where it hissed faintly. “Give me your arm,” she said.

“Why?”

“Because you’re bleeding on everything. And it’s getting annoying.”

I sat. The cloth was rough and the antiseptic stung, making me hiss between my teeth, but I didn’t pull away. Her hands were careful and efficient, precise rather than gentle, like everything else about her. She spoke without looking at me. “You ever wonder why the dungeon mirrors us?”

I blinked, considering it.

“You mean literally? Sometimes, but it's more than that. It shows us things we don’t like to see. Makes us fight copies of ourselves. Twists the rules until we break. You think it’s watching?”

“No,” she said, pulling the bandage tight. “I think it’s listening.” That thought made my skin crawl. The silence returned, heavy, and my eyes drifted to the mirror beside us. It showed us sitting close, bruised and cut, but the reflections weren’t bleeding. And mine was staring straight at me, not blinking. I turned away.

“You ever think about quitting? Just giving up and staying in the first room?” I asked quietly.

Syla didn’t answer right away. When she did, her voice was low. “No. Not anymore, not after you saved us.

“Why not?”

She secured the bandage. “Because quitting means you live with it. I’d rather die trying. Honestly, I just want to see what comes after this stupid floor.”

I looked down at the cloth around my arm, her fingers still resting there, faintly trembling for a second before she pulled away. We didn’t speak after that. We stood and walked on.

It came from nowhere.

One second I was breathing; the next, a black blur erupted from a mirrored wall on my left, emerging through the surface itself. A massive warg, all matted fur and yellow eyes, broke into the room with a snarl that rattled in my chest. Horns curled along its skull like broken tusks; its hide seemed to absorb the light, its claws striking the tile with a sharp, splintering crack.

It was on me in an instant.
