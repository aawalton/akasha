I stared at him for what felt like minutes as he stared back, straight face, no hint of a joke. My gaze was analysing his face looking for any hint, I finally gave in deciding he wasn't joking and slowly nodded my head.

“There is just one problem with your assumption.” I said with a sigh.

“You're not dead, and you are actually here.” He replied, a slight smile pulling at his lips.

I pointed my finger at him as if to say ‘bingo’ but I don’t think he got the reference by the look he gave me in return. I nodded my head a moment later to help him understand.

“Indeed, alive and well.” I said as I relaxed into my chair, “but a lot stronger, and not as old as your timeline would suggest I should be.”

“hrrumph! That’s got the ring of a mead-soaked tale, it does. Ye mean to keep it to herself, or are ye pourin’ it out for me to hear?” His dwarven accent got thicker as he became excited.

I paused for a second, as I took it in and slowly nodded, the next moment he was pouring more mead into his cup as he sat up a little straighter and motioned for me to tell my tale.

“Damn,” he rumbled, tugging softly at his beard in disbelief, “a bit o’ dimensional time dilation costs ye two thousand years.” He let out a low whistle through his teeth, eyes narrowing beneath a heavy brow.

“By the stone… That’s not a toll, that’s a sentence,” he muttered. His fingers twisted in his beard again, slower now, thoughtful. “I’ve seen clans feud for less time than that. Seen mountains rise and crack between one drink and the next—well, feels like it, anyhow.”

I let out a soft giggle as I watched him, before informing him why I had come to his station. “I am here to meet a Celestial Count's daughter.” He raised an eyebrow, so I added, “Zeo Walker.”

I saw him wince for a second before he nodded his head indifferently as he looked away. I let the wince go, deciding it wasn't important, and I was curious about something else.

“I am still curious about your last ear, why did you think I had come to hear your life story? Don’t dwarves have long lives before nanotech made life even longer?” I asked.

He burst out laughing as he grabbed his belly and nearly knocked his drink off the table. I gave him a look of confusion as I titled my head quickly, running through what I knew about dwarves' life spans and the nanotech that expanded all life spans.

“Don’t ye know better than to be askin’ folk their ages?” He laughed on, the sound deep and rough as stone on stone. “Oooh, I’ve watched planets grind together and shatter apart, with naught but time doin’ the work. I’m old, Kenna — stone-old. Me natural span, stretched even longer by the system, is near spent.”

His mirth faded to something quieter, heavier. “The sawbones told me three hundred years ago that the nanites in me blood were startin’ to break themselves down. I’m at the end o’ me road. And then ye walk in — after yer death was marked and sealed over two thousand years ago.” He huffed softly, beard shifting as he nodded to himself. “Seems only natural to think ye’ve come to be me last ear, lass.”

I nodded my head slowly before letting out a long sigh. “It's sad to hear you are coming to your end, do you know how long you have left?” I asked, feeling morbid.

“No, not exactly,” he grunted, waving a hand. “I could go any day now—though the heart attack I near had when they announced your arrival almost took me there and then.” He snapped the last bit at me, and I felt a flicker of shame for a moment before he went on.

“I hadn’t the guts to face me own ghost, so I told ‘em to send you instead. Sat myself right down here, I did, and poured a mug o’ me finest mead — kept this cask aside for a truly grand moment.” He chuckled, low and warm. “But as the cask aged, I could never find an occasion worthy o’ it… till now.” He raised his mug to me, before downing the entire drink.

“I am honoured.” I replied, feeling awkward instead. He nodded his head politely before pouring himself another mug and got to his feet.

“Ye are me ghost, come to weigh me deeds,” he said, voice dropping to a deep, steady rumble. “And I could not ask for a finer ear. So I’ll tell ye me tale, Lady Kenna of the Mackenzie clan — Honorary Ancestor of the Dwarven Bonebane Clan — and then I’ll walk into the mountain and meet the end o’ me road. I am ready.”

His eyes stayed locked with mine as he held his mug out toward me, arm steady. “Do not deny this dwarf his final sacred tradition,” he intoned. “To do so would shame me before the Great Mountain itself.”

I bowed my head slightly acknowledging his bravery, “I am ready to hear your deeds my friend,” I replied.

“And that is my tale—the good, the ill, and all that lies between. I’ve spoken no falsehoods and hidden naught. May I be judged honest and fair,” he declared.

He let the mug fall without a glance as it struck the table and bounced, what little mead remained sloshing out and spilling across the table and floor, before the mug came to a stop on the floor.

From the tale I had just heard I knew Brasdrec would not consume a drop more, let alone eat a crumb until the mountain had taken him. I slowly turned my gaze away from where the mug lay and stared at the old, tired dwarf that was ready for his end. I could see the resolve in his eyes and feel the weight of my next words resting like a cloak over me.

I rose to my feet, slowly, as if the air itself resisted. When I met his gaze again, there was no softness in my eyes — only depth, like bedrock seen through clear water.

“Brasdrec Bonebane,” I said, voice low and steady, “you have lived long, and you have lived fully. I have heard your deeds as they were wrought, not as you wished them remembered.” I drew a breath, steadying myself for what was about to come.

”you were stubborn when wisdom called for patience. You were ruthless when mercy would have been easier. Blood was spilled by your command, and some of it did not need to be. For these things, I do not excuse you.”

His jaw tightened—but he did not look away. “But hear this as well,” I continued. “You did not hide from the weight of your choices. You did not cast blame on stone, clan, or fate. When you erred, you carried it. When you broke the world, you stood in the rubble and named it your doing.”

I stepped around the table, walking closer to him. “You guarded your people when no one else would. You paid every victory in coin of flesh or soul, and you paid for it yourself. The peace you forged was not clean — but it was real. And it has endured.”

My voice softened, just a fraction. “I judge that you lived as a dwarf should: with your hands in the work, your name bound to consequence, and your honour measured not by purity, but by responsibility.”

I inclined my head forwards — a bow of sorts. “Brasdrec of the Bonebane clan, I find your tale honest. Your scales are balanced. Your road is complete.” Silence settled, thick and reverent between us, for a moment before I finished. “Go to the Mountain, my dear friend, it already knows your name.”

For a long moment, Brasdrec did not move. Then his shoulders sagged — not in weakness, but like a load finally set down after centuries of carrying it. The tension left him all at once, as if some unseen hand had untied the last knot in his spine. He bowed low, it was slow and deliberate, his beard almost brushing the floor.

“When the mountain hears my name,” he said hoarsely, “it will not turn me away.” His voice cracked on the last word, and that seemed to surprise him. He cleared his throat and straightened, blinking hard once. He brought a fist to his chest, knuckles striking stone-hard muscle.

“Ye judged me as I lived,” he rumbled. “No softer. No crueller. That is all a dwarf can ask.” He reached down and retrieved the fallen mug, setting it upright on the table with care. He did not refill it.

”For the record o’ the clans,” he said, more formally now, "I go to the mountain unburdened. If there is shame waitin’ for me beyond the stone, it is mine and mine alone.”

Then — unexpectedly — he smiled, almost proudly. “Tell the young ones,” he added, “that Brasdrec Bonebane did not run from the end o’ his road.”

He turned toward the door leading into the station, only for it to open and reveal the maw of a tunnel into a mountain. I stared at it in surprise, having not expected it to be there. I followed him out the door, glancing back to see we had been in the cargo bay of a drop-ship, and rolled my eyes before turning back to Brasdrec.

He stood at the threshold of the tunnel leading deeper into the mountain, where the rock swallowed light. He was pausing there, looking back at me. “Thank ye, Lady Kenna,” he said simply. Then he turned and walked into the mountain.

I stood there and watched him vanish into the dark, watching it for what felt like hours before I felt a gentle tap on my hip and turned to see a dwarf looking into the tunnel, a grim expression on his face.

“Thank ye, ye have given me father great honour in his final moments, I will spread his tale and remember.” The dwarf then turned and walked back to the ship.

I stepped out of the ship back onto the station and silently walked away. I had cried my eyes out in the drop-ship as it brought me back to the station, only just managing to pull myself back together as it arrived. Brasdrec’s son had then sat with me and explained that Brasdrec would walk into the mountain until his body couldn't walk anymore, and then he would then crawl, until his last breath. That would mark the length of his honour, and a shrine would be built in the wall for him to rest in until the planet died.

It was a while later that I found myself sitting in the central promenade. The space was enormous, an oval expanse with paths winding gracefully through it. Soft grass sprouted between the walkways, dotted with trees and garden beds bursting with flowers. Birds flitted among the branches, and bees drifted lazily from flower to flower. I couldn't see it, but I could hear the gentle fall of water, most likely from somewhere upstream along the river that meandered quietly through the promenade, adding a calm rhythmic backdrop to the hum of life around me.

I felt a presence sit down next to me and glance over to see a face that I had not seen in a very long time. She was not alone, and I couldn't help the slight smile that tugged at my lips as I looked at all three of them. I was about to speak when I noticed the promenade was empty of people other than the ones staring at me covered in armour, carrying weapons. My gaze shifted back to Zeo who was sitting next to me watching the gentle scenery around us.

“Hello, Zeo, have you come to kill me?” I asked softly.
