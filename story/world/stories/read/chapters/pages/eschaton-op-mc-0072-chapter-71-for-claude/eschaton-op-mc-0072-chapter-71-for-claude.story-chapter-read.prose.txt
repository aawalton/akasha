Morgan portals us both to just inside the front entrance of the California Academy of Sciences. She’s carrying the hot tub-sized tank containing the water lily and the catfish with telekinesis.

The Academy is nearly barren, and there’s only a handful of people here. All four of them are in suits. One of them steps forward. Dr. Lam is a short man with short-cut black hair and thick glasses.

“Morgan, it’s good to see you again,” he says while shaking her hand. He then turns to me.

“Machina, it’s an honor to meet you. I wish we had time for more formal pleasantries, but these days we’re all pressed for time, unfortunately.”

“I understand, it’s alright.”

“Well, let me introduce you to the team I’ve put together to help figure out what to do with the water lily. I don’t suppose you’ve got a name for it?”

“No? Why are you asking me?”

“You’re well known for your monster names,” he says dryly. “I guess it doesn’t extend to docile plants. Well, come on, we’ll walk and talk.”

I admire the T-Rex skeleton they have as we follow Dr. Lam towards the bio-dome.

Maybe I’ll see a magical dinosaur in a Fracture one of these days.

“Right, well, I’ve got with me here Doctor Cooper, Doctorate in Botany. Doctor Levy, Doctorate in Climate Science, and Doctor Beskal, Doctorate in Microbiology. They’ll have support staff too, but for now these are the three I could round up on short notice.”

We take a right, and I come face to face with a massive transparent sphere ninety feet high. It’s filled with plant and even animal life. Birds flit from tree to tree. Swarms of colorful butterflies drift around in chromatic clouds. An array of huge spot lights shine on it, though only some of the light reaches the bottom floor.

“Woah,” I say, marveling at the bio-dome. It really is a rainforest-in-a-jar.

“Yeah, it’s pretty awesome, huh?” Dr. Lam beams. “Largest of its kind in the world. Multistory with a built in aquarium underneath. Is this your first time here?”

“No, but I was very young the last time I was here. Maybe six? I don’t remember it very well.”

“Well,” he chuckles. “I guess you’ll be getting a private tour of it. If you have the time for it.”

“We’d love to,” Morgan says. She prods me in the back and whispers so quietly I’m sure the Typical scientists can’t hear her.

“You’re taking a break. This place is cool, you’ll love it.”

Her tone brooks no argument.

“Well, once we’re done here, I’ll personally give you a walk through,” Dr. Lam says, unaware of Morgan’s command.

Once we arrive at the double doors used for maintenance, we set down the tank and remove the water lily. The tank is too big to fit. Morgan pulls both the lily pad and the fish out. The catfish looks confused as it—and an orb of water—floats into the bio-dome.

“Alright, right now, the plan is to plant it in the submerged forest exhibit, and let the lily reach up to the surface of the pond in Osher Rainforest—the bio dome.”

The atmosphere changes as soon as we pass through the airlock into the bio dome itself. San Francisco was foggy and chilly. Inside the bio dome it’s warm, bright, and a little muggy.

A few birds can be heard squawking from deep in the dense foliage, high above. My omni-directional sensor suite notices trails of leaf-cutter ants marching around the forest. They carry chunks of leaves like war-banners.

We carefully make our way through the jungle to get to the edge of the pond. A few divers are already waiting for us.

“They’re here to help bury the roots and make sure it’s not tangled up. Alright, let it down.”

Morgan unspools the long stem of the water lily like a cable, and the divers grab hold of the arm-thick roots. They swim down, all the way to the bottom. Using small shovels, they bury the roots deep under the river bed under the curious eyes of dozens upon dozens of fish.

Dr. Lam glances at the fish floating in the air, and directs one of the assistants to take it. She tags it, the bright blue piece of plastic attached to the dorsal fin. After that, she tosses it in the pond, and it shoots off into a corner. I notice a few catfish even bigger than the one I just handed over.

“Holy crap, that’s a big fish,” I say, spotting one of the current residents of the aquarium. It’s a catfish nearly eight feet long.

Dr. Lam chuckles.

“Yeah, some kinds of catfish can get pretty big. Well, if you find any more living samples of more…docile creatures, we’ll be happy to take them. Well, samples of more aggressive ones too, as long as they’ve been dealt with. We’re a world-class research laboratory too, not just a museum.”

“I’ll keep that in mind, Doctor. I’ve been giving away most of what I collect to the National Guard. I haven’t really thought much about it other than that.”

“We’ve received some small sets of samples from your dives, of course. We’ve even received more than others, certainly, but there really isn’t much to go around.” He awkwardly coughs. “I should say that we are extremely grateful for what we do get. But we can always make use of whatever you bring us.”

The divers set the water lily’s roots deeper into the soil, and Morgan lets go. The smart car sized pad rests blissfully on the surface of the water. Curious fish poke at it. The water lily doesn’t react.

“A little anti-climactic,” Morgan says disappointedly. “I thought it’d start glowing or something.”

“What was it doing in the Fracture, if I may ask?” Doctor Lam cuts in.

“Sitting on a lake. Like flowers normally do.”

“Well, the good doctors here will keep an eye on it, and they’ll let us know if they figure anything out about it. Hopefully we can try to spread it, and then maybe do some more destructive testing. In the meantime, I’d love to give you both a tour of the Academy. All available resources have been diverted to research, so all of the exhibits are closed to the public. It’ll be just us. Where would you like to start?”

“I’ve been here many times,” Morgan says. “Mach, why don’t you pick?”

“Why not the bio-dome? We’re already here,” I say after musing for a few nano-seconds.

“Excellent.”

After exiting and then re-entering the bio-dome from the visitor’s entrance, Doctor Lam starts a clearly well rehearsed speech.

“Welcome to the Osher Rainforest! This bio-dome contains a tiny slice of a real flooded rainforest, as closely as modern technology can allow. It’s the largest of its kind in the world, at ninety feet across! The butterflies you see all around are actually shipped in from South America as pupae and larvae, and we then gestate them on site. Try not to touch them, their wings are very delicate.”

I stand stock still, looking all around. A few brave, curious, or maybe simply tired butterflies land on me. Morgan laughs and quickly snaps a picture with her phone. She brushes them away gently, and they scatter off back into the humid air.

After seeing a pair of macaws, long after hearing their loud squawks, the path begins to spiral up and inside the clear walls of the biodome, hugging the glass. I hesitate for a moment before taking a step onto the ramp. A blue glow appears, and though the catwalk shakes a little, it doesn’t flex under my weight or creak ominously.

The path spirals up onto another floor, where we pass by aquariums and tanks full of fish or small creatures. A pair of bright blue toads hide under leaves, and a fat brown one sits placidly in its water dish. A lizard licks its eye, and a leaf-cutter ant colony chews up the bounty of the jungle to feed the swarm. Little trails of ants leave a hive with clear sides to disappear among trees, and then they come marching home.

We continue our way up, catching flashes of feathers as birds flitter between trees. Finally, after an hour of slowly spiraling up the inside of the bio-dome, we come to the peak, and a pair of elevators await us. Doctor Lam looks at me warily.

“I don’t wish to be rude, Machina, but the elevator has a max weight of thirty-five hundred pounds. We can go back and enter the aquarium from another area, if we must?”

“Nah, it’ll be fine. I gave him a nice magic cloak that helps with that.” Morgan says smugly.

Doctor Lam blinks. “Uh, how does that work?”

“Magic?” Morgan says confusedly. “How else?”

“It doesn’t reduce his weight—ah, never mind. If you say it’s fine, it’s fine.”

“Ever the scientist, Benny.”

“It’s been my whole life. In any case, down we go.”

The elevator and the shaft is transparent, and as it goes down, we can see the forest on all sides. It dives into the pond at the base, and into the aquarium. Numerous fish of all kinds and sizes can be seen swimming lazily about. Even a few turtles are paddling around. And, of course, the water lily we just brought. Divers are still hard at work placing various scientific devices and cameras, all in an attempt to understand whatever I brought back.

“Ah, it’s beautiful, isn’t it?” Doctor Lam says.

“It is. It really is.”

“Don’t worry, Machina, there’s still plenty more to see.”

After an hour in the aquarium, we ascend a set of stairs back up to the ground floor. One final aquarium awaits us, this one an open air one. It has one very recognizable occupant.

“Meet Claude, the albino alligator! He’s been with us since 2008!”

Claude is ten feet long, and bone white. Claude lies on the central rock in the open air swamp tank. One red tinted reptilian eye flicks open for a moment. It flicks shut shortly after.

“There’s less than two hundred albino alligators in the world.” Dr. Lam adds. “Claude here is pretty unique in that regard.”

“He seems to really like that rock,” I say. Claude still hasn’t so much as twitched. His only response to our presence was the look he gave us upon our arrival.

“Alligators are ambush predators. They don’t really move much. Not to mention that rock is heated.”

“It must be a pretty good life for a gator.”

“We give him the very best,” Dr. Lam agrees. “He’s a core part of the Academy. Millions of people see him every year. Well, used to.” He sighs. “I hope that one day the Academy can open its doors to the public again, and become the bastion of public education it’s been for decades.”

“We’ll do our best,” I tell him.

“From you, Machina, that means quite a bit. Thank you, and please, let me know however we can help you. On that note, would you like to see the labs?”

“That’s more Morgan’s speed, I think,” I say awkwardly, and she rolls her eyes.

“Oh, come on Mach. It’ll be fun!” She says, poking me.

“Besides, you should say yes. Remember how I talked about relationships with people being important?” She says in a much quieter voice.

“Alright,” I say. “Show us what you’ve got, Doctor.”

He claps his hands, smiling. “Excellent! Right this way!”

“Bye, Claude,” I tell the alligator, waving.

Morgan chimes in. “See you later, Claude!”

We see through glass windows into busy labs. All of them are cramped and crowded with dozens of scientists with all levels of degrees and accreditation working furiously. They seem to have a rather small selection of samples, portioning out tiny cubes of monster parts. Some of them are so small I doubt Dr. Lam can even see them from here.

“You’re being very judicious with your samples. Do you not have enough?”

Dr. Lam shrugs helplessly.

“We don’t really have all that much. I know I said we receive more than some other labs because of our reputation and capability, and that is true. But at the same time, more than a pittance still isn’t much. There really isn’t much of it to go around, at least for public labs like ours.”

“That doesn’t make much sense,” I tell him. “The National Guard kills thousands of monsters every day. Empowered kill even more. How could there not be enough?”

Dr. Lam grimaces. “The National Guard tends to be very… zealous when it comes to monster destruction. It doesn’t help that they’re forced to use heavy weaponry and even tanks on them. They don’t tend to leave much behind. As for the Empowered, well, most of them are in private employment, or sell the corpses to corporations. We just don’t have the budget to compete with companies like Titan Defense or Bluefire.”

“It always comes down to money, doesn’t it?” I say sadly.

“Makes the world go ’round,” Dr. Lam replies tiredly.

“Well, I guess I’ll need to really work on pulling more monsters out. I’ll do my best to make sure you get supplied, Doctor.”

“I think I can help with that, too.” Morgan says. “You know Benny, it’s been a while since you’ve hosted an event here, right? With it being closed to the public, it might be the perfect time to host a little gala. Drum up some more private donors, build some more partnerships. It’ll be just like old times, huh?”

“That does sound like a good idea. I’ll bring it up to the Board,” Doctor Lam says. “If both of you are attending though, I have no doubt they’ll jump for it.”

“Perfect!”
