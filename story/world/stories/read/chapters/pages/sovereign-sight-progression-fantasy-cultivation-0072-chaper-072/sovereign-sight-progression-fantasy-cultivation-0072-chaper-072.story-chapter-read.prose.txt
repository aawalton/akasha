It has a feel to it, the Sunken City.

Of movement and unseen currents.

It isn't the river but the memory of sky.

Immediately, the cool quiet of the scholar's domain was replaced with a distant cacophony. Green grounds spread in every direction, rolling in small hills amid the thin, ruler-straight tree trunks and gnarled but lush branches. From the top of the stone steps, Harker could see outward into the city below. It twinkled, an amber and red echo of the blue-green shards above. Most of the light snaked along what Harker could only assume were winding streets, but at least a third of them were concentrated on the sides of the Gallant itself.

The docks are always busy here, even late at night. She’d said but…I suppose I never believed.

His mother had been well traveled—far more than anyone else in the Vale—and whenever she wasn’t telling him stories of Aberrants and Eidhrin, she regaled him with stories of other places and cultures. The Sunken City had gotten only a few passing mentions, and then only because it was so close.

A squalid place, but useful in its own way. I wouldn’t spend too long in its borders though. The kind of muck there has a tendency to stick to what passes through.

Not exactly a ringing endorsement, and one of the reasons Harker hadn’t ever intended on traveling through the city. I’m here now, though. Better get moving.

The grounds around the Lighthouse were expansive and well-tended, both the manicured lawns and the crushed gravel roads. It took Harker fifteen minutes to reach the tall stone walls that surrounded the property, and he found a small squad of guards at the wide open gate. They looked bored, and though they eyed him as he approached, not one shifted from their post.

Outside the walls was a wide, cobbled street lined by elevated walkways. The road coiled down the hill, making a slow, curved descent toward a neighborhood of houses that each rivaled Vale’s mayoral estate. Large and on their own swaths of walled-in land, they filled the hill with a quiet sort of grandeur that was only accentuated by the dark of night.

Lamps lined the roadways, one every fifty feet or so, enough to illuminate the neighborhood with pools of amber. Harker pulsed his Talent as he passed one, and though there was some resistance, it was easily overcome. Each lamp was atop a tall post set with a series of angular infusion lines just beneath their iron surface. All of them connected to a small tidal gem set beneath the bell of the lamp, and it was that which powered the light. Water churned inside, clean and refined. It wasn’t a large reservoir, but as he stood there watching for five minutes, it didn’t move by any measurable amount. However the infusion process worked, the lamps were efficient. Harker reminded himself once again that he had to learn such skills, if only for his own burning curiosity.

Still, it was odd that so many lamps dotted the streets—each one represented a small fortune in materials simply left out in the open. His confusion was cleared a bit when he spotted the first of the armored guardsmen patrolling between the buildings, standing in those few pools of shadow that the lamplight didn’t reach.

How long had they been watching him?

Harker pretended not to notice them and kept walking, armoring himself in obliviousness. The guards were good though—they didn’t stop watching him until he turned the corner.

City guards, judging by the heraldry on their tabards. He spotted more now he was looking for them, always in groups of two or three. Quite a few for such a quiet area.

The neighborhood atop the hill and Lighthouse was indeed calm, the night interrupted only by the occasional cry of a bat or owl and the distant rush of the Gallant far below. The high walls around the mansions were well made and often carved with beautiful nautical scenes. Even the walkways were lined with flowers, filling the air with a sweet fragrance that all but banished the smell of the river.

Warm air, too. The cavern must insulate them from the weather outside. Though Spring had arrived, Winter’s intermittent grip on the island seemed to have no effect on the Sunken City. A cool breeze still made its way to him, likely a result of the ever-flowing presence of the Gallant.

What he could see of the architecture around him was interesting. The carved walls weren’t just straight edges but flowed with the street in patterns that were distinctly fluid, like waves cresting across the top of the hill before sweeping downward. The mansions and carriage houses were the same, their upper floors and roofs designed with an understated elegance. Shadows from the trees in their gardens rustled with the breeze, hazing the edges of the manors enough that it almost looked like they were in motion.

There was also a soft, almost imperceptible shine. Harker caught it early but dismissed it as a reflection of the glinting starlight above. Eventually, though, its persistent presence caught Sovereign Sight.

Infused walls? That seemed like too much even for nobility, as Harker had to assume lived in the mansions around him. But in a place that spent Water on street lights, he should have been surprised. I wonder what they do?

The resistance was far stronger than with the lamps, but it was nothing compared to an Eidhrin or even Adhira. Harker’s sense of sight vanished, but touch, taste, and smell all intensified. Thick granite was layered atop sheets of copper, tin, and what could only be lead. Angular lines were carved into each sheet and flowed with a steady but thin stream of refined Water.

Just like with the lift, Harker could see it all in action, but the infusion made little sense to him. Knot-like lines blurred, diving deeper and connecting across the sheets of different metals with a pattern that he couldn’t parse.

One day. He patted the wall. One day.

It took half an hour, but eventually Harker escaped the manicured neighborhoods of the rich and powerful. The demarcation was obvious. The walkways lost their flowers, the streets lost some of their cobbles, and of course there was a wall. Twice again as large as the ones around the mansions and featuring a heavy, wrought iron gate, it was also manned by yet more city guards. They let Harker pass without comment, though they did eye his attire more than a little.

Outside the walls, the noise of the city increased exponentially. People, hounds, wagons, and the general ruckus of a living city enveloped Harker like a blanket. He pulled his Talent closer, keeping it no more than a foot from his body, and even then some of the chaos was like a spike to the back of his neck. Specialty shops, fine inns, and glass-fronted taverns were filled with men and women in tailored clothing bearing wide, unconcerned smiles.

All of it changed, block by block, as Harker proceeded toward the river.

The streets were the first thing. They shrank, dominated by hound-drawn wagons as the walkways vanished almost entirely. Instead, they were replaced by deep canals that ran parallel to the roads, wide passages that allowed a constant flow of water to speed downslope. Boats plied the canals, ferrying people far faster than the cobbled streets, and it was an easy decision to hop aboard one.

“One copper,” said a small boy with his feet wrapped in rags. “Ends at the docks.”

“Fine by me.” Harker dropped the coin into his grimy hand.

Harker found a seat amid a dozen others, though there was room enough for half as many more. It was a relief. His legs were burning, the mild exertion of walking downhill proving to be a bit much for his still recovering muscles. This at least allowed him to observe the city in more detail, something that was hard when he was spending so much energy on walking.

He extended Sovereign Sight, letting it stretch around him to his five-foot max.

In the distance, a series of waterfalls fell from amid the sparkling false sky. Hard to make out in the dark, his Talent immediately picked out their faintly reflective flow, as well as the splash-roar of their impact somewhere in the city. They were clearly the source of the canals, as many of them landed upslope near the nicer parts of town. It also explained why the canals weren’t present closer to the Lighthouse, though Harker’s legs would have been very grateful if they had been.

“—I didn’t know the third street canal was blocked. They aren’t even working on it! I had to run two blocks to catch this in time and nearly fell in.”

“You poor dear! The Sunken City isn’t what it used to be. I still remember when they rebuilt the storm drains—it clogged traffic for months.”

Two older women sat a few seats down from Harker, shawls wrapped around their shoulders and large bags set against their feet. One had white hair and the other looked more like iron, but both of them had only faintly wrinkled skin—a confusing detail, at least until his skin tightened. One of them held out her hand and whispered. A small wash of heat poured from her palm, distorting the air around her before rolling across the other woman’s wet legs.

She sighed in appreciation. “Thanks, love. It’s always something, isn’t it?”

“Only since we fell from the peak.”

Harker smiled to himself. He’d heard the phrase before, though never in person. According to his mother, the city had once stood atop the Gnarl. It had fallen during the War of Broken Thrones, when enemy Talents had broken the very mountain beneath it, resulting in what became known as the Sunken City.

“Ancestors protect us from such a war again.” The Talented woman touched her forehead and lips with the back of her thumb. “I couldn’t handle sinking any deeper.”

The other barked a laugh. “Any deeper and we’ll all be drowned.”

“At least we’d Descend!”

They cackled. It had the feel of an old, well-used joke.

War. Harker had seen enough violence now that he almost felt he could envision what that might be like. There hadn’t been such a conflict since the Broken Thrones, and most claimed there never would be again.

The women continued chatting, but Harker let their conversation drift out of his awareness along with the few others on the ferry. Instead he watched the buildings and streets as they made their relatively swift way toward the docks.

Specialty stores were soon replaced by second-hand shops and taverns without any glass windows at all. The streets got rougher, the wagons sturdier, and even the walls of the buildings were plastered with notices, flyers, and bounties. Those caught Harker’s attention the most. Glistening portraits stared out at him, stoic or snarling depending on the subject, each one rippling slightly as if set atop a pool. Men and women, listed with aliases and a reward for their capture, sat aside far more disturbing images. Aberrants, mostly Bestials, that were haunting the region—along with their current bodycount written in red ink.

Dozens were plastered on the passing walls, and hundreds of red slashes marred the Talent-made papers. Unlike Vale, the Sunken City was like much of the civilized isles in that it was plagued by the Aberrant threat. The corrupting influence of the untempered Sea spread far and wide, while the increase in population greatly increased the chance of something truly terrible. Such as an Emergent or Husk.

Harker frowned. He’d had his hands full with Illwrought and Bestials. True Aberrants were something else altogether. The bounties were a visceral reminder that for some, the real threats of the world weren’t other Talented, but the Aberrants that wanted to kill them all.

Even back home, that hadn’t been true for Harker. His whole life had been spent avoiding conflict with other Talents, and it was only the beast-filled wilds that offered him any sort of peace. The laws of the land might deem war illegal and condemn Talent against Talent violence, but that only went so far. Duels, spars, and a dozen other niceties gave excuse for the strong to do what they wanted. It was the poor and powerless that paid the cost.

Sitting on that bench as the city slid by, Harker let the anger rise up in him. It burned his chest and made his skin prickle. None of it was right, or fair. He shouldn’t have been chased from his home by monsters or men, nor suffered everything he’d been forced to endure. Yet he still stood, unbent.

He’d continue to do so, until either he or the world broke.
