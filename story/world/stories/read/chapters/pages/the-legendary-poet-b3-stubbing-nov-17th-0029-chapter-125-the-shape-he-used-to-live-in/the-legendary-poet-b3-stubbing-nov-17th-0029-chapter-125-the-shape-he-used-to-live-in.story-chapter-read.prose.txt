The panel held there in the air, red-bordered, patient, waiting for him to be afraid of it.

[Defeat: Permanent loss of the Legendary Class 'Architect of Verse'.]

Huh.

The first thing he felt was concern. The right thing to feel, the operator's response, a small tight pulse behind the sternum that said the bank balance is at risk. Then, a half-second later, the second thing arrived, and the second thing was much funnier.

Irony.

The second time he almost wanted to thank the thing. Because there had been a stretch of his life, not long ago and not nearly far enough behind him, where he would have signed that contract in his own blood. Lose the class. Lose the floating gibberish and the poems and the whole maddening apparatus of it. The poetry, the syllable counting, the resource bar that was technically the same as a mana bar and yet refused to be a mana bar, the synesthesia stapling itself over his UI like ivy over a window.

Roll a fresh character with a sword and numbers that meant what they said. Back when the class had landed on him like a building, he'd spent considerable mental time trying to figure out how to do exactly that.

And now they're threatening me with the divorce I asked for.

He turned the thought over while the script wall sealed and the Curator measured him from across the box.

The class was, on paper, a nightmare to operate. Nothing displayed a clean number. His Awen was a feeling in his teeth instead of a bar. Damage scaled off honesty and emotional weight and how cleanly a metaphor landed, which meant his internal spreadsheet had a column titled vibes and he hated that he'd written it. The compound mechanics stacked into each other until accounting for a single verse meant accounting for nine other systems first.

And yet.

The class had paid. That was the verdict that mattered. It had moved him up the ladder, cracked open quests no combat build could touch, hauled in six months of real, spendable, hospital-bill-shredding currency. His mother had six months because of the thing he'd wanted to delete.

Would I have gotten here on a sword build? Probably. He corrected. Yes. I would.

He was confident, in a flat way that had nothing to do with pride, that he would have ground out a number close enough on a combat class. He'd believed it the whole way down. He was a prodigy with a blade before he was anything else, and the sword never asked him to feel anything to make it work.

But somewhere along the descent, the question had quietly changed shape under him, and he resented that it had.

Because the answer was no longer just yes, for the money.

He'd noticed it the way you notice a slow leak. The grind had stopped being only about the number at the top of his bank account. The poems had started to mean something to him that wasn't profit. The fights had started to feel like more than gear acquisition. And he could not prove, with any column in any ledger, whether that creeping sentiment was him or whether the class had reached up through the system and rewired what he wanted while he wasn't auditing it.

A class. In a game. Made me notice the real prize was never the money.

He found that annoying.

He found it annoying that he found it annoying. He found it annoying the way a man finds a stone in his boot annoying after the tenth mile, the kind of small persistent wrongness that you cannot file away because it grinds against every step. He'd built an entire identity on the principle that hope was a high-interest loan and feeling was a liability, and the system had gone and turned his own escape hatch into a confession.

Annoying.

A floor plate shuddered somewhere outside the box. The Discarded Draft, four hundred and eighty thousand points of mindless paper, swung an arm at his raid, and the script wall dimmed the impact down to a distant percussion.

He came back.

There was a boss in front of him. There was a cage. There was a stance held with precision. And there was a panel threatening to take from him the exact thing he'd once begged to lose.

He took all of it in. The white floor with no horizon. The fifteen-foot box of fused letters. The one-on-one. The duel. The stakes set in jagged red, everything I have on the line, decided by a single exchange.

He laughed.

It surprised him as much as it surprised the Curator. A short, real sound, the kind he hadn't made in front of anyone in a long time, and he heard it leave him before the Operator could stamp it down. Because it was perfect. It was so neat it had to be deliberate, some cosmic editor arranging the page. A boy who'd been a duelist before he was a survivor, dragged across a hundred and twenty-four chapters of compromise and exploitation and gaming the code, deposited at last into a sealed room with a single opponent and a weapon in his hand and one exchange to decide it all.

He had lived inside this shape before.

A space the size of a dueling court. A judge somewhere overhead. An opponent who held himself the way the old men at the dojo held themselves, all weight and waiting. The grip of a blade and the long quiet before two people moved.

Something in his shoulders let go that he hadn't known was clenched. The wire-taut tension, the accounting, the constant low hum of what does this cost - it thinned out and went somewhere far off. It happened, in his own, quiet assessment, too often recently. Klaid felt weightless. Like a thing that had been balanced wrong his whole life had finally been set down on its proper face. Everything fit. The room. The rules. The blade. The space of all of it.

Home. The word arrived before he could reject it, and he didn't have it in him, just then, to argue.

The Curator's vellum face tilted.

"You laugh."

"Sorry."

"You do not sound sorry."

"I'm not really sorry."

"You laugh." The Curator repeated, voice flat, then cracking. The vellum face had no eyes, and still Kage felt it narrow. "You stand inside the Margin with my Master's stolen hand on the table, and you find it amusing."

The brush-blade lifted a degree.

"No," Kage said. He let the laugh settle, but the lightness stayed, and that was the part the Curator clearly couldn't parse. "Not at all. I'm not laughing at the stakes."

He rolled his wrist. Mumyo answered, weightless, the liquid-crimson vein along its spine pulsing once against his palm like a slow heartbeat. The blade wanted to move. It always wanted to move.

"It's just funny to me," Kage said, "that you had a better shot at what you wanted before you put us in this box."

The Curator went still.

"You want me gone." Kage tilted his head toward the dimmed wall, toward the muffled thunder of the Discarded Draft hammering at nine people who'd followed him down here. "Out there, your odds were fine. You had attrition. You had numbers. You're a level forty Axiom Entity, whatever that is."

He raised the shortsword. Point loose. Distance measured. The body remembered the geometry without consulting him.

"Then you got proud. No. Insulted?" he said. "You decided the swordsman insulted you and your master, and you pulled me in here for a fair fight. One on one. No raid mechanics. No adds I can't reach. No marks, no anchors I have to babysit." A thin smile crossed his face and vanished, there and gone. "In here, it's me and you, in a room, in a box. In a. Duel."

The vellum face didn't move for a while.

"You are confident."

"No. No, I'm not. I'm something else. I haven't figured out the word yet."

"It is arrogance."

"It might be."

The pages in the Curator's compact body turned faster, an edge somewhere in the chest flipping over and over, words sliding into view and away.

"And the legacy," Kage went on, because he found he wanted to say it, found the words rising up clean and unhurried the way a good verse arrives, "I'll be taking that with me. You think there's one shape to it. One correct hand. Your Master's hand." He shook his head. "But. I've come to realize something recently. Art… has all kinds of expression. The First Maker wrote a world with poems. Fine." He turned the blade a quarter so the white light slid down its edge. "I will write mine with this, then. In the end, it's the same hand. It just holds something different."

The pages stopped turning.

For a moment, one entire held breath, the Curator said nothing, and Kage couldn't tell which of the things had landed.

Got you, Kage thought, and then, more honestly, no. Maybe got myself. Because he'd meant it. That was the strange part. He'd said it to wind the thing up and discovered halfway through that it was true.

"All kinds of expression." The Curator repeated.

"All kinds."

"That," the Curator said, quietly, "is the sort of thing the Master would have said."

The brush came down level. The stance settled. Kage's weight dropped, his lead foot finding the angle on its own, the old school answering a call he'd thought he'd buried in a closet in a different life.

The duel was about to begin.

Or so he thought.

The Curator did not move to strike. Instead the brush-blade rose, point to the white ceiling, and the page-body straightened out of its stance, formal, ceremonial, and that wrongness pinged in Kage's spine a half-second before the system did.

"You claim your expression is worth the mantle," the Curator said. "A blade. Steel and motion. You name it art and you ask me believe it is worthy of my Master's mantle." The vellum face turned toward him, blank and absolute. "I am the preserver of his truth. If you mean to wear the signature, then prove that your hand is worth the page."

The brush-blade swept down in a single clean stroke.

Where the tip passed, the white floor split, and black script poured up out of the cut, not the dense fused letters of the cage but living text, flowing, climbing the walls, spilling across the ceiling until the whole box was sentences in motion.

Then Kage felt it leave him. The floating ink that orbited his arm lifted off his skin all at once and streamed away into the walls, drawn into the Curator's growing narrative like filings to a magnet. His arm went bare.

[Axiom Field Established: The Curator's Narrative.]

[The domain imposes its own composition. While the field holds, all reality within the Margin conforms to The Curator's authored truth.]

[Author's Ink has been claimed by the dominant narrative.]

The sentences on the walls resolved as he watched, archaic letters arranging themselves into a single verdict that wrapped the entire box, floor to ceiling, a story being told over him and around him and about him.

In the Margin, the thief moves slow, and the steel he holds is only steel, and the page does not remember his name.

The script settled. The white light dimmed to the color of old parchment. And Kage felt it take hold of the room itself, the air thickening, the floor going certain under the Curator's terms, a truth being imposed that said his blade was nothing and his hand was nothing and the page had already decided how this ended.

The Curator lowered the brush.

"Now," he said. "Write, if you can."

Alright, Kage thought, as he looked at the white floor between them, the only blank page left in this world.
