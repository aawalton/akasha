Kivamus smiled. "Go on then."

The majordomo tapped the next page in his ledger.

“Unlike the manor residents, where we are absorbing the higher food prices because we do not want them becoming too dissatisfied, we will deduct the actual inflated cost of food from those living in the longhouse blocks. We are buying each sack of grain at over 6 gold now, compared to below 4 gold in the past, and their lodging is still free. On the other hand, we provide equipment, better clothing, and similar necessities to the guards, which we do not provide to ordinary villagers, which is why the guards' deduction was 4 copper a day even with us absorbing the higher food cost for them. This way, the deduction for the villagers also comes to 4 coppers a day just because of the high food prices.”

He looked up. “That means instead of the expected 8 coppers a day, the laborers would receive 4 coppers a day in wages, while still continuing to receive their food and common supplies like soap and salt as long as they continue living in the longhouse blocks. That leaves their wage cost at 389 gold a month.”

“We could apply the same deductions to the master craftsmen and foremen too,” Duvas added, “but that would only save around 10 gold a month, since some of them already have their own houses and are not living in the blocks. But we don't want them feeling disgruntled, so I left that out.”

“Good,” Kivamus said at once. “In the big picture, 10 gold is a trivial price for keeping those people here. Nearly everything in our village depends on them.”

Duvas inclined his head, then went on.

“That settles wages for the villagers. But we still have to feed those elderly people and children who cannot work and are living in the longhouse blocks, as well as the food cost for the other people living there as well. For just above 500 people—which is everyone who isn't directly employed in the manor—eating 3 meals a day, we would need around 74 sacks of grain every month. At current prices, that comes to 441 gold a month.”

He lowered the papers. “So our monthly expenses will be 494 gold for the manor residents and guards, including wages and provisions; 66 gold for the wages of master craftsmen and foremen; 389 gold in wages for the laborers after deduction; and 441 gold for feeding and providing for everyone living in the longhouse blocks. One good thing is that this covers the cost of making all the mining and logging tools—shovels, picks, saws and axes; transportation vehicles—wagons, carts, log-movers and wheelbarrows; weapons like swords, shields, daggers, and spears, as well as the crossbows and the scorpions, since the Fedarus wood is free, and the wages have already been accounted for. We just have to spend some extra to buy iron, but its prices are still running lower than average, and even then it only comes out to 30 to 40 gold a month at most for buying a wagon load of it—even if we are buying in excess of what we need these days, anticipating a rise in prices in the future."

The majordomo swallowed once before finishing. “That brings the total to around 1,430 gold a month, which is... much higher than what we earn." He looked openly worried now. “Even after Trevalo begins the new trade route, we are only expecting 1,220 gold of revenue in the coming month. Which means that, at least for now, we would still be running at a loss..." He let the papers rest on the table. “I spent hours going over it earlier today,” he added quietly. “The numbers are correct.”

Then he looked back at Kivamus. “Now that you know the exact numbers, are you still sure we should start paying the villagers now?"

Kivamus nodded. The real amounts of their expenses were disappointing for sure, but they were still in the ballpark of what he had estimated.

“Yeah,” he said. “This is actually somewhat better than what I had guessed. We are not in profit yet, but despite the slower coal sales, we have already more than doubled our revenue from last autumn. I think we will be in the green soon. Once Trevalo’s new route settles and we sell more coal, we should become profitable within a couple of months.”

Duvas still looked uneasy. “But we are in deficit right now. We will be losing above 200 gold every month. Even if you are not keeping anything for yourself, it doesn't seem like a good idea to do this...”

“You’re not looking at the full picture,” Kivamus smiled. “You said yourself that coal production should rise to the expected 90 wagon loads within a month or two from the current 57 wagon loads, and that's based on the current number of workers in the village, which will only keep rising. That alone should increase our monthly revenue by around 330 gold from the increase of coal sales in Ulriga, and that requires no major new investment from us beyond the extra tools we were already going to make. That is enough by itself to push us into profit. But we have other sources of revenue too which will come online, uh... become active soon.”

He leaned forward a little. “Our medicine sales should increase within a month or so, once the encampment in the hills is built and we can process more losuvil there. We will still have to manage the sales carefully, but after our talks with Pydaso, I think we can raise our current medicine sales by 50% without causing a glut and lowering the market price in Ulriga. In the future, I want to increase its output much more and bring the price down so commoners can afford it too even while maintaining our revenue, but that is for later.”

He kept going, “I have also already begun speaking with and selecting workers who can make soap. That will take about a month to smooth out, but after that it will become another source of revenue. It won't earn us anywhere near the medicine's income, but it's still useful. The same goes for paper. Paper production is already rising because of the new trip hammers and the new woodpress machine. The sawmills are processing enough logs now to create more than enough sawdust to increase the paper production.”

Kivamus gave a small smile before continuing. “Once the tenement blocks are ready, that will become another source of income, though that will take longer. After that, more skilled craftsmen like Isuha should start moving here, and I want to start building the weaving and sewing machines soon after. That will probably have to wait for the winter months though. But from next spring onward, that should increase revenue again.”

He spread one hand slightly. “Of course our expenses will keep rising too as we build more and feed more people. But once the coal output rises to 90 wagon loads over the next month or two, I think we should remain profitable in a sustainable way after that.”

“And don’t forget the wheat,” he added. “Our first wheat crop should mature within a few months. It will not be enough to feed the whole village for a full year like we first hoped, because our population has already doubled and will keep growing. But it will still save us a great deal of money we would otherwise spend buying grain from visiting merchants. Once that crop comes in, our expenses will fall for a while. Or rather, it will allow us to feed the village for the winter months when we can't trade, without spending any coin on buying a large amount of food—which is still our single biggest expense at nearly 600 gold every month. That will help to make up for the loss of income from selling coal or medicines for those months.”

Duvas nodded slowly. “You’re right. I spent so many hours staring at the numbers that I stopped thinking about the longer term. The loggers are still clearing more land in the south, so by next spring we should be able to double or even triple the planted area for the next crop. And we are also planting potatoes and liwabeans in the winter. That will help too.”

“Exactly,” Kivamus said. “So for the next couple of months, we use our savings to cover the deficit until the village becomes profitable.”

He glanced at the figures again. “We have around 1,700 gold saved right now. Even if we are losing 200 gold a month for the moment, we can use that reserve to cover the deficit. By our current estimates, the deficit should be cut in half within a month as the coal production increases, and then we should break-even in another month at most. After that, coal sales alone should carry us into profit.”

He tapped the ledger lightly. “If we can increase medicine or paper sales before then, or manage to get soap selling quickly, then we become profitable earlier. But even if it takes the full 2 months, that only means spending around 400 gold to keep things running.”

He smiled faintly. “Even after setting aside 400 gold for mercenaries, we would still have around 900 gold in reserve. That can be kept for trouble with the mercenaries—in case they get greedy—or we can save it toward the autumn taxes. We do have to repay the debt to Trevalo for bringing us the wheat without taking payment, but he will understand if we have to postpone it a little more. So really, we should thank Torhan for kindly donating his savings. Without that, all this would have been much harder."

Duvas let out a short breath through his nose.

Kivamus continued, “Even if we wanted to delay the coin payments, I don’t think it would be wise. Morale would suffer a lot. The announcement has already been made anyway, and you saw how happy everyone looked. This way the village can finally start returning to something like normal. Merchants can return to their own trades instead of doing laborer work. That will increase the taxes we collect from them too. Either way, our situation is never going to get perfect for this. I think this is the right moment. We will begin immediately.”

Duvas inclined his head. “As you wish. I will make the arrangements, and I’ll have a few guards present when I begin distributing the weekly wages to the villages.”

“Good,” Kivamus said. “By the way, how is the tax demand looking this year? I know you won’t have the final number until the end of autumn, but I still want an estimate if you have it.”
