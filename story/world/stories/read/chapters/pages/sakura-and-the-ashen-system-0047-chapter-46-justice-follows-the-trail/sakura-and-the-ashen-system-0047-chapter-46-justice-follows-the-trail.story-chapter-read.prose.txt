Faerd pursued them methodically and coldly. Track by track, he rebuilt their path and moved on with the training of a man who had been taught very well how to find fugitives.

The forest changed around him, the trails tangled, and the tracks often broke off. But right now, that did not matter, because he knew how to gather small details into one line. He found fragments: a broken branch, a strange mark on the ground, a residual trace of magic. The Obsidian Hierophant and the lightning mage could not disappear without a trace. So unless they had suddenly become spirits, he knew for certain: the living always left tracks. Even when they were sure they did not.

After Ogrom, those tracks had led into the forest. At that stage, they were chaotic, blurred by blood, mud, smoke, and general panic. The city had been noisy then, the guard had been in an uproar, and the people had not known what to do with themselves. A fire was spreading, the tavern was burning down, and people were looking for someone to blame. But beneath that layer of noise, he still found the important thing: the direction.

There were tracks on the ground beyond the drains. They led into the forest, and after that everything became simpler. Not for an ordinary person, but he had not been made for pursuit for nothing.

He moved without rest longer than any normal soldier could have. Sometimes he stopped, but only to crouch beside a broken branch or touch the ground with his fingers. On the road, he did not so much listen to sounds as to traces of magic. He could distinguish it better than many mages born with great power. The Hierophant had a heavy, dark trail, unlike any mage’s. Her magic seemed foreign and wrong for their world. Though his own magic had a similar aftertaste.

Every time Faerd thought about that girl named Sakura for too long, a familiar, trained feeling rose in his chest: the Command.

The violator must be eliminated. The threat must be stopped. The Obsidian Hierophant must not survive.

Those formulas had long ago become part of the inner mechanism fused to his body as firmly as obsidian magic. Sometimes it seemed to him that if all the commands were ever cut out of him, only emptiness and exhaustion would remain inside.

On the second day of the pursuit, he came across the tracks of a wagon and a donkey harness. Some time after that, he heard music. More precisely, at first he heard not even music, but a wrong kind of carelessness, something like laughter, idle chatter, and the rattling of little metal trinkets. Someone was walking through the forest as if the world did not try to eat him alive every day. To him, that immediately seemed both stupid and almost insulting to the world he existed in.

Faerd came out to them silently. It was a group of Free Wanderers, just as he had thought. People in bright scraps of clothing, with junk and goods, with a donkey and no ability to behave quietly or carefully.

They did not notice him right away. They did not look like people who noticed danger quickly at all. More likely, they would notice a predator only when it was already right in front of their noses.

First one of the men lifted his head and narrowed his eyes in displeasure, thinking an ordinary stranger was coming toward them. Then he saw Faerd’s face, his scars, his too-straight back, his too-broad shoulders, the threat in his stride, and the smile on his face died instantly.

"Life gave you one hell of a face, friend," one of them said with a nervous chuckle, realizing too late that the joke had been a bad one.

Faerd stopped a few steps from the wagon.

"Two people passed through here. A man and a girl. They had a large beast with them. You saw them."

The Wanderers exchanged glances. Too much flashed in their eyes: caution, alarm, the desire to lie. One of them even managed to shift slightly to the side, covering the woman by the wagon with his own body.

"We see many people," answered a lean man with a bright scarf in his hair. The same one who clearly considered himself the leader. "The road loves faces, but it doesn’t oblige us to remember them all."

A lie. Faerd stepped closer.

"You saw them."

"And even if we did?" the same man said, quieter now. "Who’s asking?"

Faerd did not give his name. A name was rarely required of him, and in these lands it meant nothing anyway. Instead, he raised his hand, and a thin obsidian spike grew between his fingers with a dry crack. That was enough to make these people tense. The woman by the wagon gave a quiet gasp. The donkey jerked. One of the men instinctively reached for a knife, but froze at once when Faerd looked his way.

"You will tell me where they went."

"Listen," the lean man began quickly, trying to keep his voice even. "Maybe we can settle this peacefully..."

The obsidian spike entered the man’s shoulder before the sentence was finished. It was not a fatal blow. But it was only the first blow.

The man screamed and collapsed to his knees, clutching the wound. The others stirred, but still did not dare rush forward. Faerd had seen that moment many times, when people understood that the person in front of them was not someone they could shout down, push away, or frighten. In front of them stood disaster.

"The next one goes into the throat," he said in the same even voice. "Where did they go?"

The lean man went pale. Resistance still flickered in his eyes, saying that he wanted to somehow protect strangers without dying for them. Faerd knew such ideas never lasted long.

"East," the man breathed at last. "Through the forest. I don’t know anything else."

"You’re lying."

"No."

Faerd stepped even closer and seized him by the chin so sharply that the man’s teeth clicked together. His whole body jerked.

"You know more."

"No, I swear! We sold them some junk and parted ways!"

Faerd understood that this time the man was telling the truth. He read it by his breathing, by the trembling in his face, by his eyes and tone. After that, he released the wounded man just as abruptly.

The man staggered back, barely staying on his feet.

Faerd looked at the others, and none of them moved. No one rushed to save the wounded man. There were no heroes among these people. They only waited in silence and horror to see how much more he would take from them.

But he did not take more. He was carrying out an order, but he took no pleasure in being a bloody butcher. On one hand, he understood that leaving these people alive made no sense. On the other, killing them was just as pointless. The trail he had found in their camp mattered more than anything else.

He went on without looking back at the wounded man’s groans or the frozen silence behind him. Only when those people’s shadows dissolved deep behind the trees did something improper and memory-like stir in his chest again. He remembered the bright scarf, and someone’s ringing voice, and laughter. For one short moment, those people reminded him of something of his own, distant. Home? Summer? A woman’s voice?

Pain came after the memories. Sharp and burning, as if a brand were being seared into his skin. It was always like that when he tried to look too deeply inside himself.

Faerd clenched his teeth and walked faster.

The trail led him to the canyon. He felt it before he saw the rift itself. The forest was damp after the rain, but the ground more often shifted to clayey rock, stone more often showed through beneath the soil, and the sounds began to drop downward. Then the trees parted, and a vast gray chasm opened before him.

He stopped at the edge. The canyon was enormous and old. Convenient for those who wanted to hide. It held a whole labyrinth of ledges, cracks, caves, and stone shelves. Moving across them, it was easy to slip and die. Without knowing the way, a person could wander there until death. But with the way, one could live there for a long time.

He did not find the descent immediately, but he found it all the same. The narrow path hid between stones, and the rain had managed to erase almost everything the fugitives had left behind. On soft ground, the prints had blurred. On open stone, the water had washed away the mud, and the small impressions had turned into cloudy stains.

But the rain had not saved them completely. Under a stone overhang, a strip of disturbed dust remained. At the edge of the path, someone had shifted a small flat stone, exposing a dry pale spot beneath it. A little lower, in a crack, a clump of mud had gotten stuck, brought there from the sole of a shoe. Two people had descended here carefully, while the huge beast had been left above. It was visible from the bark cut by a claw on a root and from the long groove in the wet earth that the rain had not yet managed to wash away completely.

Faerd crouched beside one of the boulders and ran his fingers over a brown stain on the stone. Rain blurred blood too, but not at once. In the hollows, it still held in a thin rusty film.

The beast had fought someone here. Unfortunately, the rain had destroyed most of the battle traces before he could build the full picture.

He went down without haste, but faster than an ordinary person would have dared. Where others would have had to hold on with both hands and think about every foothold, his body moved more easily. He had an excellent sense of balance, and beyond that, he had no fear of heights at all. The fears sitting in his depths were certainly not waiting for him on the edge of a cliff.

On the way, he noticed the local wildlife. A thin dark creature with enormous eyes sat on a ledge and watched him. A scavenger, and not too far from the first, another. And another. They did not attack at once, but they were too nervous, as if someone had already stirred them up earlier.

He descended almost to the very bottom, found traces of traps, people, and hastily stretched tents, and understood: he was close.

The settlement was even worse than he had expected. Just a flimsy camp built so it could be abandoned in half an hour. About twenty people, if counting the youngest and the weakest. Mages. Pitiful people, hiding in a stone crack and trying not to become prey.

Faerd watched them from above for some time, not coming out immediately. He saw how they moved between the tents, how they repaired something after recent disorder. They spoke more quietly and looked around more often than they should have. Tension and paranoia hung among them. The signs of a recent battle were so large here that even the rain could not wash that ugliness away. All that tension drifting in the air clearly said one thing: the Hierophant and the lightning mage had already been here.

That was enough.

Faerd jumped down onto the lower ledge without warning. The mages panicked immediately, as expected. Someone shouted, and someone else had already raised a weapon and prepared to create a defensive spell. But it was too late.

A sturdy, grim man by the central fire stepped forward first. He raised his hand, a protective sign flared around his fingers, and someone behind him shouted:

"Marven!"

Faerd did not remember the name. It did not matter.

An obsidian spike shot from his palm and struck the man in the side of the chest. The camp leader was thrown back against the stones. He hit the back of his head, slid down, and did not rise again.

The mages froze for a moment. That moment was enough for him. The second obsidian arrow struck a tent support, and the tent collapsed sideways along with the people under it. The third, longer and thicker, pierced a crate and the man behind it in one blow.

That was when the real panic began.

He descended lower with the same frightening calm that always made people start making mistakes faster.

"Where is the Hierophant?" he asked.

No one answered. They still hoped to fight him off. One mage struck with a rune of cold, another unleashed fire from a scroll, someone hid behind a shield. All of it was weak. He simply raised his hand, and a dense, heavy obsidian sphere gathered in the air in a single breath. He threw it into the nearest cover.

The tent, the stones, and the people behind them exploded into black grit and dust. Screams rang out, full of horror and agony. Wheezing and coughing followed.

"Where is the Hierophant?" Faerd repeated.

A woman with a staff looked at him with hatred and stayed silent. He created a short obsidian spear and drove it under her ribs.

He did not enjoy it. He did not feel guilt. He felt almost nothing. It was simply the next step. If they did not answer, remove the obstacle. If the obstacle was removed, move on to the next. That was how he had been taught.

Nearby, a young mage tried to run toward a distant crack in the rock. Faerd did not even fully turn his body. He only flicked his wrist in that direction, and a thin scatter of obsidian needles pinned the fugitive to the stone.

"Where is the Hierophant?"

"Go to hell," someone rasped.

Faerd looked at the speaker. He was older than the others, with gray hair at his temples and trembling hands. The courage in his eyes was held together only by the understanding that he did not have long left anyway.

An obsidian blade grew straight out of the air beside Faerd and pierced the man’s throat. Then something in the mages around him finally broke. Sometimes people could hold on through the first killing, through the second. They held on to courage and pride exactly until the moment they saw that death was not an image and not a possibility, but a calculation already underway.

One of the scouts, wounded in the shoulder, collapsed to his knees.

"They left," he breathed. "They left this place."

"Where?"

"I don’t know."

Faerd stepped toward him. The scout lifted his head, saw the approaching obsidian stinger, and spoke faster.

"East," he breathed. "Along the upper river. They asked for the road to the old tract. I don’t know anything else!"

He withdrew the stinger. East. Upper river. Old tract. That meant the Lake of Six Mists.

He swept his gaze over the camp, watching someone groan under the wreckage, someone clutch their wounds. Several people looked at him with that dull horror that remains after slaughter, when the mind has not yet caught up with the fact that half the familiar faces will never stand up again.

Faerd felt something strange. Not pity, but something more blurred. An echo of some foreign thought about these people. That they were very weak. And still, they hid, clung on, guarded their pitiful tents and their dying. For what? A few more days? An empty hope? For a moment, something else flashed before his eyes: that same memory again, of warm light from a house, of a woman on a porch with a child in her arms.

Maev. Tinli.

Pain struck instantly, like a burning hook driven beneath his ribs. He drew in a sharp breath. He would have staggered if he had allowed himself such weakness. But Faerd did not allow it. He only shut that memory away as brutally as he had once been ordered to do every time. He reminded himself that he had no past. No family or friends. Only the target.

He turned and walked away from the ruined camp. Behind him, groans still sounded, along with the crackle of burning fabric. Somewhere, a shard of obsidian rolled over the stones, and someone was crying. Faerd did not look back. He knew enough. The Hierophant and the mage had gone to the Lake of Six Mists.

That meant the trail stretched there. And that meant he would keep following it until one day he caught them both and brought this matter to its end.
