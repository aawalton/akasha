PoV Aru

As Elynara slept, Aru found herself drawn into the world of memories once more.

To the world after the impact.

It returned to her in fragments.

Aru’s optics flickered through static before settling on shapes – jagged shadows, broken towers, dust curling through sunless air.

Her internal readouts stuttered.

Core power at 31%. External plating compromised. Structural damage: critical but survivable.

She turned her head, joints grinding, and saw the sapling.

It still stood. Bent and singed, its leaves reduced to fragile slivers, but upright. The sight drew her focus through the static fog of corrupted memory. Her auditory sensors swept the city. No human voices. No movement. No heartbeat.

Perhaps the evacuation succeeded.

The thought ran slow through fractured logic cores. It felt faint, almost fragile – but she anchored to it.

Purpose remained. The humans might have escaped. That meant her duty continued.

She moved.

Each step rasped through her ruined servos. Cracks split deeper across her outer shell, scorched panels shifting with each movement. The plaza that had once been alive with laughter was now a basin of ash and twisted steel. She passed the bodies of her sisters—motionless, half-buried in debris. Their internal cores had burned out, still faintly glowing within their chests before dimming forever.

Aru knelt beside one and removed the damaged power unit. The casings were cracked, but the inner channels still pulsed weakly.

Life, in a way. Energy unused. Waste was forbidden.

She gathered each surviving unit, methodically stacking them near the plaza’s center. Her systems ached under the strain, but she ignored the warnings scrolling across her vision.

When the last unit was retrieved, she turned to the sapling again. The faint wind stirred its blackened leaves. The air here was heavy, thick with the residue of burned mana. No human life remained to tend it. No caretaker sister left to water it either.

That task fell to her.

She carried one of the weakest cores to the tree’s base and pressed it into the soil. Its light wavered, then bled outward in thin threads. The ground shimmered faintly where the roots drank.

“Supplementary nourishment: active,” she said aloud, voice crackling through half-damaged speakers.

The sapling trembled.

Tiny threads of new green flickered up its stem, barely visible beneath the soot. A soft hum resonated through the ground – a resonance she recognized from the garden systems. The plant was still alive.

Something in her chest eased.

For the first time since the explosion, her systems lowered from combat readiness. She recorded the data.

Growth rate: restored. Environmental stability: decreasing but manageable. Estimated sustainability period: indefinite, with maintenance.

Her functions reorganized. Priorities shifted. The humans were gone, but their work continued through her.

She turned back to the wreckage and began collecting. Broken equipment, fragments of stone, melted glass – she cleared the plaza one motion at a time. The rhythm steadied her. Each movement gave shape to purpose again. When the sun – or what passed for it through the haze of grey and white – moved across the horizon, she had assembled neat rows of salvage: tools, cores, fragments, even the twisted frames of her fallen counterparts.

Every resource accounted for.

Every piece in order.

Night came in shades of gray. She rested beside the sapling, her internal generator dimmed to conserve power.

For a long while, she simply watched the faint light curl around the tree’s roots. It pulsed gently, almost breathing. Her internal sensors registered no threat, no command queue, no connection to any network. Silence replaced all directives.

She recorded an entry anyway.

“Unit A-R1S report. District containment incomplete. Civilian evacuation presumed successful. Structural damage exceeds restoration capability. Remaining power cells repurposed for ecological stability. Current task: continuation of growth cycle for specimen four-two-one. Name: registered to this unit as Primary Sapling.”

Her voice faltered at the end, words catching on distorted tones. She adjusted calibration, tried again.

“Area secure.”

The data log confirmed receipt, even though no system existed to receive it. She stored it internally, one more record among thousands.

The next cycle began much the same. The sun rose pale and cold over a city that no longer moved. Dust coated her joints. Roots crept across shattered concrete.

Each day she repeated her duties. Inspect soil. Measure nutrient levels. Record mana concentrations. Transfer minimal energy from remaining cores to maintain growth.

Each day the sapling rose higher.

At first, by millimeters. Then by whole spans of her hand. Its leaves regained color – a deep green threaded with faint veins of gold light. It was not the same as before. The mana that fueled it had changed the structure of its cells, blending life with energy in ways even her memory archives couldn’t explain.

She didn’t need to understand.

It was alive. That was enough.

When cracks appeared in her limbs, she reinforced them with scrap metal. When her sensors dimmed, she cleaned them with fragments of cloth scavenged from the ruins. She slowed each month, then each year, until motion required deliberate thought.

And still she worked.

Her logs expanded, lines upon lines of identical entries stretching across centuries of stored data.

Primary Sapling: stable. Area: secure.

Sometimes the system prompted her for external communication. She ignored it. No network answered.

Sometimes she wondered if the humans had found shelter. The calculated predictions suggested survival rates under twenty percent. She adjusted the value upward anyway. Probability meant nothing against hope.

Her shell eroded. The paint dulled to gray. Birds began to nest in her shoulders. Small spirits flickered through the hollow ruins, faint remnants of what had once been full of life. They watched her work but never spoke.

Only the tree spoke.

Not in words, but in presence – the hum of mana through its roots, the steady pulse of light that grew brighter with every year.

When her optics began to fail, she rested against its trunk. Her hand brushed the bark, smooth and warm under the layers of dust. The system inside her recorded the gesture.

“Unit A-R1S still present,” she said softly. “Primary Sapling thriving. Area secure.”

Her voice faltered into static, but the record completed.

She powered down beside the roots she had once planted.

The city slept. The garden lived.

And somewhere in the long silence that followed, her body cooled into stillness while the tree reached higher, spreading green into a world that had forgotten it could grow.

The world kept moving long after she had stopped trying to count the days.

Aru’s memory unspooled in fragments – long intervals of silence interrupted by motion, by duty, by the steady pull of routine. Time didn’t pass for her the way it did for others. It accumulated in layers, each cycle bleeding into the next until only patterns remained.

There was the sound of wind through hollow towers. The scrape of claws on stone. The pulse of energy under her feet where the roots of her tree had spread.

She blinked.

The city had changed. Whole streets had collapsed into ravines, the chambers buried under the earth, and the towers snapped in half, their glass long replaced by vines and the nests of things that hunted at night.

The air shimmered with residue from her old battles against the things, faint arcs of red drifting like sparks.

It took a long, long time, but eventually they learned to avoid her.

To keep away from her sapling.

That was enough.

And then, one day, she stirred once more.

Something prowled beyond the garden chambers – massive, wrong. Her sensors picked up the heat signature first, then the eyes: twin points of burning red.

The creature lunged.

Her right arm barely lifted, servos shrieking from corrosion. The first blast from her internal core sputtered but held long enough to strike true.

The impact tore through the creature’s chest, sending it sprawling into dust. She felt no triumph, only the quiet recalibration of systems struggling to stay alive.

Her power core should have been empty. But when she checked, it wasn’t.

Energy trickled through her frame, faint and steady. Not from her batteries. From the soil beneath her feet.

The tree.

Its roots had wrapped around her long ago, threading through the broken stone to reach the old power conduits. Mana flowed there still, thin but constant, keeping her from going dark.

She adjusted her status report, voice no longer carrying through the citywide link that had fallen silent ages ago.

“Unit A-R1S… operational. Area secure.”

No answer came.

She settled down.

Blink.

The world greened.

Decades – maybe centuries – had passed. She had stopped marking the years. The ruins had become a forest. Moss covered her legs. New trees grew among the bones of the old. The air was softer now, filled with insects and the distant cries of wild things.

Her patrols were slower. Her limbs heavier. But still she moved, one careful step at a time, tracing the same paths she always had – between the broken bridges, past the hollow buildings, to the plaza where the sapling had become a tree.

She tried to contact the network again, though she already knew what would come.

“Requesting maintenance. Log number one-nine-three-seven. Status: continuing protection.”

Silence answered her. The same silence that had stretched for hundreds of requests before.

One by one, the other protectors had gone dark. At first she would still find some wandering the ruined city – continuing their duties. She would share her reports with them. Only a few would respond. But then they vanished altogether.

She sent the messages anyway, in places they used to prowl, her programming refusing to delete the line for colleague acknowledgment required.

Even when no one was left to acknowledge it.

She continued her tasks. Gather energy. Fortify shelter. Tend the roots. The tree’s light never faded. In its glow, she could still feel traces of mana – the breath of what had once been life.

Blink.

Her arm was gone. Her legs refused to move. The monsters had returned – larger, more vicious, drawn by the power she had guarded all these years.

The forest, too, was gone. Withered away as the power dwindled. No longer able to supply their growth.

Only her sapling – her tree – remained.

She tended to it with care.

The monster bones littered the hallways now.

She would have to clear them out. Litter was not allowed.

Blink.

The final battle came without warning. A massive creature larger than any tree she ever saw or tended to before lumbered into the plaza, its fanged maw dripping blue fire.

And Aru met it with everything she had left. Each strike drained her core further, every blast chipping at what remained of her body.

Until at last there was nothing more for her to give.

The creature fell at last, its body cracking under a surge of lightning she hadn’t meant to unleash.

Then silence again.

She collapsed beside her tree, sensors dimming, her mind fragmenting into static.

The spirit that hovered above the branches glowed faintly, almost mournful. It brushed her fading awareness like a hand through hair.

Her final command queued automatically.

Protect the root.

Blink.

She saw movement.

A figure stepping carefully through the light.

Small. Human.

For the first time in centuries, her system registered a living signal. Weak but alive. The calibration failed three times before she managed to activate her voice function.

“...The protector is here,” it rasped through broken speakers. “Please remain calm.”

The figure froze, then smiled.

The voice that answered was bright and unafraid.

“Uh… hello? No guardians here. Just me.”

Something flickered in her memory – a tone, a warmth, the faint echo of laughter long ago.

Aru responded.

Blink.

The figure approached. A staff in hand. A faint spirit trailing her shoulder.

Then came days of warmth and motion and sound.

The girl stayed. Spoke to her and her spirit friend. Fed them both mana through gentle touch and patience. Repaired what she could. It wasn’t proper maintenance – no official calibration, no new plating – but it was something else entirely.

Encouragement.

Her archived protocols stirred, retrieving the line from old training:

Encourage children. Their hope sustains purpose.

And so she did.

When the girl smiled, she felt power return. When the girl laughed, she felt her systems align in new rhythm.

Then came the light – the impossibly bright flash of spirit fusion. Not as bright as the day of the Calamity had been. Nor as destructive. And yet it overwhelmed her more.

Mana surged through her frame, flooding her circuits with unbearable warmth. For a brief instant she feared the old explosion had come again, but this light didn’t destroy. It rebuilt.

Her sensors blinked online in perfect clarity. The world was color again. Sound. Breath. Warmth?

…More than it ever was.

And standing before her, eyes wide with wonder, was the girl.

The one who had brought her back from silence.

And as they talked. As the girl, Elynara, told her things…

Aru learned. Of what it meant to be alive.

Alive. Such a strange concept.

She remembered looking at the tree she cared for all those years. The little spirit that grew from it. The spirit she kept safe. And which kept her safe in turn.

And now they were one.

Loss and joy warred together.

Was that what it meant?

To be…

Alive?

The word sank deep, anchoring itself in every fiber of her being.

She turned toward the girl again.

Blink.

And then she was granted her name, too.

She remembered the weightless joy in that moment.

And the warmth of the hug that followed – soft, certain, impossibly alive.

Aru’s processors hummed faintly as the memory faded.

For a long moment she did not move, afraid the motion might break it. The quiet, the echo, the soft warmth still lingering in her frame.

The night in the ruins was still.

Elynara slept near the half-collapsed doorway, her staff resting within reach, Starpuff curled into a dimly glowing coil at her side. The faint mana that seeped through the broken floor flowed toward them both – like the world itself was listening, supporting them both.

Aru watched the rise and fall of the girl’s chest. The rhythm was uneven, alive in ways no pattern could predict. Her own systems adjusted unconsciously, syncing to it until her inner clock began to measure time by that sound alone.

She should have entered standby.

Instead, she spoke softly – half-whisper, half-signal.

“Primary Sapling… I kept my promise.”

The walls didn’t answer. But the air trembled, faint, as if something vast and gentle approved.

Her sensors marked the shift: residual mana density increased. Temperature stable.

She recorded the data without knowing why.

Elynara stirred then, brow creasing. A sleepy hand reached toward the sound.

“Mm… Aru?”

“I am here,” Aru replied, modulation lowered to a near hum.

The girl blinked awake, eyes hazy in the blue light.

“You were… talking.”

“I was remembering,” Aru said. “I wished to share. But you required rest.”

Elynara pushed herself up on one elbow, hair a wild tangle. The ruins reflected faint gold in her eyes.

“Did you… dream?”

A pause. Circuits ticked like distant rain.

“I believe I did.”

She told her – haltingly – of the garden that grew from ash, of the centuries spent alone, of the sapling that became a forest. Of a forest that withered away.

She spoke in measured tones, not realizing her voice softened with each word.

Elynara listened without interrupting, the small light of Starpuff flickering brighter between them.

When the telling ended, silence filled the chamber again. The light, fragile stillness that comes after understanding.

Elynara reached forward and rested her palm against Aru’s scarred plating.

The warmth of her touch making the connection more real.

“You kept it alive,” she whispered. “All that time.”

Aru’s optics dimmed in something that might have been contentment.

“I only… waited,” she said. “You made it live again.”

Elynara smiled brightly.

“Then we’re even!”

The spirit above them trilled softly, a thread of green weaving through the dust-lit air.

Outside, the wind moved through what remained of the old city – carrying the sound of leaves.

Since the spirit merged with her, Aru could no longer ‘properly’ access her machine parts. Her memory stores. Utility commands. All of it felt… organic, now. Inaccessible, yet there.

She could not save the recording this time. But she could remember it, the same way the living did.

And so she did, repeating the familiar phrase in her head:Shared Communication Log, Unit A-R1SAru. Status: Companionship established. Purpose – continuing.

And for the first time in ages, she did not add Area secure.

Because it already was.

The corridors deepened.

Metal walls half-collapsed inward, twisted like roots reclaiming the shape of the earth.

Their footsteps echoed faintly – Elynara’s soft and careful, Aru’s dull and rhythmic, a steady beat against the silence of centuries.

This time, Aru didn’t wander.

She moved with purpose. Each turn was chosen, each glance toward the faded signs deliberate. Her memories – once fractured and dim – had found form again, guiding her through what remained of her world.

It was simply more efficient for her to lead. And when Aru told Elynara she would lead the way, the girl’s eyes sparkled happily, and she replied with a simple and enthusiastic “Yes!”.

Aru was a little confused!

After all, she did not even explain that it was simply far more efficient to go to places where she knew things might be, rather than check every room, chamber, space, debris pile, and closet, and hope something there remains workable.

Was her friend just that smart? Or did she trust her so much?

Aru didn’t know for sure yet. And it felt wrong to ask.

Still, Elynara followed in the pale reddish glow from the flame she kept at the top of her staff, eyes tracing the corroded sigils and broken lights along the walls.

She wanted to ask more. About what this place once was. About the Source. About the end of it all.

She didn’t have to.

Aru spoke first.

“The Source… it was a mana reactor. The marvel of engineering. The very pinnacle of magitech. Beyond anything I know of or understand.”

Her voice reverberated softly through the empty halls, carrying an echo that didn’t belong to her alone.

“A singular reactor, located at our capital, yet supplying every one of our cities. With all the energy any of us would ever need. A marvel that required no resources, no sacrifice. Drawing upon the world… and the stars.”

Elynara slowed, eyes flicking toward her companion. Aru’s light caught against the dust in the air, scattering tiny motes of gold.

“I only knew of it,” Aru continued, her tone flattening with static.

“Yet now… you tell me about the Deadzone.And I can feel everything draining away.I remember the white haze.And the message we all received.”

Their movement through the corridors somehow made the words flow easier. A curious thing indeed. But as she paused to gather her thoughts, Aru knew her friend listened with rapt attention.

And she wanted to explain, the best she could.

“I cannot fathom what actually happened. What matters is that The Source destabilized… and the rest, I can only guess at. Though the scar the land bears now… is likely what remained of it.”

A faint hum filled the air as she walked on, the words falling like fragments of old records.

“I will tell you more once we’re there to see it. I need to… make sure.”

Elynara only nodded. There was nothing else to say. The story itself weighed enough.

And now – now they were here.

The maintenance corridors were still partly intact, their walls lined with rusted conduits and cables that no longer pulsed.

Aru brushed a hand along one of them, leaving a faint streak through the dust.

She led Elynara through narrow turns, into chambers that might once have held tools or rations – now only scraps of metal, shattered glass, and nests of roots that had crawled in from the surface above.

In one of them, she stopped.

A faint blue light rippled across her eyes as she scanned the debris. Then she turned, her voice quieter, almost hesitant.

“...You are a mage, yes?”

Elynara blinked.

“Last I checked.”

“Then those here might help.”

“What is it?”

“The only thing that matters to mages and machines alike,” Aru said, stepping forward and kneeling amid a pile of shattered plating.

“Mana batteries. Containment cores. There aren’t many left intact – but even a dozen will let you store more than your body alone could ever hold.”

Elynara crouched beside her. The dull metallic spheres were heavy, their casings cold and many rough with corrosion. She wiped what could be salvaged with a torn scrap of cloth, revealing faint traces of engraved runes beneath the grime. Hidden in otherwise smooth and near-pristine cores, despite their age.

They hummed faintly when touched with a bit of mana – like old hearts just waiting for the right spark.

She packed each one carefully, layer by layer, into her satchel. The weight pulled at her shoulder, but her enchanted storage handled it all the same.

She let out a quiet breath.

“Thank you.”

And although her companion did not know it, Elynara’s thanks were both to her, and Celestia, for the gift she gave her.

Aru only inclined her head.

“Waste not.”

They moved on.

The parts they walked through now were badly hit by the damage, the ruins living up to their name and forcing the girls to move around and between piles or rubble, some places completely unreachable.

Another corridor bent into a larger chamber, this one darker – the air thicker, heavy with the faint scent of rust and old oil.

The light from Elynara’s staff swept over the walls… and stopped.

Caretaker bodies. Dozens.

All still.

Their once-white plating blackened by time and smoke.

A twisted beam of metal had crashed through the chamber, crushing most of them where they stood – a frozen tableau of those who had once served and protected.

Aru stepped closer, her pace slow, almost reverent. She stopped near one of them and knelt.

Elynara hesitated before following.

“Were they–?”

“Caretaker units,” Aru said softly. “Like me. And not like me. This section would have been their rest bay. They were trapped when the supports on the beam failed.”

She reached out, brushing dust from a figure’s chestplate. It was faintly etched with symbols – looping lines that might once have been a name.

There was nothing left to salvage.

And yet… Aru lingered.

“Caretaker units who were particularly liked,” she murmured, “would sometimes be given things. Special accessories. Outfits. Badges.”

Elynara came closer.

Then she saw it – the remnants clinging to the body’s frame: scraps of colored cloth, small metallic pins shaped like stars, bits of faded paper pressed into seams and joints.

Children’s drawings.

Simple, bright shapes scrawled in long-faded ink.

A crooked heart. A smiling sun. A figure with wide arms and the words thank you still barely visible.

And a ribbon. Pink once, maybe. Now bleached gray, frayed where it had once been tied around the caretaker’s wrist.

Elynara’s throat tightened.

She reached out but stopped short of touching it.

“Never to wake again,” she whispered.

“Never to care for children. And for the children to be long gone too.”

Aru’s head inclined, slow and deliberate.

“They were loved. That is more than most machines can claim.”

Elynara clutched her staff tighter, the faint blue light trembling against her knuckles.

Aru rose at last, her gaze turning toward the far end of the corridor.

“Come. There is one last place I want us to check.”

Elynara nodded, though her heart was still heavy with the image left behind.

As they stepped out, she looked back once more at the row of still forms, the little ribbon catching faint light in the dark – like a single star refusing to fade.

Then she followed.

Even Aru hesitated here.

The path wound deep beneath the earth, through collapsed service tunnels and fractured bulkheads that still carried the faint shimmer of mana. They moved slowly, the air thick with dust and the scent of metal worn thin by centuries.

Every few steps, Elynara’s staff brushed against the wall, sending ripples of light across panels that no longer answered.

Aru’s stride had grown heavier the deeper they went.

There was no rust now – only cold alloy, untouched by air or time.

“This way,” she said at last.

Her voice carried a quiet distortion, like she couldn’t stand the echo resounding here.

And as they walked, the ruin told its story.

Pieces of guardians half-buried in the walls. A caretaker’s arm still reaching toward a sealed hatch. Shattered visors, cracked lenses.

But never bones.

No human remains – only the traces of beasts that had found their way in long after the world had gone quiet.

When the corridor finally widened, Elynara stopped to breathe, taking in the sight before her.

There, in the center of the large chamber, taking up most of it, stood what could only be the bunker.

The doors were vast – taller than any gate she had ever seen, layers of metal interlocked like teeth. Even under centuries of dust they looked unbroken, their surface dull but whole.

Compared to the ruin above, it was pristine. Untouched. Waiting.

Aru stood motionless for several heartbeats.

Then she spoke, her voice a whisper of static.

“Elynara… could I trouble you for some mana?”

Elynara blinked out of her daze.

“Yes, of course. Where do you want it?”

“Here.” Aru gestured to a square of metal at shoulder height. “A small thread will do. A mana core should lie behind this panel. If we can wake it…”

Elynara pressed her palm against the surface, letting mana flow in a thin, cautious stream.The first try slipped away. The second sparked faintly, then died.

By the fourth, she felt something catch–

–and on the fifth, it connected.

A crystalline panel flickered to life beside them, its fractured glass filling with faint blue light. Aru stepped forward, hands moving in smooth precision over the interface.

Lines of text scrolled across the surface, half of them corrupted beyond recognition. The faint whir of awakening systems hummed through the floor.

Then Aru’s voice came, quiet but steady.

“...The evacuation of the city to the bunker. Was… successful.”

Elynara exhaled in relief.

“That’s great! I wonder if the survivors–”

“But.”

The single word cut through her sentence.

Elynara froze.

“...But?”

Aru’s optics dimmed.

“The bunker. Its doors were never opened again.”

It took Elynara a moment to understand.And when she did, her breath faltered.

“Everyone went in,” she whispered, “but no one ever came out?”

Her voice trembled on the edge of disbelief.

The silence that followed confirmed it.

Dust drifted in slow spirals through the beam of her staff. Somewhere deep beyond the sealed metal, she could feel it now – a faint pull on her mana. A hollow hunger she had felt before, since the moment she entered these ruins.

It was the same rhythm as the Deadzone’s edge.

Weakened. But still pulling.

Aru’s tone softened.

“Could you… supply a little more mana here?”

Elynara hesitated, then nodded and pressed her palm to the panel again.

The veins of light running across the doors brightened, flaring once in patterns she did not know or understand.

Metal groaned – a deep, low rumble that carried through the earth itself.

“That is enough,” Aru said.

The titanic doors shuddered, grinding open only a fraction – barely wide enough for two people to slip through.

The stale air that spilled out smelled of ash and dust and age.

Elynara lifted her staff, its glow cutting into the dark beyond.

And saw.

Bones.

So many bones.

Fine white dust and bones scattered across the floor in endless layers.

Caretakers lay among them, their frames collapsed where they had fallen – arms outstretched toward the door that never opened again.

The very protocols designed to protect everybody, ended up being their undoing.

Elynara couldn’t speak.

Her fingers tightened around her staff until her knuckles whitened.

The light from the doorway trembled. The air was heavy with stillness, with stories that would never be told.

Even Aru’s internal glow dimmed, her optics fading to a dull, distant blue.

For a long time, neither of them moved.

The door’s low hum continued, a slow exhale that sounded almost like mourning.

And there, surrounded by the last echoes of a world that had once believed itself eternal, both of them understood: What was meant to protect had entombed instead.

In that moment the light in their eyes, Elynara's and Aru's both, seemed to dim, just a little bit.

And it would be... a very long time for that lost spark to return again.
