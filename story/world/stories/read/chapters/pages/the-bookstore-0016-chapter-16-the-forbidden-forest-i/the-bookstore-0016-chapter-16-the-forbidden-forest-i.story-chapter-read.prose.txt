The sun had not yet risen when Rick crossed over the limits of the Impassable Wall. He had left home hours before dawn to buy himself as much daylight as possible. This was his first real expedition since Anne’s counseling, and he meant to make full use of it. For once, he was not entering the forbidden forest with only instinct, rumors, and hope. He had notes now, and a plan. But more importantly, he had books.

He had already used his exchanged reading credit on the Beastiarium Generalis, focusing on beasts from this side of the mountains or ones he had at least seen before. He had no intention of charging blindly into the deeper forest just because he felt encouraged. First, he would conquer what he could understand. Then he would press farther.

Even so, the satchel at his side seemed heavier than it should have been.

It held his notes, his food, and the Mapping Book, but the true weight came from something else. Anne had been right. He did not lack ability, but right knowledge. Now that he had some, whatever happened next would depend much more on him.

That thought was both encouraging and unpleasant.

The land changed gradually as he advanced. The dry, weakened growth outside the Wall gave way to something older and richer. Trees stood thicker here, their bark darker, their crowns fuller, their roots sunk deep into earth that had lived with high magic for longer than any kingdom. Bushes grew in layered green. Moss crept over stones in soft damp sheets. Even the air felt different—cooler, denser, and more alive.

Outside the Wall, plants and beasts had been forced to endure the recent surge of magic and adapt as best they could. Here, life had shaped itself around it for ages. That was what made the forbidden forest so dangerous, and so valuable. It was a treasure ground built over centuries, perhaps millennia, and every hunter with ambition knew it.

For the first stretch, he still knew the landmarks. A bent rock. A split pine. A low rise where the soil thinned over roots. Since he could still find his way by memory, he saved the Mapping Book for later. No point wasting strength and pages on ground he already understood.

That changed at the first stream. Rick stopped, drank a little water, and looked around with new caution. From here on, familiarity ended, and every choice could be final.

He climbed a nearby tree quickly.

That was one of the first lessons any forest hunter learned or died before mastering. On the other side of the Wall, danger mostly came from the ground. In here, it could come from anywhere. Bushes, branches, water, burrows, trunks. A beast that looked small could leap farther than expected. One that looked slow could erupt from cover like a loosed arrow.

Rick settled against the trunk and studied the surroundings with patient care. Nothing moved nearby except leaves and insects.

Only then did he take out the Mapping Book.

It still felt strange using it. A little too precious for his hands. He marked the stream, the tree line, a broken stone ridge to the north, and the direction of the slope. Then he waited a little longer, checked the area once more, and climbed down.

After that, the day found its rhythm. He moved carefully, climbing when needed. He watched, listened, and marked. Then, he moved again.

Whenever the terrain shifted enough to warrant notice, he stopped and recorded it. Good trees for observation, places where the ground dipped, thickets dense enough to hide behind or hide within, traces of burrows, game trails, signs of old claw marks. The book made the work faster and clearer than anything he had ever used before. More importantly, it made the forest feel less like an endless threat and more like a place that could be understood.

He was not merely surviving his way through the forbidden forest now, but studying it.

By afternoon, the light had already begun to slant, and Rick put the book away. Mapping was important, just as as ambition. But neither would matter if night caught him without shelter. The forest had strict rules, and night enforced them without mercy.

A hunter who wanted to wake the next morning had to settle early, stay quiet, and trust as little as possible. Darkness belonged to different creatures than day did, and they were rarely the kind a lone man wanted to meet.

Trees were excellent for observation and sometimes for killing, but poor for sleep. If a hunter needed real rest, even the shallow, broken kind the forest allowed, he looked for a cave first. A small empty one was best, because the entrance could be blocked. Failing that, a stone recess or a rock wall could do, though those needed more work and offered less certainty.

Fire was another option, in theory. In practice, it depended on what you wished to attract. And Rick had no wish at all to invite fire essence beasts for a conversation.

He had wisely left himself several hours of daylight for the search, and even so, he found a proper place only when the day was nearly done. A narrow hollow had formed beneath a low cliff where time and erosion had eaten a shallow space into the stone. It was no cave, but it would serve. The back was solid, the roof low, and the entrance narrow enough that fallen wood could block it.

By the time night settled over the forest, Rick was already inside.

He had dragged branches and part of a dead trunk across the opening, leaving only small gaps for air and a few thin lines of moonlight. His bag served as a pillow. The floor was hard. The air smelled of dirt, bark, stone, and the faint dampness that never quite left hidden places.

By forest standards, it was comfortable. By safer standards, it was terrible.

Still, safe in the forbidden forest was always a flexible word. Plenty of creatures could have broken through his barrier if they truly wanted to. Rick knew that, as did any hunter. That was why he had covered his tracks before bedding down and rubbed certain crushed plants near the shelter to dull his scent. It would not fool everything, but it might fool enough.

He slept the way hunters slept—shallowly, uneasily, and never for long. Every sound tugged at the edge of waking. A shifting branch, a distant cry, leaves brushing leaves. Once, something moved not far from the shelter and Rick lay still with one hand on his knife until the sound passed.

But nothing came.

A few hours before dawn, he pushed the branches aside and stepped out into the cold grey of early morning.

Then he did it again.

And again.

Time passed that way, one careful day after another. Rick changed shelters four times in the next two weeks, each only after he had mapped the surrounding ground well enough to justify moving on. The Mapping Book transformed the work. Good shelters no longer vanished into the confusion of memory. Strong observation points stayed marked. Distances stopped feeling vague and hostile. He could judge how far he had gone, how long it would take to return, and how much daylight he could spend before turning back.

With each new page, the forest became less mysterious.

He covered roughly thirty kilometers along the mountain side in those two weeks. Not in a straight line, and certainly not quickly. He made separate notes on the beasts he spotted, along with numbers, habits, and the places where he had seen them. By the end of it, he had something like a hunting ground.

Not a rich one. This part of the forest was still relatively poor in anything that could be safely taken, carried back, and sold for good coin. But it was far better than what he had before, which was uncertainty and the hope that desperation might count as a strategy.

Now he had routes, shelters, observation points, and records.

Now he could hunt.

Rick crouched on a thick branch and kept his eyes on the wild boar below. Every so often he forced himself to glance away from it and scan the rest of the area. That was as important as watching the prey itself. Getting tunnel vision in the forbidden forest was one of the fastest ways to die.

The boar moved through the brush with heavy, suspicious care. It stood nearly to Rick’s navel, dark-haired, and built to charge through undergrowth and bone alike. Earth essence had thickened its body into something tougher than a normal animal. A direct hit from it could break a man open.

Rick had no intention of facing it directly. The trap lay half-hidden a short distance away, built around a natural depression about a meter long. He had deepened it as much as he could, lined the bottom with sharpened stakes, then covered the top with a crude lattice of thin wood and leaves. It was not a masterpiece. Against a stronger beast, it might have been laughable. But this boar was not especially large for its kind, and Rick did not need the trap to kill it. He only needed it to hurt, confuse, and delay.

The boar snorted and pawed at the ground. It had caught something. Perhaps his scent, or perhaps only the sense that something here was wrong. Either way, it kept circling just outside the wrong patch of earth, refusing to give him the clean chance he wanted.

Rick tightened his grip on the spear. This was where the beastiarium had helped. Without it, he would have treated the beast as a thick-skinned brute and hoped for luck. With it, he knew that females of this species had one notably weaker area in the underbelly, where the young fed. It was not a big weakness, and certainly not one that made the beast easy prey, but it was an entry point. If the stakes struck there, even shallowly, the boar would suffer more than its bulk suggested.

After another long moment, Rick decided waiting had stopped being helpful. He picked up a broken stick from the branch and threw it toward the far side of the trap.

The stick cracked against brush. The boar jerked, wheeled, and lunged exactly where Rick wanted.

The cover broke, wood snapped, and leaves collapsed. The beast dropped with a violent squeal that tore through the trees.

Rick moved at once. He jumped from the tree, hit the ground hard enough to jar his knees, and sprinted toward the pit while the boar thrashed below. Its screams were high and furious.

He drove the spear down toward its face, aiming for the eye. But the boar heaved at the same instant.

The strike slipped, scraping across the snout instead of sinking in. Rick swore under his breath, planted his feet, and yanked the spear back for another try. The pit shook beneath the beast’s struggling. One more second, perhaps two, and it would either break the stakes or climb free.

He thrust again. This time the point sank into the eye.

The boar shrieked with a sound so raw and violent that Rick felt it in his teeth. He tried to force the spear deeper, but the creature exploded upward in a frenzy of blood, pain, and earth-essence strength. The pit edge collapsed under its weight. Mud and splintered wood flew. Rick barely had time to wrench backward before the beast burst out.

The shaft snapped.

For one heartbeat, Rick found himself staring at the broken weapon still lodged in the boar’s ruined eye.

Then he ran. There was no bravery in lingering. Not against that.

He turned and sprinted for the tree as the boar screamed behind him. His feet pounded through leaves and roots, his breath tearing in his throat. He did not look back. And he did not need to. He could hear the beast coming, the rage in it, the crashing force of something wounded and very much alive.

The boar wanted revenge.

Rick reached the trunk, threw himself at it, and climbed.
