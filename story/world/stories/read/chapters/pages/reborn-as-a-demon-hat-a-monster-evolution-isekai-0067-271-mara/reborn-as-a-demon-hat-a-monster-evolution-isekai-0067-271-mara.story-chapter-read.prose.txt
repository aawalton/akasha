Mara watched her guardian and master with neutral, but curious eyes.

And when Fauna’s eyes found hers in the shroud that covered this realm, the young Hopla turned away.

She wasn’t sure why, but she couldn’t approach her mentor at that moment. Perhaps, she would later tell herself, it was because she knew that her presence would have been intruding on a moment that was not meant for anyone but Fauna. Or, perhaps she couldn’t bear to see her cradling the hat like that – cradling Ethan like that – as though simply squeezing him hard enough could bring life back into his form.

She walked off into the mist, her silken white robe flowing after her, and she kept walking even when she heard Fauna call out to her. She knew that those calls were half-hearted. Her master also knew when her student needed to be alone.

Mara walked through the wisps of low cloud and fog that clung to the mountainside ruins before activating a short [Levitation] spell and hovering over to another mountain crag above. Her thoughts had been wandering ever since they’d come here.

Ever since they’d set out on a mission which she knew herself to be futile.

For what seemed like an age she’d reached out to the very ends of Argwyll, probing the minds of anyone who had seen the great dragon that was their target, reporting to the team whenever she got anything valuable. And that was what had led them here – to this dominion of dust and ashes.

She still remembered the excitement that had flared in her breast when Fauna had told her they’d be going on a final mission together. She’d immediately volunteered, despite Agna’s wary glances in her direction. The old bard and her had grown closer in the years since the Archon’s final victory, and had swapped so many tales of their respective lives under and above the earth that they knew each other better than they even knew themselves. In a strange way, Agna was reticent to let Mara go when Fauna relayed the plans for their expedition.

But for Mara, the decision had been a no-brainer.

She couldn’t miss out on the chance to venture out with the party of Mr Ethan – those who had braved the greatest dangers of the surface and even done battle with the Lightborn himself. There were no greater heroes in the land of Argwyll, and Mara had wanted nothing more in the years since Ethan’s passing than to join them in one last adventure – an adventure she could call her own.

But now, flying amidst the shaded boughs of dead trees and the silent stones of moss-covered ruins, that excitement was gone. It was replaced by something else.

She floated down to a ruined structure partially covered by foliage and palmed the stone architecture, closing her eyes and allowing her mind to travel through time, to see the minds who had crafted this place and to know what they had wanted to protect.

She’d learned that the first beings here had not been Tialax as she’d expected, but rather a mixed tribe of Tialax and human dragon-worshipers, who had sequestered themselves in these mountains in the aftermath of Karfangg’s demise. Those who had rejected the rise of Krea had come here in veneration of the last dragon – the one who had managed to remain hidden and would remain hidden, they thought, till the end of time itself.

The Architect.

Her brows knit in consternation as the name formed in her mind. She saw hundreds of starving men and hybrids all bowed towards the silver door to the creature’s lair, all of them falling to the ground and begging the dragon to re-enter the world, to ignite the fires of rebellion that the first Archon, and all the Archons after him, had failed to do. And she felt her stomach tighten as each one of them slowly expired, their pleas falling on the deaf ears of a beast they couldn’t even be sure cared about them at all.

And yet he was here. She knew it as one with only her powers could. He was here, probably watching them rampage through his mountains with dispassion, more concerned with whatever project he’d been up to flying around the skies of Argwyll these days. Not destroying, no – apparently those who saw him said he merely circled some locations of interest and then, like a child who was bored with playtime, set off in some other seemingly random direction.

She moved away from the stone pillar when she heard vibrations nearby. She knew what that meant, and her oaken stave was at her side almost instantly.

Five of them emerged from the ruined masonry around her. Beings of stone carved in the suit and forms of men, their black mouths gaping at her and pebble eyes watching for her to make the first move.

When she simply closed her eyes instead, they charged her as a unit.

She sensed the movements of each one in turn.

The golem behind – coming at her with an overhead pummel attack.

The two to her left – barreling towards her in an attempted grapple.

Two to her right – rock-arms striking wide.

And the one ahead – a golem magus charging up a seismic gust spell that would throw her, and its allies, from the ruin.

In a matter of milliseconds, she knew their game.

And in the next second, she opened her eyes and struck the ground, once, with the butt of her staff.

“Be still once more.”

Her words were whispered on quiet winds. But they traveled – instantly the golems slammed onto their knees and bowed their heads as though ashamed. The mage’s spell fizzled, and her will gripped his head, black, pupil-less eyes wavering as Mara’s voice entered his stony-skull.

Then he was on the ground with his brethren, calm as the stone he was spawned from.

Mara stood over them and looked at each one in turn, her eyes aglow with violet light, her stave’s tip sizzling with unseen energy.

She felt their anger. She felt the mechanical compulsion to guard this place that served as the tomb of their brethren. But she also felt the sense of duty each man of stone had towards this compulsion – it was something born from respect, not hatred.

“Return to the stone,” she told them. “Your brothers will not be disturbed by me.”

She spoke these words as Fauna had taught her – with dignity, with grace – and with authority. And slowly the five golems bowed their heads and crumpled into pebbles of their own accord, sliding right back into the masonry which they’d crawled out of.

She let out a breath of relief and satisfaction. She’d been practicing her pacification charm ever since they’d got here, thinking that the stony automatons of the mountains would make for good training. She hadn’t known at all what they really were, however. Not until she’d looked inside their minds and understood why they were here.

She ran a hand over the ground, watching each pebble return to the ancient architecture that lived on this mountain.

“You still keep your vigil after all these years,” she said. “Even with all the Archons and the Lightborns gone.”

The words were spoken as a kind of meditation. She didn’t expect any reply. It was the kind of thing Agna would say to spirits of the dead as she sang funeral dirges for soldiers lost to bandits on the roads of Westerweald. Mara had been fascinated by them as a child for the poetry contained in the words. Now, as an adolescent, she knew that those words had power.

Power that you don’t wish to employ.

Her mind reeled, almost buckling at the strength of that statement. The voice that spoke those words stirred the better part of her soul, and forced her to grip her chest lest it burst. She took a few deep, controlled breaths, scanning the skies for the speaker.

And even though she knew deep inside who it was, her mind wouldn’t allow her to make the connection until he spoke again:

Tell me, little rabbit, why did you not slay them when they opposed you?

Mara turned to see the creature staring down at her from the mountain’s peak – the being that had been shrouded in fog during her short-lived battle.

A pair of giant amber eyes blinked at her within that fog.

Amber eyes attached to a face that slowly let the mists clear with one puff of its massive flaring nostrils.

And all at once the memories of the Lightborn, and the Archon, and even the world-devouring parasite Sys disappeared from Mara’s mind. In their place were those staring eyes, and the ridged spines that ran down the great beast’s aged neck – a neck that barely kept the bubbling fire within contained.

She was face to face with a dragon.
