King Theron Flavius stood before the floor-to-ceiling mirror in his private armory. The room was usually a place of quiet focus, a sanctuary where he prepared his mind for the burdens of rule. Today, it was a cage.

His hands, thick and scarred from a lifetime of war, trembled violently as he fastened the heavy, crimson-lacquered greaves to his shins. He squeezed his eyes shut, trying to banish the tremor, but the anger was a living thing in his blood, hot and uncontrollable.

He had brought shame to his house. He had brought shame to the memory of his ancestors.

The low, vibrating shriek of the Mother Phoenix echoed through the thick stone walls, a sound that rattled his teeth and settled like a stone in his gut. The number of times the progenitor phoenix had risen from the caldera could be counted on one hand. And every single time, it signified the same terrifying truth: the royal family had failed. The kingdom was on the brink of collapse, and the progenitor had to be awakened to fix the mistakes of mortal kings.

His name would not join the glorious, triumphant roll call in the ancestral hall. He would not be remembered as Theron the Conqueror, who expanded the Hegemony's borders. He would be recorded in the histories as a failure. The king who lost the sky. The king who woke the Mother.

"Father."

The voice broke through his dark reverie. Theron didn't turn around. He continued strapping the ornate breastplate over his tunic, his movements stiff and mechanical.

"Let me join you in battle," Ignis pleaded, stepping further into the armory. The Crown Prince was already clad in his own golden armor, the polished metal gleaming obnoxiously in the torchlight. "Grant me the honor to fly alongside the progenitor. We will scour that upstart from the earth together."

Rage, pure and blinding, boiled through Theron's veins.

He stared at his own reflection, seeing the heavy lines of stress and failure etched into his face. Then he looked at the reflection of his son standing behind him. Ignis looked eager. Entitled. Completely and utterly oblivious to the catastrophe he had orchestrated.

That cur Kaelen Wight must be laughing in his grave, Theron thought, his hands clenching into fists at his sides. His son has done him proud. A boy who builds fortresses that swim and ships that fly. While my blood... my blood is a babbling buffoon.

He had lost the game to a dead man.

"And who would take charge of the kingdom if I am to fall in battle?" Theron choked out, his voice a low, dangerous rumble that barely contained his fury. "There is a reason the Crown Prince must stay behind. The line of succession must be preserved."

"But Father, it is impossible to lose now that the progenitor is here!" Ignis protested, taking a step closer, his golden armor clanking loudly. "They are Tier 9! The enemy has no such power backing them! No such thing may happen to you, their dragon is weak and dying, hiding in their cave!"

Theron turned slowly, fixing his son with a look of profound, exhausted contempt. He walked past Ignis, heading toward the specialized mounting platform where his own mount, a Tier 8 phoenix, waited impatiently.

The royal family of Cinderfall did not contract with the Tier 9 phoenix. It was a progenitor. According to the ancient laws of the world, progenitors could only have one contractee, and then they were bound to the World Tree they protected. The laws of magic prevented them from making a second contract, which meant Cinderfall only ever had one Tier 9 combatant: the bird itself.

The dragon progenitors, however, were Tier 10 entities. They could have Tier 9 offspring. Which meant the offspring was free to contract, effectively doubling the combat capability of their lineage. A knight and his dragon.

But the phoenix progenitor still held an advantage. The raw power they received directly from the World Tree, the limitless well of restorative mana, evened out the battlefield. They were unkillable engines of destruction.

"Father, I implore you," Ignis pressed, his voice taking on a desperate, whining edge that made Theron's teeth grind together. "Let me talk to Nyxia! I know she was forced into this! She was excited to marry me, she told me so! Once she sees me, she will come back to me. She just needs rescuing from that... that phony!"

Theron had been trying to ignore the boy, trying to focus on the impossible task ahead. But that statement made a vein in his temple pulse visibly.

If his son wasn't so incredibly, breathtakingly stupid, they wouldn't have lost House Black. They wouldn't be fighting a civil war in their own backyard while a fleet of iron monsters bombarded their coast.

"Pray tell," Theron barked, whirling around so fast his heavy crimson cape snapped like a whip. "How did he manage to force her out from right next to Morpheus Black's Mage Tower? A fortress that could withstand an army!"

He stepped into Ignis's personal space, towering over the boy. "And do I need to remind you of the basic laws of this world? A marriage contract cannot be forced! The person needs to agree, or else the World System does not accept the union! She chose him, you idiot. She chose the boy who didn't flaunt his concubines in front of her!"

Ignis flinched, taking a step back, his golden armor suddenly looking very fragile against his father's wrath. "I... I..."

"I worry about the kingdom," Theron said, his voice dropping to a harsh whisper, filled with a despair he couldn't hide. "If something happens to me today... Cinderfall is doomed in your naive hands."

He didn't wait for a response. He didn't want to see the petulant anger forming on his son's face. King Theron clamped his helmet on, the heavy visor obscuring his face, and climbed onto the back of his waiting phoenix.

He spurred the beast forward, launching from the balcony into the smoke-filled sky, leaving his failure of a son standing alone in the armory.

…

The Cinderfall vanguard was stuck. Yesterday, this valley had been a dry, frozen rut. Today, a roaring river of black, freezing rapids cut straight across their path, severing the advance.

While the officers huddled in their warded tents, trying to figure out how to cross without marching the entire column into the firing arcs of the Black Mage Towers, the regular infantry collapsed in the mud. It was the first real rest they’d had in days.

Small, smoky fires sprang up across the bruised-red dirt. Around one fire, a squad of conscripts huddled close, their crimson armor dull, scraped, and caked with grey ash.

A young soldier, his face hollowed out by exhaustion, leaned back against a supply crate. He let out a dry, hacking laugh that ended in a cough.

"I honestly don't know what's going to kill me first," he rasped, tossing a piece of rock-hard, moldy hardtack into the flames. "The rations, or the silent ghosts dropping fire from the sky."

His squadmates chuckled darkly. They gathered closer to the fire, watching a meager portion of oats boiling in a battered iron bucket. It smelled like wet dog.

An older soldier, his forearms crisscrossed with the pale scars of a dozen border skirmishes, let out a heavy sigh. He poked the fire with a stick, his eyes fixed on the embers.

"If our Crown Prince was half as capable as the new Lord of Wight," he muttered, his voice dropping to a cautious whisper. "We wouldn't be sitting in this freezing mud. It would be the enemy laying siege to the Mage Towers of House Black right now, not the other way around."

A week ago, saying that out loud would have meant a flogging. Today, nobody argued. The general populace was waking up to the reality of Ignis Flavius. The downfall of Cinderfall had his golden, arrogant face stamped all over it.

A grizzled veteran, missing two fingers on his left hand, snorted. He leaned forward to warm his remaining digits. "Capable? The boy is a strutting peacock. Even my daughter wouldn't marry that fool if he introduced her to his concubines before the marriage bed." He spat into the ash-stained dirt. "And she's a seamstress in the lower wards. Has more sense in her thimble than he does in his entire gilded head."

Another soldier rubbed his face, the grime grinding into his skin. "Aye. I remember seeing the Lady Nyxia during the parades in the capital. Anyone with eyes could see she was uncomfortable. Annoyed at his constant preening. If only he had a sliver of decency, she might have stayed. House Black wouldn't be turning our own fortresses against us."

The scarred soldier shook his head, his grip tightening on his spear shaft until his knuckles popped. "If that fool takes the throne, Cinderfall is doomed. The King can't fall in battle. He just can't. He's the only thing holding this mess together."

"The King, and the Mother," the veteran corrected, looking up at the sky. A flicker of awe, mixed with desperate hope, crossed his weathered face. "The progenitor is awake. I saw the golden light over the capital before we marched. It’s a Tier 9. They say it can revive an entire army from ash. Once the King and the Mother break the Lion Cub’s lines, this will all be over."

The squad fell silent. It was a fragile hope, easily broken by the reality of their empty stomachs and the cold wind.

One of their comrades, a young recruit barely old enough to shave, was sitting apart from the group. He wasn't looking at the boiling oats. He wasn't listening to the talk of titans and kings. He was staring out at the darkening horizon, his knees pulled to his chest, his arms wrapped around his legs.

"Hey, man," the scarred soldier called out, nudging him with a boot. "Are you okay? What are you looking at out there?"

The young man broke from his stupor, blinking rapidly. "It's nothing," he mumbled, his voice thick. He pulled his thin cloak tighter around his shivering shoulders. "I'm just worried about my family. They live in a village close to here."

The mood around the fire shifted instantly. The grim camaraderie vanished.

All of them shared the same thought.

The recent pay cuts to fund the war effort meant they couldn't send money back home. The sudden, brutal conscription had emptied the fields of able-bodied men right before the harvest. Most of the kingdom was starving.

And to their supreme, humiliating horror, their own kingdom hadn't helped them. The King had taken their grain.

It was their enemy who had fed their starving mothers, fathers, brothers, sisters wives, and children.

The silver packages raining from the sky were the only reason many of their families were still alive. Letters from home, smuggled through the chaotic supply lines, spoke not of the King's glory, but of the Lion's mercy.

The scarred soldier looked down at his calloused hands. He balled them into fists. They were polishing their armor and sharpening their blades, preparing to rise in arms against their benefactor. They were marching to kill the man who had shown their families more mercy than their own King ever had.

This was the sentiment spreading like a slow-acting poison across the entire Cinderfall army. They were marching to war, but as they looked at the meager, boiling oats and thought of the silver packages falling from the sky, their hearts were no longer in the fight.
