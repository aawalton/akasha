Dusks in Camatoz Ik’Balam arrived earlier than almost any other part of the continent. Given the Attini Empire Front was the only front out of the six that spanned the entire width of the continent, it was also the only front where one could experience the earliest dawns and the latest dusks—and it had been a while since Sora visited the Rampaging Hinterland Front and the Mori Masif Front, the far eastern fronts.

She’d forgotten what an early dusk felt like.

“Try the sugar glass,” she said, tapping a shard with the nib of her pen. “I hear the confectioners blow it from mushroom sap and pure pretension.”

Zora snorted. For him that counted as laughter. “If pretension keeps, we could bottle it for winter.”

“Mhm. Try the kalesto stick as well. It’s not local, is it?”

“They imported it from the Plagueplain Front. No idea how the Camatoz Lord did that.”

The two of them were back out in the living room, sitting across each other on the sofas with a low snack table between them heaped with regional sweets: savory dried slices, and that stubborn little bowl of roasted beetle-nuts that squeaked between the teeth. Sora liked the nuts the most. It reminded her a little of what her parents used to serve her in the Fabre Household.

They crunched through another minute of companionable snacking, and then Sora grinned at her cousin over the rim of her teacup.

“... How has life been, Master Zora?”

He angled his head—a blind, listening-bird habit—and set a piece of candied stem down like a punctuation mark. “Tiring,” he said. “But who isn’t tired these days?”

“True,” she agreed. “Even the sun looks like it’s going to sleep in tomorrow.”

They allowed the silence to breathe a little. The Fabres were old hands at not leaping to the heavy things all the time. It was common sense to let the room settle first—but they were out of practice as Fabres, and Zora broke the silence a little earlier than he should.

“How many?” he asked at last.

Sora shrugged mid-bite. “I dunno. As far as I know, it’s just you and me left. When the Witches attacked, all twenty-five of us Hundred Tongues scattered like sparks into the wind, but the wind is a hungry god.” She set her shard of sugar glass down and watched the amber lights of the city outside pass through it. “Still… if the two of us survived the massacre of the Fabre Household, perhaps your other servants did too. Auntie liked to say a good proverb has seven lives, and so do good Fabres.”

“What does that even mean?”

“I dunno.”

Zora made a small sound—agreement or grief’s twin, she couldn’t tell. Outside, the city’s lanterns continued sharpening and brightening.

Sora stood to refill her tea—more to stretch dread from her spine than for thirst—but Zora waved a hand and glanced in the direction of the drink table.

“Refill her glass,” he said. The jug by the side floated, no fuss, and tilted over once it reached the glass in her hand.

“... That’s so cool,” she whispered. “Did you know I also have a Cicada Class? It’s not much, but I can speak really loudly, too.”

“How coincidental. We both have Cicada Class,” he said plainly. Then, once she swallowed another mouthful, “But why now? You have been given your own life, and you have kept to it—cleverly. Why come to me now as a Fabre?”

Sora shrugged again. She could dodge this. Fabres were born navigators of conversation, and she knew how to turn a sentence like a boat in a tight harbour. Instead, she took the straight channel.

“Because I am Sora Fabre, Nineteenth of the Hundred Tongues,” she said, cheerful as a bell. “I was born and trained only to serve the Thousand Tongue, Head of the Fabre Household and Keeper of the Old Lexicons. My life is yours to—”

“Stop,” Zora said sternly, soft but slightly annoyed. “Traditions are winter coats. There is a season for wearing them and a season for hanging them up, and the Fabre Household is dead; its oaths with it. Even if it hadn’t died… I would’ve given all twenty-five of you cousins a knife and told you to cut your own chains.”

Then the corners of his mouth tilted in what might pass for a smile. “But you know this about me. You’ve been following my trail in ink.”

“Oh?” Sora feigned innocence, one shoulder raised. “So you’ve been… reading my little sketches and articles about your adventures?”

Zora scowled faintly and tapped two fingers against his sightless eyes. Sora lifted both hands in mock surrender, a smile tugging at her lips. The gesture amused her more than it should.

“Whoops,” she teased. “So you’ve heard my little sketches and articles about your adventures?”

“I know of them,” he replied. “They are… exaggerated—sometimes absurdly so—yet perhaps that is what humanity needs: an indomitable figure larger than life, a champion whom the weary can lean upon and the faithless can put their faith in. The Swarm can claim our lands and our lives, but they must not claim even our spirits. Your articles are wonderful in that respect.”

They let the silence steep a while, broken only by the steady sound of chewing.

Then Zora asked, “Did you see the effigies on the road?”

Sora waved her pen in the air. “Hard to miss. Five-pointed stars made of bug carcass,and worms eating their own tails… very tacky design, if you ask me. A grotesque pageant. But,” she popped a seed and chewed thoughtfully, “they’ll give the Swarm something to think about, and fear is a language even bugs can read.” Her eyes flicked to him. “Tell me, cousin: are you going to be that champion of humanity the Swarm will whisper rumours about?”

Zora turned his face toward the window, letting lanternlight catch on half of his face. Sora followed his gaze, though she saw only the growing night, while he seemed to measure something far, far older.

“... No,” he said calmly. “I’m hardly driven enough to loudly proclaim I wish to wipe out the entire Swarm. My hunger is only for the Magicicada Witches. Besides, someone better than me can bear the title of ‘Champion of Humanity’, and the best part is, he wouldn’t even care about the weight of it. He can bear it forever and never lose heart.”

“Oh?” She started jotting notes down in her journal. “But you, the Thousand Tongue, are the one who has been wreaking havoc across the Attini Empire the past two years and nine months. Surely the people of the empire would consider you the Champion of Humanity?”

He rested his hands on his staff. “A role I never claimed, and never wanted. The Fabres, if nothing else, have always known the parts they needed to play of their own volition, and my role in this war is not to be the Champion of Humanity.”

“What is your role, then?”

Zora tilted his head. “The same as yours in coming to meet me now after so many years of separation, though, perhaps, on a slightly larger scale.”

Her quill hovered, poised above the page.

Then she shut her journal with a snap—and reopened it just as fast to restart the interview properly, her grin rekindling.

“... Well then,” she said, sitting up straighter. “To business.”

Zora nodded. “To business.”

“Representing the Spinneret Society of Writers and Journalists, I, Sora Fabre, would like to conduct an official interview with the Thousand Tongue.”

“Sure.”

“Let’s put it plainly for the people who’ll be reading this in the news: what will you and the Worm Mage bring to the Reunification Army—if you intend to bring them anything at all for this final, decisive battle?”

But instead of answering plainly, Zora looked right instead.

Right on cue, Kita exited the ice room with Machi in tow. The heiress’ eyes were dry now, but the corners were still a little red where she’d rubbed them the most—not that anyone not looking for hints of crying would notice, given how stern and dignified she looked at Zora now.

The silence that followed was the good kind—the sort that makes everyone aware of their breathing.

“... So,” Kita eventually said. “You’re the writer of The Earthen Princess.”

Zora’s fingers found the snack platter again. He turned a nut between thumb and knuckle, and for a moment he gave her an amused smile. “I did,” he admitted. “ I didn’t think it’d be a popular story at all, but I suppose men love the stories they wish to be true.”

“Why’d you write it to begin with, then?”

“Because I was a boy and thought the world would bend if I only stamped my feet loud enough.” He chucked the nut into his mouth, crunching low. “But I have long since departed from the ideals I extolled in The Earthen Princess. Men cannot live alone, and men cannot fight alone. In that same vein, shared courage is double the courage, but shared fear is half the fear, so tell me now, Heiress of the Salaqa Lord: do you still idolize the Earthen Princess?”

Kita’s jaw eased just a fraction as she smiled.

Though, Sora didn’t think she was going to answer.

“How many soldiers can the east provide?” she said instead, blunt as a spear. “Five thousand? Maybe even ten? Please say ten thousand, at least. Father is expecting some amount of support from the east, and anything less than ten would make our full frontal assault… difficult.”

Zora’s reply was quieter than the question. “You may enter.”

The front door opened behind his couch. Eleven men stepped through in a slow line—robes cut and patterned in the wilder eastern manner, belts neat, faces set with the careful patience of people who’d long practiced saying important things. Sora’s pen paused mid scribble, and Kita’s pupils widened until the whites showed.

The eleven eastern Outer Regional Lords who had yet to pledge their support to the Reunification Army.

They were all here together?

The eleven dipped their heads to Kita in synchronised courtesy—not theatrical but absolute, the kind bred in courts where a nod carried both favor and warning.

“... When the Thousand Tongue passed through our valleys, he did more than make promises,” one of the Regional Lords stepped forward and said. “He cleared nests, taught patrols, and helped with reconstruction efforts all over. We remember favours, and we intend to repay them.”

And Zora put it as simply as he could: “Between the eleven Outer Regions, a hundred thousand soldiers from the east will march with the Reunification Army.”

The numbers landed like stones.

Kita’s mouth worked. Sora’s eyebrow shot up so fast her pen made a mark on the page from the motion.

“Impossible,” Kita said reflexively. “Salaqa scouts measured the eastern muster at ten thousand soldiers, twenty thousand tops. How could there suddenly be—”

Zora shrugged, which in him was not flippant so much as weatherwise. “That figure might’ve been true two years ago, but you read old maps, cousin. Where I had cleared nests and taught, the eastern men had nine more months to breathe, train, and raise arms. The east has hated the Swarm longer and then—having been tended—grew stronger. Thus, when I called, eleven lords answered.”

Sora’s writer’s instinct immediately began to hum: all interesting stories liked a good reversal, and numbers that stretched the imagination made for good ink on paper.

What a scoop!

I’m getting a pay raise for sure!

“... So that is a hundred and fifty thousand soldiers on our side,” Kita breathed. “The Reunification Army and the Divine Capital are evenly matched in terms of sheer manpower. The walls and fortifications still favour the Divine Capital, but… the odds sing a different tune now.”

Sora lifted her chin and pointed her pen at Zora. “That’s great and all, but what about the elite strike force? Will you and the Worm Mage infiltrate the Divine Temple while the full frontal assault is underway and cut Decima down?”

Zora’s laugh was a brief, dry wind. “Stupid question,” he said. “As if I haven’t spent two years and nine months of my life hunting her down. The real question is, what does the Worm Mage think about this whole plan?”

Now the entire room turned to Enki, who was still frozen before the window. Sora watched him closely, though also with polite, taut curiosity. The boy had said nothing since they entered the room, and even now—nothing.

So Zora rose slowly, walked over, and tapped the boy once on the skull with his staff.

“Wake up already,” he muttered. “They’re here.”

And the boy jolted as if a spring had finally uncoiled, making everyone flinch.

He was sleeping?

He wasn’t even listening to any of us?

Sora blinked, her pen half-raised in disbelief as the boy turned and fixed his gaze on them—those eyes that were swirls of hard, unnatural blue, cold and gleaming like storm-churned water.

She felt her throat tighten, but her teeth bared in nervous amusement.

Strong.

That was the word that burned itself across her mind. The boy felt stronger than even her cousin.

So that is the ‘Champion of Humanity’ you wish to raise?

The Worm Mage’s hand closed around his rifle, and he rose his feet, stretching his spine so unnaturally far back Sora couldn’t help but wonder if he was actually a worm.

“... Okay,” he said. “Is it time to kill some bugs?”
