Chapter 43

“Thirty-one years in the collar. I have buried the faithful, married the hopeful, and baptised the new. None of it prepared me for the possibility that the scripture was not metaphor.” – Father Matthieu Renaud, Marseille

The notification hung in Arthur’s vision like a door that hadn’t been there a moment ago.

ARCHETYPE RECOGNITION AVAILABLE

“Archie,” Arthur said. “What is this?”

“The Nadi Matrix has accumulated sufficient behavioural data to identify your dominant operational pattern. Archetype recognition formalises this identification and evolves your existing technique accordingly.”

“How do we activate it?” Steve asked. He was still on the cistern floor, split palms between his knees, but his voice carried the intensity of a man who had been waiting for this since Level 5.

“Acceptance is sufficient. The recognition is a diagnosis, not a selection. You do not choose what the Matrix has observed.”

Arthur and Donna looked at each other across the cistern floor. Steve was already reaching for his notification with the urgency of someone opening exam results.

“Will this knock us out as well?” Donna asked.

“Archetype recognition is primarily a Nadi-Matrix change. It will not have an adverse physical reaction,” Archie replied.

Arthur accepted without hesitation.

He’d braced for the usual restructuring, the body being rebuilt from the inside. But as Archie had foretold, this was different. A blue lattice raced beneath his skin, slow and deliberate, shoulder to wrist to shoulder again, the light tracing a path and finding its settling places. The Matrix had read him and now it was restructuring itself. Ten levels of data compressed into a pattern and reflected back.

ARCHETYPE RECOGNITION: GUARDIAN

DOMINANT PATTERN: STRUCTURAL DEFENCE

TECHNIQUE EVOLUTION: ACTIVE

Structural Reinforcement had evolved. The technique was still there, the lock, the hardening, the immovable wall. But the boundaries had moved. He pressed his palm flat against the cistern wall and channelled deliberately. The limestone hardened under his touch; he could feel the molecular structure tightening, the stone becoming denser for as long as he held the connection. This had a much wider range than channelling into the ground during combat. He could feel the potential to push it through the shield, through the floor, possibly through a person.

He pulled his hand away. The wall returned to normal.

“Guardian,” he said. “Structural Defence. The reinforcement could extend to anything I’m touching now. Not just me. I’ll have to experiment to find the limits.”

“Your technique has evolved from self-reinforcement to contact-reinforcement,” Archie confirmed. “Range of effect is limited to physical contact at this developmental stage.”

“What about at later stages?” Steve asked immediately.

“Later stages require later data.”

“Of course they do.”

Donna accepted next, eyes closed as the recognition took her. The blue lattice raced beneath her skin, spine to fingertips, faster than Arthur’s had, the light tracing her nervous system in a single rapid surge that took four seconds and changed everything.

ARCHETYPE RECOGNITION: EXECUTOR

DOMINANT PATTERN: PRECISION ELIMINATION

TECHNIQUE EVOLUTION: ACTIVE

She opened her eyes, blinked, and frowned.

“Arthur, you’re about to shift your weight to your left foot.”

Arthur looked down at his feet. He hadn’t been planning to move. But his right knee was slightly bent from the fight, and his body was, yes, his weight was already transferring left to compensate. She’d read the physics of his posture and projected the result before his own nervous system had committed to the adjustment.

“How did you -”

“I don’t know.” Arthur heard uncertainty in Donna’s voice, a register he had almost never heard from her. “Your shoulder dropped two millimetres and I saw the whole sequence play out. The weight shift, the knee correction, the foot placement. I saw it happen before it happened.”

She turned her attention to Steve. “You’re going to reach for the guidebook with your left hand.”

Steve froze mid-motion. His left hand was six inches from the guidebook, and he hadn’t been aware he was reaching.

“That’s deeply unsettling,” he said.

“Imagine how it feels from this side.” Donna covered her eyes with her palms. “I can’t turn it off. Every movement anyone makes, I can see where it ends before it starts. It’s like watching a room full of people fall in slow motion.”

“Pre-cognitive combat reading,” Archie said. “Your neurological processing now extends fractionally ahead of observed events based on physical data extrapolation. Involuntary activation is typical during the initial integration period. Voluntary control develops with practice.”

“How much practice?” Donna asked.

“Your species presents a wide range of neurological adaptability. I cannot be certain.”

Donna lowered her hands and looked at the axe, then at Arthur’s feet, which had now completed the weight shift she’d predicted ten seconds ago. “Executor,” she said. “Precision Elimination. I’m going to need everyone to stop moving for a while.”

Steve accepted last, and had been vibrating since the notification appeared. When the recognition hit, his eyes went wide for three seconds and Ether flickered between his fingers.

ARCHETYPE RECOGNITION: CONDUIT

DOMINANT PATTERN: ENERGY MANIPULATION

TECHNIQUE EVOLUTION: ACTIVE

“Oh,” Steve said and then fell silent.

“Steve?” Arthur said.

“I’m trying to make fire.” He held up his hands. The Ether between his fingers turned to heat first, then a compression of air that made Arthur’s ears pop, then a vibration that set the stone dust on the floor trembling. Three effects in three seconds, none of them what he’d intended. “That is not fire, none of that was fire. I wanted fire and I got a concussion grenade and an earthquake.”

“Your Ether projection has expanded from single-mode ether agitation thermal output to multi-modal environmental interaction,” Archie said. “Isolation of individual modes requires focused intent. You are currently broadcasting across all available channels simultaneously.”

“So I’m a radio stuck on every station at once.”

“The analogy is imprecise but captures the relevant dysfunction.”

“Dysfunction. Thank you, Archie.”

“Accurate terminology facilitates efficient correction.”

Steve tried again with narrower focus. Flame. Just flame. The Ether between his fingers flickered blue-white and a small, controlled tongue of fire appeared above his right palm. He held it for two seconds. Then a pressure ripple knocked the guidebook away beside him.

“That was supposed to be just fire.”

“It was predominantly fire,” Archie said. “Approximately seventy-three percent thermal output with twenty-seven percent concussive bleed. Your channel isolation will improve with repetition. The intensity will scale with the amount of ether pushed.”

“Seventy-three percent. He’s grading me.”

“I am providing diagnostic data. Grading implies a qualitative assessment I am not authorised to make.”

“That is a qualitative assessment.”

“It is a quantitative observation that you have interpreted qualitatively. This is a recurring pattern.”

Donna spoke from behind her hands. “Conduit. Energy Manipulation. He’s going to be insufferable.”

“I just opened a toolkit that lets me manipulate matter at a molecular level through pure Ether projection and you’re worried about my personality?”

“Yes.”

* * *

Steve found the symbol on the back wall while Arthur and Donna were testing their evolved techniques.

The pool had drained to a shallow film across the cistern floor. Behind where the water had been deepest, carved into limestone that predated the fort, the basilica, and every human structure above them, was a spiral.

The mark was Seeder notation. Complete and precise, its geometry carrying a mathematical progression that Steve’s fingers traced without touching. He’d seen fragments at every node, partial symbols and eroded marks. This one was whole.

“They were here,” he said as Arthur and Donna came to look. “Before Pierre’s chapel. Before the Romans. Before the Phocaean Greeks who founded the city. This was carved into the bedrock of this hill by people who left the planet before any of those civilisations existed.”

He pulled out the guidebook and sketched the spiral alongside the fragments from London, Saint-Victor, and every other site where the marks had appeared. Each fragment was part of a larger system, a notation language that recurred across thousands of kilometres and tens of thousands of years.

“Every layer of this city is built on top of something older,” Steve said. “The oldest layer is always theirs.”

Archie’s geometry hovered at the edge of the spiral’s glow. For a moment, Arthur thought the construct looked different. Brighter. The facets spun faster, the way they did when Archie was processing at capacity.

“Archie,” Arthur said. “You recognise this.”

“The symbol is a standard infrastructure marker. It indicates sustained Ether flow in a directional pattern. This notation denotes a monitoring function.”

“Monitoring what?”

Archie’s silence was the familiar wall.

Arthur decided to let it go. They’d learned more from this dungeon than Ether and iron. They’d learned what the hill was built on. What everything was built on.

They climbed out of the cistern, up through the magazine level and the garrison corridors and into the grey light of Marseille.

Arthur squinted against the glare. The golden Madonna caught the light above them, broadcasting on frequencies the parish priests who’d served beneath her for centuries had never suspected.

* * *

Maryam met them at the compound gate with the same canvas bag and an expression that had graduated from expecting worse to expecting exactly this.

“Again.” She shook her head and got to work.

Arthur’s forearm. Donna’s ribs. Steve’s hands. The same three patients, the same three injuries in slightly different configurations. Her hands were warm against Steve’s palms as she cleaned and rewrapped them.

A priest was there this time, helping around the makeshift ward.

Arthur had seen him around the compound, a quiet man in his sixties, clerical collar worn over a fisherman’s jumper, helping where hands were needed. Carrying water or stacking supplies. Sitting with the injured when Maryam was busy, offering support that didn’t require words. The flock had scattered, but the shepherd was still tending.

He was the one who brought Steve water while Maryam worked on his hands. A clay cup, offered without ceremony, held to Steve’s lips.

“Merci,” Steve said.

“De rien.” The priest sat on the crate beside him. His French carried the Provençal warmth of a man who had spent his life in this city. “I’m Father Matthieu. You are the one who understands the numbers, yes? The system. The levels.”

“I try to,” Steve said.

The priest was quiet for a moment. His face had the conflict of someone who had been carrying a thought for days and was deciding whether to let it out.

“The notification,” he said finally. “When the sky changed. Everyone received it.”

“The system message. Yes.”

“It said 1,260 days.” The priest’s hands were folded in his lap, trying to keep them steady. “I have been a priest for thirty-one years, Monsieur. I have read the Book of Revelation more times than I can count. I never once took it literally.” He looked at Steve. “But that number. 1,260 days. That number is in my scripture. Exactly.”

Steve went still with something clicking into place. A pattern that might be bigger than he was ready for.

“Revelation, Chapter twelve, verse six. The woman fled into the wilderness, to a place prepared for her by God, where she might be taken care of for 1,260 days.” The priest’s voice was steady and practised, a man reciting scripture he’d recited a thousand times, hearing it differently for the first time. “And in Daniel, Chapter seven. Times, times, and half a time. Three and a half years. The same duration. The woman in the wilderness is protected for exactly this long.”

“Protected,” Steve repeated. “In a place prepared for her.”

The priest’s hands tightened on each other. “The woman is given a refuge. A place of safety. For exactly 1,260 days. And now a barrier appears around our planet. A refuge. For exactly 1,260 days. I don’t know what to do with this, Monsieur. But I cannot stop thinking about it.”

Steve was staring at nothing. Arthur could see the calculation happening behind his eyes.

“1,260 days,” Steve said slowly. “Divided by thirty. Forty-two months.”

The number landed in the cistern-cold air of the compound like a stone into still water.

“Forty-two,” Steve said again. “The Seeder calibration constant. The attribute baseline. The integer built into the levelling formula. The number the Curators gave to Douglas Adams the same way they gave the stat system to Gary Gygax. And the Fence, the barrier protecting the planet, lasts exactly forty-two months.”

He looked at the priest. “Who wrote Revelation?”

“John of Patmos. First century AD. Exiled to a Greek island.”

“A Greek island,” Steve said. “In the Mediterranean. On the Grid.”

Donna had been listening from the next crate. She hadn’t moved or interrupted. But now she spoke, and her voice carried the flat certainty of someone who had reached a conclusion the others hadn’t.

“The woman fled into the wilderness,” she said. “Protected for 1,260 days. Protected from what?”

The compound went quiet around them.

“The Fence isn’t keeping us in,” Donna said. “It’s keeping something out.”

The priest crossed himself slowly. His theology might have been right all along, in a shape no theologian had imagined.

Steve pulled out the guidebook and began writing. His hands were bandaged. The pen shook. He didn’t care and wrote anyway. 1,260 days. 42 months. The Fence. The woman in the wilderness. The place prepared. Protected from something that was waiting for the protection to end.

Arthur sat with the Ironhold across his knees and thought about walls. He’d been building them his whole life, reinforcing structures, shoring up failures, putting himself between the load and the collapse. The Fence was a wall. The biggest wall anyone had ever built. And walls existed for one reason: because something on the other side was trying to get through.

The archetype had never been a choice; it was a recognition. The system had watched him build walls for ten levels and named what it saw. The question it hadn’t answered was the one Donna had just asked. What was on the other side?

He looked at the shield and at his hands. He was a Guardian. He will hold.

* * *

That evening, Arthur found Donna on the quay wall, staring south toward the Calanques.

“Léonie,” she said. “The diver Steve mentioned. Let’s talk to her tomorrow.”

They had one node left. Thirty-seven metres underwater. A painted hall with twenty-seven-thousand-year-old handprints and something the Seeders had prepared before the people who made those prints had been born.

“Something is waiting for the Fence to drop,” she said, her hand moving to the axe at her hip.

Arthur didn’t reply. The wall had always known what it was for.
