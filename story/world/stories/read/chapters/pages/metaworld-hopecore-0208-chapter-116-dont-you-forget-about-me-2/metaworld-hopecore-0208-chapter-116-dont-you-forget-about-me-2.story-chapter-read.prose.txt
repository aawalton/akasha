The last four minutes hit Valorie harder than anybody else in the room. Her Eppie was going to an arts academy to be enrolled. For the first time since the film began, they left the city.

Was it symbolic of renewal? Of leaving behind the past?

The gate wasn’t hidden like in LAPA. It was old and enormous, made out of red bricks formed by the hands of master masons. The sign on top read Saint Cecilia’s, and an expensive lady in a pencil skirt and half-glasses processed Josie’s admission with a smile and a welcome.

Having lived a whole life in California, Valorie had no idea where this place was. Even so, she found parallels in the long corridors with a gaggle of girls on the far end, laughing just out of focus, wearing the same uniform that Josie now wore. It was in a corridor like this that she had walked with William and thought herself on top of the world. And it was in a corridor like this that she had rediscovered Euphemia, back from the dead, to set her world ablaze.

Beneath her cheeks, her friend’s body was warm but bony.

On the screen, Josie Novak found her dorm, every step weighed down by the events of two weeks where she had lost a family that didn’t care, and found one that did, then lost it all again.

She carried Marcel’s plant. Valorie felt the symbolism was too sentimental, too romantic, even for her. She wondered if Eppie noticed as well the softness of it, how it seemed to disconnect from the rest of the film, which had a perfect cyclical script.

Should she tell her friend? Would the critics find the epilogue disagreeable?

With great ceremony, Josie deposited the plant with a trowel in her new garden, pressing the soil down around it before sitting back on her Mary Janes and staring at it with dazed recollection.
