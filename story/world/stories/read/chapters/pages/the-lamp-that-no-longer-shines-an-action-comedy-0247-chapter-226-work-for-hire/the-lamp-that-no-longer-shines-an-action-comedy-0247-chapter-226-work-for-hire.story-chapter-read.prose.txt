[Time]: Autumn Semester, Day 106 — 9:00 AM[Location]: West Campus · Third Alchemy Tower · Assessment Cells

[Final Examination: Doll-Making Basics & Soul Architecture]Construct a doll. Weighted equally between Autonomy and Operability.

Hathaway sat alone in a sealed cell with the standard-issue materials case open at her elbow, and let her developer brain read the patch notes.

Autonomy was the local intelligence: how convincingly the chassis walked around being a person while nobody was driving it. Operability was the remote session: sensory latency, motor fidelity, and the ceiling on how much mana a host could shove through the frame before the frame stopped being a frame. Sharon wanted both, in one object, with the handoff clean.

Hathaway turned a carving knife over her knuckles and felt something in her chest begin to enjoy itself. I know exactly what this is.

It was a bot.

Tier one handles the queue. She pulled the specification sheet across the bench. Transfer to a human agent the moment somebody says the word manager.

She got about four lines in. Direct question requiring context the doll did not possess: ping. Any member of faculty entering the room: ping. Unsolicited physical contact: ping. Anyone asking a question that opened with the word why: ping, immediately, no buffering, because nothing that started with why had ever once been survivable on autopilot. She built the cascade three pages deep, nested the conditionals until the logic gates squeaked, priority-ranked the whole tree, and then at the bottom of the last page she wrote one final line and underlined it twice.

Victoria.

No condition attached.

With the backend sealed she lifted the mercury out of the case. Nothing in the kit ran further from a specialist, which was exactly why she wanted it. She picked up the knives, and the metal obeyed. That was the part she had not been ready for. On the first morning of term this same silver had gone under her blade like it had opinions about her, and what came off the bench eleven minutes later had been wrong enough that Surtrina had looked directly at it and asked whether it was supposed to be one of her mortal friends.

Fourteen weeks later the metal parted like warm clay. She laid in the jawline, the stubborn arch of the brow, the exact slope of her own nose, and she did not shift a single proportion off true, because a body double that only mostly looks like you is a liability with legs. She sat back and audited it against the one benchmark that mattered.

Surtrina would recognize this. Her inner art director closed the ticket with enormous satisfaction. Surtrina would recognize this from across a courtyard.

The case also ran to silk thread, bolt cloth, and a jar of spirit pigment that lifted a color straight off whatever you held it against. Fourteen weeks ago she had produced a garment with two sleeves and no opinion whatsoever about where a shoulder belonged. She wove the coat in eleven minutes, held the jar against her own cuff, and took the shade exactly.

The persona took four minutes, because she had been running it since her first performance review in a previous life. Bright. Compliant. Aggressively helpful. Yes, Professor. Understood, Professor. I will have those compiled by this afternoon.

That left the capacity question, which was the only genuinely hard call on the bench. The whole examination lived in the combination: substrates that took a personality, substrates that took a load, and not one pairing in the kit that was generous about both. A semester of bleeding on this bench had given her an eye for the thresholds. Built out of the best of it, without a single wasted gram, the case topped out at two thousand M-Units of host possession, and at two thousand and one it converted into a wide dispersal pattern of very expensive shrapnel.

Hathaway capped hers at twelve hundred, put everything she saved into the behavioral matrix, seated the core, sealed the chest cavity, and stepped back.

She took the pigment last. The jar against the back of her own hand for the skin, against a lifted strand for the hair, held a knuckle's width off her own open eye for the last of it, and the silver stopped being silver.

The doll opened them, blinked twice to calibrate, and stood up off the bench at her own height, wearing the forgery, with a bright and professional smile.

Hathaway folded her arms and pointed grandly at the door.

"You," she said. "Go assassinate Professor Deiner for me."

The doll looked at the door. It looked at its own hands. It raised one finger and pointed it at the middle of its own chest.

"Me?"

Then the professional smile came off its face like a sticker in the rain. The doll dropped the hand, put both fists on its hips, shifted its weight onto one leg, and rolled its eyes hard enough to move air.

"I'm hungry," it announced, at the exact register Hathaway used when she had run out of patience. "I want lunch. Feed me or I'm striking."

Hathaway stared at it. "You are a doll. You do not have a digestive tract. What are you going to eat?"

"Oh my god, lady." The doll's lip curled with genuine artistry. "You want me working your shift and there's no meal allowance? Where's the labor board? What, am I supposed to take this face down to Victoria and apply for a position warming her bed just to get fed around here?"

Hathaway's composure came apart in one piece, like a dropped pane.

"Shut your mouth," she hissed, lunging across the bench with both hands out. "One more word and I am rolling you back to factory settings."

A small, polite cough sounded from the doorway behind her.

Hathaway went absolutely still.

Sharon Curie stood two feet away with one oversized white sleeve folded over the other and a slender unlit cigarette held loosely between two fingers, and the perpetually wet golden eyes were doing something that had no business happening in a face built like that.

"An incredibly vivid execution, Miss Hathaway," the Grandmaster said, in a voice like a feather landing. "The autonomy is... robust."

On the bench, the doll had already snapped to attention. Hands clasped neatly at its waist, spine straight, chin level, beaming up at the professor with the pure and radiant obedience of a student who had never once had a thought worth hiding.

Inside Hathaway's skull, the private channel caught fire.

[Doll → Host]: Say something. Say something.[Doll → Host]: Faculty in proximity. This is condition two. This is literally condition two.[Doll → Host]: ESCALATING. TAKE THE WHEEL. TAKE THE WHEEL.

Hathaway kept her face in the shape of a smile through what felt like structural effort.

[Host → Doll]: I am standing three inches away from you.[Host → Doll]: Why would I remote into a body when I am physically present at the same coordinates? Do you want me working two shifts simultaneously? Is that the ask?

[Doll → Host]: TICKET IS OPEN. TICKET REMAINS OPEN.

She had written three pages of conditionals and left out the one line that mattered: if the host is in the room, handle it yourself.

"Thank you, Professor," Hathaway said, at a pitch she did not recognize. "It's still indexing."

Sharon looked at the sweating student, and then at the angelic doll beside her, and made a small mark on her slate. The corner of her mouth moved once before she got it back under management, and she drifted back out toward the next cell with her sleeves swallowing her hands.

The instant the white robe cleared the door, Hathaway slapped the shutdown rune between the doll's shoulder blades, caught the body as it went slack, and posted the whole thing into her spatial ring.

She sat down and put her face in both hands.

Nobody had ever mentioned that her own face could look that punchable.

[Time]: Autumn Semester, Day 107 — 1:45 PM[Location]: West Campus · Yggdrasil Academy · Wondrous Item Hall

Emilia Lucas's final came with a waiver attached to it.

[Final Submission: Wondrous Item Blueprint]Option A. Retain all rights. The blueprint is graded to standard.Option B. Assign all rights to the House of Lucas. The blueprint is graded to standard, and becomes eligible for physical fabrication.

The justification ran three lines and was legally airtight, because Emilia Lucas could not build an object she did not hold the right to build.

It was a perfectly fair choice, and every second-year in the hall could see the hollow at the center of it.

One blueprint would be selected for fabrication. All forty-five would belong, permanently and at no cost, to the finest wondrous item designer currently alive.

Game jam. Hathaway's inner producer had recognized the shape of it from the doorway and clicked through the terms without slowing down. Sponsored by a publisher, judged by the publisher, and the entry form is a rights grab. Read it. Sign it anyway. Everybody does.

There was no procedural reason to be in the room. Emilia read a submitted blueprint faster than a student could stand up from the terminal she had submitted it on, and the grade pushed straight to the profile.

All forty-five of them were in their seats.

Hathaway had spent a while watching the hall before she committed her own file, which was how she ended up submitting after everyone she cared about. A chime went off in the seat directly in front of her. Surtrina let out a long breath that lifted the hair off her forehead, glanced down at her screen, and raised one hand. A soundproof ward closed over the five of them and cut the rest of the hall away mid-noise. She twisted around, reversed the terminal, and set it down on Hathaway's desk.

82.

"Right," Surtrina said, and her mouth pulled sideways. "Looks like I didn't hit it. What have the rest of you got?"

Victoria read her screen, laid the slate face-down on the desk, and folded her hands on top of it. "Eighty-three."

Out past the ward, students were standing up and packing without speaking to anybody. Hathaway watched two of them go, and neither had any color left above the collar.

"Ninety." It came from the seat beside Surtrina, and the flat unbothered water of Spectra's voice had a crack running through it.

Hathaway came upright. "What did you build?"

Spectra's pale brows had drawn all the way in. "A perfume," she said, to her own screen, as though it had lied to her. "An atomizer with no reservoir limit. The target smells nothing but the scent she loves most, rendered out of her own memory."

"Only ninety?" Surtrina's tact went out from under her entirely.

Hathaway had wanted one before the sentence finished, and nobody inside the ward was making much of a job of pretending otherwise.

Yenna sat with her hands folded in her lap and her ears perfectly level. "I received ninety-four."

The number came down on the table like a dropped ingot.

Victoria's pale eyes narrowed a fraction. "You submitted the flower field."

"I did." Yenna's voice barely disturbed the air. "The most beautiful flower field in the world."

Hathaway frowned, tipping her head. "Is 'most beautiful in the world' a quantifiable parameter? How do you even hand that to a fabricator?"

"You cannot," Yenna said. "So the artifact does not carry a field of its own. It renders the field the woman looking at it would call the most beautiful in the world, whether or not she has ever once thought about what that would be."

Spectra's reads a file that is already sitting there. Hathaway's developer brain sat down heavily somewhere. This one has to write the file when there isn't one, and be right the first time.

Her own terminal buzzed against her thigh. She drew a slow breath, arranged her face into something that could survive a bad number, and turned the screen over.

100.

Hathaway set the terminal down on the desk. Her expression moved through roughly four separate arrangements inside two seconds and came to rest on a grin considerably wider than the situation could support, and her mouth opened before a single one of her threads got a hand on it.

"Haha," she said. "Funny thing, guys. The hundred is me."

The silence inside the ward acquired mass.

Hathaway wanted to slap herself. Two entire lifetimes of professional survival, and my social layer is still actively attempting to get me killed.

Four separate faces arrived at the same exhausted expression. Before any of them could begin, the ward thinned out and let go.

Emilia Lucas was coming down the aisle. Several students who were on their feet with bags over their shoulders sat quietly back down.

She stopped at the end of their row. The severe eyes moved across the five of them, and for something under a second, they softened.

Hathaway knew that look. Her manager had worn it in another life, on a carpeted stage, handing her a plaque with Employee of the Year stamped into it: the exact fondness of a man appraising an animal that pulls well and eats very little.

Then Emilia's eyes came to rest on her.

"When you designed the behavioral parameters of this item," Emilia said, quietly enough that it went no further than the desks, "did you reference a prototype?"

Hathaway's throat had gone dry. Her tactical thread produced deflections, all of them serviceable.

"Yes," she said.

Emilia nodded once and turned to include the rest of the table.

"Your designs were all spectacular," she said. "I have not seen this many excellent concepts arrive in a single cycle in years."

Surtrina managed a nod that did not sit properly on her neck. The others accepted it with careful, level courtesy.

Emilia walked back up the aisle and out.

She just complimented her own inventory, Hathaway's accountant thread observed, watching the door swing shut on forty-five blueprints that had stopped belonging to anybody in the room. Warmly. Sincerely. Out loud.

Victoria turned her head, and the lacquer came off her voice.

"Emilia Lucas did not want a gift," she said. "She wanted an answer. It appears that out of the five of us, you are the only one who gave her one."

Somewhere behind Hathaway's sternum a cold thing turned over and settled. "But what answer did I give her?"

Yenna's three golden tails lay perfectly still behind her chair, and she was looking at the empty doorway.

"That," the fox witch said, "depends entirely on the person receiving the gift."
