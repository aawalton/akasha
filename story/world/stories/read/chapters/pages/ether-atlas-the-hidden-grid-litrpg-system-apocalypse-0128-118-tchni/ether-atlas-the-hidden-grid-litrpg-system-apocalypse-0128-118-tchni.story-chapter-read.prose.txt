Chapter 118

“Every god on that mountain is beautiful and idle except one. They threw Hephaestus off it, he broke on landing, and he came back with a limp and a forge, and not one of them ever stopped needing what he made.”— The Ether Atlas: Field Notes of Steven Parr, Piraeus

Arthur opened the gate at two in the morning, and the hinge he had reset on the morning the archbishop came did not make a sound. Arthur had been pleased with the work as he was pleased with any repair that would outlast the man who made it, and it had not occurred to him then that a silent gate is also a gate nobody hears.

Petros came through without a word. Whatever he had been wearing for the past four hours was still on him, the shoulders were wrong and his face was dead. Then he saw who was holding the gate, and it came back. The ease climbed into the shoulders. The mouth found its shape and he was the man who fixed the winches and bought the rounds again. The mask had come down, or did it come back up, Arthur wondered.

“Ela,” Petros said. “You are awake.”

“I’m awake. So are others in their room, I suspect.”

“You sit in the dark with cold coffee. Kala. Very healthy.” He put his hand briefly on Arthur’s shoulder on the way past. “Thank you. Go to bed, wall. I’m no good to anybody tonight.”

Eleni fed them before anybody was allowed to say anything, which Arthur had stopped fighting some time ago. Petros came down late and ate standing until his mother made him sit with the plate. She stood at the table with a pan in hand, as if daring them to leave the plates unfinished.

“Twelve in the ring,” Petros said. “Thirteen with me. Chairs in a circle, a wide room behind a plain door. No incense, no ikon, nothing on the walls. Nobody drinking, which in a room of men who had lost everything is the strangest thing I saw all night.”

“Two men at the inner door,” he said, and put his fork down to show it with his hands. “Standing like her.” He tipped his head at Donna. “Like bodyguards or bouncers. They did not speak to anybody and nobody spoke to them.”

“Did you just compare me to a bouncer?” Donna asked, her eyes narrowing.

“Re, I only mean they stood like people waiting for something to happen.” Petros waved.

“There was a man. Lefteris. He spoke to me. Took both my hands like I was his brother home from a war.” Petros lifted a shoulder. “There were four or five like him working the ones who were crying. Sitting close. Holding the hands. Listening with the face and sorting with the eyes.”

Sarah had unrolled a piece of bandage and was rolling it again. The edges were coming out crooked. She started it over.

Steve had the pencil out and had written almost nothing. “What did they preach?”

“They preached nothing.”

“They must have preached something. A cult that takes people out to a hill has to have a doctrine. It has a god with a name and a story about why the suffering is worth it and a scripture of some sort. That is what the room is for.”

“All he told me was I was not weak, I was only awake and I had made the right decision.” Petros opened his hands. “That is not a religion, Re. That is a man selling boats.”

Steve put the pencil down with the care of somebody putting something down that he wanted to throw. He had in his head multiple scenarios ready. The apparatus, the doctrine, the liturgy. He had no idea what to do with chairs.

“After, he told me I was brave for coming. He told me the strong take everything now and that I was owed better.” He set the cup down. “He told me to come back and when I come back he will tell me more.”

“That is not a congregation,” Donna said.

She had been still through the report, her hands flat on the table, reading the layout Petros had described and trying to make sense of it.

“No shared meal,” she said. “No prayer. No preaching to the room. Someone decides who comes back and who does not.” She looked at Arthur. “That is a queue.”

Nobody said anything for a moment. Eleni took Petros’s empty plate and did not fill it again, which from Eleni was a statement.

Petros turned his coffee cup a quarter-turn on the saucer. “At the end, Lefteris sits with me. He is very good, this one. He does not ask what I want. He tells me what has been done to me and he waits to see if I flinch.” He looked at his sister and then away from her. “So I told him about Sarah.”

Sarah’s hands stopped on the bandage. She flattened it on the wood with the heel of her hand and began a fourth time.

“And he liked it,” Petros said. “Po po po, he liked it very much. He walked me to the door himself and he said, come back tomorrow in the evening, Petro, and we will talk properly, the two of us.”

“That is the interview.” Steve was up and pacing the three steps the kitchen allowed. “They screened you and you passed something, and now there is an interview.”

“Or they turned him out gently and told him to come back so he would not make trouble in the room,” Arthur said, because somebody had to say the boring version out loud.

“They did not turn me out.” Petros spread his hands. “Re, I did not eat door.”

Steve stopped pacing and looked at him.

“He means nobody shut him out,” Sarah said, to the bandage in her lap. “He means he was let in.”

“I was let in,” Petros agreed, satisfied.

The forge under the old rail bridge announced itself two streets early with the hard bright ring of a hammer working alone. The smell of it reached them at the corner and it was familiar enough now that Arthur’s shoulders came down a notch as he walked into it.

Everything else about the shop had changed. The floor space that had been clear when they carried the trident in was stacked to the roof beam with grilles, bar welded into bar, dozens of them, leaning in ranks against the brickwork like church railings waiting for a church. There was raw stock everywhere, cut and bundled and labelled. Milo was at the second anvil working on a hinge and dozens lay beside him.

Stavros came round the anvil, wiping his hands.

“You are late,” he said. “You told me you were coming for the gear and then you did not come, and I have had your weapons sitting in the back sweating for you like a bride.”

“We had a meeting with the Council,” Arthur said.

“Then you have my sympathy and none of my surprise.” Stavros jerked his chin at the ranked grilles. “There it is. That is what the Council does. Gates and bars and grilles, from a shop with two men in it, and they nail the paper by my door so I can look at it while I work.”

Arthur went and looked at the paper. It was a requisition, ruled and stamped, and it listed quantities and a delivery date. “Forty gate leaves,” Arthur read. “Three hundred and twenty grilles. Four hundred hinge lugs.”

“Iron gates. Grilles. Reinforcement bars for the acropolis. Every smith on the coast between here and Megara has a paper like that one,” Stavros said, jabbing a finger at the list. “And if I do not work for Council, they take my charcoal.”

“You could have asked us to wait,” Arthur said.

“I could.” Stavros shrugged. “A deal is a deal. And I did not want a Council gate to be the last thing I ever make.”

He went and got the gear. He laid it out on the bench piece by piece and stood back from it.

Donna picked up the mace before Stavros had finished arranging. The head was flanged, six raised ridges radiating from a central hub, forward-weighted so the balance sat an inch ahead of the grip where her wrist would drive it. She turned it over and found the pry on the butt, a curved steel tooth forged into the base of the haft.

“Finally,” Donna said, giving Stavros a single nod. “I won’t be carrying a hardware tool for a secondary weapon.”

Arthur took the Messer next. Single-edged, the curve running forward from a wide choil. He took it through a slow cut and felt the mass carry into the swing where a hammer’s mass carried. Arthur was no swordsman and this weapon did not pretend to be anything other than what it was, a blade shaped for a man who hit things.

“You do not thrust with it,” Stavros said.

“I know.”

“The point is for a man who is already finished,” Stavros repeated.

“I know.”

Steve picked up the rod last. A straight length of transmuted iron, the width of a finger and tapering to a clean point at one end. Stavros had turned it on whatever lathe he had improvised and the taper was precise, the surface worked to a low sheen that caught the forge light. Steve held it up and turned it slowly.

“Go ahead,” Donna said. “Call it what it is.”

“It is a focus,” Steve said. “A precision-engineered channelling instrument with a directional output taper.”

“It is a wand, Steve,” Donna said. “You are a grown man holding a wand.”

Stavros laid out the remaining pieces without ceremony. Uniform iron plates cut to the dimensions Maria had given him, and two bundles of thin drawn iron strips for Sarah’s bracelet.

“We can’t take the plates right now,” Arthur said.

Stavros stopped with the rag in his hand and waited.

“We have nothing to bind them with,” Arthur said. “The leather is not finished and we do not have spare cores right now. Will you hold them here until we can use them?”

Stavros shrugged and pointed to a shelf above the quench tub. “They will be on the shelf,” he said. “Come for them.”

“We only have one item to bind, might as well get it over with,” Arthur said, looking at Steve.

“Here?” he asked.

Arthur looked at Stavros. Stavros folded his arms across his chest and put his back to the masonry, which was the answer he had given the last time.

“I already did the shield here,” Arthur said. “He isn’t going to faint.”

Steve tipped the alpha core out into his palm. It was the larger of the two, half again the size of the ordinary one, and the hue in it ran deeper. A dark blue-violet that took the firelight and gave very little of it back. He laid the rod flat across the anvil face and set the stone against the tapered end and covered it with his hand.

Arthur watched him close his eyes and go still. Steve’s face, which ordinarily ran three associations behind the eyes at once, went quiet and singular. Intent found its line.

Arthur knew roughly what Steve was thinking, because they had discussed it on the walk down. A rod was a pipe. What he wanted was a pipe that did not leak. Cleaner channelling, less bleed, a straight run from the Matrix to the air and to the target.

The core sank into the iron.

It went slowly, over the better part of two minutes, and where it dissolved the iron woke and rewrote itself. The grain along the shaft realigned, the dark whorls of the trident metal straightening and running true from grip to tip. The taper drew itself finer. When the change reached the point, the last centimetre of it darkened almost to black.

The jeweller kept his shop behind a shuttered arcade off the market street, and it was cold inside after the forge, and it smelled of pitch and borax and, underneath those, the thin sharp bite of acid.

Alekos Fotiadis was somewhere past seventy. He worked at a bench in the window with a leather apron slung underneath it, hanging like a hammock to catch the filings, and he did not stand up when Sarah’s mother’s name was mentioned, though he did put down his file.

“You are Eleni’s daughter,” he said. “The doctor. Kala. Show me the iron.”

Sarah unrolled the cloth on the bench and laid the strips on it. The old man picked one up, bent it a few degrees between his thumbs, sighted along it and put it down.

“Drawn well. Stavros?”

“Stavros.”

“He has good hands for a butcher.” Fotiadis reached under the bench and swung out a torch with a nozzle no thicker than a pencil. He started it with a click and a blue flame came up without the hiss of gas. Steve made a sound at the back of his throat.

“The Council took my gas in the second month,” Fotiadis said, to the torch. “Along with my brother’s van and the good scales.”

“Is that a core?” Steve had come forward, his hand was hovering. He took it back seeing the look on the jeweller’s face. “You have a bound torch.”

“A fox came into the yard. It had a stone in it.” The old man adjusted the flame down with a finger and did not look up. “My nephew put the stone in the torch and the torch works. Before, I had a bottle. Now I have this.”

“Hand,” Fotiadis said, looking up at Sarah.

Sarah put her left hand on the bench, palm down, and the old man took her wrist between two fingers and turned it as though it were a piece of artwork.

“A bracciale schiava con anello,” Steve said. “Although the Italian is a nineteenth-century revival term and the object is much older than that. You get it in Rajasthan, where it’s a haath phool. You get it across the Levant. And the genuinely interesting thing is that—”

“I have known the word since I was fifteen,” Sarah said, not turning round. “It is a baciamano and I had one. It was gold, and my father hated it.”

“I have made forty of them,” Fotiadis said. He had a strip of paper round her wrist and was marking it with a pencil. “Twenty-one in gold and nineteen in silver. Now let me work.” He drew the paper off and marked it again. “Middle finger.”

He measured the middle finger against the same strip. He measured across the back of the hand, corner to corner, thumb bone to little bone, and marked the paper there too, and it was three marks on a scrap of paper and it took him under a minute.

“Chains from the wrist to the ring, and a thicker chain here across the back where the other chains gather,” he said, touching the wrist. “You want for both hands same bracelet?”

“Yes,” Sarah said.

“Choose design,” the jeweller said, opening a large book of pictures in front of her. “I can’t work all with iron. Too hard. But some I can.”

What followed next was about thirty minutes of Sarah and the jeweller going back and forth on the bracelet design.

“How did she survive in the MSF?” Donna asked Arthur, who had no answer, so he shrugged instead.

“It is a tool,” Donna said finally, after checking the shop and street the third time.

“It’s jewellery.”

“Sarah.”

“It is jewellery,” Sarah said, “that I am going to put a core into, so I can channel Ether more effectively.” Her hand stayed flat on the bench. “It is also jewellery.”

“Kala.” Fotiadis put the balance out and laid the strips in one pan until the beam came level. He set half the strips to one side and swept the rest into a wooden tray. “One kilo for the piece. One kilo for me. Same as the butcher.”

“Same as the butcher,” Sarah agreed.

“Tomorrow morning,” he said. “Not early. I am old and the light comes when it comes.”

“The last hands I measured were for a wedding,” he said, to the work. “In the spring. A boy from Keratsini and a girl I have known since she was born. Two rings and it took me a week and they were good rings. You are the first hands since.”

“Tell Eleni to give me more time when you want wedding rings,” the jeweller said, turning back to the window and the light, and Arthur saw colour rising into Sarah’s face.

The next evening, Arthur was in the courtyard with the Messer across his knees and an oiled rag in his hand, giving the blade a coat it did not need, because the alternative was sitting still.

Petros came down the stairs and Arthur knew from the sound of it on the steps that the other man had come down instead. He crossed the kitchen without saying anything to anybody, his hands were in his coat pockets and his face did not have the Petros grin.

Petros drew the bolt and swung the gate open into the dark lane.

The gate made no sound at all.

FIELD NOTE OF STEVEN PARR

The Wrong Variable

I have taught the Iron Age as the great levelling, and I have taught it well enough that people have paid to hear it. The argument runs like this. Bronze is an alloy, and one half of that alloy is tin, and there is almost no tin in the Aegean, so every sword in Mycenae stood at the end of a supply line two thousand miles long, and a supply line like that can only be run by a palace with scribes and warehouses and a fleet. Arm the men, and the palace owns the men. Then the routes fell over around 1200 BCE, and the smiths went to iron, which is the fourth most abundant element in the crust of this planet and lies in the bog at the bottom of the field. Anybody could dig it. Anybody could smelt it, badly, and then better. And out of that, within four centuries, you get a farmer who can buy his own spear and his own shield, and a phalanx made of farmers who bought their own, and a city that has to ask those farmers what they think. Hierarchy follows metallurgy. Change the metal, change who holds the city.

Today I stood in a shop under a railway bridge where a man is turning out the best ironwork on this coast, and then in a shop behind an arcade where an old man with a bench and one lamp is doing the finest, and between them they have every skill and every scrap of material required to arm this entire city to a standard that would have made a Mycenaean wanax weep. And neither of them can finish a single piece that matters without a stone. And the stones come out of things you have killed. We left a stack of perfectly good iron plates on Stavros’s shelf this afternoon, faced and filed and worth more than a house, because we did not have four small pebbles to put in them. The metal was never the variable. I have had it by the wrong end for twenty years. What decides who holds the weapons is not what the weapons are made of, it is the method of acquisition, and iron levelled the ancient world for one reason only, which is that acquisition was digging, and any fool with a spade can dig. Cores are not dug. They are harvested, and only the strong can harvest, and the harvest makes them stronger, and they take the next one more easily than the last.

✎ Iron levelled us because anyone could dig. Cores invert it, because only the strong can harvest. We have built an economy in which the weapons flow, automatically and by design, to whoever needs them least.
