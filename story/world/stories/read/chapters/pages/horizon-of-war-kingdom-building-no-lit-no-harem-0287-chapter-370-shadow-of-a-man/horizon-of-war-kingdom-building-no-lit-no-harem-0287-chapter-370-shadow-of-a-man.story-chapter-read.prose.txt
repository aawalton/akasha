Chapter 370

Shadow of a Man

Great Keep

It was well past nightfall, yet every chandelier and lantern in the hall burned at full light. Candles that would ordinarily have been spared for another evening now blazed from every fitting, casting hard shadows across the walls as news of the crisis spread.

The Lord of Galdia, who had arrived only moments earlier with his squire, heard of the betrayal for the first time. He gasped and went still. Then his gaze moved across the gathered lords, officers, and officials. The same grim knowledge was already written on every face.

“How certain are we of this news?” he asked, as though pleading to be awakened from a nightmare. Never in his life had he heard of anything so vile.

Sir Michael adjusted his eyepatch, damp with sweat, and motioned to the signalman standing in the corner.

The man bearing the insignia of the Korelia City Guard stepped forward and reported bitterly, “The message bears Sir Harold’s cipher. The sequence and verification signs all matched.”

“There must be some mistake,” the old lord protested.

Sir Michael stepped in. “Three days ago, the Navalnian army began advancing against Sir Harold’s positions alone. Then the troops marching up from the rear to reinforce our garrison were ambushed.”

“It could have been the Margrave’s men,” the old lord argued. “Some of them may have been bought by Navalnia. That does not mean the Margrave himself...”

“We received reports of suspicious movements even before this,” Sir Michael answered patiently. “Now Sir Harold has sent word that a few men from the Margravate risked their lives to warn him of the betrayal.”

The strain was plain on the old lord’s face, but there was no gentler way to say what came next.

“Furthermore, from what we know of the ambush, the columns were attacked while they were resting. That means—”

“The attackers were close,” the old lord realized aloud, his voice low. “Close enough to watch them from nearby...”

“The only one who could do that was the Margrave’s army, most likely watching from the nearby cities and castles.” Sir Michael's voice was bitter. “If it had happened in only one place, we might blame a rogue officer. But at two or three different locations? That could not have happened without the Margrave’s blessing.”

The Lord of Galdia reached for the table and gripped its edge to keep himself upright. His squire quickly moved to support him.

Lord Jorge of Three Hills motioned for him to sit. He poured the older man a drink and took the seat beside him. They spoke quietly over their drinks, trying to make sense of the betrayal.

The war in Edessa had ended the previous year after the Shogunate intervened in force. Now, it had flared up again, with the Margrave turning to the other side.

A few seats away, Calub, the governor of Korelia, conferred with Cecile, the Lord’s chamberlain, over what measures they might need to take at home. Beyond them sat Lady Ingrid, the Lady’s chamberlain, with Lady Ella of Dawn and Lady Astrid of White Lake. Their voices remained low as they considered the grim implications.

Meanwhile, Sir Michael remained standing beside a separate table, his squire at his side, both poring over the map of Edessa.

At another table nearby, the two scribes conferred in hushed voices.

The door opened, and an assistant signalman entered under the escort of a guard.

Lady Ingrid rose from her seat, drawing the eyes of those around her. “Has it been done?” she asked.

“Yes, Lady. We relayed the same message to Canardia,” the assistant answered, standing beside his superior.

“Good,” Ingrid said. “Now send it again.”

Her order drew several glances.

“Pardon, Lady?” the assistant asked.

“Relay the same message to Canardia again,” Ingrid repeated, her voice clear.

“But, Lady, we just—”

“I know,” she said. “The House needs to be certain that Canardia receives the message without error. And this time, ask for confirmation as soon as they receive it. Treat it with the utmost urgency.”

The assistant signalman bowed and hurried from the hall.

As they watched him leave, weary breaths and muted sighs passed through the hall.

Low murmurs soon followed as those gathered spoke among themselves, each trying to grasp the full weight of the situation.

“I’m telling you, it’s impossible!” the Lord of Galdia suddenly exclaimed after draining his second cup of wine. “The Margrave may be cruel, and he may be a bastard, but he would never betray the Imperium.”

Hearing that, Lord Jorge responded calmly, “The Imperium is no more. We cannot rely on his—”

“But to turn against us, his allies, and join Navalnia?” the old lord burst out bitterly. “He cannot be that mad. I refuse to believe it.”

The hall remained quiet. They all understood the reason for his outburst. The old lord's son was serving with Sir Harold and had likely been entrusted with an important task, perhaps even leading one of the reinforcement columns they now knew had been destroyed in the ambush.

Edessa had become one vast snare.

Sir Michael could only turn back to the map, his expression hardening as he considered what could still be done with the limited forces presently available. Until now, the Shogunate and the Margrave had depended upon one another to withstand the Navalnian onslaught. With the Margrave now turned against them, the situation was hopeless.

Unable to remain seated, the Lord of Galdia rose and joined Sir Michael at the map.

Lord Jorge remained seated and poured himself another drink. Even his squire-cupbearer did not have the heart to caution him. He, too, had men serving in the Edessan campaign. Nearly everyone in the chamber did. But his fears went beyond those he had sent to war. Should the Shogunate suffer a crushing defeat, the alliance might finally begin to fracture. He understood better than anyone that disunity would doom them all.

Sir Michael showed the old lord where their forces were positioned within Edessa.

Near the center of the province, close to the contested frontier with Navalnia, stood a small ring of fortified cities and towns. Behind them lay Sighisa, where the greater part of their garrison was stationed.

Farther south stood Ostaria, Derenza, and Corna, forming the line that connected the Shogunate’s army to White Lake Province.

Together, the strongholds were manned by nearly ten thousand men, supported by several thousand mounted auxiliaries. Their position had once been likened to a blade driven into the heart of Navalnia’s conquest.

Now, the very ally they had come to save had betrayed them.

Sir Michael and the Lord of Galdia studied the map, considering one plan after another, but each ran into the same uncertainty. They were certain the Navalnians would deliver the decisive blow, but they could not tell whether the Margrave would continue to escalate his attacks.

At last, the old lord muttered, “It’s useless. We don’t know where the Margrave’s forces are.”

“Even if we did, the Margrave is cunning.” Sir Michael stroked his chin. “He will not make his intentions easy to read.”

“Just like last time.” The old lord sighed deeply. “He burned the crops under our noses.”

Their exchange caught Ingrid’s attention. Rising from her seat, she asked, “Pardon my lack of military knowledge, but why do we not know the positions of the Margrave’s forces? That seems like a grave oversight.”

“In hindsight, it was,” Sir Michael answered. “But we don’t usually gather detailed reports on our own allies, especially when we’re helping them fight their war.”

“I’ll contact the Orange Skalds. They may know something we don't,” Ingrid offered before approaching the scribes and signalmen to have a message prepared.

At the long table, Calub finished conferring with several others, then cleared his throat to draw Sir Michael’s attention.

“On behalf of those gathered here, may I ask whether our forces can withdraw safely?” he asked.

“Under normal circumstances, they should be able to withdraw from Sighisa in good order and continue southwest,” Sir Michael answered. “But with the Margrave’s forces behind them, nothing about this is normal.”

“Don’t we have the nomads to help cover the withdrawal?” Cecile asked.

“Navalnia has plenty of irregular horsemen of its own,” Sir Michael replied. “And the Margrave also commands their own tribesmen riders.”

The Lord of Galdia turned sharply. “And where do the loyalties of those tribesmen lie?”

Sir Michael’s single eye widened as he turned toward the old lord. “They hate the Empire,” he said. “Do you think they might change sides?”

“Pray that they will,” was the old lord’s answer.

Sir Michael crossed to the scribes and said, “We need to send a message to Batu Noyan. We’ll need his help to win over the tribes in Edessa.”

One of the two scribes nodded at once, while Ingrid, standing nearby, read over her message to the Orange Skalds in Edessa.

“I’ll help as well,” the Lord of Galdia said. “I know my tribesmen.” He murmured several instructions to his squire, who opened a small writing case and began setting out the parchment, ink, and quill.

As they busied themselves preparing the messages, Lord Jorge continued enjoying his drink.

Lady Astrid turned to Cecile. “Have we heard anything from Lady Audrey?”

Cecile shook her head. “The telegraph remains incomplete along several stretches, and we still lack trained signalmen. For now, we’re relying on hawk messages. It’ll take at least two full days.”

Meanwhile, Calub turned to the young Lady Ella of Dawn, who had yet to speak. “My Lady, have you heard anything from Lord of Dawn concerning this?”

The red-haired heiress of Dawn shook her head. “If he had learned of it, he would have sent word.” Even so, her fingers tightened around the hem of her sleeve. Her grandfather must have heard something by now, and knowing him, he had probably already acted on his own to steady the front.

“I heard he has only two airships left, and only one of them is airworthy,” Lady Astrid said quietly.

“The Bat is more than twice the size of the Pride of Korimor,” Lady Ella replied. “If there is any role it can play, it will do so, even alone.”

The signalman had been waiting near the scribes’ table. He glanced toward the marked candle used to keep the hour, then stepped forward and addressed the hall. “Honored members of the council, I beg your attention. The army is awaiting your answer. If a decision is made now, there is still enough time for the message to travel by telegraph and hawk and reach Sighisa before dawn.”

“We tell Sir Harold to retreat,” the Lord of Galdia said without hesitation.

Sir Michael shook his head. “No. We still have no reliable information. Sir Harold should—”

“There is no way we can win this!” the old lord burst out. “No one could!”

As the debate continued, raised voices sounded beyond the doors. Ingrid, who stood beside the scribes’ table, quickly grew irritated by the disturbance. She still held the unfinished letter to Batu Noyan, though the argument across the hall had drawn most of her attention away from it.

The doors suddenly slammed open, and the commotion from the passage spilled into the hall.

“Close that door!” she shouted. “We’re trying to—” Whatever she saw made her shriek.

A person who should not have been there stood in the doorway, dragging a guard with his right arm locked around the man’s neck while gripping a halberd in his left hand. Traces of blood marked his face.

...

Several people in the hall shot to their feet, while others recoiled in their seats. Gasps rose throughout the hall.

Lord Jorge dropped his goblet. Wine spilled across the table and streamed onto the floor as he stared at the doorway.

“I’m seeing things,” he muttered to his squire, who stood speechless in shock.

“My Lord,” Sir Michael called out, the first among them to find his voice.

The person coughed as he dragged the guard into the hall, one arm locked around the man’s throat as he used his body for a shield. A crowd of guards followed cautiously behind, but several people in the hall motioned for them to stand down.

As the person drew closer, those nearest to him could see the dried blood around his mouth and nose, staining his thin black beard and smearing the loose ringmail he had thrown on over his clothes. He held a halberd firmly in his left hand.

Meanwhile, his sharp gaze swept across the hall, searching every face, every empty corner, and even the darkened beams overhead.

“See...” the guard rasped through the arm pressed against his throat. “There are no liches or undead here...”

“Interesting,” the person muttered. Without warning, he released the guard, though not carelessly. He caught the man by the shoulder and steadied him before letting go completely.

“My Lord,” Ingrid called, hurrying toward him.

Instead of replying, the person leveled his halberd at her, shocking the entire hall. “Stop there, woman.”

“But, My Lord...” Ingrid stopped where she stood, confusion and hurt plain on her face.

Ignoring every eye upon him, the person walked toward Lord Jorge’s table.

“Lansius!” Lord Jorge merrily called from his seat. “You’re back!”

The person ignored him, perhaps dismissing him as drunk. Keeping one hand on the halberd, he righted the fallen goblet upon the table, then motioned for the squire to hand him the wineskin. A wet cough seized him as he poured, but he held the goblet steady. The hall watched in tense silence as he filled the goblet and drained it in one swallow.

“Ahh, that is good wine,” he said, turning to Lord Jorge.

Lord Jorge snorted. “Of course! The finest from Three Hills!”

Seeing the person's mood improve, the Lord of Galdia tried his luck. “Lord Shogun, it’s me. Don’t you recognize me?”

The person merely frowned and remained silent, prompting the old lord to exchange uneasy glances with the others in the hall.

Sir Michael stepped forward. “My Lord, it’s Michael, your knight from White Lake.” He hastily added, “We’re inside Korelia’s Great Keep.”

The person studied the one-eyed man for a long moment before replying, “I don’t remember knowing a pirate.”

His answer left everyone confused.

“My Lord,” Ingrid pleaded again, almost to the point of tears. The last two years had been brutal for her, with the burden of managing the House in his absence. The two scribes had now joined her, hope and pain plain on their faces.

“Quiet. I don’t remember being your lord,” the person snapped, only for the words to break into a fit of coughing. Even then, he forced himself to continue. “I don’t remember this place. I care only about the liches and the undead. Where are you hiding them?”

The hall erupted into uneasy murmurs.

Unconcerned by the confusion around him, the person cleared his throat and poured himself another drink. Across the hall, the old lord and several others also reached for their goblets and drank deeply. The shock had left them suddenly in need of a strong drink.

The person saw a tanned man approaching, with a blond woman close behind him.

“My Lord,” the man called, concern heavy in his voice.

The person merely let out a weary sigh, refusing even to turn toward him.

But the blond woman advanced boldly and curtsied. “My Lord, don’t you remember me? I’m Cecile, your chamberlain and cup-bearer.”

“Cup-bearer? I don’t have—” The person stopped. The hall fell silent.

He frowned and studied Cecile more closely, his gaze lingering on her face as though searching for something he could not yet name. Only after a long moment did he turn to the man beside her. Something long buried seemed to stir within him. His eyes then moved slowly across the hall, resting upon each familiar face with a different intensity.

His eyes lingered with curiosity on Lady Ella’s fiery red hair, then shifted to Lady Astrid. When they returned to Lady Ingrid, she had already begun to weep.

The person said nothing, but it was clear that recognition was returning.

He raised the wineskin and drank from it directly. Only after lowering it did he pull out the chair beside Lord Jorge, who still stared at him in disbelief, and sit down.

On a whim, he turned to the map and studied the forces marked across it in silence. His expression gave nothing away. When he had seen enough, he raised his gaze to Calub and Cecile.

“What year is this?” His voice was rough, yet the old authority had begun to return. His gaze moved between them. “And where is... Audrey?”

East Nicopola, Skodra

It was morning in Skodra. For the third time during the journey, Sagarius ate pasta, this time in a small parlor near the inn. Like the inn, this place had been recommended by the clerk they had met the day before. Behind a broad wooden counter, three muscular men prepared the dish, mixing flour and eggs into dough and kneading it with such practiced ease that the work seemed almost an art.

One of them rolled the dough flat and cut it into long, narrow strands before lowering them into a pot of boiling water. Another warmed a little cream with butter, then added grated cheese and stirred until it melted into a smooth sauce.

Once the pasta was ready, it was drained and tossed through the sauce until every strand was lightly coated.

This time, there were no sour cherries or rich meat sauce, yet neither seemed necessary. A slice of freshly baked bread completed the dish.

Served in a simple wooden bowl, the warm meal carried the rich scent of melted cheese. Sagarius took her first bite slowly. Beside her, however, Marc was already devouring his portion as though his life depended upon it. The sight was so excessive that Sagarius sometimes wondered whether he was still in the midst of growing.

“Husband, it is tasty, is it not?” she asked.

Marc nodded with his mouth full, his cheeks bulging like those of a squirrel hoarding nuts.

“You know, even in the cave, your brother did not eat like that,” she said softly as she continued her own meal with practiced grace.

Only then did Marc begin to slow down. He still carried that peculiar pride in being better behaved than his foreign-born brother.

Sagarius had once been a noblewoman, and she knew how to use a few carefully chosen words to correct someone without openly rebuking him. Now that Marc was posing as her husband, she could not allow him to behave like an untrained peasant. Until recently, they had traveled only in the company of armed men. But from this point onward, they might find themselves sharing a table with merchants, officials, or even nobles.

It seemed she would have to teach him some table manners.

Aside from her primary goal of finding Lans, Sagarius still intended to meet the Lord of Korelia. For all she knew, the two men might even be related. If her memory served, both had black hair, though one had appeared older than the other.

Unfortunately, no one seemed to know the lord’s name. Bald Eagle did, but sending Marik all the way back to Vindebona to ask him would be impractical. She would simply have to find someone else who knew. As they drew closer to the lord’s territories, surely his name would become common knowledge.

She had to admit that the airship had greatly increased her interest in him. Even without it, however, the stories of his victories she had heard during the journey had already captured her interest. In only a few years, this mysterious lord had risen from a minor warlord to become one of the most powerful rulers.

The tales of his lightning-fast campaigns, the vast territories he had united, and the great alliances he had forged made the sheer scale of his accomplishments difficult to believe. There were also stories of the founding of the Shogunate and his unbelievable victories against the undead. Most impressive of all, he had helped thwart the Navalnian Empire’s designs against the former Imperium.

Even if only half of those stories were true, this person was far more capable than even Lord Bengrieve. Yet conquest was not his only talent. He was also said to have created many remarkable things, including the pasta she was currently enjoying.

Sagarius finished her meal with her usual grace and politely declined a second serving, despite wanting another.

After paying, they returned to their inn, one of the finest available in Skodra.

The clerk from the day before was already waiting for them in the common room and waved when he saw them enter. A younger man, perhaps his assistant or apprentice, had accompanied him but remained seated at a separate table. Sagarius and Marc joined the clerk and ordered him a pale ale, as courtesy demanded.

“So, how was the pasta?” the clerk asked.

“It was a wonderful meal,” Marc replied cheerfully.

Sagarius dipped her head. “Gratitude for the recommendation. We found it most enjoyable.”

The clerk returned the gesture. “It was a small favor. Think nothing of it.”

They then resumed the discussion from the day before, which had been cut short because the clerk had errands of his own.

“As I was saying yesterday, Skodra only recently completed an airship shelter, and they have already begun taking passengers. But nearly every route goes first to Korelia rather than directly into Midlandia.”

“And you said you could arrange for us to travel aboard one?” Sagarius asked.

“Yes. We seldom have clients of the Bank who can afford the fare, so we are always on the lookout for those who can.”

Sagarius nodded, though she still did not fully understand what the Bank was. From what she had gathered, it was some manner of moneylenders’ guild, only far more sophisticated.

Finding that Sagarius had nothing more to ask, Marc filled the silence. “Meister, who built the airships?”

Sagarius quickly added, “Has the Shogunate established contact with the Elves?”

“Elves?” The meister looked surprised, then chuckled. “Lady, you have quite the imagination. The Shogunate built them. As far as I know, no Elves were involved.”

Marc nodded, while Sagarius murmured, “So the Shogunate is that advanced...”

A serving maid arrived with three goblets and a jug of pale ale. The clerk and Marc each took a drink, while Sagarius merely sipped hers. It was pleasantly rich. With the next harvest still a season away, such good ale suggested that the region had suffered no shortage.

Sagarius then changed the subject. “Still, if the fare proves too costly, or we cannot secure a place aboard for some other reason, what is the best route into Midlandia? You mentioned the Gallery Road.”

“I doubt the Bank would turn away a new client, but yes, the Gallery Road is the quickest route,” the clerk confirmed.

At that, Marc asked, “Is it safe?”

“Of course.” The clerk looked around, then pointed through the inn’s window toward the city center, where blue and bronze banners flew above the crossroads. “As long as you see the lord’s banners, the road is safe. You will find no bandits there. They are all terrified of him. He and his bannerets have even slain the undead.”

The clerk chuckled at his own boast, while Marc gave an amused snort.

Sagarius, however, found herself growing more interested in this lord. “This place seems peaceful, yet I have heard of wars against the Mountain Men.”

“There is still fighting, but on a much smaller scale now,” the clerk replied. “The Mountain Clans no longer have the strength to trouble anyone beyond their territory. Things are far better than they were. Praise Sir Servius, his legion, and the Lord Shogun.”

Seeing an opportunity, Sagarius asked, “Meister, do you happen to know the lord’s name?”

“The Lord Shogun?” The clerk paused to think. “Not that I can recall. Most people simply call him the Lord Shogun or the Lord of Korelia.” His voice dropped to a whisper. “Some did call him the Black Lord.”

“The Black Lord?” Sagarius echoed, matching his lowered voice.

“Not so loudly,” the clerk cautioned. “Only his enemies call him that.”

Marc frowned. “But why?”

“No one knows." He shrugged. "Perhaps because he is a foreigner.”

“A foreigner?” Marc blurted out.

“Yes. Some say he is not truly of the Imperium, but came from the Mercantile Kingdom. Knights from there have served as mercenaries in the past. He may be descended from one of them.”

Sagarius and Marc exchanged glances. She had never told him that the Lord of Korelia might have black hair, yet the mention of a foreigner had clearly stirred the same suspicion in him.

She quietly tried to recall Lans as she had known him. He had never carried himself like a lord. A mercenary, perhaps, but never a great ruler. Even so, there were too many coincidences to ignore.

As expected, Marc ventured, “Does the lord happen to have black hair?”

The clerk frowned, seemingly uncomfortable with the question. “That, I do not know. I work for the Bank, but I have never been summoned to Korelia.”

He studied them for a moment. “Are you hoping to see the Lord Shogun?”

Marc hesitated, while Sagarius replied, “I assume he is in Korelia?”

“Indeed. And as I said, nearly every road leads to the great city.” The clerk shifted uneasily. “Though I suppose you may not have heard the latest news, since you only just learned about airships.”

Sagarius kept her voice soft. “What news, Meister?”

“Well...” He paused. “The lord is unfortunately ill. He has been for over a year.”

“Sick?” Sagarius’ eyes widened before she quickly composed herself. “What manner of—”

“It’s the undead,” the clerk answered before she could finish, shaking his head grimly. “The lord personally dueled the lich and slew it, but not before the creature laid a curse upon him.”

“A lich?” Sagarius repeated.

“Yes, he—” The clerk stopped as Sagarius abruptly rose from her seat. He stood as well, though he clearly did not understand why.

“My apologies. I need some air.” She grasped for the first excuse that came to mind. “What you said of the creature... it frightens me a little.”

Before either man could say anything, she hurried away from the table. Marc followed after hastily excusing them to the clerk.

It was not the lich that frightened her.

How could I have forgotten?

Lans had always spoken of fighting liches and the undead.

She crossed the common room and stepped out of the inn onto the main street. The summer sun immediately beat down upon her hair and face. Carts and carriages crept through the dusty streets of Skodra, their wheels grinding over the road and throwing grit into the air. From the entrance of a nearby market, peddlers called out to passing crowds, while sweat gathered beneath her clothes.

Yet through the heat, the dust, and the noise, a smile slowly spread across Sagarius’ lips.

At last, she knew who the Lord Shogun was.
