ARC III: THE WORLD REMEMBERS

SYNOPSIS: Day 16. Week three begins. The wildcard round is announced. Caelric sends a proposal. It is very polite. That is the problem.

Pure voting. Three days.

Livia read it twice. Then she looked at the voting portal numbers: Yuki at 198,000, her at 152,000, and a gap of 46,000 that had been roughly the same for a week.

She has 198,000 people who will vote because she asks them to.

I have 152,000 people who vote because they want to.

In a pure voting round, the difference between those two things matters more than it does in a scored round.

She’s going to widen the gap.

She put the leaderboard down. Three days of pure voting was three days. She had sessions to run.

The wildcard announcement broke across the server at 08:00 and the external forums responded the way forums responded to things that changed the calculation: immediately, loudly, and in several contradictory directions simultaneously.

Livia read the Blorbo update.

Then she read it again.

“Alaric,” she said.

Alaric appeared from the direction of his room with his tablet already open. He had been expecting this. “Tab Twenty-Five,” he said. “Blorbo analysis. Seventeen days of data. I have a theory.”

“What’s the theory?”

“Blorbo is a collective protest vote,” Alaric said. “Players who don’t want to vote for Yuki or Livia but want to participate. Blorbo is the option that represents opting out while still opting in. It’s…” he checked his tab, “…structurally fascinating.”

“Can Blorbo actually win?”

Alaric was quiet for a moment. “Tab Twenty-Five, page four,” he said. “If current trajectory holds and both Yuki and Livia’s voting bases split their votes between the two of them…”

“Alaric.”

“Mathematically yes,” he said. “Blorbo could win the wildcard round.”

A silence.

“What happens if a placeholder texture wins a server-wide event?” Livia asked.

Alaric looked at his tablet. “Tab Twenty-Five doesn’t cover that,” he said. “I’m making Tab Twenty-Six.”

Yuki went live at 09:00.

Not the usual stream setup — no game footage, no overlay, just her face and the specific focused energy of someone who had been planning this since the wildcard announcement dropped. Her chat was already at capacity.

“Okay, chat,” she said, and her voice had the particular register she used when she was serious. Not loud. Direct. “Three days. Pure voting. You know I don’t usually ask for things. I’m asking now. Not because I need to beat an NPC —” she paused, something shifting in her expression, “— actually, that’s not the reason anymore. The reason is I want to finish what I started. Eight years of competitive play and I want to see this one through.”

Chat:

“LET’S GO” “we got you yuki” “VOTING NOW” “wait she said not because she needs to beat an npc” “DID SHE JUST” “she’s been different since the taming grounds” “STILL VOTING THO”

Yuki watched the chat run. Something at the corner of her mouth.

“Yeah,” she said. “I’ve been different. That’s fine. I’m still going to win. Both things are true.”

Chat erupted. The vote counter moved.

Alaric, watching the external feed from Tab Twenty-Two: “She gained four thousand votes in the first minute.” He paused. “She also gained twelve thousand new followers.” Another pause. “From people who liked that she said she’s been different.” He looked up. “The window.”

Livia looked at him.

“From your session,” Alaric said. “She told 340,000 people she’s been building a window. They’re voting for the window.” He went back to his tablet. “This is going in Tab Twenty-Six.”

The morning was full. Three sessions, two external, one walk-in from a Crestfall Keep member who had somehow found the side entrance and stood in the corridor looking lost until Nyx materialized behind him and pointed him toward the planning room without speaking.

The Crestfall member had said: “I’ve been raiding the same content for eighteen months and I don’t feel anything when I clear it anymore.”

Structural Read had shown Livia the architecture immediately: someone who had confused progression with purpose and was now at the top of a ladder that didn’t lean against anything they actually wanted.

The session had run forty minutes. The Crestfall member had left looking like someone who had been given a question rather than an answer, which was exactly right. Questions were the beginning of the work. Answers came later, from the patient, at the right time.

At noon, Livia was in the guild garden with her jellyfish.

The jellyfish drifted at approximately shoulder height, which had become its preferred altitude when they were not traveling. It had settled into the rhythm of the guild hall quickly — it seemed to like the garden specifically, the way it liked open spaces where the ambient emotional temperature was varied and interesting. The lightning in its cloud-body moved in the slow, contented arcs of something that was where it wanted to be.

Bron arrived and sat on the bench near the eastern flower beds. Boris settled beside him with the patient stillness of a mount that had decided its rider’s rest was also its rest.

Bron looked at the jellyfish for a while.

“You haven’t named it,” he said.

“No,” Livia said.

“I named Boris on the first day.”

“I know.”

Boris made the fortification-settling sound. Bron patted him.

“Why not?” Bron asked. Not pushing. Genuinely curious in the way Bron was genuinely curious about things — direct, uncomplicated, with no agenda beyond the question itself.

Livia looked at the jellyfish. The lightning moved. Thunder, quiet, in the register she felt more than heard.

“I think,” she said slowly, “I keep waiting until I’m sure I’m staying.”

Bron was quiet for a moment.

“Are you staying?” he asked.

“I don’t know how to leave,” she said. “I’ve never known how to leave. But that’s not the same as deciding to stay.”

Bron thought about this with the focused seriousness he brought to things that mattered.

“I stayed because I ran out of reasons to go,” he said. “Then I stayed because I had reasons to be here. They’re different.” He looked at the jellyfish. “Maybe you give it a name when you figure out which one you’re doing.”

I stayed because I ran out of reasons to go.

Then I stayed because I had reasons to be here.

Bron. Who I have had forty-three sessions with. Who I know carries something small in his pocket that I have never asked about.

He just described my situation more precisely than I have.

The jellyfish drifted closer. Its lightning moved once, warm.

“Bron,” she said.

“Yeah.”

“The small thing you carry. In your right pocket. I saw it in Morty’s footage from two years ago.”

Bron went still. Not alarmed — the stillness of someone who had been carrying something private for a long time and had just found out someone else knew.

Then, slowly, he reached into his right pocket and held out his hand.

It was a small carved token. Old, worn smooth at the edges from years of handling. A simple shape — a shield, miniature, the kind that was not armor but symbol. It had a crack across the center that had been repaired, not perfectly, with what looked like gold resin. The crack was visible. It had not been hidden.

“My first squad,” Bron said. “Before immortality. Before any of this. We all had one. Matching.” He looked at the token. “They’re gone now. Server wipe, years ago. The tokens didn’t wipe because they were in my inventory and I was logged out.”

He closed his hand around it.

“I carry it because they can’t,” he said. Simply. The way Bron said things that were simple and large at the same time.

I carry it because they can’t.

Forty-three sessions.

He never said this. I never asked.

I’m asking now.

This is what Structural Read does that listening alone couldn’t.

Not the architecture. The thing inside it that the architecture was built to hold.

Livia did not write in her notepad. She sat with him in the garden for a while, the jellyfish drifting overhead, Boris patient beside them, the afternoon light moving through the guild garden at the angle it moved through things that had been in the same place long enough to know the light.

She saw them from the garden at 14:22.

Not close enough to hear. Just close enough to see.

Alondra was in the east corridor, walking toward the main hall. Miles was coming from the other direction. They met at the midpoint — the same place Livia had seen them meet before, three weeks ago, when she had watched from the planning room doorway and caught thirty-eight seconds of something she could not fully read.

This time she was further away. She could see their postures but not their faces.

Alondra stopped. Miles stopped.

They stood for a moment. Not long — six seconds, maybe eight. Long enough for something to pass between them that was not a conversation.

Then Alondra nodded. Once. Small.

And kept walking.

Miles watched her go for three seconds. Then turned back the way he had come.

Not today.

She said not today.

But something just shifted. That nod was not the same nod as three weeks ago.

Three weeks ago she was managing the encounter. Today she was… deciding something. In real time. In a corridor.

Soon.

It’s soon.

The proposal arrived at 16:00.

Via the official guild communication portal. Sealed with Eclipse Covenant’s double-crescent crest. Addressed to: Guild Master M. Vexley, Bloodbath and Beyond. Cc: Livia Marcelline Quinn, Mental Architect.

She was copied.

She read it standing in the strategy room with Miles, who had already read it twice.

The proposal was four pages. It was impeccably formatted. Every paragraph was precisely the right length. The font was correct. The margins were correct. The language was the language of someone who had written many formal documents and understood that tone was itself an argument.

It read, in relevant part:

Eclipse Covenant wishes to formally propose a Collaborative Therapeutic Arrangement for the remainder of the Aethelgard Resonance Championship event period. We request that Mental Architect Livia Marcelline Quinn provide no fewer than three (3) consultation sessions per week to Eclipse Covenant guild members, on a schedule mutually agreed upon by both parties.

In recognition of Ms. Quinn’s time and expertise, Eclipse Covenant proposes compensation of 3,000 gold per session — a rate which we believe reflects the genuine value of her work and the server’s growing recognition of Mental Architect as a classification without precedent.

We wish to note that Ms. Quinn’s services have previously been made available to Eclipse Covenant members (ref: Arc II consultation, Healer Mira, resolved successfully). We further wish to note that the Resonance Championship’s stated purpose is server-wide emotional wellbeing and community flourishing. We believe all guild members, regardless of competitive standing, deserve access to qualified therapeutic support during a high-stress event period.

Should Bloodbath and Beyond decline this proposal, Eclipse Covenant reserves the right to note, in the public event record, that B&B has chosen to limit access to server-wide therapeutic resources for competitive advantage. We do not anticipate this will be necessary. We are confident that B&B shares our commitment to the server community’s wellbeing.

We look forward to your response at your earliest convenience. Warm regards, Caelric, Guild Master, Eclipse Covenant.

Warm regards.

Livia put the proposal down.

“Warm regards,” she said.

“Yes,” Miles said.

“He wrote warm regards.”

“He did.”

She looked at the proposal. At the four pages of impeccable formatting. At the paragraph that said: should B&B decline, we reserve the right to note that they’ve chosen to limit access to therapeutic resources for competitive advantage.

He’s not threatening us.

He’s threatening the framing.

If B&B declines, the story becomes: B&B is hoarding their therapist as a competitive weapon. And in an event that’s explicitly about server-wide emotional wellbeing, that story is… damaging.

He knows that.

He wrote warm regards.

“He’s very good at this,” Livia said.

“Yes,” Miles said. His voice was the flat one — the one that meant he was managing something carefully.

“He’s not wrong that I consulted for them before,” she said.

“No.”

“And he’s not wrong that the event is supposed to be about server-wide wellbeing.”

“No.”

“He’s taken two true things and built a cage out of them.”

Miles looked at her. “Yes.”

Livia picked up the proposal again. Read it a third time. The language was perfect. Every sentence technically accurate. Every implication precisely placed.

He doesn’t lie.

He’s very careful about what he says and what he allows you to conclude.

I know someone who said exactly that about him once.

Alondra.

In session three.

And she said: that’s not lying, exactly.

No. It’s not.

It’s something more structured than lying.

Something that takes more skill and leaves fewer traces.

“What do you want to do?” Miles asked. Not telling her. Asking.

She looked at him. At the Emberwing on his shoulder, still and attentive. At the strategy room with its display dark and the proposal on the table between them.

“I want to respond,” she said. “But not yet. I need to think about what kind of response doesn’t let him define the frame.”

Miles nodded.

“Take your time,” he said. Then: “Within reason.”

“Within reason,” she agreed.

She picked up the proposal and folded it precisely along its original creases.

She set it on the corner of the table.

She looked at it for a moment.

He wrote warm regards.

He is going to regret warm regards.

I don’t know how yet.

But I am a Mental Architect and I can read the structure of what he built here.

And every structure has a load-bearing wall.

And every load-bearing wall has a point where the weight exceeds the design.

I just have to find it.
