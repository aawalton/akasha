A loud metallic bang seemed to reverberate through the car as if something slammed into it, and I jumped. My head snapped up, and my heart pounded as I looked away from my phone to scan my surroundings, expecting to see someone holding a gun or a sledgehammer or something nearby. Instead, my eyes found my mom. She stood beside the car, a petrol pump clasped delicately in her gloved hand.

I pressed my lips together as a relieved, almost hysterical laugh threatened to escape. It was just the door to the petrol tank, I realised as I watched my mom insert the nozzle and begin to fill up the car. It had been broken for ages, and Mom always blames Dad for the fact that to get the door to open, she basically has to punch it. I’m almost certain it’s her fault, though. She’s always slammed the cover shut so hard, even before it was broken, and that sounds so loud in the car.

Resting one hand on my chest, I took a deep breath to calm my racing heart as I flipped the sun visor down to watch Mom in the mirror. It barely helped, though. I could still feel it fluttering against my ribs, and I forced myself to inhale slowly again as I tried to convince myself that everything was fine. Nothing bad was happening. We’d just stopped to get petrol, and that was fine. It was normal.

My eyes shut as I leant my head back against the headrest and took another deep breath. Dizziness began to reach out to me, and a smile pulled at my face as I allowed it to draw me in before something stopped me. A thought occurred to me suddenly, and my eyes snapped open again.

Why was I so scared? If everything actually was fine, why was I so terrified that I’d dragged myself out of the other world? That only happened when something bad was happening or about to happen, and I was having a nice time there. Sure, I was kind of nervous, but I was enjoying being around Seth. Whenever my thoughts stopped racing for a few minutes, it was pretty peaceful.

I wanted to go back, but I couldn’t. The nagging feeling that I was missing something obvious pulled at me, and I couldn’t ignore it. There had to be something there, some kind of threat or danger.

Letting out a slow breath, I reluctantly looked around, trying to figure out what was out of place. It all looked perfectly normal, though. The petrol station we’d stopped at was a small one. There were only four pumps, and someone else was using one of the other ones, but they weren’t paying any attention to me. Their eyes were fixed on their phone as they filled up their car.

My gaze remained on them for a moment later, waiting for their eyes to dart towards me or Mom, but they didn’t. It seemed as though they were actually using their phone rather than just pretending to, so I doubted they were the reason I was so scared. There had to be something else.

I glanced at my mom again before continuing to search. There was no one else around, really. I could see someone inside the station. The cashier sat behind the counter, staring down, and I assumed she was probably on her phone too. There was no one else inside, though. It must have been something or someone outside the station.

Swallowing down the anxiety that was steadily rising within me, I glanced around at the forecourt, taking it in. The petrol station was set back away from the road, but I could just about see it in the distance on one side. On the other was a thick forest. The trees were wild. They seemed to be trying to reclaim the land that was stolen from them when the petrol station was built. Vines and roots spilt out over the concrete, slowly clawing it back.

But that wasn’t all, I realised as I leant to the side to see more. They’d begun to snake around one of the pillars that held up the canopy. The sight of the thick, rope-like vines wrapped tightly around it sent panic through me.

Was that it? Had I found the reason that I was so scared? The pillar could be able to give way at any moment, and when it did, it would crush me. It would crush us both. One of my hands twitched towards my seat belt, preparing to undo it in case I needed to run to safety.

Mom would never follow me, though. She was too stubborn, and if I told her the pillar was about to collapse, she probably wouldn’t believe me. I wouldn’t have time to explain. If I saw it start to go, I’d need to drag her out of the way, and that would infuriate her. She’d probably forgive me if I did manage to save her life, though.

But what about the others? The guy filling up his car and the woman inside? Was there any way I could save them too, or would I just have to live with the knowledge that I’d not done enough? The woman might survive. The canopy didn’t cover the whole building, so maybe that would protect her, but the man was out in the open. He’d be crushed.

I glanced at the guy again, watching him for a few seconds. He still didn’t look up. He seemed utterly absorbed by his phone, and I had no idea what he was doing on it, but that worried me. I knew I wouldn’t be able to grab him as well as my mom, so I’d just need to shout, and that might not work. He might not pay any attention, and even if he did, I wasn’t sure whether he’d move quickly enough to get out in time.

My heart sank as my eyes found his car. There was a car seat in the back. I couldn’t see inside from where I was sitting, so there was no way to know whether there was a baby in there, but there was no way he’d just leave them if I told him to run. He’d want to save the kid, and that might kill them both. Maybe he’d be able to get into his car again, but I doubted it was strong enough to withstand the metal canopy falling on it without crumpling.

I looked back at the pillar, trying to work out what to do. It didn’t look too bad, I told myself. The more I stared at it, the better it looked. I was pretty sure my mind was just playing tricks on me. It jumped to the worst-case scenario when, actually, the pillar was pretty much fine. Obviously, there were vines wrapped around it, but it didn’t seem damaged.

Surely, if it was, someone would have noticed before then, wouldn’t they? Like the person who worked inside. She probably visited the petrol station most days, so I had to assume she would have noticed if it was starting to get worse or someone would have mentioned it to her.

That made me feel a little bit better. My eyes still flicked towards the pillar a few times as I waited for my mom to finish filling the car, but my hand left my seat belt and returned to gripping my phone.

Exhaustion washed over me as Mom finally pulled the nozzle back. For some reason, the anxiety had really drained me. I felt as though I’d been sprinting through the forest behind my grandparents’ house for hours. My body was weak, and my eyelids were heavy. I had to fight to keep them open, but it was hard. I longed to let the sleepiness wash over me and wrap me in its comforting embrace.

Another bang rocked the car, chasing away some of my tiredness, and my eyes snapped open again. It was slightly less jarring that time. I knew it was coming, and I’d been expecting it more, so I barely even jumped as my mom slammed the petrol cover back into place.

I glanced at her, watching as she ripped her gloves off and shoved them into a nearby bin before turning back towards the car. Faint anxiety pulled at me, and my eyes darted towards the mirror. My eyes were a little too wide, I realised. And my cheeks were flushed. I still looked kind of scared, but I doubted my mom would notice. She probably wouldn’t even look at me, so I was fine, I told myself.

And I was right. My mom pulled the back door of the car open and grabbed her handbag. I was silent as she rooted around in it for a few seconds before pulling out her cigarettes and straightening up. She turned, shutting the door again without saying a word and marching across the forecourt.

Unease started to bubble in the pit of my stomach again as I watched my mom move towards the trees at the far side. It was probably the safest place for her to have a cigarette, and I knew that. Petrol was flammable, so it would be stupid for her to stand by the car and smoke, but it still made me worried for some reason.

What if she didn’t go far enough, I couldn’t help but ask myself. What if she thought she was out of range of the fumes, but she wasn’t? Would the whole place explode, or would it be a slower burn? Perhaps it would be gradual. The fumes might spark up, or maybe one pump would light. It would be like a fuse, slowly making its way towards the petrol tank before exploding.

How quick would my death be? The question floated through my mind, causing sweat to bead on my palms. If the petrol station ignited, how long would it take me to die? I had no idea. I’d never set petrol on fire or seen it happen. Not in that world, anyway. In others, I had.

A shudder tore through me as phantom flames melted the flesh from my body and licked at my bones. A scream seemed to echo in my ears, and the undercurrent of monotonous chanting made my skin crawl.

I wasn’t there, I told myself, my grip tightening around my phone until the edge of the case bit into my hands. I wasn’t in whatever world that was. No one was setting me on fire, and everything would be okay. Nothing would happen. My mom would just smoke her cigarette and then return, and we could leave.

Still, I couldn’t stop myself from tensing as I watched my mom lift her lighter. The flare of light that leapt out of it sent fear through my heart, and I waited for death to claim me, but nothing happened. The petrol station did not ignite.

A sharp exhale escaped my lips, and my head dropped back against the headrest again.

“Everything’s okay,” I whispered to myself.

I’d hoped that hearing it out loud might make me feel better, but it didn’t. If anything, it just made me feel more anxious, and I glanced around, checking to make sure no one had heard me.

They couldn’t have, though. The other guy who’d been filling his car was inside the station, chatting to the cashier. Even if he were still outside, it was pretty unlikely he would have heard me, but that didn’t make me feel any better. The creeping sensation that something or someone was listening in, that they were watching me, continued to pull at me, and I glanced around.

There was no one else, though. The forecourt was empty, and the people inside the petrol station weren’t even looking in my direction. The road was too far away for anyone to be watching me from there, and the cars on it were moving too fast anyway. The only other place that someone could have been watching me from was…

My eyes found the forest again, and my eyebrows pulled together. Something about it was… familiar. It was just some trees, I told myself. They all looked the same, and there was no way I recognised those exact trees, but a voice in the back of my head whispered that I’d been there before.

Ice slipped down my spine as a flashback forced its way into my head. I couldn’t help but flinch as the memory of the forest I’d had to run into to escape the people who emerged from the service station pulled me under. My heart began to race as I glanced at the petrol station.

Would it happen again? Would the people dressed in that all-black uniform flood out of the doors, forcing me to flee into the trees? They’d hunt me down. Their laughter and crowing shouts would bounce around the forest, attacking me from all angles as I tried desperately to get away from them again and again.

There was no use. They’d catch up with me, just like they always did. Either I’d reach the boundary, and somehow, the world and my memories would reset, or they’d capture me. My death would be slow. They’d drag it out, torturing me until my heart finally gave out, and then it would start again. The cycle would continue over and over forever. There was no escape.

But I wasn’t there, I told myself, gritting my teeth. I was at a tiny petrol station in the middle of nowhere, not that giant one that I always woke up at. And I wasn’t even in that world. I was in reality. No one wanted to hunt me there.

Still, I found myself reaching out towards the dizzinesses that waited at the back of my mind, checking to make sure I couldn’t feel the familiar touch of my world. It wasn’t there. I was in my world, and nothing would hurt me.

My eyes flicked towards the mirror, checking on my mom. She still stood at the edge of the forest, a cigarette in one hand and her phone in the other. She seemed entirely calm and unaware of the anxiety that thrashed inside me, looking for anything to focus on.

The trees.

Right behind my mom, the trees moved. My hands shot out. One closed around the door handle, and the other landed on the seat belt release again, but I didn’t look away from the forest.

Something was in there. The way the underbrush was moving, the way it shook, wasn’t right. It wasn’t normal. I wanted to believe I was just being paranoid, that my anxiety was trying to trick me, and it was nothing more than the wind, but I couldn’t.

Only one part of the trees were moving. If it was the wind that was causing it, the entire section would have been swaying as the gust hit it, but most of it was motionless. That wasn’t right, and I was certain I’d seen that before.

Images flashed behind my eyes, and pain gripped my heart as I tried to push them away. I didn’t want to see my mom being ripped apart by some giant beast, but they refused to leave my mind. They were too vivid, too realistic, and that made me almost certain that they’d happened before.

In some other world, I must have watched as a bear’s jaw closed around her arm. My mom tried to fight it off. Her fist hammered against the bear’s face, but nothing happened. It didn’t relinquish its hold. Instead, it just pulled back, tearing her arm free from the socket with a spurt of blood.

I shook my head, trying to force the image aside, but it was just replaced with another. A hell beast, some monstrous mix of a wolf and a moose but with horns longer than my forearm, charged at my mom. She looked down, confusion appearing on her face as she caught sight of the tip protruding from her chest. Blood dribbled from her lips, and she met my gaze again, mouthing something at me.

Help.

We don’t have bears in England or Scotland, I told myself, and the other creature didn’t even exist. It wasn’t real. I thought I’d seen some kind of bear or wolf thing earlier, but I hadn’t. It was just someone’s dog. That was the most likely answer. If anything, I’d just seen a dog.

Or maybe it was a fox, I realised. Wild foxes existed in this country. I’d seen them from time to time, but they were small. If there was one in the trees behind my mom and it tried to attack her, I could probably take it. No. I definitely could.

I nodded to myself before glancing around again, hoping that no one had spotted me. It was a weird thing to do, and they’d probably wonder why I’d done it, but it didn’t matter. The guy probably wouldn’t knock on the window or open the door and ask me, and the cashier was too far away. They probably didn’t even care too.

My eyes flicked towards my mom, checking to make sure she wasn’t looking at me, but she was still distracted by her phone. A relieved sigh slipped from my lips as she took one more puff of her cigarette before dropping it onto the ground. She crushed it beneath her heel before starting to walk away, her gaze still on her phone.

I couldn’t let my guard down yet, though. She was moving away from the trees, moving away from the dangers that could be lurking in there, but she still wasn’t safe. My gaze darted between her and the forest again as the trees rustled violently, and my grip on the door handle tightened.

“Everything’s fine,” I whispered to myself again, but it came out more like a whimper.

My mom passed the car without looking in my direction, and I only glanced at her for a moment before looking back at the trees. I was terrified that if I let my attention wander, if I looked away for more than a second, something would happen. I knew it was impossible, but I was convinced some kind of creature would appear or a person holding a gun would. That had happened before. I’d watched as someone shot my mom.

The door swung open suddenly, and I jumped, ripping my hand away from the handle. I didn’t want her to see that I was prepared to throw myself out of the car if I had to. She’d think I’d lost my mind or something.

“Take these,” Mom ordered, thrusting two bottles of water towards me as she leant into the car.

I hurried to grab them from her, my heart still pounding in my ears. My gaze darted towards the mirror, checking on the forest before looking back at my mom as she grabbed the empty coffee cups from the console.

A groan almost escaped my lips as she straightened and strolled towards the nearest bin, dropping them in there before returning. Still, she didn’t get in the car, though. She began pulling trash out of the pocket on the door and binning that before leaning into the car again and staring inside without getting in.

I waited anxiously, trying not to let her notice the fact that I was glancing at the trees every couple of seconds. I could feel her eyes on my face, and I turned to look at her quickly. Her expression was demanding, but she hadn’t said anything. I had no clue what she wanted from me.

“What?” I asked, trying to make the word sound as polite as possible.

She let out an irritated sigh.

“Give me the rubbish,” she snapped, pointing towards the bag at my feet.

“Oh, sorry!”

I fumbled as I hurried to put the bottles of water I was still holding in the cup holders before grabbing the empty bag and shoving a couple stray wrappers inside. She sighed again as I held it out to her, making no move to take it.

“And the stuff in the door,” she said after a few seconds of silence.

She shook her head as I grabbed the empty milkshake bottle and a crisp packet, adding it to the bag before passing it to her. As she turned to bin the trash, my eyes returned to the trees.

They’d fallen still. The movement inside had stopped. The creature or person must have gotten bored or decided not to do anything. Or maybe the wind had just died down, I told myself. Perhaps that was all it was the whole time.

I still didn’t really believe it, but I wanted to.

“One of those is for you, by the way,” Mom said as she finally got into the car and flipped the sun visor down.

Confusion washed over me as I looked at her, but she didn’t look at me. Instead, she stared at her reflection, her eyebrows pulling together as a hint of irritation appeared on her face.

“What is?” I asked.

Her eyes flicked towards me, and her expression became even more annoyed.

“The water.”

“Oh,” I said, glancing at it. “Why?”

I hadn’t meant to say that. It just slipped out, and I could tell Mom didn’t like that I was questioning her.

“You haven’t had any all morning. The only thing you’ve had to drink is that disgusting milkshake,” she sneered as she started the car again. “You’re probably dehydrated.”

Her response only made me more confused, though. It wasn’t like her to think about something like that, and I was surprised she’d even noticed. If she had normally, I was pretty sure she would have just made a snide comment about how unhealthy I was being rather than actually getting me another drink or anything that would help.

She was being too thoughtful, and that wasn’t like her. Concern started creeping into my heart again, and I couldn’t help but reach out towards the dizzinesses that hovered at the edge of my awareness. I’d already checked that I was in my reality, but I just needed to be certain.

“Oh, thanks,” I said awkwardly.

My reality wasn’t there. I couldn’t feel it at all, I realised as my eyes found the trees again. That meant Mom had actually thought about me. Warmth flared in my chest, and I bit my lip as we started to pull away from the petrol station.

“Dehydration causes your skin to age more quickly,” she continued as if I hadn’t spoken. “You need to start drinking more water because, at this rate, you’ll be saggy and covered in wrinkles by the time you’re twenty.”

I had to look down to hide the smile that appeared on my lips. That sounded more like my mom.
