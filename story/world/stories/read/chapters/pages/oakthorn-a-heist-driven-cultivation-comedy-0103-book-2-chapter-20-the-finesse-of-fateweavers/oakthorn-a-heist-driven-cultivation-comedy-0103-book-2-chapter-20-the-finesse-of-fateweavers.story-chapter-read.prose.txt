In the grand tradition of crypt exploration gone terribly wrong, Cade Stormhollow found himself pressed against a wall of ancient stone, listening to distant rhythmic pulsing that sounded distressingly like a heartbeat. The fact that said heartbeat belonged to a structure rather than any living creature was, in his expert assessment, not an improvement over standard dungeon fare.

At all.

“So,” he whispered, blue eyes tracking the shadows that seemed too eager to move when no one was looking directly at them. “On a scale of ‘mild inconvenience’ to ‘cosmic catastrophe,’ where would you rate our current situation?”

Nora’s lips twitched—not quite a smile, but the battlefield equivalent for someone whose emotional range typically spanned from ‘stoic’ to ‘slightly less stoic.’ He’d seen her armor crack several times now, and so went out of his way to see more of the gushy paladin she hid behind steel and a really big sword.

“Somewhere between ‘disastrous’ and ‘potentially extinction-level event,’” she replied, her massive blade reflecting the faint bioluminescence that clung to the walls. “Though I’m reserving judgment until something tries to eat us.”

“Always the optimist.”

The heartbeat rhythm continued, neither increasing nor decreasing in tempo, maintaining the precise cadence of something ancient and patient. Very, very patient.

Cade pushed away from the wall, surveying the corridor that stretched before them. Unlike the temple chambers they’d fled, this passage showed different architectural influences—older, more alien. The walls weren’t merely smooth but seemed to flow like liquid caught in the moment of freezing, creating organic curves that defied conventional masonry.

“These weren’t built by human hands,” Nora observed, running her fingers along a section where symbols had been carved directly into the flowing stone. “This is pure Progenitor craftsmanship, so far as I can tell.”

“Ahh, a fellow lover of stone, I see!” Cade furrowed his eyebrows. That came out wrong. “How did you draw that conclusion, Nora?” Cade asked, genuinely curious rather than challenging. “I thought the Fateweavers considered Progenitor knowledge heretical, or some other convenient lie.”

“They do,” she confirmed, a hint of bitterness coloring her words. “Which is why the forbidden texts were the first things I studied once I became sect leader. Fearing the knowledge of a potential enemy is the height of folly, if you ask me.”

Cade’s respect for the ex-paladin notched upward. “Sounds like we have more in common than I thought.”

“Don’t get excited,” she replied dryly. “I still think your moral compass needs recalibration.”

“It points to treasure,” he agreed cheerfully. “Very reliable.”

They moved cautiously deeper into the corridor, the feeling of descending becoming more pronounced with each step. The air grew thicker, carrying scents that defied easy identification—something metallic and sweet simultaneously, like blood mixed with honey.

“How’s your magic holding up?” Nora asked after several minutes of silent progress. “That lava trick earlier wasn’t standard destruction repertoire.”

Cade flexed his fingers, watching as silver energy danced across his knuckles. Something felt different since his advancement to Late stage—the power more responsive, but also somehow wilder, harder to fully contain.

“It’s... evolving. I don’t know of any other way to put how it feels,” he admitted. “Destruction’s always been about breaking things apart, but now it feels like I can reshape rather than just demolish.”

He hesitated. Remembering how secretive Hugh had gotten—and how much that had hurt—he chose to open up just a little about what was truly bothering him.

Before he could regret it, he added, “Sometimes there’s something else too. Something red.”

Nora’s steps faltered momentarily. “Red? Not silver-gold?”

“Just flashes,” he clarified. “Like glimpsing something in your peripheral vision that vanishes when you turn to look directly at it. But, for the life of me, I can’t seem to figure out where it’s coming from.”

Her expression grew thoughtful. “The Fateweavers have techniques for examining one’s magical core—meditative practices that let you sort of... map your own power.”

“Sounds delightfully introspective,” Cade replied, though his interest was genuine despite the flippant response. “Is that how you got so precise with your fear magic?”

Nora nodded, surprising him with her openness. She unconsciously rotated her shoulder, stretching out some old wound. “Initially, I was a raw conduit—all power, no control. Left a lot of traumatized training partners in my wake. I had to hide what I was, so practicing in public was out of the question. Still, it was brutal, forcing control on something that so fiercely resists it.”

“I struggle to imagine you as anything but perfectly controlled,” Cade admitted, the words more true than they were playful.

Something resembling a genuine smile briefly graced her features. “There was a time when I made Jer look disciplined by comparison.”

“Now that’s genuinely terrifying.”

“The Fateweavers taught meditation techniques that I used to help map my magical landscape,” she continued. “Finding the boundaries, the sources, the flow patterns. It’s like... like learning a landscape so thoroughly you can navigate it blindfolded.”

Cade considered this. His own approach to magic had always been intuitive rather than methodical—grabbing power when needed and directing it through sheer force of will. Effective, but crude.

“Could you teach me?” he asked, dropping the usual facade of casual disinterest.

Nora studied him for a long moment. “It’s not a quick process. Takes months of daily practice to—”

“I’m a fast learner,” Cade interrupted. “And my timeline for figuring out whatever’s happening inside me seems to be accelerating whether I like it or not.”

She nodded slowly. “We could start with the basics. A simple centering exercise.” She gestured to a wider section of the corridor ahead. “That looks stable enough.”

They reached the area she’d indicated—a natural alcove where the corridor widened briefly before narrowing again. The heartbeat rhythm felt more distant here, as if temporarily muffled by the chamber’s geometry.

“Sit,” Nora instructed, folding her own legs beneath her with practiced grace.

Cade complied, mirroring her cross-legged position with considerably less elegance. “Just to be clear, if something tries to eat us while I’m communing with my inner thief, I’m blaming you entirely.”

“Naturally,” she agreed. “Now close your eyes.”

He did so, immediately feeling more vulnerable. The darkness behind his eyelids seemed deeper than natural, as if the crypt’s shadows somehow penetrated even this small barrier.

“Focus on your breath,” Nora’s voice came, steady and measured. “Not controlling it, just observing. The rise and fall.”

Cade attempted to follow her instructions, though his mind immediately cataloged seventeen different escape routes, thirty-four potential threats, and approximately eight hundred reasons why meditation was a terrible idea while trapped in an ancient, possibly sentient crypt.

“You’re thinking,” Nora observed dryly, somehow sensing his mental acrobatics despite his physical stillness.

“It’s a nasty habit,” he replied without opening his eyes. “I’m, trying to quit, but nothing’s worked. Have any suggestions, oh great healer?”

“Try again. Focus on a single point within your chest. Imagine your core—not just as a source of power, but as a landscape. A place you can explore.”

Cade made another attempt, drawing his attention inward. At first, there was only darkness and the usual chaotic swirl of thoughts.

He took in a deep breath.

“This isn’t—” he started.

“Try again, Cade.”

He let the breath out.

A landscape? Hells, I really hope this isn’t how I die.

Cade Stormhollow took in another breath. In. Out. In.

“Acknowledge each distracting thought,” Nora instructed. “Then let it slip past. Don’t ignore them. Merely imagine yourself holding them and then letting go. Return each time to that point in your chest. Feel how it branches out through your body.”

Cade peeked, and saw that her eyes were closed too. Despite that, however, her entire body seemed to be in a state of readiness, akin to those coiled vipers Orro used to play with.

“Try again, Stormhollow,” Nora admonished without opening her eyes.

“How—”

“Again.”

Gradually, the errant thoughts settled, having been given the time to relax and depressurize in his overflowing mind. In their absence, something took shape—a silver-gold sphere of energy, pulsing in time with his heartbeat. He focused on it, imagining it as a tangible object he could examine.

“I see it,” he murmured, voice barely above a whisper. “My core—it’s like a ball of silver fire with gold threads woven through it.”

“Good,” Nora’s voice seemed to come from very far away. “Now try to look deeper. Past the surface. Into the heart of it.”

Cade concentrated, mentally circling the sphere, searching for a way to penetrate its brilliance. As he focused, the sphere seemed to respond, its surface becoming less solid, more permeable. He reached toward it with imagined hands—

And saw it.

A thread of crimson, thin as a hair but vibrant as fresh blood, woven through the silver-gold matrix. Not just one thread, but many, forming an intricate pattern that seemed to stabilize the otherwise chaotic energies of his destruction magic.

“There’s something red,” he said, voice tight with concentration. “Red threads, holding everything together, making it... coherent somehow.”

“Can you touch it?” Nora asked.

Cade mentally reached for one of the crimson strands. The moment his imagined fingertips brushed against it, a shock of foreign awareness jolted through him—ancient, vast, primordial. Not destruction, not creation, but something that preceded both. Something that existed in the spaces between defined forces.

His eyes snapped open, body jerking as if struck. “Holy shit.”

Nora was watching him intently, her expression unreadable. “What did you see?”

“I’m not sure,” he admitted, heart racing despite having barely moved. “It felt... old. Older than anything I’ve ever encountered. And aware, somehow. Like it knew I was looking at it.”

“The Fateweavers would say that all magic has some level of consciousness,” Nora said, her voice carefully neutral. “A reflection of the will that shapes it.”

“This didn’t feel like a reflection,” Cade countered, rubbing his temples where pressure had begun to build. “It felt like... like I was the reflection. Like it was allowing me to exist rather than the other way around.”

The implications were unsettling.

If the red energy wasn’t simply a variant of his destruction magic but something else entirely—something ancient enough to have its own awareness—what did that mean for him? Was he a vessel? A conduit? A temporary housing for something that would eventually consume him?

“We should try again,” he decided, settling back into position. “I need to understand what’s happening to me.”

Nora hesitated, then nodded. “Focus on your breath first. Center yourself before attempting to—”

“Hey,” Cade interrupted suddenly, head tilting as he finally registered the silence surrounding them. “Where is everyone?”

For the first time, both of them cued into the silence. No shuffling footsteps. No comments or complaints. Not even the occasional breath, save their own.

They were alone.

The corridor stretched empty in both directions, no sign of Jer’s animated gestures or Evie’s quiet humming or Gav’s amber eyes gleaming in the darkness.

“Rayka?” Cade called, rising quickly to his feet. “Jer?”

His voice echoed down the corridor, bouncing off alien architecture without response.

“Birdie?” Nora tried, already retrieving her massive blade from where she’d leaned it against the wall. “Gav?”

Nothing but the persistent, distant heartbeat rhythm.

“When did we lose them?” Cade demanded, a cold knot forming in his stomach. “They were right behind us after we sealed the door.”

“They were...” Nora’s certainty faltered. “Weren’t they?”

They exchanged a look that communicated the same troubling realization: neither could actually remember the last time they’d seen or heard the others. The transition from group to isolation had happened so seamlessly that it hadn’t registered until this moment.

“This is your fault,” Cade declared, though without real heat. “You and your fancy paladin meditation distracted me. This is why thinking is important.”

“My fault?” Nora’s eyebrows rose. “You’re the one who asked for magical training in the middle of a death crypt.”

“Because you brought it up!”

“After you mentioned the mysterious red energy!”

They stared at each other for a moment before Cade’s lips twitched, breaking the tension.

“We’re both idiots,” he conceded.

“Agreed,” Nora sighed, already moving back the way they’d come. “They can’t have gone far. We just need to—”

She stopped abruptly as the corridor before them rippled like disturbed water, stone flowing into new configurations without sound or seam. Where they had passed minutes before, the passage now curved sharply downward, its architecture entirely unfamiliar.

“That’s... not good,” Cade breathed, watching as the very structure of the crypt finished it'sits eerily silent rearrangement around them.

The heartbeat rhythm accelerated slightly, filling the air with palpable anticipation.

“The crypt is alive,” Cade realized aloud. “Or at least sentient enough to respond to our presence.”

“And it’s separated us from the others deliberately,” Nora added, backing away from the still-shifting corridor. “Which means—”

“It’s divided us for a reason,” Cade finished, his mind racing through implications and escape scenarios with practiced speed. “Nothing good ever comes from being deliberately separated in ancient ruins. It’s practically the first rule of dungeon exploration. And first dates, strangely enough.”

“I suspect the crypt doesn’t care about your rulebook.”

The temperature dropped suddenly, frost crystallizing along the edges of the flowing stone. The heartbeat rhythm faltered, skipping several beats before resuming at a slower cadence, as if something had momentarily shocked the living architecture.

Cade knew that feeling. This was the third time he’d experienced it in recent memory, and familiarity did nothing to blunt the primal dread that accompanied it.

“Your companions are alive,” said a voice like velvet dragged across broken glass. “For now.”

Nora’s blade was in her hands instantly, her body shifting into a combat stance that looked as natural as breathing. Cade didn’t bother drawing his daggers—they would be as effective against this visitor as toothpicks against a mountain.

Death stepped from shadows that hadn’t existed a moment before, his elegant suit absorbing rather than reflecting the faint bioluminescence. Perfectly coiffed dark hair framed features too symmetrical to be human, while eyes like wells of midnight regarded them with ancient patience.

“You,” Cade acknowledged, forcing casualness into his tone despite the rush of adrenaline. “Twice in one week. I’m beginning to think you’re developing a crush.”

Death’s perfect lips curved into what might have been a smile on a human face. On him, it resembled the moment before an avalanche—beauty and destruction captured in a single expression.

“Cade Stormhollow,” he greeted, his voice vibrating the very molecules of air between them. “Still collecting impossible situations like others collect trinkets, I see.”

Nora’s knuckles whitened around her sword hilt. “What are you?” she demanded, though her voice suggested she already knew the answer.

Death turned those depthless eyes toward her. “I am exactly what I appear to be, Nora Dreadcaller.” He studied her with academic interest. “Though perhaps you should ask yourself why your fear magic doesn’t respond to my presence. After all, what inspires greater terror than inevitability?”

“Maybe some of us are just really hard to impress,” Cade interjected, stepping forward to draw Death’s attention away from Nora. “You mentioned our friends. Where are they?”

Death gestured elegantly toward the altered corridor, trails of frost following his long fingers through the air. “The crypt has... redistributed them. It’s quite fascinating, actually. This structure was designed with multiple parallel pathways that reconfigure based on the number and nature of intruders.”

“Why?” Nora asked, her blade still raised though she seemed to recognize its futility.

“To test worthiness, of course,” Death replied as if explaining something obvious to a particularly slow child. “The Progenitors were nothing if not thorough in their security measures. When they sealed away artifacts of significant power, they ensured only those of sufficient skill could retrieve them.”

“The crown,” Cade concluded. “This whole place is a trial to determine who gets the Soulkeeper’s Crown.”

“Among other artifacts,” Death agreed. “Though I believe your Bernard has already claimed several of the lesser prizes.”

The casual mention of their quarry sent a spike of urgency through Cade. “Is he still here? In the crypt?”

“Oh yes,” Death confirmed, examining his immaculate nails with feigned interest. “Quite thoroughly trapped, in fact. The crypt recognized his... dishonest intentions... and has arranged a particularly challenging path for him.”

“But not for us?” Cade asked skeptically.

Death’s smile widened marginally. “The crypt judges based on intent, Cade Stormhollow. Your companions have been separated accordingly and are being tested as we speak for worthy traits. What those are, only time will tell.”

“And us?” Nora pressed. “Anything that might clue us into what we might face?”

“Leadership,” Death replied simply. “And trust.”

The word hung between them, laden with implications neither was prepared to fully examine.

“How do we find them?” Cade demanded, fear for his crew overriding caution.

“You don’t,” Death said with frustrating finality. “At least, not directly. The crypt has created a unique path for each of you—challenges that must be overcome to reach the central chamber where all paths ultimately converge.”

“And if we refuse to play along?” Nora questioned. “If we force our way through?”

Death’s expression shifted subtly, something ancient and terrible momentarily visible beneath the perfect facade. “The crypt’s defenses grow more lethal with each attempt to circumvent its trials. Your flaming skeletal friends were merely the first line of protection.”

“So we’re stuck following the crypt’s sick game,” Cade summarized, frustration evident. “Fantastic.”

“Time is of some essence,” Death added, gesturing to where frost patterns had begun spreading across the walls without his direct influence. “The structure has become aware of... inconsistencies.”

“Meaning?”

“Meaning it was not designed to accommodate a fragment of divinity,” Death explained, his gaze fixing on Cade’s chest where his core pulsed with silver-gold energy. “A certain child has apparently threatened this old beast. And like any cornered animal, it strikes.”

“By killing Chubbs,” Cade translated, his pulse competing with the cadence of the crypt.

“Why tell us this?” Nora interrupted, suspicion evident in her tone. “What do you gain from warning us?”

Death regarded her for a long moment, his expression unreadable. “Balance,” he finally said, the same answer he’d given Cade in their first meeting. “And perhaps... entertainment. Eternity grows tedious without occasional variation. And it would not do for Creation to die here. She must reach my beloved.”

“Glad we could provide your immortal existence with some childcare,” Cade replied dryly.

“Trust me, you have no idea” Death agreed with disturbing sincerity. “Few mortals navigate divine engagements with such... creative determination.”

The heartbeat rhythm suddenly accelerated, the walls around them trembling in response. Minute cracks appeared in the flowing stone, spreading like capillaries across the alien architecture.

“It appears our conversation has been noticed,” Death observed with mild interest. “The crypt grows restless.”

“How do we reach our friends?” Cade pressed, sensing their time was running short.

“Down,” Death replied simply. “Always down. The heart of this structure lies at its deepest point, where all paths converge. Survive your trials, and you will find both your companions and your prize.”

The cracks in the walls widened, golden light spilling through like blood from stone veins.

“One final warning,” Death added as his form began to dissolve into shadow. “The crown is not what you believe it to be. Do not attempt to use it, no matter the temptation.”

“You said that before,” Cade noted. “But you never explained what it actually is.”

Death’s smile was his most terrible yet—knowing and ancient and filled with secrets older than human civilization.

“A key,” he said, his voice fading as his physical presence dispersed. “To a door that should remain forever locked.”

With that cryptic pronouncement, he vanished completely, leaving only a lingering chill in the air and frost patterns that slowly melted into ordinary condensation.

The moment he disappeared, the crypt seemed to exhale—stones settling, cracks sealing, the heartbeat rhythm returning to its previous steady cadence.

Cade and Nora stood in silence for several seconds, processing both the encounter and its implications.

“So,” Cade finally said, “on our earlier scale of ‘mild inconvenience’ to ‘cosmic catastrophe,’ I think we’ve officially reached ‘oh shit.’”

“That seems accurate,” Nora agreed, finally lowering her blade. “You want to explain how you’re on a first-name basis with Death?”

“Long story involving a midnight chat in the woods,” Cade replied, already moving toward the altered corridor that now spiraled downward. “I’ll fill you in while we walk.”

“Walk where, exactly?”

“Down. Always down,” Cade repeated in his best Death-impression.

Nora raised an eyebrow, clearly secretly impressed but unwilling to admit it.

“We find the heart of this place, we find our friends.” He paused, adding, “And hopefully before Bernard claims whatever the hell this crown really is.”

Nora nodded, falling into step beside him. “You realize we’re walking directly into whatever trap this place has designed specifically for us.”

“Of course,” Cade agreed with a flash of his usual reckless grin. “But unlike with our skeletal friends upstairs, at least we’ve been warned.”

“Small comfort.”

“I’ll take what I can get,” he replied, silver eyes fixed on the descending path. “Besides, when has anything ever gone according to plan?”

“Never,” Nora admitted.

“Exactly. So we improvise. We survive. We find our friends.” His expression hardened with resolve. “And we remind this crypt why it’s a terrible idea to separate a thief from his crew.”

They stepped onto the spiraling pathway together, the living stone seeming to pulse beneath their feet with anticipation. Whatever trials awaited them in the depths below, one thing was certain: time was running out not just for them, but for their scattered companions facing their own tests in the crypt’s twisted labyrinth.

The descent had begun.
