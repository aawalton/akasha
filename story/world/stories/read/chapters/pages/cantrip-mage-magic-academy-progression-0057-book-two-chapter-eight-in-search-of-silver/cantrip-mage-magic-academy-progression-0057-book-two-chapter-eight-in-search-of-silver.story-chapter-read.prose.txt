Time didn’t exactly fly.

Emerald Cursive was a brutal language, and for almost three weeks, Eola found her free time wrapped up in it. Worse, The Craft of Permanent Magic was dense. It was written in a dialect of Emerald Cursive that was anything but modern, and the translation had to account for that. So, far too often, she found herself translating it at her desk while Colin looked over her shoulder and pointed out possible flaws.

Not that she minded his presence at all. Colin was, among other things, fastidious. Between the two of them, they found that The Craft of Permanent Magic was less indecipherable than Eola had feared. And, when she got sick to death of hammering her brain against the dense, maze-like language, he was more than willing to fund trips to Varin’s Town.

They visited several different inns and restaurants, but none as good as what Ursula made at the Chubby Saber.

It wasn’t as if Eola’s days were nothing but studying the book on ensorcellment and spending time with Colin, though.

For one thing, there was Patrol Duty. Lieutenant-Librarian Fringe’s crash course on the different monsters of the Spine Mountains made even the final exam in Introduction to Monsters the previous year feel like a fond memory. Instead of carefully remembered notes from a bestiary or lecture, though, the yellow gnome insisted on rapid-fire, blow-by-blow descriptions of how to fight any given monster, from snow giants to frozen river hydras. According to him, any and all of the monsters on his list could be a threat in the Spine Mountains—and with time running out before student teams would be sent out, Eola, Colin, and Roth had their hands full with learning.

And the gnome was no less monstrous in Spellblade Tactics. Two days a week, Eola hobbled off the track after her hour with Fringe, legs shaking, arms feeling like jelly, and so sweat-covered she needed what felt like gallons of water to recover. Maestro Yarrowbloom was no easier on them. His two dueling days were a horrific barrage of spars, as fast as the students could find new partners, all while the elf lectured them on the differences between the Taron-Li and the kind of combat they’d actually be doing. If he seemed frustrated to abandon the Taron-Li, he hid it well in Eola’s estimation.

The theory day, every Third-Day, was the most interesting, though. Eola began truly learning Imbuement—a craft she’d started to pick up from Yarrowbloom last year. According to the professors, it was central to their role as spellblades in open combat.

In Extinct Languages, Instructor Tarik began with translation primers for Old Alemic. It was review for Eola—at least the language part—but the cultural implications of the ancient, post-Y’aer Alemic Empire were astounding. Not for Eola, but for Colin, who read the manual over her shoulder every night. It had been an empire of fortress cities and magecraft holds. Like many other empires, they’d attempted to subvert their subjects’ cultures, but unlike some more successful attempts, they’d ended up losing control of their own as it encountered and learned from its conquered rivals’.

It was a sort of reverse invasion, which had only sped up as the magecraft holds grew in power and the central rulers lost it. Each hold’s culture developed differently, usually around the culture of what had been there before, and the Alemic Empire fractured in a matter of two generations. The class agreed, almost universally, that it was for the best. Alemia wasn’t a land suited to empire.

Eola was less interested in all that than in the languages, of course, but Colin started ‘borrowing’ her primers, leaving her with nothing to do but study Emerald Script.

And so it went, until Eola was certain she had everything she needed.

Including, oddly enough, an unexpected visitor.

Roth.

The boy’s expression hadn’t lightened up with time. He still looked like he’d woken up on the wrong side of every bed in Varin’s Academy. And Atta had found a new victim in his pure white songbird. Right now, the blue cat familiar was up in the loft, stalking the bird. The bird was very carefully watching Atta from the windowsill.

“My list,” Roth said without any preamble. He plopped it down on the desk, and Eola started going over it. “Yours?”

“Oh. Right.” Eola fished through her stack of parchment and produced her own. She didn’t need to look at it to know what it said. After all, she’d been refining it for days.

Yew (Hamadryad’s preferred)ChromiteSilverBloodstone (alchemical)Winterstalk Musk - Must be harvested fresh and then frozenAmber - Perhaps a mining community? Yellow TarAmethyst (powdered)Amethyst (crystal, ten carats)

It was a respectable list.

As she looked over Roth’s, she nodded. “You’ve been reading the same sources I have.”

“Yes, but I decided on a mix of caneseed oil and goatshorn epoxy. It’ll carry the notes better. And I won’t need Winterstalk Musk, but will need a snow giant’s tear. That’ll be a problem. According to my research—“

“They’re—“

“The kind of monster archmages struggle with,” Roth finished. He looked a little more put out than usual. “I’ll ask Lannith for an exception, but I doubt she’ll be so kind.”

“Not likely. So, Instructor Clearance, then?”

“You think my list is fine?”

Eola sighed. “You’re worried about what I think?”

“Yes. You study harder than anyone I know. If there’s anyone who’ll have some idea of what ensorcellment takes, it’ll be you. I’d be an idiot not to take advantage of that kind of friend. Though it is a relief to see your list so similar to mine.”

“Fine then. Let’s go.”

Eola pulled her cloak around her shoulders, fastened it, and flipped the hood over her head. Neither of them had earned their Journeymage hat or badge yet, though Eola was sure she was closer than Roth. He didn’t cast multiple spells. Instead, he put all his effort into a single one and sustained it. But without a hat to show off, she was relegated to the cloak and hood, and it had started to get a bit colder. Already, it had snowed twice—though it hadn’t stuck, and the grass clung to a faint green.

Instructor Clearance’s greenhouse was out in the academy’s yard, and her yew grove was even further, and neither Eola nor Roth wanted to be cold. They trudged against the late autumn wind and arrived in the center of the grove, then waited.

They didn’t have to wait long.

The instructor stepped out of a yew’s trunk, her body forming out of rich heartwood the color of Catrine’s perfectly blonde hair. It darkened as she separated from the tree, barking over in the suggestion of clothing. She nodded. “Miss Lemiene. Mr. Gerr. Neither of you is in my courses this term. To what do I owe the visit?”

“We have, um, a favor,” Eola asked. She glanced at Roth, only to see that he’d stiffened up in his cloak. His dark eyes had widened, too. And, of course, Instructor Clearance’s carved eyes were locked on his, not on Eola. She smiled slightly, and Eola gulped, then pressed on. “It’s about ensorcellment.”

Clearance turned slowly, her eyes remaining locked on Roth but her body and face shifting to face Eola. “You understand that what you’re about to ask is—“

“Highly dangerous, taboo, and potentially insulting, yes. I’ve read The Craft of Permanent Magic and Ensorcellment Projects for Emerging Mages. They both say the same thing.”

“And you’re both still going to ask?”

Eola swallowed again. “Yes.”

Roth nodded silently, seeming to shrink into himself.

Dryads and Hamadryads, according to both guides, considered their home-groves to be an extension of themselves. Eola wouldn’t just give out a lock of hair to anyone for them to use in a ritual, and The Craft had warned her that both types of monstrous women would be extremely reluctant to do so with their forests’ branches and boughs. Eola had—very briefly—considered taking one without permission. But only briefly. There were two problems with that plan.

First, and most pressingly, Instructor Clearance would have a connection to whatever Eola made. She’d know it came from wood borrowed without permission, and the book had also made it clear that, while many Dryads and Hamadryads were very calm, reasonable monsters, they were still monsters. In Instructor Clearance’s case, she was a monster with archmage-level Ordered Magic, a great deal of respect at Varin’s Academy, and the ability to kill Eola, or get her expelled. Either would be bad.

And at a distant second, a gifted branch or bough would be much more potent as a base for Eola’s attempt at ensorcellment.

Instructor Clearance stared at Roth for a long time. Then she nodded. “Miss Lemiene, come with me. We’ll discuss my terms. Mr. Gerr, you’ll wait here until I’m finished with your classmate.”

“Yes, ma’am.” Roth spoke quietly, but Eola picked up the quiver in his voice.

Clearance led Eola through the ever-thicker forest. When she finally turned, Eola had no idea where she was. Atta was warm in her pocket, though, and that—if nothing else about the situation—was reassuring.

For a while, neither spoke. Then Instructor Clearance sighed. “I won’t give up a single branch without proof they’ll be used wisely. Show me the list.”

Eola held it out silently, and Clearance read through it, wooden lips moving as she went. No one spoke. Eola held her breath.

Then Clearance nodded. “Your list, at least, seems reasonable. Acquire the rest of it, then come see me. I’ll consider your request then. And if I say no…”

“I can make my design work with oak, Instructor.”

“Wise girl. I need to talk to your friend. Wait here. I’ll come get you when I’m done with him.”

By the time Clearance rematerialized from a nearby yew and guided Eola back out of the grove, then collected Roth, the cold front had started moving in in earnest. It was going to be the first truly frigid night of the year; the storm the night before was clearing off, and the temperature hadn’t risen enough to be truly comfortable.

But while she shivered on and off in her fur-lined cloak, Roth couldn’t stop shaking when he returned.

They left quickly, and Eola waited until they were out of earshot of the grove and well on their way to her next stop before hissing, “What’s got you so worked up?”

“Hamadryads.” Roth shivered even more.

Hamadryads? Was Roth afraid of Hamadryads? Yes, Eola had a very healthy respect for her professor—one that Lieutenant-Librarian Fringe’s lectures and Lannith’s presence had rekindled. But Roth wasn’t afraid of much, and he’d sat through Instructor Clearance’s lectures last year. “How did you survive the first term?”

“I kept my head down, like I’d been taught.” Roth went quiet as they left the academy’s grounds and headed into the steep roofs and narrow streets of Varin’s Town. “The smith? What was his name again?”

“Parunet. He’s our best bet at finding silver and chromite. Why Hamadryads?”

“You won’t laugh?” Roth’s eyes narrowed suspiciously.

“No.”

“Fine.” Roth seemed to shrink into himself even more, and he went quiet for a long time. “We had Hamadryads near my village. Not like Clearance. She’s terrifying, but you can talk to her. These were pure monsters. Not friendly at all. And the village was always at war with them. We needed their groves to stay warm during the winters.”

“So you’ve been afraid of them…”

“Since I was a kid, yes. Don’t laugh. You promised not to laugh.”

“I see.” Eola focused on navigating the busy streets. Why would she laugh about that? Childhood memories caused fears. That was the way things were. Instead, she concentrated on navigating the streets of Varin’s Town.

Many of the people in her way, to her surprise, wore the mask and robes of the Cult of Y’aer. What were they all doing here? She shook her head. They didn’t matter right now.

As they approached the relatively flat roof and the pounding steel-on-steel sound of Smith Parunet’s workspace grew louder, Eola focused on what did. The sooner she had all her other components, the sooner she could get a branch and start working on her wand in earnest. The sooner she finished it, the sooner she could—hopefully, at least—attempt the Journeymage trial and earn her hat and badge. And the sooner she did that…

Her parents were waiting, somewhere in that dungeon.

Smith Parunet’s smithy looked much the same as it had the first time Eola had visited, only without an entire class of late teenagers sitting and standing on the ashy dirt workyard. Smith Parunet’s green skin was almost entirely covered by his blacksmith’s apron, a pair of goggles under a leather hood, and two thick gloves that made the gnome’s hands look almost comically oversized. Eola and Roth waited patiently while he worked a slab of iron into something that slowly took the shape of a plow.

After half an hour, the gnome’s hood and goggles came off, and he closed his eyes. “Ensorcellment, right?”

“How’d you—“ Roth started, but Eola cut him off.

“Yes. We need access to silver and chromite, and time in your forge if we can get it. We’re willing to pay.”

The green man snorted. "You’re the fourth group of students this term. Willing to pay’ll get you somewhere. Chromite’s no problem, either. Gonna have plenty of that in the next few weeks.”

Roth’s eyes narrowed. Eola’s did, too. “But the silver…?”

“Ayup. The silver. I get a shipment from Highstone once a month. Comes in a wagon up the Spines. But everyone buys in early. Next four shipments are all bought up, and the price is out of control. Nearly triple last year’s cost.”

Eola winced. Triple the price. No silver for four months. This was…a problem. A serious problem. Next to her, Roth’s eyes narrowed even further.

“Any idea why?” he asked.

Smith Parunet shook his head. “Nope. Governing Houses do what they do. House Andrese’s got a chokehold on silver. Everyone has to do business with them.”

“There’s nothing you can do, then,” Eola said.

“Not true. Be glad to ‘prentice you two for a bit. You get the metal, I teach you the basics. But silver doesn’t work like chromite, and a silver an’ chromite alloy doesn’t work like steel. No point teaching you without the metal you need in your hands.”

That was something. Eola’s shoulders were still tense, but at least it was only one problem, not a half-dozen. She nodded slowly. “We’ll…manage, I suppose. Thank you, Smith.”

The green gnome pulled his goggles back on, nodded, and got back to work on a long, curved scythe’s blade—also made from iron. Eola recognized a dismissal when she saw one.

“So, that was a waste of time,” Roth said as they returned to the academy.

“Not exactly. We learned where we can get silver. And we learned that Smith Parunet’s willing to help with the forging work. That’s something.”

“Not much. Not enough.”

“We just have to find out how to get the silver from House Andrese.”

“Sure,” Roth said. “That’s impossible, though. I already told you about my village, right? What happened there? And your extremely tentative snuggle buddy—“

Eola’s face heated as she blushed a deep crimson.

“—and Patrice started figuring out what House Andrese’s doing with that metal, or at least that they’re controlling it. So unless you’re going to walk right up to Latrine Catrine and ask her for a dozen pounds of silver, you’re not getting a hold of it in time to pass this class.”

“Your village…” Eola pushed a lock of hair out of her eyes. It was about time she got it trimmed, but she’d put it off a few more days; there was too much to do as it was.

“What about it?”

Eola opened her mouth, then closed it. “Never mind.”

“Suit yourself,” Roth grumbled.

The two of them walked silently through the freezing wind. The temperature was already dropping rapidly as the storm came in, and Eola made a mental note to change out her summer leggings for the thick woolen ones she’d worn almost the entire first term. Or maybe to buy new ones—her old pair was getting…a little ratty.

They slipped through the dorm doors, then climbed the stairs to their suite. Patrice and Colin were both out and about, and Roth visibly relaxed as he pushed his room’s door open.

“Wait,” Eola said.

“What?”

“Instructor Clearance. She told me I had to find the entire rest of my list before she’d give me a branch to work. Did she ask you for all the ingredients, too?”

“No.” Roth shuddered. “Worse.”

“Then what?”

“She wants to…don’t laugh! She wants to have a formal tea with me. An hour of conversation and drinks. Alone. Just the two of us.”

“I see. Well, I’ll see you later.” Eola managed to keep a straight face until she had her own door closed, but when Atta sprang off the loft and hit the desk, already laughing, she couldn’t help but giggle quietly. It was one thing to be scared of Hamadryads, after all. Perfectly reasonable, in fact. But Roth—angry, too-tough Roth—was terrified of a tea party!
