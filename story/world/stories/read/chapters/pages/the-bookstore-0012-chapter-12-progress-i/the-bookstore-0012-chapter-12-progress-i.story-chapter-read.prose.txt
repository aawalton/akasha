Two months after opening the bookstore, Anne received the most pleasant kind of surprise.

Ceres came back.

When she had first struck the deal with the half-beast mercenary, Anne had prepared herself to wait at least a year before seeing her again. Mercenaries lived under the tyranny of chance more than planning. Routes changed, jobs vanished, escorts appeared without warning, and distant opportunities often swallowed months whole. So when Ceres stepped into the bookstore carrying a heavy crate with her usual straightforward bearing, Anne felt a piercing, intimate thrill she did not bother trying to deny to herself.

Fortune, for once, had moved faster than expected.

As it turned out, nearly a month after leaving Mountainend, Ceres had come across a merchant selling antiques in bulk. He was a man of Oryner descent dragging four carts of old goods all the way from the northern border toward the Emperor Seat, hoping to make his profit through blind sales and the greed of fools willing to gamble on sealed junk. Ceres had no intention of risking coin that way. But then she saw the crate of books.

The crate was old, heavy, and unattractive to anyone without a certain perspective. Most people around it had laughed when she showed interest. They laughed even harder after she haggled with the merchant—or, more accurately, pretended to haggle just enough to keep him from realizing how eager she really was—and bought it for sixty-three silver. Had the chest itself not been of such good quality, the old goat might have settled for forty-five.

Ceres had laughed with the others at the time. It cost less than explaining herself.

Then she loaded the crate onto her cart and left, ignoring the mockery of the crowd. To them it was a foolish purchase. To Anne, it would turn into gold.

Ceres had not stopped there.

While in the Emperor Seat, she had followed Anne’s instructions and approached the Royal Academy of Magic through a proper middleman. There she spent a full gold coin on research scrolls, choosing carefully and paying fair price for fair knowledge. Two weeks after that, she finally secured an escort job to Riverend, which brought her back into the orbit of Mountainend.

The truth was that Ceres had been eager for the return long before the route appeared.

She wanted to repay Anne’s kindness. She wanted to show what she had found. And more than that, she wanted help with the new obstacle that had emerged in her training. Somewhere along the road, she had already started seeing the bookstore owner less as a useful contact and more as a kind of wise master.

Anne noticed all of that in the way the woman carried herself.

Even with the crate in her arms, Ceres looked lighter than she had during their first meeting. More comfortable and confident. There was still weariness in her movements, of course. A traveling mercenary could not avoid that. But the tightness around her mouth had eased, and the heaviness that once clung to her posture was no longer so pronounced.

It seemed her training had finally begun to advance again.

Anne, smiling warmly, welcomed her like a close friend and quite heroically pretended not to care about the crate.

Actually, she was dying to open it. Still, she was a storeowner, not a starving scavenger attacking a fallen bag. Standards had to be upheld.

“Come, sit,” she said, already setting tea before Ceres. “Drink first and tell me how the trip went.”

“Thank you, Miss Anne.” Ceres let out a tired breath after lowering the crate and taking the offered seat. “I have been busy. When I returned to the province capital, there was already an escort job waiting for me to the Emperor Seat. Then I got another one to bring some materials back, and a week ago I finally went to Riverend escorting an adventuring group.”

Anne listened carefully and offered the proper amount of impressed sympathy at all the right moments. They exchanged a few more words before Ceres explained about the crate of books and the scrolls from the academy.

At that point, Anne’s restraint nearly broke.

A gold and a half in books and scrolls. More than that, not only value but variety. Unexpected variety. The sort that could strengthen the bookstore in several directions at once.

She thanked Ceres so many times that the mercenary eventually had to wave her down with embarrassed amusement. Only then did Anne allow herself to focus on the crate.

The carved wooden chest itself was of solid quality, though not remarkable beyond that. The lock was strong dark-grey metal, practical and well-made, but not magical. Anne opened it in less than ten seconds and found herself staring at roughly thirty books packed inside.

Her hands immediately moved with the reverence of long habit.

As her craft demanded, she took each volume out one by one, studying cover, spine, integrity, size, binding, and weight before setting it down. Soon, thirty-three books lay spread across the nearby tables, none stacked over another. Anne would never pile newly acquired books if she could help it. Whatever their quality, each deserved first inspection properly.

And this harvest, she quickly realized, was bountiful.

If bought individually, the collection would have cost at least three gold, likely more depending on seller mood and location. More importantly, it contained potential across several kinds of use—sale stock, lending stock, restoration work, and archival additions.

Ten of the books were intact and would need little more than cleaning and careful storage.

Eighteen required restoration, re-covering, or both.

The remaining five were in worse condition, damaged enough that they would need translation, rewriting, or partial reconstruction, and even then Anne could not guarantee every piece of information within them would survive perfectly.

Even so, she considered the whole thing a triumph.

As for the research scrolls, they were common by academy standards and reasonably priced, exactly as expected when two informed parties dealt fairly. The gold coin had bought her twenty-one reports, eight theories, and two technique scrolls.

Knowledge was expensive.

Anne had long accepted that the correct response to such a truth was not resentment, but readiness to pay.

After finishing the inventory, she finally sat down across from Ceres and turned her full attention to the more personal matter. Counseling was, after all, among the parts of her craft she loved most.

“You were right, Miss Anne,” Ceres said, and there was no hesitation in the admission. “After I studied the book and changed my training, I started progressing again. It is still difficult, but now I can already feel myself moving toward the next step.” Her expression tightened with embarrassment. “But I... I do not know what skill I should learn. Most of the known ones are made either for humans or for beastkin.”

Anne let out a slow breath. Of all the things Ceres could have returned asking for, a skill pleased her most.

The trust in it touched her far more deeply than she showed. After all, the favorite weapon of Anne’s greatest enemy was the skill scroll. Those mass-produced magitech were cheap enough, easy enough, and fast enough to tempt almost anyone. Most people, given the choice, would buy one without a second thought.

The rubbish could indeed teach a skill to a dull warrior in a matter of days.

But only that.

A proper skill book or martial text demanded study, effort, correction, repetition, and patience. In exchange, the resulting understanding was leagues deeper. Someone who learned through such a book might gain mastery rivaling a full year of experience from one who relied on a scroll. The fact that noble houses and ancient martial lines still taught their descendants without using those scrolls was proof enough.

The magitech version was the lesser product. And yet those damned lesser products had helped drive her craft toward disappearance.

Ceres, sadly, was not choosing books because she valued the better path in theory. She was choosing them because the common skill scrolls did not fit her at all. Her unusual lineage made that unavoidable. More than that, the method of energy absorption she had learned from Anne’s recommended book already set her apart from ordinary human or beastkin approaches.

“I do have some options,” Anne said carefully. “Let me fetch the books first. Then we can see what suits you best.”

She promised nothing more than that.

Ten minutes later, she returned carrying eight books of varying sizes.

By now she understood Ceres’ situation well enough to frame the problem precisely. Humans maintained a clearer but more distant relationship with magical energy. Beastkin, by contrast, were closer to it, sometimes almost too close, their bodies treating it as something  almost natural. Ceres stood somewhere between those two conditions. Compared to a human, she should absorb energy more easily. But when controlling it, separating external energy from her own would become more difficult.

That meant her future skills should favor lower precision control, higher tolerance for direct energy use, and little concern for efficiency. She was not the kind of fighter who would thrive on refined, delicate techniques that saved every scrap of power. She needed something that accepted force and motion first, control second.

The discussion lasted a little over an hour.

Anne explained. Ceres questioned. Then Anne refined her explanation and cut away options that sounded attractive but would ultimately fail. By the end, they had reduced the choice to two books.

The first was Martial Laws of the Skull Crushing Sect, a training book containing the basic martial techniques of an ancient sect that specialized in bursting forward with violent force, overwhelming opponents through speed, strength, and sudden pressure while disregarding the safety of distance.

The second was Body Pathways, a more technical study of the body’s energy channels, one that would help Ceres understand and refine how her own power moved through her frame.

The problem was that Anne could not sell the training book.

Its uniqueness alone made that impossible, and even a copy would lose much of what made it valuable. Under ordinary circumstances, she would have insisted Ceres study it only within the bookstore. But learning an entire martial foundation that way would be painfully inefficient, and Anne had already decided that this customer was no longer ordinary.

So she made a choice.

A premium customer, then. A trusted one.

She would lend the book.

Not permanently, of course. Temporary ownership only, with careful terms and full expectation of return. Taking into account the complexity of the techniques and the amount of time true study would require, Anne set the return deadline at one year.

As for Body Pathways, Ceres only needed to remain at the bookstore a few days to study it directly. She would not comprehend the whole of it in that time, not even close, but even a small portion properly understood would make a meaningful difference.

By the time the arrangements were finished, Anne felt warm inside in a way coin alone never produced. This was exactly what she had wanted when she opened the bookstore.

Not just trade or survival. But this feeling of rightful work being carried in the proper way, knowledge passing where it was needed most, and another person leaving stronger because she had been here.
