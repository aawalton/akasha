My body jolted, and my muscles tensed as panic shot through me, making my heart race. I turned slowly to face my mom, my mind racing. I had no clue how things were about to go with my mom, and I didn’t even know what I was going to say to her about the dog. I just hadn’t thought that far ahead.

Mom’s gaze wasn’t on me as she stood in the open doorway of the French windows, though. Even from the end of the garden, I could see her eyes scanning the field behind me, and I was pretty sure she expected to see a hastily retreating figure sprinting across the gentle hill.

After a few seconds, once she was satisfied that no one was there, she finally looked at me and spotted the dog cowering behind my legs. I held my breath, waiting for her seemingly inevitable anger, but her eyebrows just twitched slightly before drawing together.

“Whose dog is that?” she demanded.

“I…” I started before trailing off and looking back at the dog uncertainly. I hadn’t planned what to say to that question either. “I don’t know. It doesn’t have a collar.”

My tone was so unconvincing that I almost winced. My answer was so vague, and it just seemed like a lie. It seemed ridiculous that I would just stumble across a dog in my garden. That kind of thing didn’t really happen. It was far-fetched, and I knew that, but it was the truth. I really didn’t know whose dog it was.

The furrow between Mom’s eyebrows grew deeper, and she stepped through the door, standing on the paving slab outside in her slippers. Her eyes darted between the dog and me a few times before flicking towards the field again.

“Well, who did you get him from?” she asked, a note of accusation in her voice.

She thought I’d bought him from someone, I realised. She must have assumed I’d had him delivered to the house in secret, hoping that we wouldn’t be able to return him once he arrived, but there was no way I could have done that. I didn’t really have any money. I didn’t have a job, and whenever I needed to buy something, I had to ask either her or Dad, so how could I have saved up for him?

“No one!”

“Where did he come from then?” she shot back, clearly not believing me.

I opened my mouth to respond, meaning to say that I didn’t know, but the words died in my throat as a memory flashed behind my eyes. I’d seen him before. I was certain of it. A black dog had run alongside the car as we drove back from Scotland, and I thought he was a monster. I assumed the giant shadow was some blood thirsty creature, intent on murdering me and eating my corpse, but I was no longer so convinced.

Another memory jostled with that one, pushing its way into my awareness. It felt hazier and more distant. The image was blurry, slightly warped and faded, but it was a memory nonetheless, and it sent ice down my spine.

The world around me seemed to shift, and in a moment, I was there. It wasn’t real. I knew it wasn’t real. I was simply reliving something I had seen before, but everything about it felt real. I could hear the screams and cries of my sisters around me as they were murdered. The thick scent of blood and rotting flesh filled the air, and my body trembled as the dog turned towards me.

I was sure it was about to leap forward and crush my head in its powerful jaw, like it had countless others, but it didn’t attack. It just stared at me, seemingly checking whether I was the one it was looking for, and that was when I realised it wasn’t there to kill me. It was there to save me.

The memory receded so quickly that it left me breathless, and I almost shook my head to help clear it. What I’d just relived wasn’t from my reality, I told myself, and it was not the same dog. The one in that world had been so huge that I wasn’t sure he was really a dog, and neither was the thing I’d seen on the journey home. That was most likely a wolf or just my eyes playing tricks on me. I was probably just drifting in and out of sleep and thought I was seeing something that wasn’t truly there.

And the thing hiding behind my legs was not a monster. He was far too small for that; his head barely even reached my knee, and I wasn’t sure whether he was just a puppy or if he was fully grown and would never get any taller, but there was no way he could be a monster. He was too cute.

Mom was still staring at me, waiting impatiently for me to answer her, I realised.

“I don’t know. He was caught in the fence when I came out, so I assume he came from somewhere on the other side of the field?” I explained quickly, hoping that would be enough to satisfy her.

Her expression did not change.

“Okay, so put him back out there,” she told me. “I’m sure he’ll find his way home.”

“What? No!”

Panic came out of nowhere and surged into me. It gripped my heart, squeezing it painfully, and I looked down at the door in horror. The thought of lifting the fence and letting him slip back under and run off into the distance filled me with such dread that I couldn’t breathe for a few seconds. All I could do was stare at my mom.

Her eyebrows rose slowly, and an incredulous expression appeared on her face. Even in my panic, I was aware I’d said the wrong thing. I’d spoken back to my mom. I’d ignored her, gone against a direct instruction, and she hated it when I did that. She saw it as proof that I did not respect her, and all children should respect their parents. They should blindly follow any direction they were given without asking questions, which Mom had told me many times, but I could never quite bring myself to do that.

A brief, humourless laugh came from Mom, and she took a step closer.

“Excuse me?” she asked.

It was clear she had not misheard me. She was not asking me to repeat myself; she was giving me a chance to apologise and not make another mistake, and I felt my mind spinning as I tried desperately to think of something I could say that wouldn’t make her angry. I needed a different approach, something that Mom would want to agree with more.

“I mean… we can’t just put him back out there,” I said, too aware of the pleading note that crept into my voice. “What if he doesn’t have a home or gets lost? And he’s injured. Look! He could die if we put him back in the field.”

Nervously, I watched my mom’s face as her eyes found the still-bleeding wounds on the dog’s back. I expected her to argue more, but instead, her expression softened slightly, and she took another step forward. The dog let out a pitiful whimper, and I glanced back at it, worried that it was in pain, but it hadn’t moved. It seemed almost like it had sensed that Mom was looking at it, and it wanted to seem as heartbreaking as possible to win her over.

And it seemed to be working.

“What happened to him?” she asked, but there was no more venom in her voice. In fact, her tone was softer than I’d heard in a long time.

“I think he must have been trying to squeeze under the fence and got caught,” I said, reaching down to stroke his head. “He was still stuck in it when I found him, but I got him out.”

The dog pressed closer to my legs, nuzzling into my hand, and I smiled gently at him before glancing up at my mom. Her eyes were still fixed on the dog, and it felt wrong, but her expression made hope leap within my heart. She looked almost pained, and I should have known that was how she’d react.

Mom could be cruel at times, but that was always focused on either Dad or me. It was never directed at animals, and she’d grown up with dogs. I’d heard the way she spoke about her childhood pets. She loved them more than anything or anyone else, and there was no way she could ignore a dog in pain.

“Be careful. He could be rabid or have fleas,” Mom said as I stroked him again.

I snatched my hand away from him. I hadn’t even thought about that before Mom mentioned it, and suddenly, I was concerned. It had licked my fingers, I remembered. When I’d held my hand out to it, it had licked my fingers. Was that all it took for rabies to be transmitted, or did the virus need to get into my body somehow? Did it have to get into a cut, and did I have any on my hands?

The urge to glance down at them and check slammed into me, but I couldn’t do that with Mom still looking at me. She might demand to know what I was doing, and I didn’t want her to know I was worried. It might make her change her mind about the dog.

I didn’t think I had any cuts on my hands, though. No open ones, at least. I probably had a couple of small cuts around my nails. I always picked at the skin there, and I’d tried when I noticed I was doing it, but I rarely actually realised at the time.

The dog wouldn’t be acting normally if it had rabies, though. I didn’t know much about the disease. It wasn’t something I’d ever really learnt about, but I was sure it had been mentioned in books or on television or something. Animals that got it started foaming at the mouth, and the dog at my side was not.

It was pressed against me, though. Its body had been touching my leggings pretty much since Mom came out into the garden, and if it had fleas, they could have already been passed onto me. Tiny bugs could be crawling all over my leggings already, and there was no way I’d know until it was too late.

I could almost feel them, though. A shudder went through me at the phantom touch of millions of minuscule legs running up and down my own. My skin itched, and I longed to rip the trousers off and throw them far away before sprinting into the shower. I wasn’t sure whether that would be enough to shake the fleas loose, but I wanted to do it.

The dog appeared fine, though. It wasn’t scratching itself, and it didn’t seem like there was anything wrong with it. It was just sitting there and staring up at me with an almost disapproving expression. It looked like a completely normal dog, and I wasn’t sure whether I would have been able to tell otherwise, but I hoped I would.

“We should take him to the vet,” I suggested. “They’d be able to make sure he’s okay, right?”

Mom hesitated for just a moment before nodding.

“And they’ll be able to check and see if he has a chip.”

“A chip?” I repeated, not quite knowing what she was talking about.

I’d definitely heard people mention chips in regard to dogs before, but I didn’t really know what they were or how they worked. I’d never had a dog, so I must have just not really paid much attention to it when they were brought up.

Dogs kind of made me nervous at times. All animals did. I didn’t know what to expect from them, and they couldn’t speak, so there was no way to know what they were thinking, which I didn’t like, but I did always kind of want a dog. I think most kids did. Mom shot me down every time, though. She said they were too much work, too much responsibility, and she didn’t want to have to look after yet another living thing.

“A microchip,” Mom explained, her tone bordering on condescending. “All dogs have to have one with the contact details of the owner on it.”

If it had an owner. I was pretty sure it didn’t, though, and I didn’t know why I thought that. I just had a feeling the dog was a stray, but maybe that was just wistful thinking. I was so hopeful that the dog was a stray, and that it didn’t have rabies or fleas, because perhaps then Mom would let me keep him.

“Oh,” I said. “So… we have to take him to the vet, right? So they can call the owner and let them know their dog’s been found?

Mom pursed her lips, staring at the dog again. I could almost see her mind turning as she debated what to do, and it felt promising that she was taking so long. Surely, that had to mean she couldn’t think of a reason not to do it. She was probably trying to, but it seemed like she wasn’t coming up with much.

The silence stretched out between us, and I was so tempted to break it. I wanted to blurt something out, to try one last time to convince her, but I forced myself to remain quiet. That was always the best option when Mom was trying to work out what to do. She often went against what I wanted; it seemed like an instinctual reaction for her, and if she knew how much I’d already fallen in love with the dog, I was worried that would make her want to get rid of him even more.

“Yes,” Mom said finally. “We can’t just put him out in the field to die or get out into the road and get hit by a car. That would be irresponsible.”

She shot me a reproachful look, as if I had been the one to suggest that, and I couldn’t even bring myself to point out that was what she’d wanted to do. I was too happy.

“Okay!”

I’d tried to keep my tone neutral. I didn’t want Mom to know how relieved I was that we were taking him to the vet to get his wounds looked at and that we might maybe be able to keep him afterwards. I knew it was unlikely. Mom hadn’t even mentioned that yet, but the hope had started to blossom in me, and it was clear she noticed that.

Mom’s eyes narrowed, and her expression hardened ever so slightly. Her spine straightened, and she crossed her arms over her chest.

“But that’s it,” she told me. “We’re not adopting the damn thing, so don’t even think about it. Once the vet scans his chip, she’ll call his owner, and they’ll come pick him up, alright?”

My heart sank a little, but I clung desperately to the hope that he had no owner, refusing to give up on it yet.

“Yeah.”

“The poor sod’s probably been running around the village all morning trying to find him,” she said with a scoff. “And honestly! Who lets a dog get out when it’s not even wearing a collar! It’s so irresponsible.”

She shook her head, her eyes darting towards the houses on either side of ours.

“Mmm,” I replied as I leaned down to stroke the dog again, knowing Mom didn’t really need an answer.

“You know, I bet it was number seventeen,” she decided. “You know what they’re like.”

They weren’t bad. Not really. Mom just didn’t like the family that lived at the end of our street, and I couldn’t remember when that had started or if she had an actual reason for her hatred. They seemed nice enough to me, but Mom always made little comments about them.

Apparently, they were lazy. That was one of the things Mom hated about them. They’d sometimes leave their wheelie bins out until the next morning, rather than bringing them in as soon as the rubbish had been collected. The dad works from home, she’d pointed out multiple times. He had no excuse not to do it. And they left their Christmas lights up until January. That was inexcusable too.

“Yeah, maybe,” I agreed.

They already had a dog, though. I’d seen the dad walking it fairly often. It was some kind of Labrador or golden retriever, though, and I wasn’t sure whether they’d get another. It seemed like kind of a handful.

“Alright,” Mom said. “I’ll go find an old towel to put on the seat in the car for the dog. You take the dog to the gate, and I’ll knock when I’m ready for you to bring him out. Keep hold of its scruff, and don’t let it run out into the road.”

I glanced down at the dog, suddenly nervous. I couldn’t remember ever even walking a dog with a leash, and I had no clue how to hold onto one without a collar. I’d definitely seen puppies get picked up by the loose skin on the back of their necks by their mom in cartoons, and I assumed that was what Mom meant for me to hold on to, but I had no idea how easy it would be or if it would hurt the dog. It didn’t seem like it did normally, but the cuts on the dog’s back might make it more painful.

Fear fluttered in my heart. The road was so close to our house. Cars often went down it too quickly, and if the puppy managed to get away from me, it could get to the road before I could catch up with it. It was so little. The cars might not even see it until it was too late.

The dog didn’t seem too energetic, though. I wasn’t sure whether it was the injuries or if it was normal for it, but I didn’t think the dog would try to run. I’d still hold onto it tightly, though.

“Okay,” I told Mom.

She examined me, her lips pursing again.

“Do you want me to hold him when I open the gate?” she asked.

The dog shuffled slightly, pressing more firmly against my legs, and I glanced down at it. As much as I wanted to do it myself, I knew Mom had more experience with dogs than I did, and she’d know how to hold it better than I would.

“Yeah, if that’s okay?”

“It’s fine,” she said before starting to turn back towards the house. “Alright, wait by the gate.”

I nodded and looked down at the dog again, but something else caught my eye.

“Oh, wait!” I called to Mom. “I’m not wearing my shoes. Can you grab them for me, please? And my socks? They’re in the kitchen.”

Mom glanced back over her shoulder at me.

“Why are your socks in the kitchen?”

“I put them there when I grabbed a drink earlier, but then I saw the dog and came out here,” I explained.

“Fine. Where are they?”

“Just on the counter,” I said. “By the sink.”

A disgusted expression flitted across Mom’s face, and she visibly recoiled.

“On the work surface?” she asked before adding, “Where we prepare food?”

“They’re clean.”

That didn’t seem to reassure Mom. She still looked just as disgusted as she had before.

“And? They go on your feet. That’s disgusting and unhygienic,” she snapped. “I’m going to have to disinfect the entire kitchen.”

I didn’t have a chance to respond to my mom. She stormed into the house, slamming the French window shut behind her, before I could say anything. I really didn’t think it was that bad, though. They’d been washed, so surely, they were clean. That was the whole point of washing things, wasn’t it?

Shaking my head slightly, I looked down at the dog again. It was staring up at me, its tail wagging gently.

“Come on, boy. Let’s go to the gate,” I said, cocking my head towards the gate before stopping. “Actually, are you a boy?”

The dog didn’t answer me, obviously. It just blinked at me slowly, and I shrugged at it before starting to walk again and patting my leg to get it to come with me. I kept accidentally calling it a boy, and so did Mom. I think it was because all of her dogs had always been male. I wasn’t sure if there was a reason for that, but my mind just defaulted to expect most dogs to be boys.

A smile grew on my face as the dog trotted alongside me towards the gate at the side of the house. I had no idea if it understood me at all or if it was just following me for no reason, but I felt weirdly proud that it was doing what I wanted, even though I had no idea what I was doing.

But the feeling was chased away far too soon as a thought occurred to me. If it understood me, did that mean it had been trained? Surely, it couldn’t just instinctively understand people, and if so, did that mean I was wrong? Did it actually have an owner who, like Mom said, had spent hours out searching for it?
