I blinked against the sunlight streaming through the slits in the carriage window, raising a hand to shield my eyes. A soft groan slipped out as I stretched, feeling as though I’d woken from a long, heavy nap.

Once my head cleared, I turned toward Dralos, still seated beside me. “How long?”

“Few minutes,” he said. When my narrowed eyes lingered, he quickly amended, “Less than five, Your Grace.”

Given I’d spent close to an hour, perhaps a little less, in Orpheus’ mindscape, that put the ratio at roughly five minutes in reality for every hour there.

I didn’t answer. Instead, I sighed, leaning my forehead against the carriage’s wooden side wall as I stared out the window.

I’d long suspected there were other worlds—how could I not, after reincarnating into one?—but the thought of a vast network of habitable planets had never crossed my mind.

What did that mean for me? For my goals?

Orpheus’ words left me feeling small. My ambitions, smaller still. If he spoke the truth—and that was far from certain—then Graedon and Ordite were nothing more than lesser worlds. And what did it matter to rule over a lesser world? Did it mean anything at all?

Even if I returned to Aedronir and reclaimed it from whoever sat on its throne now, what then? What if a portal like the one Nasq created opened between Ordite and a far stronger world? I couldn’t hope to defend the world. I couldn’t even hope to protect my kingdom. The truth settled over me like a weight I couldn’t shake. I was not, and had never been, someone of true power.

He had said a realm existed beyond diamond. How was that possible? Diamond was the limit. I knew that. I had reached diamond and had felt that bottleneck down to the depth of my soul. Even if I had not yet known about the ability to form a mana core and combine it with a heart core, that did not mean there was something beyond the diamond realm. It simply meant I could reach the realm twice in different ways.

But that was not what Orpheus had said. He had spoken of something greater.

What did he call it? True Convergence?

The thought clung to me. Orpheus’ words tangled themselves through my mind until I could not separate one implication from the next. My attention drifted so completely that I did not notice when we crossed out of the damp forest. Only when the carriage lurched back onto the main road, jolting me face-first into the wooden wall, did I return to the present.

An involuntary “oof” slipped out.

Dralos, though he knew full well I could not have been hurt by something so minor, sprang to his feet and seized my shoulders, concern twisting his features. “My lady, are you alr—”

I pushed him back with an irritated grimace. “Sit down, Dralos.” A flicker of chagrin passed through me as the draconian obeyed, especially when I saw the long crack running down the wall. A faint outline of my face was etched into the wood.

Through the window, Marcus sat rigid in his saddle, staring ahead as though the road were the most fascinating thing in the world. My glare promised trouble if he dared comment.

He wisely kept his gaze fixed forward.

The rest of the afternoon passed with me staring out at the countryside, my thoughts returning again and again to what Orpheus had told me about the System and the network of worlds.

A small, persistent idea gnawed at the back of my mind. Hours later, it shoved itself to the forefront.

If the worlds were connected through the Main System, could they also be connected through my Desire System? That was not just possible.

It felt inevitable.

The sun finally slipped beneath the horizon, and I dragged myself out of the long, quiet stupor I had sunk into.

Enough, Lilith. You can think about that after you’ve handled this world and your own business.

In any case, assuming Orpheus spoke the truth, that kind of goal was far beyond anything my current power could hope to reach.

I clenched a fist and thumped my thigh, forcing my thoughts back to the present. Besides, Orpheus was a progenitor. Every other word that left his mouth, unless bound by our agreement, could have been a lie. I had no way to prove or disprove his stories.

I slid the carriage window open. It moved soundlessly, one panel swinging outward, and a rush of fresh air swept in to sting my senses.

The forest was far behind us now, but stray clusters of trees still dotted the roadside, tangled with low shrubs and stubborn undergrowth I barely glanced at. As the city drew nearer, those remnants of greenery gave way to Elyndor’s broad, open plains. The land had once been heavily wooded, but I had ordered it cleared months ago as a defensive measure, stripping away cover for any enemy force.

Over the steady rush of wind came a familiar voice calling my name. Nida was a blur of black and white fur, sprinting toward the carriage, her golden feline eyes bright with excitement. Whatever she shouted was swallowed by the thunderous roar of Vespera overhead. The dragon’s wings beat the air into heavy gusts that rattled the carriage, each downstroke pitching her vast shadow across our entire column. Her long, sinuous body rippled with muscle as she flew, the movement fluid and naturally predatory. Obsidian scales gleamed like polished onyx, catching the dusk light with flashes of shifting color.

“Welcome back,” Vespera’s voice murmured in my mind through our link. “You missed many battles. When will we battle together once more?”

I laughed and lifted a hand in greeting. “Fear not, my dragon. Soon enough, there shall be many great battles to fight.”

The carriage rumbled as Vespera let out another powerful roar, her excitement palpable.

Nida slowed as she reached us, turned on her heels, and then matched pace with the carriage. “How was your trip, my lady?” she asked, grinning wide enough to show her sharp teeth. She had grown taller since I’d last seen her, nearly matching my own height now.

I ran my tongue over my upper teeth, considering. “It was… interesting.”

She arched a brow. “Interesting? Did you find anything?”

I gestured toward the carriage trailing behind us, where Raphael still lay unconscious. “A few things. We can debrief later.” It was not that I distrusted Nida—every knight here already knew most of the story—but I had no intention of discussing Raphael or the Doctrine where curious ears might overhear. Those curious could wait for my audience with the King. He would summon me soon enough to present the Doctrine in court.

“How’s the March?” I asked, leaning out to spot Ethan near the gates, towering over the soldiers beside him. “By the Gods, has Ethan grown taller?”

Nida laughed, then lowered her voice so only I could hear. “The System gave him a bloodline that makes him grow every dozen levels… or something like that.” She glanced toward the carriage door, opened it with a shove, and slipped inside as if we weren’t moving at all. Taking the seat opposite me, she continued, “The March is going as planned. We’ve brought back all our assigned targets.”

I nodded, lifting a hand before she could add more. “Later.”

Dralos took the chance to speak. “We discovered the Duchess’ brother. He was in bad shape. I am surprised her grace recognized him at all.”

Nida’s eyes narrowed, her lips curling into a half-formed snarl. “Which brother?” she spat.

I motioned for her to settle. “One of the Silverwater siblings. The eldest. Raphael.”

She blinked. “What in the Goddess’ name was he doing there? Wasn’t the city destroyed?”

“That is what I am wondering as well,” Dralos said.

“We’ll find out when he wakes.”

The carriage jolted as it halted at the city gates. The guards performed a brisk inspection, their eyes flicking from the wheels to the interior. The moment they saw me, they bowed low. One appeared so terrified I half-expected him to lose control of his bladder. I offered a wry smile and a nod. The inspection ended in seconds after that.

We rolled on into Elyndor without any more obstacles.

The city sprawled ahead in the fading light, white stone walls and tiled roofs glowing with the warm afterglow of sunset. As the knights hauled us through cobbled streets toward the duchy’s spires, the signs of recovery stood plain for all to see.

The residential quarters had come first. I had argued for the walls to be rebuilt before anything else, but the sheer number of displaced citizens made that impossible. Who would spend long hours on the ramparts when they had no home to return to?

So houses rose from the rubble first. Lodgings for returning soldiers followed. Streets of shops and open markets reappeared, sprouting from the scars of ruin like stubborn weeds through cracked stone. It was not glamorous work, especially with the number of corpses that had to be removed, but everyone contributed. People needed shelter. Children needed schools. Merchants needed stalls beneath which to haggle and trade.

Some had fled to districts spared from the Hydra’s rampage, but those areas had been overcrowded even before the attack.

Only after the homes came the public buildings. The courts and lower administrative halls were rebuilt first, the few surviving temples last. I had nearly refused to allow them at all, and I certainly forbade any expansion. Yet in the end, I agreed. After all the city had endured, even a false refuge could still be a place where some found peace.

Only after the city had resumed breathing and its walls were rebuilt enough to repel an attack did I turn the workforce toward the noble towers and the parts of the ducal spires that had been damaged. Most still stood, the Duke's Spire most of all, but those with weaker magical protection had fallen. Many nobles claimed it was disrespectful to the centuries of prestige carried by the Alistar Ducal House to delay restoring the historic spires. I disagreed. There was no point in rebuilding the towers if no one remained to stand beneath them.

The aristocracy had not been pleased, but I had summarily told them to fuck off.

When I had left, the ducal spires were still under construction. Now, at last, they stood again, taller than before, sharper, less ornamental, and far more fortified against future conflicts. A declaration of resilience rather than a hollow statement of status.

Flags bearing my personal sigil fluttered at the highest points. I could not help but grin at the memories of Graedon the sight brought to mind. The banners were black, stained with silver crescent moons at their center, a diagonal red line slicing from upper left to lower right.

Just below them hung the ducal flags of House Alistar, a golden lion on a field of red.

Something stirred in my chest, both warm and hollow.

Home.

Or something close to it.
