Carian approached the same river with a deep frown. She wanted to keep her distance from Hayden’s team, lulling the group into a false sense of security, whereby his team knew they were being followed, but grew accustomed to it. If that worked, she could prevent mind games after two or three days. Unfortunately, things took a wrong turn when they came to the river and learned four of the seven continued upriver, and the other three were missing.

“This place’s been brushed clean,” Carian commented, noting how wind magic had covered footprints around the river. “You see anything?”

Pan shook her head, too. “It’s brushed perfectly. I mean, if there was something there, Celia can’t find it.”

Celia was the name of her tracking spirit.

“It’s eerie,” she added.

“It is,” Carian muttered. She wiped the sweat under her eye as she scanned the area, wishing it were a little cooler outside. Upon deeper examination, she saw that flowers were glistening up the river, moving toward the purple mountain to the northeast, which could indicate that they walked east up the river. That could be plausible—if the team didn’t start by going west. There were strange mind games going on.

Carian focused on the purple mountain. It was too eerie. Having an entire area filled with only purple flora, trees, and fauna was deeply unnatural. Whatever was underneath the soil was dangerous.

Carian turned to Pan. “Go check out where the footprints lead to.”

“Yes, ma’am.”

Pan crossed the river to see where the four footprint sets went. She returned five minutes later. “They’re moving east.”

“How far?”

“Celia says it’s at least a few hundred meters, but it’s murky. They’re good at hiding their tracks.”

Carian studied the area around them. There was no indication of Hayden's team having ever been there. She looked upriver again.

“We should survey up top,” Caro said, “play it off like they’re a runner. I doubt they’ll actually kill one of us. No one wants to start a war with the Sickles.”

Mot and the other wings stiffened.

“Unless they have four militaries on their side,” Carian countered, easing the stress. “We’re in a rift. If there’s conflict, the RDA will show up, and when they do, they’ll claim we attacked them. It’s not a hard sell when we broke the law to follow them.”

“So what? We just split up our team?” Breaker examined their team. “We can compete if we’re together, but we’re screwed if we’re split up.”

That was true. Hayden’s team was likely composed exclusively of A-rank rifters. By contrast, Carian’s team was a reserve team. There were three A-rank, three Bs, and fourteen C-ranks. Of the C-ranks, half were runners or support roles. This wasn’t a skill squad—it was an overpowered scouting party. If they split up, Hayden could overwhelm them with ease.

“That doesn’t matter,” Carian said. “We’re not here to fight them. We’re here to protect them. It wouldn’t hurt to be less threatening and make contact and tell them our situation.” She turned to Breaker. “Take Haasa and move east up the river. We’ll cross and follow the tracks east for about five miles. If you spot their tracks, send a sky signal. If you don’t, give two and we’ll wait for you to return.”

“Got it,” Breaker said.

Haasa nodded. “Understood.”

The A-B-rank pair walked upriver, searching for tracks as the rest followed Pan across the water. Pan’s tracking spirit skillfully led them through areas that didn’t even appear to have footsteps.

“I can’t even tell that there’s steps here,” Caro commented.

“They have a skilled brusher. You can tell because the plant stems are too exposed.” She plucked a plant to show that the bottom was yellow. “They’ve been using wind magic to destroy their tracks, but you can only brush so hard before it rips up plants or makes it obvious in other ways. But this one’s capable of spreading dirt across the forest’s floor without gusts, and not rattling trees. It’s crazy.”

“It’s just a reminder who we’re up against,” Carian said. “Don’t forget it.”

It was a steep hike up the mountain. The further they went, the steeper the incline became. Yet no one paid it much attention. As superhumans, they could hike twenty miles uphill and be fine. They could even jog, but didn’t. At any moment, they could find themselves fighting massive beasts and bugs, and birds of all shapes and grotesque descriptions. Rifts weren’t a place to run around haphazardly. Countless rifters died teaching that to those who survived.

Carian moved from idle thoughts back to Breaker. They should’ve sent a signal by now, she thought. Just as she was thinking that, Hayden’s tracks diverted north—away from the river.

Carian clicked her tongue and looked at Pan. “How far north do they go?”

“At least four hundred meters.”

“Run that far, see if it keeps going, then return.”

“Right.” Pan unclipped her backpack and ran up the hill with superhuman speed. She returned shortly after. “They’re going east again. If we go half way up, I can track them without moving too far from the river.”

Carian considered it and nodded. “It’s two hundred yards. We’ll walk one more mile. After that, we’ll make contact with Breaker.” The others nodded and followed her, becoming nervous as they navigated around obstacles.

Suddenly, a crack of thunder came from the south. Carian waited for a second. It didn’t come.

“So he’s spotted them, huh?” she muttered.

“What now?” Caro asked.

She reflected on it.

“I saw a cliff in this general direction,” Mot said. He flew upward before almost getting shot down. “If we hurry up, we’ll be able to assess the situation from uptop.”

“Good call.” The team continued upward with a brisk pace, but abruptly stopped when Pan’s face turned deathly pale.

“What?” Carian snapped.

“It’s the footsteps…”

“Don’t whisper! Communicate fast and effectively!”

“There’s…” Pan swallowed. “Seven pairs of footsteps. They’ve been piggybacking.”

“Oh no…” Carian muttered. “Breaker!”

-

Breaker didn’t know when things started going wrong. All he knew was that he didn’t know…

Something was wrong. He didn’t know what was wrong. He couldn’t even remember what he was supposed to be doing. He tried to shoot three lightning bolts for some reason, but only shot one, and then forgot why he did it. It seemed important, so he kept remembering it before losing it.

He looked at Haasa and found her face warping—becoming bigger and smaller in areas as if she were underneath a glass of water. “Hey…” he said.

Haasa looked at him. “What?”

“Uh…” Breaker tried to ask about the lightning, but nothing came to mind, so he turned his gaze to the ground. There was something strange going on, but it was hard to feel that way. The weather was nice, the water was calming, and the flowers…

The flowers were… glowing.

He giggled, crouching to examine the flowers. They ballooned and contracted, becoming big and small, sharp and round. They were delightful.

But wrong. There was definitely something wrong with…

Flowers.

There were beautiful flowers in front of him. He couldn’t remember when he started looking at the flowers, but they were very pretty. Glorious, even.

They matched the warm weather and soothing sound of the river. He wanted one, so he picked the one closest to him. As he lifted it, golden pollen glimmered out of it like pixie dust. He giggled.

A distant voice called out. “What are you doing?”

He tilted his head and saw Haasa. Her entire face was morphing into a formless blend of color and brown mercury. There was something terribly wrong. He had to tell her…

Breaker blinked and suddenly saw Haasa. He didn’t know when she got there, but he laughed when he saw her face. It was suddenly so clear and visible, while the world around her had distorted. He thought that was the funniest thing, so he laughed at that too. It suddenly became really funny—the thought of giving flowers to the one woman he had eyes for.

Flowers.

He had just seen some flowers.

He looked at the flower in his hand and then realized ones similar to it were everywhere. So he reached down with both hands and picked about twenty of them and offered them to Haasa.

“No… Keep those away…” Haasa said. She didn’t want them, but a moment later, her eyes widened, and she accepted them with a bright smile.

That’s right—he was charming.

If it were anyone else, she wouldn’t have taken them. Then it hit him—

He couldn’t remember who he was.

He tried to ask the woman, but she suddenly disappeared, blending into swirling spirals and kaleidoscopes. He suddenly felt sick and fell onto his hands and knees. He fell right into the flowers, and golden dust erupted like a landmine. He sneezed, finding it hard to breathe with that dust in his lungs. It was making them expand and swell and push against his lungs.

Water. He needed water.

There was water. He remembered…

What?

What was he…

Water. He needed water.

The primal need for water was enough to keep remembering, so he crawled to the side of the river—and fell into the water.
