Eola glared at the door as another knock disturbed her work.

“Are you coming to classes today?” Patrice asked.

“I wasn’t planning on it, no.” Eola tore her eyes away from the door and back to her desk.

“What are you doing for food?” Colin asked. “You’ve been in there for a while, and it’s breakfast time.”

Was it all of them this time? “I’m managing,” she said, ignoring her aching stomach. The work was far too important to let her friends interrupt her. She was close. She had to be close. Atta hadn’t been any help at all, but even so, she’d made progress. Just a few more hours, and—

“You’re not really managing, Eola,” Colin said. “You keep saying that, but you’re not.”

“This is ridiculous,” Roth muttered. It was all of them! “I’m coming in there and dragging you out.”

“Wait!”

But before she could even stand up, the door flew open, and her friends stared in horror at her—and at her mess.

Eola Lemiene hadn’t eaten in three days, with the exception of cold leftovers from dinner on the night she’d made her breakthrough. She’d stretched that out as long as she could, but leaving her room meant interrupting her work, her train of thought, and her tenuous understanding of the parchment haphazardly strewn around the room. The crumbs of that meal sat on her unmade bedsheets. She was covered in ink and paper cuts, and her hair resembled a rat’s nest.

And as for the parchment…it was everywhere. The only uncovered surfaces in the whole room were the chair she sat in and a small space for Atta on the desk. Sketches of possible modifying marks using the trog shaman’s incomplete one. A mound of scorched, burnt paper—the result of backlash when she applied those marks to cantrips in hopes of a result. Stacks of it on her pillow and blanket. Eola hadn’t had time for sleep, and her room was so small that her discarded experiments only had one place to go.

She yawned loudly, trying and failing to cover her mouth. Atta blinked at her slowly, then yawned herself.

Colin spoke first. “You…haven’t washed up in a while, have you, Eola?”

“Why would you think that?” Eola asked, waving at the—to her at least—perfectly organized chaos.

“It smells like Latrine Catrine’s magic in here, that’s why. Actually, most of it’s you. Some of it smells like…old fish…though,” Patrice said.

“Y’aer save us. Eola, you’ve been ‘managing’ by eating three-day-old leftovers?” Colin asked.

“Oh.” Eola ignored Colin and sniffed herself, wrinkled her nose, and flushed a deep red as the full extent of her situation sank in. “Oh.”

“Oh indeed,” Patrice said primly, channeling every second of her training as a noblewoman. “Get freshened up. You’ve got fifteen minutes. We’ll wait that long, then we’re going to breakfast. And you are coming to your classes today. All of them—especially Mana Studies. Today’s the last day before exams, remember?”

“Y’aer curse it! I completely forgot!”

Eola’s friends scattered as she, covered in ink and wearing the same robes she’d been in when she first locked herself in her room, made a mad dash for the suite’s washroom.

Eola’s last day of classes before exams was a blur of information.

Madame Reyanna didn’t even acknowledge that she’d been missing for the last three sessions, which was nice. In fact, the tiny gnomish professor hardly even looked her way before diving right into her lecture. “We’ve covered Mana purification fairly extensively. Ideally, none of you should need more than that for the library exam. You’ll only be in there for so long, and we professors will be teaming up with the librarians to secure the testing area so nothing too outrageous finds you.

“That being said…things do go wrong. Ideally, the professors and librarians will be on hand to manage that, too.”

“But if they’re not?” Catrine asked. She seemed nervous, but there was something else there, too. She stared straight ahead at the empty chalkboard.

“If they’re not, and you find your soul stretched, purification is slow. It will work, but you may need more purified, attuned Mana than it can provide, more quickly than it can provide it. In that case, this technique may save your life.” Madame Reyanna turned, hand on the bottom rung of her ladder, and stared straight at the class, a serious scowl on her face. “It will put you in the hospital, however, and Instructor Clearance will have some questions about why you had to use it. Don’t think of it as a free pass to victory.”

As the professor climbed her ladder and began sketching the rune, Eola dutifully copied it into her spellbook. She wouldn’t have time to develop it into a viable spell chain, but the parts were familiar. It was a First Order spell, a jumble of Emerald Script and Old Alemic, with the Personal, Hasty, Echoing, and Stilled modifying marks arrayed around it, along with a single open space for an attunement modifier.

“Do not attempt to cast this except in an emergency or in a controlled setting—preferably a dungeon cage with a healer nearby.”

Eola examined the rune she’d just drawn. Then she nodded slowly. The four modifying marks took the tangle of messy language and forced it all inward, over and over, as quickly as possible. It looked profoundly uncomfortable, like being stuffed full of too much unrisen bread.

“This spell reverses the flow of Mana, temporarily overwhelming your soul’s natural Mana cycle in favor of quickly refilling it with Mana matching your attunement. That reversal will cause damage to your arms and legs, manifesting as bright blue discolorations of the veins and arteries and widespread bruising as those vessels rupture.” Madame Reyanna sounded formal and scripted. Eola paid closer attention, and, sure enough, the professor had an actual script for this part. “Please pass these sketches of said injuries around. Hopefully, that will drive the point home; Soul Bleeding is an emergency option only.”

No one practiced the spell. Not a single student even started to sketch it outside of their spellbooks. Madame Reyanna’s room was silent as the sketches slowly made their way around the class, and by the end of it, Eola was convinced.

Soul Bleed was not something she’d ever need to cast, and she’d do her best to keep her friends from using it, but if they’d had it, Colin could have been much more effective against the trogs.

Colin was quiet the whole walk to Introduction to Monsters. He was probably thinking the same thing.

Instructor Clearance’s class had moved out of the greenhouse and into a grove of yew trees. Today’s lecture was a review of the library’s third-floor threats, from Srillas to Dalyn/Davek pairs. They even ran through the common deterrents for Mana Mites, then spent the last fifteen minutes of the class discussing the Scholar’s Cult.

“The librarians haven’t heard anything from the cult in almost a month,” the hamadryad said as time ran down, “but all that means is that they’re likely planning something. Be on your guard. Mr. Tremory, Miss Lemiene, please come see me. The rest of you, you’re dismissed, and good luck with your exams—especially the library exam.”

Eola and Colin glanced at each other. He shrugged and walked down the makeshift aisle between root-woven benches, and Eola followed. Instructor Clearance nodded as he arrived. “Mr. Tremory, please let Maestro Yarrowbloom know that Miss Lemiene may be late to Magical Dueling. She and I have some business to discuss.”

“Yes, ma’am.” Colin hurried away, glancing over his shoulder as he went.

“Miss Lemiene, it’s been a while since I had an update on the monster you encountered before midterms. In fact, I haven’t heard anything in months, either from the librarians or from my contacts at the other magic schools. No one knows anything. Most of them are pretty confident that you encountered a feral book, but I have my doubts.”

“I do, too,” Eola said quietly. She’d…not forgotten about the monster, exactly, but she’d had a lot on her mind, and her self-given task of reassembling Varin’s notebook had fallen by the wayside.

“In any case, it’s unusual that no one would know anything about it. These are the authors of the bestiaries I study from. I trust their work implicitly, and yet, I can’t shake the feeling that they’re wrong. Whatever you found in the library is something new, and the fact that the librarians haven’t encountered it only makes me more convinced that it’s dangerous. Be careful in the library, in case it remembers you.”

“Thank you. I will be,” Eola said quietly. She hesitated. “And the man from the Scholar’s Cult? Eddarn? You haven’t heard anything about him, either?”

“No. I checked the school’s records, and there was a student with that name, but he graduated fifteen years ago. He would have been a year or two younger than Instructor Tarik. I believe he’s a spell-sword, sworn to one of the noble houses.”

Eola thanked the dryad professor again, then hurried off across the mud and snow. But when she arrived at the gymnasium where Maestro Yarrowbloom typically taught the Taron-Li’s dance, she only found an uncomfortable-looking Colin and a note on the door.

“We’re supposed to meet at the smithy in Varin’s Town,” Colin said. “You weren’t that far behind me. What’d Instructor Clearance want?”

“Just to give me some advice about my last trip to the third floor, and how it might affect this one,” Eola said. “We’ve already covered most of it. I’ll tell you more when we’re all together again.”

“Huh.” Colin went quiet, then started walking. “Let’s not be too late. I think Yarrowbloom’s got something interesting for us.”

“Or it’s another lesson on how to survive the third floor,” Eola added.

“That’s interesting!”

Eola couldn’t help but agree, but even so, she was starting to get bored with the lectures. Her spell-work was waiting in her room, and she was so close to figuring it out. That was how she’d help her friends with the library exam. Not through knowing different monsters’ breeding habits or being able to quickly purify Mana.

Through her own spell-work.

Maestro Yarrowbloom wore a leather apron, and gloves covered his hands, when Eola and Colin arrived at the only blacksmith’s shop in Varin’s Town. Nails, shovel heads, and parts of cart yokes covered the shelves; the place looked like it did a brisk business with the locals.

But Eola noticed a conspicuous lack of two metals. There was no chromite to be seen, and the only silver in the entire place was a pair of smallswords. One hung from the elven professor’s waist, while the other sat on the dirt and ash in front of him. It had been snapped in two, leaving a jagged edge about a foot from the hilt, and its ensorcelment was broken, too.

The professor wasn’t alone, either. A gnome, around the same height as Madame Reyanna or Ursula—the Chubby Saber’s cook—stood nearby, a hammer resting head-first on the ground at his feet. His green hands were meshed over the oak haft, and his eyes were closed.

“Today, we leave behind the Taron-Li to discuss what happens when you can no longer dance it,” the professor was saying. Eola relaxed; they hadn’t missed too much of the lecture.

“You die, right?” one of the girls who was always riding Catrine’s coattails asked.

“That’s the usual result,” Maestro Yarrowbloom agreed. “However, in your upcoming examination, you will have a team to rely on. There’s every reason to believe they’ll be able to cut in and buy you time to rejoin on your own terms. Injuries are beyond the scope of this class. Simple ones can be healed with cantrips, and if you want to learn how to treat life-threatening ones, Instructor Clearance has a course for second-year students. Half a term of theory, half a term of work in the hospital to get the practice, and you’ll be a functional field medic.”

Colin nodded. He licked his lips, and Eola rolled her eyes. He would want to work with the dryad more; she was the best ritualist at Varin’s Academy.

“But injuries aren’t the only thing that can go wrong out there.” Maestro Yarrowbloom gestured at the short, green-skinned man standing next to him. “This is Smith Parunet. He owns this shop, and he’s an expert on building and forging with metal.”

The gnome nodded. His eyes stayed closed. “Ayup.”

“How long would it take you to reforge this sword, Smith?” the professor asked.

“For a simple weld? An hour, plus heating time for the forge, plus cooling time to get the glow down. Silver on steel is tricky. Different melting points. To put a good edge on after the forging? A few hours more.”

“And to maintain the ensorcellment?”

The smith shook his head. “More time than any of you’d believe. Be easier to reforge it and re-ensorcel it.”

“One last question. How long will your work last?” Yarrowbloom asked.

Now, the green-skinned man opened his eyes. “Longer than the rest of the blade, most likely.”

“Thank you.” The professor turned back to his class. “Smith Parunet has trained his entire life. He’s an expert with his attunement, even as a Journeymage, and his work is highly requested by spellswords and nobles alike. That’s why, as some of you mentioned earlier, there’s not a scrap of silver or chromite in this shop. He sells it all as soon as it comes in. I won’t try to teach you to do what he does. Instead, I’ll teach you how to get back into the dance as quickly as you can, because speed just might save your life.”

For the rest of the class, Maestro Yarrowbloom taught three First Order runes. The first heated metal slowly, while the second forced two pieces of silvered steel into the optimal alignment with each other. The third, a mess of Forsooth Script and Old Alemic, bound the metal together while rapidly cooling it.

As he cast it, Parunet’s eyes darkened. “Shoddy work.”

“It is. It absolutely is,” Yarrowbloom agreed. “This weapon is dull, weak, and prone to breaking at the same point. It’s a far cry from the sword at my hip. However, its tip’s still sharp enough to kill monsters, its reach is still long enough to strike before you’re struck, and it’s still ensorcelled. It will serve until you can bring it to someone like Smith Parunet or Lannith in the library for a proper repair job.”

Then, before anyone could move, Maestro Yarrowbloom broke the sword over his knee. Eola jumped at the sharp crack as the magicked-together bond broke. “Your turn, students. Practice makes perfect.”

Another note awaited Eola, Colin, Patrice, and Roth when they arrived at the classroom Instructor Tarik used for their apprenticeship.

Apprentices,

I will be unable to attend our meeting today. In lieu of a proper lesson, please treat yourselves to dinner in Varin’s Town. Your next two days will be quite hectic, and the four of you are due a chance to take a breather.

- Tarik

There were also a half-dozen coins on the desk.

Come to think of it, Eola hadn’t attended a single one of her grading sessions in the last three days, and the professor hadn’t hunted her down, or even sent a note with one of the other students. Had he? No, Eola would have remembered something like that.

“He’s still missing?” Roth asked, almost sounding angry about it.

“Still?” Eola asked.

“Yes, he’s still missing,” Patrice said. “He hasn’t been teaching his classes, either.”

“Well, if he’s not here, I have very important work to—ow!” Eola rubbed her arm right where Patrice had pinched it. “What was that for?”

“Absolutely not. You’ve eaten one meal in the last four days, Eola.”

“Two. I’ve eaten two…”

“Old fish doesn’t count, and the professor is right. You need time away from your room, and we need food that’s not from the cafeteria.” Patrice nodded sagely. “I’m thinking the Chubby Saber.”

“Seconded,” Colin said.

Before Eola could resist, Patrice had her by one arm, and Colin had her by the other. They marched her out of the classroom and down the hall, and by the time she struggled free, the aroma of Cook Ursula’s never-ending stew pot was all she could think about.

She didn’t try to escape as the tiny woman put a bowl in front of her. “Chives? You put chives in it?” she asked immediately, without even tasting it.

Ursula nodded, looking almost sad. “You’re too good, girl. I’m going to have to order from herbalists if you keep this up.”

Eola laughed. “Not the sumac! Anything but the sumac!”

“Yes, the sumac,” Cook Ursula said jokingly.

And as Eola brought the spoon to her lips and tasted the piping-hot stew, she decided that her friends were right. The work would be there when she got back to her room, but Ursula’s stew needed to be enjoyed now.
