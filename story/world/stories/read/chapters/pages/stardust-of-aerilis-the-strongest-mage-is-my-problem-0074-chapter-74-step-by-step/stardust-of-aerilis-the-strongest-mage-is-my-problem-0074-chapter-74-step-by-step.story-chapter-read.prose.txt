Aiven held her hand.

He did not squeeze. He did not stroke. He simply rested his palm against the back of hers, with his fingers curled lightly around the side of her smaller hand, and he waited.

He had nothing to say yet. Nothing that would not be too small for the moment. So he waited.

He waited while she finished crying. He waited while the shudder that had been moving through her shoulders quieted down to a slow, steady breath. He waited while she lifted the back of her free wrist to her eyes and wiped at them with the clumsy, ineffective motion of a person not used to crying. He waited while a few last tears found their way down to her chin, and while she made a small sound that was almost a laugh, the kind a person makes when they realize they are still alive after saying something they thought might kill them.

When she had grown still, he reached up with his other hand.

Slowly. So she could see him doing it. He brushed his thumb under her eye, catching the last of the wetness on the soft skin there. Then the other eye. The motion was careful. Almost the way someone might handle a piece of paper they did not want to tear.

Virelle held very still while he did it.

Her eyes stayed on his. They were red at the rims.

“Thank you,” he said.

His voice was quiet. He meant it.

“Thank you for being honest with me. I needed to know all of that. I really did. I would not have been able to fix anything if you had let me keep walking into that wall in the dark.”

Virelle blinked at him.

Of all the things she had been bracing for, gratitude appeared not to have been one of them.

Aiven let out a small breath. He kept hold of her hand.

“There is nothing wrong with being selfish.”

He saw her open her mouth to argue and he raised his eyebrows just slightly, the way he sometimes used to do with a delivery clerk who was about to file an objection out of habit. She closed her mouth again.

“I mean it,” he said. “I was selfish earlier today. I told you so. I asked you to be honest with me, and I framed it as if it were for your benefit, but a lot of it was for mine. I was uncomfortable. I had felt the wall between us and it was making my chest tight and I wanted it to stop. So I sat you down on this bed and asked you to make it stop. That was an act of selfishness. I dressed it up as concern, but it was also that.”

He paused.

“Wanting things for yourself is not the failure, Virelle. Everyone does that. The wanting is just... part of what we are.”

He shifted slightly on the bed, turning a little more to face her.

“What matters,” he said carefully, “is what we do with the wanting. How we handle it. I am no expert on this. I have never been good at the part of being a person where you have to manage your own feelings before they start managing you. But I think one of the things you do, is exactly the thing you just did. You tell someone close to you. You say it out loud. You let it sit in the room with another person so that it stops being the only thing in your head.”

He looked down at their joined hands.

“If you had kept all of that inside you, Virelle, it would have eaten you. Slowly. From the inside out. I have seen it before. I did it to myself, after Hearthport. I sat in my apartment for days not telling a soul what I felt, and what I felt grew teeth in there. It grew teeth, and it learned to chew on me, and by the end of those two weeks I was barely a person anymore.”

He lifted his eyes again.

“You did not let it eat you. You told me. That is not nothing. That is the opposite of nothing.”

Virelle’s lower lip trembled. She did not say anything.

Aiven went on.

“I am not in a position to tell you what to do with what you are feeling. I would not know how to. I am barely managing my own feelings about the same situation. But I can tell you this.”

He held her gaze.

“I do not blame you. For any of it. Not for the jealousy, not for the envy, not for wanting Lyra gone, not for being glad when Lucien took her. Not even for grabbing my wrist for the reason you said. I heard all of it. I am not glad to have heard it, but I am not running from the room either. I am here. I am still here. And I am going to keep being here.”

He drew in a breath.

“The thing with Lucien comes first. We have six days. I have to be ready by the end of them. That has to come first. I am sorry that it has to come first. I wish I had time to sit with this conversation properly. I wish I could give you a week of evenings to talk it all through.”

He gave her hand the smallest squeeze.

“But after this is over. After Lucien is dealt with, and we are not on a clock anymore. I am going to think things through. Properly. I am going to be honest with myself about what I feel, about you, about Lyra, about all of it. And then I am going to be honest with you. I will not leave any of us guessing.”

He paused.

Then, more quietly:

“Also. About one thing.”

He met her eyes carefully.

“You are not a burden.”

Virelle’s breath caught.

“I know that word,” he said. “I used to use it about myself. It is not a good word, Virelle. It is not a clean word. It is the kind of word that builds a small dark room in your head and then locks you inside it. I would prefer that you not use it on yourself. Especially not in front of me. Especially not when you have just done a very hard thing by telling me the truth.”

He gave her a small, tired smile.

Virelle looked at him for a long moment.

Her voice, when it came, was almost a whisper.

“Will you really,” she said, “always be by my side, Master? Even if I become more selfish than this? Even if there is worse than this in me?”

Aiven did not look away.

“As long as you keep being honest with me,” he said, “yes.”

He let that sit for a moment.

“I am not promising it will always be easy. I am not promising I will always know what to say. There are probably going to be more nights like this. There will probably be nights worse than this. I am not great at this kind of thing. I will say the wrong thing, sometimes. I will get something wrong, I am sure of it. But if you keep telling me what is in your head, then I think we can figure it out, step by step. We can get better at this together. Both of us.”

He drew in a breath.

“And I am going to need you to do the same for me. I am going to mess up, Virelle. Probably soon. Probably in some way I do not see coming. And when I do, I am going to need you to stay. I am going to need you to be on my side too.”

Virelle did not speak for a moment.

Her free hand came up, slowly, and rested against his chest, just below the collarbone. She felt the steady rhythm of his heart through the thin cotton of his shirt. Then, with a small, half-laughed breath that was the closest thing to a smile she had managed all evening, she leaned forward and put her arms around his neck.

“I will,” she said, into the curve of his shoulder. “Always. Master. No matter what.”

Aiven’s arms came up around her.

He held her carefully. One hand settled flat against the small of her back. The other came up to rest at the nape of her neck, just under the fall of her hair. She was warm. She was small in his arms. Her prismatic orb had drifted close and was hovering at the level of his shoulder, pulsing softly, as if it too were waiting to see if everything in the room was going to be all right.

He turned his face slightly so his cheek rested against her hair.

“I want you to know something,” he said quietly.

Virelle did not move.

“No matter what happens. With Lucien. With Lyra. With any of it. You are important to me, Virelle. I do not have the words for it tonight. I do not have time to find them tonight. But I want you to know it before you fall asleep. Before tomorrow. Before any more days pass with you sitting beside me and wondering. You are important to me.”

He felt her hand tighten very slightly against the back of his shirt.

“I know,” she said softly.

And then, even more softly:

“I know, Master.”

She closed her eyes.

Her cheek rested against the warm cotton of his shirt. The two of them sat there, on the edge of the small bed in the small cabin on the small ship, in the lantern-light, and listened to each other’s heartbeats. His was steady. Hers was a little faster. After a few minutes, they began to fall into something close to the same rhythm.

Outside the porthole, the cloud-cover had gone fully black.

Somewhere in the dark, a single night-bird called once, low and patient, and went quiet again.

Morning came gently to the islet.

The clouds below the cliff had thinned overnight, and a soft, pale gold spilled across the grass by the time Aiven and Virelle came down the ramp of the scout-ship. The air smelled faintly of salt and the burnt edges of yesterday’s training. Pelka was already at the rail with two mugs of something hot, which she handed off with a small bow and the muttered, anxious well-wishing of a person who had decided that her job today was to be useful and very, very quiet.

Aiven took a sip. Tea, strong enough to file paperwork by.

He nodded his thanks.

By the time they had reached the scorched half-circle of grass from the previous day, Virelle was already drifting ahead of him, hands clasped behind her back.

“We shall warm with bolts,” she announced. “Half an hour. Not because you need the warming, Master. Because if you stride into more advanced exercises with cold conduits, I’m worried that something stupid might happen and you would be down in the dumps for the day.”

Aiven looked at her.

It was a small thing. The smallest of things, really. The little remark, the affectionate-insult shape of it, the way she lifted her chin a quarter-degree on the word stupid. The faint sniff at the end of it.

She was trying.

He could see it. She was not all the way back. There was still a careful edge to how she held herself, a softness in her voice she usually did not bother with. But she was trying, and the trying was the warmest thing he had felt in two days.

He raised his hand toward the open sky.

“Yes, my brilliant and merciful instructor.”

She sniffed.

He fired.

The bolts came easier today.

It was as if his body had spent the night quietly figuring out something his mind had not been able to teach it. The first bolt left his palm like an exhale. The second, smoother still. By the time he had cycled through twenty in a row, the apple-sized rounds were not just consistent, they had taken on a small, faint hum at the moment of release, a tone he could feel in his teeth, the way Marnie had once described.

Virelle drifted closer at the fifteenth.

“Your stance has improved overnight,” she observed. “Which is impossible. So either I am wrong, which is extremely unlikely, or your body is learning while you sleep, which is mildly disturbing.”

“I vote impossible,” Aiven said.

He almost smiled.

After the half hour, she lifted a hand.

“Enough bolts. We are moving on.”

Aiven lowered his arm and shook the heat out of his fingers. “On to what?”

“Elemental magic.”

He blinked.

Virelle drifted around to face him, her hands clasped at the small of her back, the prismatic orb floating in a slow, satisfied loop around her shoulder.

“Fire,” she said. “Water. Lightning. Wind. Earth. The five common branches of elemental conjuration. We will not have time to properly teach you all of them, of course.”

She raised a single finger.

“So we shall start with lightning and earth. They are the simplest to grasp at speed, and the most immediately useful.”

Aiven thought about that for a moment.

“Come to think of it,” he said, “I almost never see you use elemental magic.”

Virelle’s chin lifted by a familiar quarter-degree.

“Correct.”

“Why?”

“Because there is no need to. Unless a situation specifically calls for an elemental effect, the kind of magic I prefer is more than sufficient to erase most enemies. Why fashion an elaborate spear of ice when a beam of pure mana will accomplish the same erasure faster, cleaner, and with significantly less posing?”

She paused.

“Although I will admit. The posing can be a pleasure.”

Aiven thought about it.

Mana-form magic. That must be what she called it, the kind of magic she had been using since the moment she had been summoned. The slashes. The beams. The spears. The shields. All of it the same pure lavender glow, unshaped by element, drawn straight from her core in whatever form she happened to want at the moment. He had been seeing it for a month and never once thought to put a name to it.

It made sense. It suited her. Mana-form was, in a way, the most arrogant kind of magic possible. It said: I do not need the world’s elements to give me a shape. I will simply will the shape into being and the world will agree with me about it.

Very her.

“That said,” she added, with a small, magnanimous tilt of her head, “for flexibility, elemental magic has its uses. Lightning will paralyze a foe. A small, well-aimed strike can stop a heart, stun a mage mid-incantation, or simply make a person twitch on the ground while you decide whether to bother killing them.”

Aiven flinched slightly.

“Earth,” she went on, ignoring the flinch, “is the most defensively versatile of the branches. It modifies terrain. It builds walls. It opens pits. It produces cover from nothing in the middle of an open field. A skilled earth-mage is, in many ways, more troublesome than a skilled fire-mage, because a fire-mage can only hit you, whereas an earth-mage can rearrange the entire battlefield while you are not looking.”

She pointed at him.

“Knowing these two will not make you a master of either. But it will give you options you do not currently have. The fight will have moments. You will need things in your pocket.”

Aiven nodded slowly. “Teach me.”

He tried to inject some lightness into it.

“Honestly. Lightning. I have always wanted to throw lightning. Rysa used to be able to wreathe her fists in fire and I always thought it was the coolest thing I had ever seen. There were stories, in the Guild logbooks, about a top-rank adventurer who could summon chains of lightning between his fingers like he was hanging laundry. I used to read those at lunch.”

He paused.

The lightness did not quite arrive where he had hoped to put it. There was a small, hollow space where the excitement should have been. The urgency of the week sat in that space instead, and the urgency was heavier than the excitement. He had wanted to throw lightning since he was fourteen, and now he was about to, and most of him was thinking about how long it would take him to do it well enough to matter.

He let the not-quite-arrived feeling pass without naming it.

Lightning came first.

It was, Virelle insisted, the more dramatic branch and therefore the more rewarding to begin with. She had him hold his hand out in the same shape as the mana bolt, but with the fingers slightly more spread, and asked him to feel for the same place inside his chest where the white roar lived, except, this time, to imagine the place beside it.

“Do not try to draw the mana into a different shape,” she instructed. “Draw a different mana. Lightning is not white-light pushed sideways. It is its own current. You must feel for it specifically. It is smaller and faster and angrier. If pure mana is an ocean, lightning is a particular fish that lives in it. You must locate the fish.”

“Locate the fish,” Aiven repeated.

“Yes.”

“Right.”

He closed his eyes.

He felt for the ocean first. The familiar place. The white roar behind the regulator. Then, very carefully, he tried to feel beside it. Beneath it. He was not sure how to feel for a smaller, faster, angrier thing inside a larger thing. He thought about the way the air felt before a storm, the prickle in his hair, the smell of charged metal.

Something pricked at the back of his teeth.

He grabbed at it, mentally, and pushed.

ZRRT.

A short, jagged white-blue arc snapped out of his palm and missed the open sky entirely. It dipped at a forty-five degree angle, traveled twenty paces in approximately a thousandth of a second, and struck a small, single horn protruding from the head of an unfortunate sheep who had wandered out onto the deck of the scout-ship.

Pelka stood frozen on the deck, her clipboard pressed against her chest, her wool faintly standing on end. A thin curl of smoke was rising from the tip of her single horn. Her bangs had been blown back from her glasses, leaving her eyes startlingly large and wet.

“Pelka!”

Aiven was sprinting toward the ship before he had finished saying her name.

“Oh no,” he was saying. “I am so sorry, are you all right, please, are you all right, please tell me your horn is still attached.”

Pelka stared at him.

“I,” she said. “I am.”

She paused, blinked, and patted her own horn with one careful hand.

“I am... fine. Sir. I think. My fleece feels strange. And I can taste copper for some reason. But I am fine. Yes. I am fine.”

She tried, valiantly, to smile.

From behind Aiven, Virelle’s voice drifted across the grass, dry as a desert.

“It seems the sheep is also a lightning rod. Useful information. Master, we shall move farther from the ship.”

Aiven groaned, palmed his face, and offered Pelka a small, mortified bow.

“Please,” he said. “Go inside. Please. Stay inside. I will explain to Cyria personally if she asks why her analyst has a singed horn.”

Pelka backed away from the rail, holding her horn protectively.

Earth, mercifully, did not involve any nearby livestock.

Virelle moved them to the far end of the islet, where the cliff dropped away on three sides and there was no one within a hundred paces to be incidentally struck. She had Aiven kneel and place his hand flat against the grass.

“Earth is the slowest of the elemental branches,” she said, “which is also why it is the most stable. You do not throw earth. You ask it. You request a shape from it, with sufficient firmness in the request, and the earth will, in time, deliver. The trick is to feel the weight beneath you, and then to feel the lines along which the weight is willing to move.”

She crouched, hovering, beside him.

“For your first attempt. A simple raise. A pillar of stone, a single foot in diameter, beneath your palm. Modest. Controlled. Do not be ambitious.”

“Modest,” Aiven repeated. “Controlled. Got it.”

He closed his eyes.

He felt for the weight beneath him. He found it, more easily than he expected, a great patient sleeping bulk of dirt and stone and root. He felt for the lines, the way Virelle had said. He found those too. He requested the shape, firmly. He asked for a pillar one foot wide.

He must have asked, in retrospect, rather more firmly than he intended.

FWUMP.

The ground beneath him bucked.

An eight-foot column of compacted earth and bedrock erupted under his hand at approximately the speed of an angry catapult, with Aiven still on top of it. The world dropped away beneath him. The cliff dropped away beneath him. The sky did a small, polite spin.

“V-VIRELLE!”

His arms windmilled.

There was a sharp violet flash, and then a small, exasperated cry of, “I have you, Master, do stop kicking,” and a pair of arms slid neatly underneath him, one beneath his shoulders and one beneath the bend of his knees, and lifted him cleanly off the top of the pillar.

Virelle, hovering thirty feet above the islet, holding her Master horizontally in her arms in the precise carrying-pose used in romantic tales, raised one elegant eyebrow.

“Well,” Aiven said. “This is humiliating.”

“I do find this quite enjoyable,” Virelle said.

She floated, slowly, back down to the grass, set him gently on his feet, and dusted invisible specks of debris off his shoulders with the put-upon air of a noblewoman who had just rescued a kitten from a tree.

Aiven was crimson.

Behind them, the eight-foot pillar of earth stood in the middle of the islet like a single rude finger.

The day continued, in this fashion, for several more hours.

There were minor accidents. There were not-so-minor accidents. Aiven discharged a lightning bolt into a small puddle and gave himself an unexpected static shock that made every hair on his arms stand straight up for half an hour. He attempted a more controlled pillar and accidentally produced three smaller pillars in an aesthetically interesting triangle around the original. He tried to flatten the three smaller pillars and instead produced a low, ribbed wall that looked, faintly, like a row of teeth.

But the results, by late afternoon, were undeniable.

He could call lightning. Not the chained, laundry-line variety of his lunchtime daydreams, but a clean, single, jagged bolt that struck where he aimed it, more often than not. He could raise pillars of compacted earth from the ground beneath his feet. He could shape them, with effort, into rough shields or angled spear-poles. He could lower the ground back into itself, if he was patient. He could thicken a patch of soil into something solid enough to deflect a thrown stone.

Two new branches. In a day.

He sat down on the grass and looked at his hand.

By any normal standard, none of this should have been possible. Apprentice mages spent months on a single branch. Real proficiency in two branches typically took months of dedicated study under a competent instructor. He had done two branches in an afternoon, and the lightning had not even singed his hair.

It had to be the ocean of mana.

It was the only explanation that made any sense. The vast, quiet sea of mana that lived behind the new regulator, the one Lyra had set in him, the one he was learning to draw from a quarter-turn at a time. When Virelle gave him an instruction, when she described the principle, when she asked him to imagine a shape, something inside him simply met the request halfway. The ocean did not seem to care which shape he was asking for. It only seemed to care that he was asking.

Was that a good thing?

He was not sure. There was a part of him that suspected it was not. That an ocean which agreed to anything was not the kind of ocean a person ought to be carrying around inside their chest. That a power which learned this fast was a power which had probably learned this fast for a reason, and the reason probably had a name, and the name was probably one he was not going to like.

He did not have time to wonder about it.

Lucien was six days away.

If the ocean wanted to deliver, the ocean could deliver. He would worry about the bill later.

He stood up. He brushed the grass off his trousers.

“Virelle,” he said. “Again.”

Virelle, who had been watching him with a quiet, unreadable expression, gave a small sniff.

“Yes, Master.”

The training continued.
