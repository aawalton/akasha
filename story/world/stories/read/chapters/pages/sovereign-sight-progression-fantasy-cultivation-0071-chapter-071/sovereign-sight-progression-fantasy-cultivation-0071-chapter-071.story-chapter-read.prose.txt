Brooks and idiots babble.

Only one is worth listening to.

Harker stepped off the lift and immediately turned around to watch the wooden doors close. They moved just as quietly as the stone, though these were not designed to be hidden. Instead, they were gorgeously stained a dark cherry, with raised panels and gilded carvings of waves along the edges. Sovereign Sight took that all in and beyond, piercing the wall as the lift sped upward through the green stone cavity.

“It goes further?” he asked.

Oswick stood beside him, both hands resting on the broken circle of his hammer. “All the way up to the Elder Keeper’s offices.”

"Who built such a thing?"

"A skilled Thaumist, I’m told. It was years before my time." The big man’s head tilted, as if he were seeing it in a new light. "It truly is miraculous, isn't it?”

“I've never seen its like," Harker admitted.

"Well, I don't know the thaumist's name, but he didn't invent it anyways. He just adapted it to the Lighthouse. Like most things, the design was made in the Nine Spires." Oswick tapped his hammer against the ground. It plinked against the polished stone. “But you didn’t come to look at moving boxes. Come.”

The giant scholar stepped away, hammer swinging at his feet, and it spoke to how marvelous he found the lift that he barely noticed the opulence of what surrounded him. They were stood in the center of a grand hall. The floor was polished slabs of green marble, inlaid with squares of tiled mosaics interrupting its veined flow. More depictions of a stormy sea and dark coasts lit only by Lighthouses.

“The light of knowledge, keeping the darkness at bay,” the scholar said as he stepped over them.

Harker liked the sentiment. More than any of the others, Ancestor Pellus valued knowledge above all else. The Lighthouses had many functions, but they were primarily libraries—ones that were open to all.

"We're on the shrine level currently. You can see it back there through those doors," Oswick said, gesturing behind them.

The grand hall extended past a series of lifts for at least thirty feet, and beyond that it opened into a circular room fronted by a pair of enormous double doors. Made of the same paneled wood as the lift entrance, they were taller than most houses and cracked open halfway. Inside, he could make out a series of ringed sections that lifted up toward a dais and a complicated plume of metal and stone. The Shrine to Pellus.

The blue-green glow of water emanated from between the doors, like light off of a reflecting pool.

"If you're interested, you can visit it later. There’s even a reliquary."

“An artifact of Pellus?”

“Oh yes. We are one of the few Lighthouses containing an object that Pellus himself crafted."

“Really. What is it? Some sort of thaumic device?” Harker slowed. “A weapon?”

Oswick laughed. "Of course not a weapon. Pellus was a man of peace. It's a brick.”

“A brick." Harker repeated.

"Yes. A fundamental building block for civilization. They say that when the first brick was laid, it was when humanity began to establish a true community. Without it, we’d still be warring clans."

"I've heard it differently."

Oswick adjusted his glasses. “Oh?”

“When the first woman mixed the first remedy for her ailing neighbors.” Harker smiled softly. “Not for her children or parents. Hope and comfort offered to a stranger that could not care for themselves.”

Oswick was quiet for a while as they walked the hall. Heels and hammer clunked softly in their wake. "That is quite something. Are you sure you're not a scholar yourself?”

Harker’s smile sharpened. “I’ve just had a good teacher.”

“Those are hard to find.” Oswick sighed, before gesturing ahead. “Up here are the front doors. They will lead you down to the grounds and beyond them, the streets of the city.”

Harker made note of them. They were just as grand as the entry to the shrine, though smaller man doors were set into their expanse—likely the true entrances for most.

“The staircase here will take you on a slow climb for the first three floors. That’s the guest chambers, scholar’s quarters, and the library.”

Inside the grand entryway, the doubled staircase swept majestically around the circular exterior. Tapestries hung from high above as well as amber-lit chandeliers on long, nearly invisible woven cords. The effect was beautiful and a touch eerie, as if supernatural forces had built the place. Harker supposed they had.

Off to the side, a duty desk was set into the wall, containing a trio of scholars busily checking over piles of papers.

“If you wish to take a room, you need only speak with the scholars at the main desk. They will make the arrangements so long as there’s space.”

Harker nodded. One of the other reasons he'd sought out the Lighthouse was for a place to lay his head, at least for a night. There was no point in traveling onward from the Sunken City until daybreak—not with everything else he had to accomplish.

"As to your current needs," Oswick continued, as he led him down a wide corridor off the entryway. "This is the galley.”

Through a pair of pocket doors was a wide hall filled with benches, tables, and chairs hewn from stout oak and stained a deep brown. The floors were cobbled with heavy granite slabs that had settled into uneven blocks worn down by the passage of countless feet. The tables were half filled with people, despite the late hour. Food was being served in a long line along the walls where fireplaces held simmering pots of some bubbling brew slowly being ladled into the bowls of waiting scholars.

Harker sniffed deeply, his Talent pulling in a concentrated burst of delightful stew. “Oh that’s exactly what I’m after.”

A clock on the wall rang, its chime barely heard over the low rustle of conversation. The copper hands pointed firmly at ten and twelve. Quite late, but early enough to grab a spot of dinner before he got to work.

"Well.” Oswick shuffled, which made the seven-foot giant sway like an oak tree. "I will leave you here. Food is there, drink along the other side, and you can feel free to sit anywhere. Eat your fill, Harker.”

“I will.” He smiled up at the bespectacled giant. “And thank you, Oswick. I appreciate the guidance."

"It was my pleasure. I don’t often get to spend time with…anyone that isn’t a corpse, really.”

Oswick turned on his heel, hammer lifted effortlessly.

“Just…”

Oswick stopped, glasses slipping down his nose.

"Just be careful,” Harker continued. “Those huts… I don't trust them."

To Harker's surprise, Oswick didn't laugh off the concern or scoff at his caution. Instead, the big man looked touched.

"I appreciate that.” He put his hand to his chest. For the first time, Harker noticed scars across the backs of them. “I will be fine."

The big scholar strode away, headed back to the lift no doubt. In moments, he was gone.

Harker grabbed a tray and in short order filled its surface with several tin dishes. A seafood stew with a thick broth and heavy tubers, simmered beside a tall glass of watered-down wine, a full loaf of dark bread, and a small section of honeycomb. By the time he sat down at a slightly rickety table, he was all but drooling.

Once the first mouthful of stew hit his lips, Harker almost groaned. Ironwine had fed him well enough, but everything he made was prepared and spiced for an Eidhrin’s palate. This, however, was rife with the flavors of humanity. It was also distinctly enhanced by Sovereign Sight, which he held close, and all but bathed him in scents, taste, and the homey sensation of a hot meal. He devoured it all, especially the honeycomb, which he chewed appreciatively along with the bread.

"Did you see the big oaf?”

“Oh, I did. Him and his hammer."

The room was fairly buzzing with conversation, but the cascade of laughter caught Harker’s ear. Chewing the last of the bread, he cautiously extended Sovereign Sight. The conversation of dozens pricked his ears, and Harker squeezed the shape of his sphere. It distorted, bulging forward to envelop a group no more than ten feet away. The Talent cut through the treacle of murmured conversation, and suddenly the voices of a handful of blue-robed scholars was clear to him.

"...What was Nightlight doing up here? I thought he got banished to the river, like always.” A young man with a wispy mustache sat with a cup of wine cradled between his soft hands.

“Brought that traveler in. Anything special about him?”

Harker’s ears all but burned, and it took concentration not to look up.

“You mean, does he look like the tithing type? Doubtful. If he was with Nightlight, then you won’t squeeze an ampule outta him.”

“Abyss. My quota is falling behind this month.”

“Can’t be worse than the giant. He never gets tithes.”

“Is that why he’s always at the river? As punishment?" another asked, this one a bit older, though he didn’t look capable of growing facial hair at all. He shuddered. “I promise to get more tithes, Ancestor Pellus. Truly.”

"Has nothing to do with tithing," said a man with floppy hair and an insoucient posture that immediately riled Harker. He snorted. "You've seen his fair skin. That blonde hair."

"I did. I thought it was a throwback."

"Nightlight ain't no throwback. That frame, with that complexion: the boy's a reaver.”

A few of their crowd gasped, and others joined closer. The floppy-haired speaker puffed up, clearly appreciating his growing audience.

“A reaver here in the Lighthouse?”

“That isn’t allowed, I thought?”

“No no, I heard he gave up being a reaver.”

“...that hammer…”

“You don’t give up being a reaver,” Floppy Hair said, cutting through the murmurs. “It stains you. Imagine what that hammer of his has done.”

Harker frowned. Reavers were another story he had only half believed. Phaar was a large island but it had a notoriously rocky coastline. That didn’t accommodate ports, especially north of the Gnarl, and the rough terrain further insulated them. However, further north were hundreds of other, smaller islands that boasted one or two villages each. Merchant fleets regularly traded with those islands, plying the waters between their splintered coastlines…and provided perfect targets for the reavers.

Reputed to be enormous, men and women alike sailed atop their Aberrant-shaped longboats, falling onto the merchant vessels like a bloody storm. The villages too. Reavers burned and pillaged, only leaving an island once they’d stripped everything of use. All that remained was a wash of blood.

Or so the stories said.

Oswick, while certainly built for it, did not strike Harker as the reaver type. The man was quiet, calm, and possessed of a certain bashfulness that only made sense when you saw how he handled that hammer. He placed it delicately, almost as if afraid it would damage something around him. Someone dedicated to brutal bloodshed would not be so careful. Even when Oswick had thought someone was desecrating the dry houses, he'd done nothing more than call on his Talent. A ball of light. Startling, but passive.

“...doesn’t he have a Talent?”

Harker refocused on the conversation. It had grown quieter in the hubbub of the galley, but he had no problem hearing them.

“Is that why he’s down in the dark?”

“...those dry houses give me the creeps…”

“Light.” Someone laughed. “What else could it be good for?”

A few others snickered in that sharp, self-comforting way he was so familiar with—at least a few of the scholars were uncomfortable that Oswick had a Talent. Even if it was elemental light.

Light, like fire and even air, was quite useful. It was, however, very difficult to Descend the Depths with such a Talent. Abundant as light may be in their world or on the Surface of the Infinite Sea, in the clutch of the dark waves, light was in short supply. Dragging it with you beyond Second Depth was considered all but impossible.

“Talent’s Talent, eh?” Floppy Hair said. “Even light could be useful…in the right hands."

Wispy Mustache finished his drink. "Yeah, if I had a Talent, even a worthless elemental light one, I'd be headed to the Academy faster than you can spit."

"What would you do there, Rourke?”

“Get strong, come back here, clean out the ratlords and the Shroud. Get awarded by the Elder Keepers and the citylord. Be set for life."

Food scraps hurled at Rourke and his bad mustache.

“Boring!”

“Liar! You’d stay in Thalor and grow fat on pastries at the Spires.”

Rourke laughed. “True enough.”

“Well,” said Floppy Hair, once the commotion died down. "I heard Nightlight took the mark."

"What? That passive brute?"

"No, not a chance."

"No, no, it's true.” Floppy Hair grinned, happy to have all the attention again. “Tenna saw the silver gleam when he was doing his ablutions." Appreciative murmurs flitted through the crowd. Tenna, apparently, was not known for being a liar.

"And he's still here?” a rounder scholar asked. “The last of the marks left a month ago. What's he playing at?”

Someone cackled. “Does the fool not know about the time limit?"

A good question. Harker pushed the last of his stew around the bowl. And none of my business.

He left the gossiping scholars as he should have done ten minutes ago. Their prattle wasn’t worth his time. He picked up his tray, bringing it to where he saw others like it stacked. There, a white-robed servant took it from him.

"Have a good evening, sir."

Sir. Harker gave him an awkward nod before making his way back to the entrance. Not sure I like that.

Spending a lifetime being disrespected only made him feel uneasy with deference. It was unnatural, being above someone else. That the servant was older than him only made it feel worse.

Only a few short steps away was the duty desk. Several blue-robed scholars stood behind it, a man and three women, and all of them looked patiently bored. Their hair was done up in simple but severe styles, keeping it away from their shoulders and paperwork that they scribbled into constantly. Most had robes that were slashed with panels of deeper blue—different than Oswicks relatively plain set—no doubt some manner of ranking system.

"How may we help you, good sir?" The woman set down her board and papers, giving Harker a somewhat stiff smile.

"I’m looking to spend the night.”

“A traveler. Of course. Will it just be yourself?”

“It will.”

“Wonderful.” She checked over a list at her side. “We can accommodate a variety of needs.

Do you have a preference for room type? I have several veranda types if you’d like a view of the city and the glinting sky. There is also the inner garden area, which is accentuated by the year-round aromatics grown there—”

“Whatever is cheapest.”

“I see.” She pursed her lips and flipped through several pages. "I have something. It's one of the inner chambers, so no window. If that doesn't bother you—”

“That's fine."

"Very well." The pen scratched across his paperwork. “Your name?”

Harker smiled, sliding a silver piece across the desk. “Jeren."

"I see." The woman considered the silver. "While we do take coin, you will find it is far cheaper to simply tithe your refined Water. Most prefer that."

Harker didn't flinch, but pressed the silver coin forward. "This will be fine."

"Very well, sir." The scholar took the coin and gave change, five copper pieces handed back to him. "You will have the room until tomorrow at noon." A brass contraption followed—it was square and edged with odd teeth along one side. A key, though it looked nothing like the keys he'd seen back home. “The room is up the main staircase to the second floor and down the third hallway on your left. The number will match the one etched into the key."

Harker inclined his head and left. Up the stairs, he found his room quickly enough. It was as small as advertised, but both the door and lock were sturdy. There was even a thick wooden bar inside for extra security. By contrast the bed was thin and narrow, there was no rug on the bare stone floor, and it lacked even a small chest to store things.

This will do.

His pack came off for the first time in nearly a day. Harker had grown so used to it that simply slipping it from his shoulders was a surprising relief. Next came his pouches and bags he usually wore strapped to himself—useful as their contents were, they’d only put him in more trouble if their contents were stolen. He kept his weapons and a few of the more useful reagents.

Hopefully he wouldn’t need them.

Soon enough, he was back down the stairs, lighter and focused. Fewer scholars were about, and almost no one else, save for a young woman in a simple gown walking toward the shrine. Harker made for the man door at the grand entrance, steeling himself as he pushed it open.

It was time to get to work.
