I awoke in my estate.

Not in the throne, not in the void, not in the gilded half-dream of soul and self. But here, in the familiar gravity of linens and morning light. Breath warm against pillow. Eyelids heavy. Mind…strangely clear.

Scent returned first.

Cordelia’s flowers — honeysweet and sharp, like spring trying to flirt with winter. Ten’s cooking — something savory, eggs and herbs and a hint of smoked salt. Fractal’s trilling song and tiny stamping feet — the melody of a creature who had never once feared becoming anything other than herself. Basoriel’s cheerful little chirps in the corner — ancient spirit, eternal soft idiot.

These were anchors. Home. Mortal. Me.

Which is why the figures standing in front of my bed made no sense at all.

I blinked. Once. Twice. A third time, just to be offended by reality.

Two figures stood there. One in the ink-stained apron of a binder, sleeves rolled. The other in old scholar’s robes, regal and archaic. Both rubbing sleep from their eyes. Both me. Both utterly, painfully real.

“What. The. Hells?!” I shouted, voice cracking on the last syllable because shock does not care for dignity.

Before panic could really sink its teeth in, soft jingling preceded disaster.

Gin trotted in, bells chiming in perfect mischief-ridden harmony. His grin arrived half a second before the rest of him, sharp and delighted and entirely too entertained by my existence.

“Well!” he sang, as though announcing a festival and not my psychological collapse. “Congratulations! You just became a Hydra-Blooded.”

I stared. I pointed at the two half-yawning versions of myself now stretching like they’d rolled out of a shared nap in my soul’s sunlit study.

“Excuse me? You—neither you nor Barbatos thought to mention this? Before it happened?”

Gin waved a careless hand, jingling like a choir of smug windchimes.

“If you succeeded, we would have. Eventually. Because, oh look”—he gestured grandly at the two clones—“they’re going to grow in number anyway.”

The binder-me squinted around the room with a bleary frown, then picked up a book someone had left on my nightstand and began checking its binding critically. The scholar-me looked deeply offended by the wallpaper.

I dragged a hand down my face. “So these are real? Not illusions? Not soul-residue? Not, I don’t know, spiritual echoes or philosophical metaphors made flesh?”

Gin’s grin widened. “Oh, very real. Bodies, minds, dreams, morning breath. They are you, but also not-you. They get to be people. And you get to be a Hydra-Blooded.”

He said it like it should be obvious.

He also said it like “Hydra-Blooded” was a punchline at my expense. It probably was.

My clones yawned in eerie unison. The binder-me stretched like a cat and immediately began tidying my stacks of paper. The scholar-me gave him a superior glance, then started reorganizing my bookshelves by date of publication rather than alphabetical order.

I willed my pulse not to spike. It spiked anyway.

“You mean…” I took a slow breath, voice tight. “The visions I saw. All those faceless figures at the table speaking as one. That wasn’t metaphor.”

“Nope!” Gin beamed, teeth too sharp to be comforting. “Each was their own person. Original self first, then sprouted siblings of selfhood over time. Congratulations again. Big milestone.”

I stared at him. He rocked happily on his heels.

“And you didn’t think,” I said, very carefully, “to warn me?”

Gin clapped once, delighted. “If I warned you, you might not have done it. And then where would we be? Nowhere fun, I’ll tell you that.”

He leaned in close, whispering with glee:

“You grew teeth.”

My jaw clenched. “I did not eat myself.”

“Not physically.” Gin shrugged, bells jingling. “Philosophically? Spiritually? Debatable.”

I opened my mouth to keep yelling but Gin held up a finger.

“Before you ask — no, they are nothing like your mother’s Arte. You don’t share their abilities, or their memories, or, usually, their personalities. You do, however, feel a pull toward each other. Coordination, not control.”

The scholar-me sniffed and nodded gravely, clearly already forming a daily study regimen. The binder-me hummed contentedly while smoothing the corner of a loose page like it was a wounded bird.

A quiet horror unfurled in my stomach.

“So I didn’t hallucinate. This is permanent. They’re me. Sort of. And…more are coming?”

Gin nodded enthusiastically, bells chiming their approval. “Oh yes. Eventually you’ll have many. A whole little council of you. It's adorable.”

“Adorable?!” My voice broke again. “That meeting wasn’t adorable. That meeting was existential disembowelment!”

“Growth!” he corrected. “Existential growth. Very healthy. Very murder-chrysalis of you.”

I silently mouthed the phrase murder-chrysalis because I hated it and also it was correct.

Gin’s tone became falsely bright. “But good news!”

He paused so long I knew the bad news was immense.

“You gained a form of immortality early!”

I waited.

He grinned wider.

“Bad news! If this body dies, you might have your memories split between two of you by accident.”

Silence. Thick as oil.

“So if I die,” I said slowly, “I might wake up like one of them. With only half my memories.”

“Or one-third. Or one-fifth. Or you might wake up in three bodies thinking you are three different people, none of whom know they’re supposed to be one. Statistically rare, but, you know.” He wiggled his hand. “Hydra things.”

I stared at him. He stared joyfully back. Somewhere in the estate, Fractal sang a happy little tune like the universe wasn’t collapsing inside my skull.

I inhaled through my nose, long and slow. “Is there any truly good news in this conversation?”

Gin tapped his chin thoughtfully. “Well… you’re harder to kill now. And you have built-in debate partners. And immunity to existential monotony.”

“And the downside is… fractured identity and eternal multiplicity?”

“Oh, that’s not the downside.” Gin’s bells chimed like laughter from something very old. “That’s the journey.”

I sunk back into the bed, staring up at the ceiling, feeling the weight of fate like a crown made of questions and claws.

The binder-me pat my shoulder gently. The scholar-me tutted and handed me a list of Things That Must Now Be Considered When Existing.

Gin jingled his bells one more time. “Be proud, Alexander. Few mortals become legion while still mortal.”

Then he winked.

“And fewer still remain themselves after.”

My heart did not freeze. It burned — quiet, steady, resolved. Fear and pride in the same breath.

I whispered, mostly to myself:

“I will not lose who I am.”

The binder-me smiled softly. The scholar-me approved with a nod. Gin tilted his head, amused.

“Oh, darling hydra-child,” he murmured. “That is exactly what every head says.”

And then he vanished in a sound like laughter wearing bells.

“Okay. So we probably need their Artes and mana types assessed.” Barbra — Barbatos herself — nodded once, crisp and imperious, like she was confirming the movement of armies rather than the fate of two groggy… me's.

Currently, the other two were still wobbling around like newborn fawns assembled incorrectly. One tried to stand upright and instead rotated an arm the wrong direction. The other blinked at a chair suspiciously, as if it had personally insulted him in a past life and he was contemplating the ethics of punching furniture.

“Wait.” My eyes bulged. “They are that independent of me?” I waved a hand at them. “They look like puppets whose strings were braided by a drunk spider. Does that mean—” My jaw dropped. “Wait. WAIT. Does this mean they have their own Skillcube slots?!”

Leraje, looking about as patient as a librarian forced to babysit gods, nodded. “Yes. They do. They are effectively both you — and not you. Think of them as diverging branches of the same tree. Metaphysically and administratively — you’re one entity. But personality-wise…”

“They’re idiots with limbs,” Barbra supplied, helpful in the least helpful way possible.

“—they have independent alterations of your psyche,” Leraje finished, casting her a glare.

Morres rubbed the bridge of his nose. “This is a known complication among hydraborn. Once their monster blood awakens, they must navigate the hormonal burden of being a teenager and the hormonal storms of an additional one to nine consciousnesses.”

He gestured vaguely at my other selves, who were at that moment attempting to high-five and missing by such magnitude they nearly invented a new martial art accidentally.

“Two, like yourself, is near average,” Morres continued.

I stared. “So hypothetically — hypothetically — how do I get more heads? If I wanted to.” (Not that I wanted to. But what if one day I did. You know. For reasons.)

Ranah winced. “Either snuff the budding ego yourself, or feed enough miasma into your mana circuits to fracture again and manifest another self.”

Barbra clicked her tongue. “Or get cursed. Or perform certain rituals. Or—”

Morres shot her a look sharp enough to cleave mountains. “STOP HELPING.”

I swallowed.

“…Okay. So it’s possible.”

“You considering it,” Leraje muttered, “is how we all die.”

Morres coughed. “Frankly what’s terrifying is that you may be the first hydra in history with the potential to ascend to Dominus.”

Silence. Absolute.

I blinked. “What?”

Ranah crossed her arms. “Most hydras go insane long before that. Power without unity devours itself. Those who don’t fall to madness are slain by those terrified of what they could become.”

Barbra nodded. “Hydras don’t rise. They… burst.”

“Comforting,” I croaked.

Leraje flicked a trio of mana motes into the air — one shimmering human-bright, one fractal-prismatic like Almiraj aether, one violent and hungry at the edges like a heartbeat made of teeth.

“You are approximately twenty percent human, forty percent Almiraj… and forty percent Hydra.”

I blinked again. “…Forty?”

“Yes,” Morres said, voice heavy. “And unusually stable. For now.”

There was a silence wherein everyone imagined me destabilizing and multiplying until I was a screaming fractal nightmare eating time.

I exhaled. “So the fear isn’t the strength.”

They all nodded.

“It’s losing the part of me that chooses not to abuse it.”

“That,” Ranah said softly, “is a noble terror to carry.”

Leraje stared at me like I was a particularly interesting bomb. “Power doesn’t frighten you. The gluttony of it does.”

Morres clapped once, sharply. “Enough dread. You two — line up. We’re testing your mana before you imprint on the wallpaper or attempt to duel a teapot.”

Clone #1 saluted a wall. Clone #2 saluted Clone #1 and slapped himself. I simply reconsidered every life decision I'd ever made.

“Why,” I whispered, “did the universe look at me and say, yes… but what if two more?”

Barbra patted my shoulder sympathetically. “Because fate has excellent comedic instincts.”

Ranah nodded. “And poor mercy.”

Morres pointed. “Tea. Now. Before the copies unionize.”

One of my clones perked up, attempted to stand, tripped over nothing, and folded into a cushion.

I stared.

Leraje whispered to Ranah, “They really are him.”

I groaned.  “Please tell me gods don’t find this entertaining.”

Barbra sighed. “Oh, they absolutely do.”
