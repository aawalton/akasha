“How are you?” Kylinissa spoke as he approached. Her misty-green eyes pierced the darkness of her hood.

“Tired,” he said. “Have they come for me already? I thought I’d have until the morning at least.”

“Not exactly.” She faltered and looked aside. “I have a message.”

“No point in delaying.”

Kylinissa sighed. “The Coven are calling for the Sixth Tonnage’s decimation.”

Skippii’s stomach dropped–but he caught it quickly; he had seen it coming.

“They want Custos Maritor executed,” she went on. “They are adamant that you are a heretic… I think they are embarrassed. When they took you away, they took advantage of the legion being in disarray. And your Octio… He handed you over, but it wasn’t his right to do so. The Coven wanted to end it swiftly." Kylinissa scowled. “And now, they’re ashamed of their failures. They feel that the Gods scorn them, and so they wish for revenge.”

“They’re zealots,” Skippii said plainly. ”They’re putting their own pride before the legion.”

Kylinissa bowed her head and fidgetted with the hem of her cloak. “I tried to reason… But they only heart what Kylin demands.”

“The Goddess herself has spoken?” Skippii said.

“Only Aetheria knows,” Kylinissa said. “But she has grown silent. The rest of her Coven have made their demands…” Her pale lips hung open, and once again she looked aside.

“They want me,” Skippii said. “But they won’t kill me. I won’t let them. They’re afraid of me, just a little bit. I embarrassed them, like you said. I shamed them before their Gods. And now, they need to kill my image. They want me to desert.”

She whispered, “That’s right,” then jumped like a flash of lightning. “But you could fight it. Make your case to the Imperator. He may-”

“And force him again to choose between me and his Pantheon?” Skippii said. “Beg for his forgiveness. Explain why I set fire to the camp, and how I knew the cyclops would attack. I don’t know. Should I beg for Aetheria’s forgiveness? Should I lie, and pretend that I have discovered the truth of my magia, and that I am certain… that I can swear on my certainty that there is nothing heretical within me? I know this feeling now. It has been with me for years. I know it is true to my will, but I cannot explain it. I cannot show it, I cannot attach it to one of the Gods. It’s just me. It’s just mine. Judge me, not what you think you see, or you fear. Why don’t they just judge me?”

“Because they see a threat.” Kylinissa’s voice shook, and she grasped his wrists and looked deep into his eyes. “But you can prove them wrong. Submit to the Gods. I will perform the ritual, and the Imperator will bear witness. Spread your soul before the Gods and accept their judgement. It can be done, but only the pure of heart can withstand it.”

Skippii twisted free of her grasp. “I need not their decree of my heart, nor purity.”

“But if you have nothing to hide-”

“I may well,” he barked. “I have seen now how your Gods react, without reason, with haste. With vengeance. Do I trust their judgement? Will I surrender my soul to them? No.”

Kylinissa’s bright eyes were a plea. “Oyaltun guides you, she will defend you.”

Skippii scoffed. “Where has her guidance been of late, or is she too afraid of Kylin to intervene? Was that her guidance in my dreams to form nightmares? I would rather be guided by mules.”

“I thought you would fight them,” Kylinissa said. “Skippii, I swore my allegiance to the Pantheon–the Coven of Kylin are my masters–and I have the courage to seek what’s right. My Gods give me that strength. I thought you would have the same courage.”

“Courage to do the wrong thing is no courage at all.” His voice grew low. “If I remain, I only weaken the legion. I’ve divided its regiments, and undermined command. Rumours will spread. The Fifth Legion will invent their own, and they’ll fear me, and despise my allies. And the Coven will find a way to restore their pride. A knife in the dark. My companions are not safe while they feel dishonoured. And all the while, I would be made to hide this power. To submit it, and myself.”

As he spoke, his hand drifted to his mother’s broach which held the tatters of his legionnaire’s cloak to his chest, and he clasped it. “It’s over. I’m not suited to be a legionnaire anymore.”

“Then you’ll go?” Kylinissa asked softly.

“I’ve made my peace.”

“Then, the Coven have sworn not to seek retribution,” Kylinissa said. “As for your Primus, his career won’t be good after this.”

“I can’t help that,” Skippii said.

“Yes, I know. But the tonnage will be safe. The Coven would rather this disappear. No punishment. No fanfare. Just you, gone. For good.”

Skippii stood rigidly and mute. His heart froze over. He refused to let himself grieve until it was done.

“Where will you go?” Kylinissa asked. He did not know her voice was capable of such softness; the concern in her eyes shook his resolve.

“Far away,” he said.

“Will you be safe?”

“Why do you care?”

The light of her eyes darkened as a storm clouded her face. “It’s just common decency.”

“I’ll be fine.”

Kylinissa withdrew a small pouch from her pocket. “I collected this, in case this was your decision. It’s what you’re owed, though it isn’t much. It’ll buy you a few meals at least.”

“I don’t need it,” he said. “Give it to the companeight whose tent I burned. Let it pay for their possessions.”

Kylinissa’s eyes drifted to the ground once more, where they lay. He regarded her, and suppressed the reflex to reach out and comfort her. His heart ached, but to embrace now would do them each no good.

“Thank you,” he said. “Thank you for seeing me.”

And with that, he turned and marched back to his companeight’s tent. His companions looked at him expectedly as he entered the firelight.

“A message,” Skippii muttered. “They want to bargain.”

“The Coven?” Orsin asked.

“I’m to meet the Coven and the Imperator in the morning,” Skippii lied, and swore it was the last he would ever tell.

“Did they say anything about us?” Cur asked. “About… you know… us being traitors or something?”

“We know what we are,” Orsin said.

“Yeah but, does the diciplinarium?” Cur said.

“We were following Custos Maritor’s lead,” Kaesii appealed. “Following orders.”

“No, no,” Orsin said. “That’s not right. I won’t throw him to the lashing. I went on my own volition. And so did you.”

“The only man of our tonnage who did not rally was that viperous Octio,” said Tenoris. “He too, is to blame for condemning Skippii to the Coven.”

“We should slit his throat,” Fulmin said.

Cur groaned. “Don’t draw me into some crazy…” He trailed off with a hiss. “That really would be treason. No excuses of honour or this and that.”

“Maybe just bash his head in,” Fulmin amended.

“I like that idea,” Drusilla nodded.

“As much as I’d love to, we’re not savages,” Orsin said. “It’ll only make it worse. Let’s see what the Imperator has to say next, and suffer the consequences with our heads held high.”

“Okay,” Kaesii said. “And don’t go having anymore nightmares, Skip, if you think you can help it.”

“Or, if you do, hope that they do not come true,” Arius said ominously.

One-by-one, his companions retired to their beds, but he remained by the campfire. The veterans went first, but each of the recruits stuck around, sharing few words, staring into the firelight. The day’s excitement took its time to wear off, but once it did, exhaustion fell over their eyes like a soft blanket. At last, just he and Tenoris remained outside in the late hours of night. The big legionnaire began to snore where he sat, until Skippii prompted him to retire to the tent.

“And yourself?” Tenoris asked. “Shall you join us?”

“The fire gives me strength,” he said. “Let me draw on its embers, and I’ll be with you shortly.”

“I shall wait by your side.”

“No,” he insisted. “Get your rest, we all need it. Tomorrow will host its own trials.”

Nodding, Tenoris turned, then stopped at the tent’s flap, gazing at the stars. The night was clear, and all the heavens shone upon them, glittering in the farmhand’s big blue eyes.

“There is much debate amongst the Pantheon, as with a senate,” he said. “I sense it. A disturbance, and an excitement. They are wondering what to do with you, Skip.”

“They aren’t the only ones.”

“Ahh, too, the Imperator and his servants. But I should think that their position hasn’t changed. You are still a legionnaire.”

“You’d hope.”

“Yes.” His voice dipped, taking on a solemn tone. “No matter their verdict, or the Gods’ decree…” Tenoris froze, then finally took his eyes off the heavens and faced Skippii wholeheartedly “I shall stand by your side.”

Warmth flushed him, stinging his eyes. His chin fell to his chest, suddenly heavy with gratitude. His voice warbled. “Thanks.”

“Hespera, bless us. Spare us the rains. Bring us gladder tidings. Summitor, your impatience, please, employ it now. Render the heavens. End this squabbling. Let us return to the simplicity of war.” Tenoris sighed tiredly. “Oyaltun, make sure he stays safe. Dissuade his demons. Protect him from nightmares. And alert me, much sooner this time, should he need my aid.”

Skippii watched his friend slip under the tent flap into the bodily warmth of its den. Alone now, in the quiet camp, he let the flames die down. In the absence of his magia, the charred wood cooled quickly. Beside him was his legionnaire’s gear. He had paid for it with blood, and deserved to bear it, all but the cloak. That, he would leave behind. Taking up his pack and his kukri, he fetched a slave’s blanket from their cart. The fabric was rough and sodden, perhaps never cleaned; lucky that he would not have need for its warmth.

Picking a stone out of the fire, he clutched it. He wanted to leave something for his companions to remember him by, though had no means to write, and no skill for carving or craft. Instead, he sought to mark the stone with his handprint. A primitive trinket, but something. Clutching it, he let his magia radiate in his palm, intending to burn its surface.

Among the angry grazes on his forearm was his recent tattoo–the symbol of his companeight. Tears flowed freely, falling upon the crusted ink, but he kept his sobs silent, gripping the stone. As the final embers died down, Skippii closed his eyes. Now it was time. He had done all he could. He had said all he needed to say.

A stone plummeted into his gut as though falling down a well, rippling with grief. Now it was time. The legionnaires of Tonnage VI had truly honoured him today–spared his life, risked their own. He owed them everything–a debt he could never repay. The only way he could see to honour them in return was to do the right thing. He would desert them, unchain them from his own cursed fate. The legion simply couldn’t function with him in their midst. He thought he could adapt, compromise, but too much of it was out of his control. A Golden Spear. No. He was a hot brand; grasped from the wrong end, it would maim the wielder. That was no weapon for a legion’s Imperator, nor legionnaire. He would not shame his superiors any longer, nor force them to make ruinous choices.

However, he would not die, for that would disgrace his companion’s sacrifice in saving him. His flesh was a living medallion of their honour. He would find a way to repay them, in this life or the next. And it started tonight, with his own sacrifice.

Unclenching the stone, he lay it atop the embers. But the mark which he had burned caught his eye: an umber ringlet of entwined thorns–simple in design, bearing a distinct ‘IV’ in its centre. It was an exact replica of his companeight’s tattoo.

“Huh.” Skippii stared at the stone and his craft. He’d hadn’t intended to form such a precise burning, but had kept the image of his companeight’s symbol in mind as he’d applied his magia. Another new evocation which he had yet to explore. At least, once he was alone, he would have the time to delve into his powers and discover what he was capable of. But to what end? How exactly would he employ his gift? To be exiled from the Legion and disgraced amongst Auctoritas, stranded in a foreign land… What good could his powers do?

All questions for the coming weeks. The longer he lingered, the harder it would be to leave.

Rising from the campfire, he made his way quickly through camp, carrying his spear as a crutch. Though his muscles ached and his ribs creaked, life had returned to his limbs. Fire was in his blood, simmering away, lending him strength. He at least had the strength to complete his task.

Stepping out onto the empty parade ground, he made his way to the camp’s perimeter. Never again would he line up in formation with his brothers in arms, and listen the morning’s sermon, and receive orders from their superiors. Never again would he raise his chin to his cohort’s standard, and know that he was amongst the greatest men in the realm. Skippii tread slowly, lamenting the passing of each step, and found himself by the palisade’s gate all too quickly.

He expected the watchmen to question his leave, but none spoke up. The auxiliaries eyed him from beneath their hoods, and grew silent as he approached. Surely, rumour had reached them of his encounter with the Coven. However, it seemed that Tonnage VI’s reckoning would wait until morning. How severe would their punishment be for opposing the Coven? Would his companions be flogged? Would the tonnage be decimated? Surely not, not with Skippii gone from sight. His very presence caused a rift in the legion, a fracture of authority and doctrine. Truly, he had no choice. At least, once he was far away, he would no longer be burdened by his oaths, and no longer be a strain on his allies.

“Knuk for tot,” he said to himself, forcing his legs to press forwards as all of his boyhood dreams fell at his feet in ruin.

“Hey,” a gateman shouted from the tower above. “Name and cohort.”

Skippii opened his mouth to speak his honours one final time, but a voice superseded him from behind.

“Spurius Altivus, Octio of Tonnage Six.” The Octio strode from the shadows and came close. All around, the sounds of the camp died down as the night set in. A cheer cut through the quiet; there was laughter and a howl, but his superior’s composure was unflinching as he stared into Skippii’s soul.

“You were not worth Custos Maritor’s reputation,” Spurius Altivas sneered. “Though, it seems, neither was he fit for the job. Once disciplined, I shall be promoted to Primus. I should be your new superior, if you were to remain.”

Skippii glowered at him but did not say a word.

“You are not stupid, are you, Altay? You were not born a fool?”

He shook his head slowly.

“Then… You must know how this ends?”

“I do,” Skippii whispered.

The Octio raised his thick eyebrows, tilting his head backwards to catch the light of the waning moon. “Is that so?”

“I’ll be gone.”

The Octio sighed, and the cruelty in his expression ebbed. Beneath it, a sleepless stress that weighed on the bags of his eyes. “Perform this one act well, and irrevocably. Show your colours. Vanish and die. Prove me wrong, legio, for I do not think your vanity is capable of such a  sacrifice.”

“Sacrifice,” Skippii hissed. “You don’t care about the legion. You only care about your career.”

“Is that so?”

As Skippii glared at the snide veteran, the fear he had once held for his stature melted away. Standing before him was no longer a superior, but simply a man.

“You know very little of what I’m capable of.” He took a long step, bringing his face close to Spurius Altivus’. “However, I think I have seen all of your capabilities, Octio. They’re stretched thin, like a pig’s hide, opaque in the sunlight.”

Surprisingly, the Octio smirked, then lay a hand on his ribs. Even such a light touch sent pain tumbling through his chest, stinging his eyes. Without meaning to, he took a step backwards, and saw that the Octio had half-unsheathed his sword. His superior was glaring at him as a fisherman watches his lur, waiting for a reaction. But Skippii lowered his arms and stood before him, unarmed.

“A sword?” Skippii mocked. “I bested Aetheria. What could you do?”

“Be gone by the morning,” the Octio snarled.

“And if I do, what will happen to the tonnage? To the Primus?” Skippii’s voice cracked. “Will you make sure the Coven hold to their promise?”

“What promise?” the Octio sneered.

“They don’t deserve decimation.”

“No, they don’t,” he said firmly. “But I suspect they will be punished… not severely. No blood was shed afterall. But they were willing to… for you.” He spat at Skippii’s feet. “You disfigure their good nature and twist it for your own. You are no legionnaire. You are scum. You do not belong amongst men of doctrine and honour.”

Skippii’s tongue felt heavy in his mouth; his heart was too heavy to challenge the Octio’s words, for in them there was some truth.

“You shan’t see me again,” he said. “Except in your nightmares.”

The Octio’s snarl widened into a grin. “Don’t flatter yourself, velvet.”

Gritting his teeth, Skippii bowed his head. Bearing the shame, he strode from camp–his home–into the wilderness, still a man, but no longer a legionnaire.
