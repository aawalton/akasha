Skulls had been arranged in neat rows.

They varied, these skulls: some sun-bleached and picked clean; others adorned with shreds of flesh and gore frozen solid; while yet others had eager weeds growing between jaws possessed of shattered teeth and eye sockets bright with blooming winter flowers. Whatever reason could be found in such differing decay could only be communicated by the vultures and foxes, neither of whom were on speaking terms with Liao Hua. Perhaps they would speak in chitters and yips to the shaman who stared at this field of skulls, disbelieving that human cruelty could range beyond castrating dozens of corpses and removing of them their heads.

Each skull had been carefully staked to the earth, metal twine curving around jaw and tooth to join that wooden spike. Hua saw this with mild illness and refused to approach the skulls.

The shaman clasped her hands together and brought them to her chest. She was a short woman who hardly came up to Hua’s chest. If she stood on the toes of her unbound feet, she might reach Hua’s shoulder. Maybe. Most certainly she could if she stacked two skulls atop one another and balanced on them.

“These skulls do not belong to this land,” Shaman Yu pronounced finally. “They carry no connection to the flows of the earth in this region.”

“Useless mysticism,” Weiji said. For someone who liked mortals, he was very specific about the groups he did like and very eager to kill anyone outside of them. “Meaningless drivel.”

“I speak truth to power. Your blindness ain’t a smidge my business.”

“You are no Cultivator. I can see the thinness of your existence. You speaking of nothing real, nothing true, just as a mortal mistaking jin for jing.”

Hua raised a hand before they descended into further bickering. Playing peacekeeper was not a new role for Hua but usually, she was trying to keep her younger sisters from killing each other, and she’d never been so badly hungover at the time.

“Tell us something of import. Prove yourself worthy of the title you have claimed.” Hua loomed over her, unfurling the crackling storm of her presence. “If Liao must stand sentinel, then surely a shaman can dance.”

A daughter of Liao made lightning, a son of Yu read the stars, a daughter of Zhao heard the plants, and they were bound together by their shared history against shamans who danced.

“Show us the truth. Give it power,” Hua taunted.

“Can’t say I ever met someone I hated as much as you.”

“The feeling is returned with interest. Now, dance, or die.”

And so, the Shaman danced.

Her steps were slow at first. Almost tentative as she established a boundary of her space, her hemp shoes ghosting over grass. Her feet were unbound, Hua noticed uselessly. It spoke to a difference of class, culture, and shared history, as the Shaman traced a space over frostbitten soil.

With neither heed nor concern for her audience, Shaman Yan sped up, the arc of her jangling staff tracing a boundary that only she could know.

Hua narrowed her eyes. Dust should not have been thrown up from movements that slow. Not when the conditions had been so wet for so long. She blinked quickly but the dust was still there, falling slowly. Not dust then. Something else, Something more. Golden light, golden sand.

The dance rose in power, the world almost quivering with each step the shaman took. Her skin became as gooseflesh and her nerves felt raw as she watched, chest heavy, fists clenched.

Light rose as a spectrum of colour with no origin. Splintering, breaking, engulfing the shaman. Moving along the edge of Wu Yan’s staff, embracing the length of her robes and painting the blackness of her lips and teeth and tongue in new colours.

Teeth chattering and stomach roiling, Hua watched. This was like standing in the prayer room where the Liao Clan’s Scripture was housed and lightning was most powerful. Like the green green green meadow filled with Qi that she and Weiji had found. Neither dragon vein nor claw, but all the same, it was deeply entrenched with that immaculate aspect known as Qi.

Wu Yan, the Shaman Yan, danced, and the world’s Qi rose in answer to her movements, becoming her dance partner.

Hua had rarely seen so awesome a sight. Colour so deep, so bright, moving to embrace a single human. The world embracing one person.

A land beneath the world she knew was exposed and thought it should have been the same, there were differences. Like capturing a perfect painting of a place and looking at it a year later, a decade later. It might be the same elements, the same trees and grass and shape, but the details would change. This was what Hua saw. A truer land. Or a land that was true in another sense.

It undulated, this new land. The world brightened. Grass grew greener. The skulls which were not a true white gained a quality like pearl, like moonlight, a whiteness that was death in its truest sense. The myriad flows of Qi enhanced the world and danced to the pace Wu Yan set.

Weiji flinched. He could sense something even if he could not see it. Hua knew he could not. This had the same quality of seeing the Great Net of Heaven. It strained her golden eyes in a way never felt before. Like holding them open for too long, they grew dry and itchy, but also like this new world was cutting at her, seeking to have pools of blood to replace her whites.

It was said that a shaman could reveal the true path of the world. Hua hadn’t believed it. But maybe there was something to be believed. That even the most mundane steps one walked were part of something greater.

Hua watched the air shimmer around the shaman, shivering and twitching, unable to stay still. The path of her staff shifted the light, like a finger dragging through honey. It would always return to its place, but for a lingering moment, the disturbance would remain.

The shaman danced and it was beautiful.

Hua did not know how treasonous such a thought was. Her ancestors had fought the shamans, killed them to a man, and now here Hua was, finding beauty in what one did. Her father’s great legend was built on slaughtering them. Hua felt like a poor legacy, but she could not deny the grace in Shaman Yan’s movements. The pull of her arm muscles as she stretched her staff out, rippling through her body, and the strength in her legs, unbound feet carrying her through the motions of her dance, and

She has tempered herself through dancing, Hua thought, mesmerised.

Hua had always loved dancing. She had once danced with Qing even as Qing complained and mocked Hua relentlessly. It was a way to be close, to remain with each other and know one another. The ghost of Qing, the apparition that forever haunted her, stood beside her. Hua did not look at it, would not dare because the expression it wore might destroy her.

She looked down and to the left.

Their fingers were so close. It would take nothing at all for them to brush. An intentional temptation, one that was painfully obvious. Only a fool would fall for it.

Because she was a fool, and because she would always reach for Qing, Hua closed that last tiny gap and reached back.

It felt of nothing because it was nothing. Qing was dead. And yet, Hua thought she felt something. A solidity that existed for but a moment. As though the true world was being revealed, exposed by a shaman’s dancing. Maybe this was nothing more than a memory her body was remembering. Hadn’t Hua engraved the very essence of Qing within her?

“Do you think she could find my body?” Qing the cruel truth-lie asked. “I drown, Hua. Dark waters are all I have. They fill my mouth and throat and lungs. I am bloated. The fish eat me in the dark. They rip the flesh from my bones. Make a home in my eyes. I drown and you keep me chained there.”

Words could cut surer than any blade and hurt worse than any Qi deviation. But to hear that voice, even if it was a lie, soothed all wounds.

I will not let you go. Your Qi is all I have. I will keep you with me until we meet again. You were the foundation of my world and now you are the foundation of my dantian.

The shaman slowed, the paths of natural Qi converging unto an answer, and Hua felt her grief rise. When she stopped, would she never feel Qing again? Even this phantom touch that was but a lie was precious.

Step by step, moment by moment, the world dimmed, darkened, faded and lost lustre. Finally, after an eternity that passed in a moment, did Shaman Yan stop.

Hua looked down helplessly and found only the false world. The world without Qing. Of course, only her pale fingers greeted her, and there was no one else, and the true world was not real, and it had never been real. Just a lie that Hua foolishly believed in. It was so stupid to believe in a lie but as grandmother oft reminded her, Hua was a fool.

“What happens now?” Weiji asked despite having an answer and Hua focused on that instead of her impotent grief. His body was already tilted in the right direction. He knew the answer even if he did not recognise that yet. “Where do we go?”

“We follow the path laid down for us.”

“Without the mysticism please.”

“Your golden-eyed Lord sees it already.”

Hua huffed. There was a stripe of grass that was slightly greener than the others, trails of frost that gleamed sharper than the rest, and a corridor of air slightly thicker than the rest. Hua didn’t know how real that greenness was, but she took it. Weiji followed it and the Shaman followed behind her.

The three of them, a shaman and the descendants of the man who had killed her people, walked together in common cause. She looked at Weiji and thought, we are never speaking of this to anyone, and she saw in his pursed lips and lined forehead an agreement. The idea of telling the Liao Patriarch that they consorted with his great enemies made her stomach recoil. It might just give her father another Qi deviation, the force of which might destroy the entire mountain.

The path was a loping one that led them out of the village grounds.

There, they found a patch of soil that stretched half a lie across and deep. It did not match with the long grasses, abrupt as it was. Men had gone and dug up the earth. They had even left the implements they used to make it.

Hua knew what she was looking at. The villagers had been buried in a mass grave.

“Nuwa preserve us. Digging up a resting ground is an abomination, true, but needs must. Know it ain’t for fancy Cultivators, but I need your help, lord Liao.”

“Stop wasting time with prayers,” Hua said, already reaching for a shovel. Weiji followed behind a moment later, not at all hiding his confusion at Hua’s willingness to help.

He could not understand the debt Hua owed.

The shaman had given her Qing. If only for a short time, with words that cut, her beloved had been there. A figment of her imagination, a lie from a vengeful ghost, whatever it could be did not matter more than the privilege to feel her once more. To touch her even if only for a moment.

And so, Hua would repay that kindness a thousand-fold.

Hua dug. They all dug. Even with two Cultivators, it was not fast going. As they got deeper, their pace slowed. It would not do to damage the corpses.

The stench is awful, Hua thought when the bodies were exposed. Men, women and children for worms to make a home. Forty of them, all in varying stages of decomposition. Their clothes were tattered and they showed signs of brutality. Arms snapped like twigs. The men had their skulls caved in. Some of the men had limbs removed. But the women were largely unharmed except for what Hua believed were shattered pelvis bones and broken hip jutting out of flesh. Deep bruises that could only come from the hands of men in Body Tempering engulfed thin thighs, bruises so deep they showed even now. The women hadn’t died from those wounds, she knew, her training in inflicting death told her so.

“This is monstrous.”

“No, it’s efficient to bury them like this,” Hua said.

“You are both wrong. Great care was put into this. Look, every corpse has a space, and they were buried with the things they loved.” Weiji pointed at the skeletal remains of a child. Gold glittered around his neck. “Their valuables weren’t stolen. Burying this many people at once isn’t an easy task. But this was done as kindly as they could. Someone cared about these villagers. Cared enough to give them a kind burial.”

“But why?” Wu Yan asked.

“This story is a mundane one. Two bandit groups most likely. I would guess this village was being extorted for protection by one group when another swept through and killed them all for any number of reasons. Then the original group found some form of honour and killed their rivals.”

Hua shot her cousin a considering look. “This isn’t one of your plays. You can’t know that.”

“Then what other reason can you come up with?” Hua would have come up with many if Weiji did not speak over her. “The bandits were castrated before they were hung? All of this is to send a message. Just staking them would be enough without taking their manhood. That’s not the calculated act of someone extorting an entire region and hiding it under our noses. That’s intentional cruelty.”

Wu Yan paled, eyes widening, and stepped away to be sick in the tall grass nearby. The sound of her retching made Hua uncomfortable.

“Oh,” Weiji said, realisation crossing his face. His eyes pinched, features taking a turn to pure discomfort.

“What?” With a grimace, her cousin explained. “Oh,” Hua said uselessly, fighting the urge to be sick herself.

But combined with a hangover, Hua could not win. She made it a dozen steps before she was ill.

Thirty of them, she thought, shuddering. All doing this. Violence was easy to process. This was not.

Weiji held her hair back as she was sick, his large hand spread across her back. Groundings her. “I shouldn’t have let you come here. I’m sorry.”

“I’m your lord. I can handle this.”

You’re a girl of fifteen, Weiji did not say but Hua heard it anyway. She glared at him. He was being foolishly protective simply because she nearly had a deviation.

“Because he cared for you more than you ever cared for me,” said the ghost of the true world.

“This is what we face,” Shaman Yan said shakily as she returned. Hua did not begrudge her that. “This is what been terrorising us. Bandits roving and killing us. You owe us, you and yours. Every coin of tax, every drop of blood, Liao lightning is owed to us.”

“Even after everything my family did to your people?”

“I ain’t got a drop of pride. That’s rich folk business, powerful folk nonsense. I got people I want to see live old and be happy. That’s all I have ever wanted since I was a kid. The reason I learned these dances and all the chants I knew. Ain’t got shit to do with your old history, your old legends, and heroic vendettas. This is about people and there isn’t a damned thing more important.”

This was faith, this was hope, this was determination.

“We’ll give them full burial rights,” Hua decided. “Their spirits will be given rest. And, I promise you this, Shaman Yan, I will kill all responsible for this.”

And then she was sick again.

***

Giving forty people burial rites whilst managing her convoy and the displaced villagers was a matter Hua left to those more suited to it. There was nothing she could do but give an order and be sick in her tent for a while longer. Young Ren gave her a cursory examination and concluded her symptoms were a side-effect of nearly having a deviation. The matter of burial rites would slow them another day, another few days, but this was a cause worth something more than just Hua’s entertainment.

Besides, she owed Wu Yan. Once, Hua had offered the gods a compromise. Her forgiveness and servitude if they let her stay with Qing. The gods had betrayed her.

Wu Yan had given her a moment, a touch, a second that lasted an eternity, and for that gift, Hua would owe her a thousandfold. Like drops of snow, add them together long enough and you would create an avalanche.

“My lord, we found another grave. This one is different.”

She followed after the soldier. It was further away, hidden in a grove of trees. Not easy to see from a distance. A beautiful location exposed to the sky.

The grave was marked by a tall stone. Flowers had been planted, twined about a wood wreath whose ends were dug into the soil. The small coffin held a corpse dressed in white, neither stained nor eaten by moths. This was the first individual grave they had come across. It was a child they found in the wooden box, a boy if Hua had to guess.

“Besides being buried away from everyone else, what makes this child so special?”

“Perhaps the villagers were burying him when they were attacked.

Young Ren said, “He has a broken hip joint. A broken pelvis. Handprints that bruised his flesh. It is possible… he may not have been alive.”

Hua hissed out a dozen foul curses, but nothing she said could match this. “They are lucky I did not encounter them alive. They would have suffered a lifetime and a day.”

That night, she dreamt of a young boy. He had the same lifeless eyes as the unlucky child in Liajiangkou but he wore the white clothes of the boy with the broken pelvis. An amalgamation of horrors, of those taken early. He, they, judged Hua and her alone responsible for what they had suffered.

“You are the lord Weilong,” he, they, said in a painfully young voice. “You were meant to save us.”

“False lord.”

“Lying lord.”

“Dying lord.”

“My lord!”

Hua woke with a flash of fear lancing across her. Bewildered, confused, she saw a frazzled cousin waking her and almost mistook him for a corpse child demanding more from her.

It was past midnight, she recognised distantly by the odd quality of the light on the tent’s surface, staring at his stark features. He had been shaking her. Hua was the lightest of sleepers and had not needed another to wake her in years. Steps approaching too quickly often woke her from her slumber, and though she was used to disturbed sleep, it was not a state she enjoyed.

For her to need Weiji to physically shake her was concerning. With bleary eyes, she rose, saying, “I’m awake, I’m awake. What is it now?”

“My Lord, the Jolly Ranger is awake.”

Hua bolted out of bed, fully awake, all thoughts of dead children gone from her mind.
