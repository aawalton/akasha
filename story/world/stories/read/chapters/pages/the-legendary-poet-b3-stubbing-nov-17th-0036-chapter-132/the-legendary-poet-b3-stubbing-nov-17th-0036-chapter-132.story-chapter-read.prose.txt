The phone buzzed against the desk.

Klaid looked down without lifting his head.

Bank Notification: Incoming transfer received.

Amount: $20,000.00 USD

From: [Wire / Verified Sender]

Memo: usual rate + bonus bro

He did not move for a moment. Then he picked up the phone, double-checked the digits, swiped through to the bank app, opened the actual statement, looked at the balance, closed the app, opened it again, and looked at the balance from a slightly different angle. The number stayed where it was.

Twenty thousand. Closer. Real closer.

He set the phone face down on the desk.

The monitor light caught the side of his face. The forum tabs sat in a neat strip across the top of the browser, each one still loaded with consequences. Somewhere outside the window, a bird made the kind of noise that suggested it had personal problems.

Klaid sat with the number.

The number sat with him.

He waited for the usual feeling. The clean ledger feeling, the good, secure, continue feeling, the small mechanical click that came when an entry resolved correctly.

Instead, something off-key arrived.

It sat in his chest like a wrong chord struck on a tuned instrument. He noticed it. He noted the location. He poked it like a tooth he suspected of betrayal.

He tried his usual labels and they slid off the side of the feeling.

He sat with it longer.

The honest answer arrived from a direction he had not prepared for. He had enjoyed the raid. Not in the sense of money confirmed and survival extended. He had enjoyed it. The puzzle of the shifting galleries. The fight against the Curator. The instant before mushin. The way the ten of them had moved by the end of the second draft, like a group that had stopped questioning each other's edges.

He had received the lion's share of loot. By an embarrassing margin. The keyword, the ink, the legendary gloves, ownership of an entire dungeon, a wisp of mythic conceptual material currently waiting in his inventory like a pet that did not understand its own size. The others had also walked out heavier than they walked in.

And now Zherdev had wired twenty thousand dollars because that was their arrangement.

Usual rate.

The phrase sat oddly across the entire transaction.

Getting paid to have fun.

He rubbed his jaw.

I am not having an artistic crisis about a wire transfer.

He almost convinced himself.

He gave the feeling sixty more seconds. He did not name it, because naming it would extend it, and right now he could not afford to extend anything. The number had landed. The number was good. He would think about the chord later.

Klaid picked up the phone.

Klaid: Received. Thanks.

He paused. Considered. Added one more line.

Klaid: Don't send more.

Klaid placed the phone face down again.

He pushed the chair back from the desk and stood up.

The apartment offered him six feet of usable floor. He used three of them, paced a slow line, and let the second problem take over from the first.

His character. Recognizable. Documented. Annotated. Memed. Circled in red on every front page of the forum he had just closed.

Autonomy. Past tense.

That was the honest accounting. The avatar was visible. The gear was distinct. The fighting style was traceable. Half the internet had already organized itself into theory cells around a single charcoal longcoat and a beret the color of bruised wine. The other half was building a spreadsheet.

He had a small consolation. His avatar's face did not match his real one. Mixed Slavic-Japanese features did not appear in the Gray Man's bland gallery of unmemorable angles. He had created himself a stranger to wear, and the stranger had become famous. The body in this room remained safely anonymous. The body in the game did not.

He could not change his avatar. Crown of Destiny locked appearance changes. There were no cosmetic shops. Identity persistence integrity. Marketing words for you are stuck with your face.

He paced another line and tried to argue with the situation.

The situation did not argue back. The situation simply sat there, finished.

Okay. The milk is on the floor. It's already spilled.

He shifted modes.

Solutions.

A cheap gear swap could handle cities. Strip the charcoal coat. Strip the beret. Find a generic linen tunic and the kind of boots that a normal 9-to-5 player wears. In Korros's plazas he could disappear into the crowd of mid-game grinders. Mumyo would have to live in the inventory until needed, which the sword would dislike. The faint pulse at his hip already disliked the idea of being put away. He could feel that small protest in advance, the way you could feel a dog deciding to sulk before the leash was even off the hook.

Cities, solvable.

The field was the problem. In open zones, in dungeons, in farming routes, he would be Kage in full kit because Kage in full kit was who Kage was in combat. If anyone saw him out there, identification would happen in under three seconds. The longcoat had its own silhouette. The blade had its personality.

He let that part be unsolved. Some problems did not get fixed. Some got managed.

He kept walking.

Individuals were tolerable. Curious players, fanboys, beggars for tips, rookies wanting an autograph. Annoyances. Cost in time and patience. Manageable cost.

The zerg guilds were the real entry on the ledger.

He knew the species. He had stripped enough of their members on dark gamer contracts in earlier games to know how the inside of the org chart smelled. Officers ran on ego and access. Recruitment captains ran on numbers. Guild leaders ran on visibility. The whole machine had one consistent failure mode. When the machine failed to acquire something it wanted, the machine started breaking the thing instead.

He would be approached. He would decline. He would be approached again with better offers. He would decline again. After that, some weeks in, the offers would stop and the harassment would start. Tail squads in his quest zones. Trash-pull aggro into his pulls. Mass-reported screenshots. PK trains during travel. The unsubtle suggestion that joining had been the easier road, and look how rocky the other road has become, friend.

He could handle a tail squad. He could handle ten, twenty, even. He could not, however, handle a slow indefinite campaign of nuisance from people who had nothing else to do with their week.

An individual cannot outlast a system. Even if the individual is the strongest individual in the system.

He stopped pacing.

He was, with reasonable confidence, in the top single digits of stats and abilities on the server. He was not a combat class, which made that placement faintly insulting to everyone who was. He had area control, terrain editing, hard CC, anything, really, that he could think of with his Verse-Crafting. Against five players, against ten, against twenty, he had answers.

Against two hundred, he had geography and a running speed.

The math came out the same as it always did. The only solution to numbers was numbers.

He stared at the wall.

The raid party arranged itself in his head before he gave it permission to. Ten people. He let the image hold for one second. He looked at it. He saw what shape it would make if he pushed on it.

He shook his head once, sharp, like clearing a screen.

Later.

He had spent too much of one morning on emotional ledger entries already. The roster could think for itself for a few days. Pieces moved on their own. He would observe.

He walked back to the desk, glanced at the dive pod in the corner. The pale blue LEDs pulsed at the same rate they always did. The thing waited for him with the patience of equipment.

He moved toward it, then stopped.

His eyes had caught on the shinai rack.

He stood for a moment with one hand half-lifted.

Maintenance.

The word sat in his head and refused to fit. He had used it for years. Mechanical maintenance. Drills. Footwork to keep his hands from forgetting. The kind of practice you did because letting muscle memory die felt obscene and because full-dive benefited from it.

The word felt costumed today.

He crossed the floor.

The shinai rack lived in its small column of light beside the window, where one shaft cut through the gap in the blackout curtain and fell across the bottom of the rack. The bottom shinai was clean. The middle shinai's grip wore the dark of long use. The top shinai sat under a grey coat of accumulated apartment.

His hand started to move toward the middle.

It hesitated.

His hand kept going, past the workhorse, past the leather darkened to mahogany, and reached for the top.

The dust had settled into a soft fur along the bamboo. The red tape at the grip had faded to the color of a healed scar. The handle felt cool. It felt like something patient that had been left alone for a long time and did not blame him.

His fingers paused half an inch from the leather. He noticed the small tremor in his own hand. Just a vibration along the tendon.

He took the shinai.

The wrap fit his palm.

He held the sword without doing anything with it. He did not lift it into a stance. He did not test the balance. He simply stood in his own apartment with a dusty shinai in his hand and let the air around him do whatever it wanted to do.

His eyes closed.

He breathed.

The kitchenette tap dripped twice. The fridge ran its low hum. Somewhere on the floor above, a chair scraped. The pipes in the wall ticked through their own small grievances. Klaid registered each sound, accepted each sound, and stopped registering anything in particular.

Time moved without making a point about it.

If a stranger had walked into the apartment in that moment, the stranger would not have seen anything happen. They would have seen a young man standing in a six-by-four-foot stretch of floor with a wooden practice sword in his hand and his eyes closed. They might have decided he was tired. They might have decided he was praying. They might not have decided anything at all and then later, walking home, they might have remembered the room and not been able to say what was different about it.

He held the shinai for a long time.

He set it back in the rack.

The dust on the bamboo was disturbed where his hand had touched. The rest still wore its grey coat. He left it that way.

He took the middle shinai down.

The grip met his palm like a sentence he had been writing for years.

He moved into the small clear stretch of floor between the bed and the kitchenette. He set his feet. He breathed in once, slow, through the nose. He breathed out through the mouth. He performed the most basic suburi he knew.

He did it ten times.

He did it ten more.

The shinai cut a quiet line through the apartment air. His shoulder kept its place. His wrist kept its angle. His feet did not slide on the floor. The water stain on the ceiling watched him from its archipelago of mold, indifferent.

He stopped on a clean exhale.

He returned the middle shinai to its place on the rack.

He looked at the top shinai one more time. The dust had not been replaced. The faded red tape sat in its small column of sun.

He turned away from the rack, crossed the room, and opened the dive pod.

The blue light pulsed slowly across the foam interior. The cold metal of the neural nodes touched his temple as he lay back. The lid closed over him with the soft sigh of compressed air.

He logged in.
