Barry stood at the edge of the only perfectly level stretch of floor in the place, knuckles throbbing where the broken Bifrost crystals had torn into them.

"Why isn't this shit healing? Don't I have some Odinforce thing going on?"

He reached for that energy, tried to coax something out of it, and got exactly what he'd expected: nothing. No Asgardian blood, no way to process a force like that.

He'd hoped the System might throw him a bone. It stayed silent. Again. Apparently it wasn't in the mood for a sane conversation.

So he dug into the status panel the hard way, like pulling up a log file, and found one new entry at the bottom.

[Regeneration attempt failed.]

[Foreign interference detected.]

[Source: unknown.]

"Bandages it is."

He flexed his hand, still stiff from his own clumsy stitching. The skin under the gauze should hold for now.

Across the big room, his team of dwarves was hard at work on the jobs he'd handed out. The only pay he'd promised was a chance to step onto his floor, and somehow that was enough to keep them moving.

He remembered every one of them now. Names, wants, needs, what they hoped to become, even which relatives a few of them had no business getting close to. None of it mattered to him. All he needed was competence, and they had it. The dexterity in those hands was ridiculous.

"Lord Barry," one of them called, stepping forward. He couldn't quite look Barry in the eye.

"Skip the lord, Dwarf-One. What do you want?"

The dwarf stopped dead at the line, though his feet clearly wanted to carry him the rest of the way.

"I finished the wiring like you told me, lo— sir Barry. And I brought the book on Aesir magic."

Barry crooked a finger. The dwarf stepped over the line, and his shoulders dropped.

"Ohhh..."

"Don't start."

"Lord Barry, this floor—"

"It's a floor. A simple floor."

"It is the greatest floor I have ever experienced."

"I know, I know."

"Please, please, teach us the ways of this technology, and..." He faltered, hunting for the word. "Science. Yes. Science."

"Ha. Get used to the floor first. Then you can work your way up to kissing my ass." Barry plucked the book out of his hands.

He read it carefully, committing every page to memory. Five minutes later he tossed it back. The dwarf caught it and stumbled off the level floor, looking like a man who'd just been hauled out of a hot bath.

"Ohhh..." Dwarf-One sighed. "It's not enough, but I can work with it."

Barry started writing, then drawing, boiling down everything he'd just read along with every other book he'd gone through these past few days. The science of energy he already knew down to the last detail. One thing still bothered him. There was an absurd amount of it flowing through everything here, enough to run his home planet for eons. Even he couldn't pull that off.

"Where does all this infinite energy actually come from?"

Bit by bit, as the calculations clicked through and the puzzle pieces slid into place, a theory took shape. With Barry, that usually just meant a description of how things actually worked.

The key to all this manufactured magic, all this energy, was Yggdrasil.

[Hypothesis confirmed.]

[Yggdrasil is not merely transmitting energy.]

[It is generating its own energy as well.]

Barry's pen stopped.

"...Oh."

[Warning: theoretical extraction would constitute interference with a foundational universal structure.]

Barry stared at the words. Then he smiled.

"So it works."

The System had meant it as a warning. Barry read it as a feasibility study.

His hand itched to harness it, to take every last drop for himself. He could already picture the inventions he'd unleash on this wretched fantasy world. Then he glanced down at the page. At some point, without his noticing, his pen had started on something else, and there it sat, dead center: his long-lost project, the one he'd died trying to finish. The multiversal portal gun.

Nothing like the magic or the Bifrost he'd found here. A machine that could open a doorway between realities. He'd already confirmed the theory, and his past experiments had shown him it was real. All that remained was building the thing.

"Not now," he said, folding the page. "Eventually."

He turned back to the work at hand and tossed a stack of papers to the dwarves, the exact figures they needed to fix the bridge. They followed his numbers with such blind faith that they'd stopped using their own methods altogether.

He could fix the bridge. He'd also told everyone it would blow again in a few eons, same as before, and a lot sooner if Thor kept hitting things with that hammer. Making it Thor-proof, or Asgardian-proof in general, required a special mineral from one of the books he'd read.

Thor had promised to take him to get it. That was four days ago, and Barry was still waiting.

"Oh, there he is," Barry muttered.

Thor strode toward him, Mjolnir resting on his shoulder, and waved. "Barry! Heimdall's at the bridge. He's got something he wants you to look at before we leave."

Everyone else knows my schedule before I even decide on it, Barry thought. Worth noting. Not worth commenting on.

He nodded, and they headed over together.

At the end of the damaged stretch, Heimdall stood staring into the void, spear in hand, watching over all the realms with his all-seeing eye.

Thor nodded to him, then gestured at Barry. "Heimdall, this is Barry. The genius who leveled that floor so well the dwarves forgot how to stand."

"A mortal who holds Thor's attention?" Heimdall said. "That's rarer than a stable bridge! Hahahaha!"

Barry didn't laugh. He was looking at the spear, and he knew right away it wasn't Asgardian work. The clasp on the casing was too clean, too precise. Machined, not forged by dwarven hands.

Heimdall's smile faded. "You're not admiring it."

"No."

Heimdall caught the meaning behind that look, and the smile slowly crept back. "You're taking it apart."

"In my head," Barry said. It struck him then that Heimdall didn't just watch the realms. He understood them. A true observer.

"That's what worries me." Heimdall turned the spear in his hand anyway. "It's also why I called for you. This spear was born from the Bifrost itself, centuries ago, before the last realm collapse. No smith since has worked out what it's made of. I carry it because I hate unsolved things sitting around."

"If I'd had this when the bridge went," Barry said flatly, "Mjolnir would've been my second choice."

That got Heimdall's attention. It got the System's, too. So it did talk when it had something to say.

[Composition: unresolved. 34% match to Divine Alloy signature. Insufficient data.]

"Not nothing," Barry said after a pause. "That's about a third of what I'm looking for. Can I have it, Sir Heimdall?"

"Ah, I'm afraid I can't part with it, Sir Barry. With this, I guide the whole of the Nine Realms." Heimdall stepped closer, as if to give him a friendly pat on the back. "I'm sor—"

Barry stepped back, out of reach. "No touching, please. If you touch me while I'm wearing this jacket, you're dead."

"Ha! Hahahahaha!" Heimdall roared. "He's quite amusing, isn't he, Thor?"

Thor's face didn't change. "Mm. He's not lying."

Heimdall studied the mortal a moment longer, then nodded. "You keep interesting company, Thor. I'd happily keep you both here a while, but you have a mission waiting." He raised his spear and slammed it down on the floor.

Tang!

The Bifrost opened at once. With a bow, they stepped through.

The trip was short, but Muspelheim was the farthest of the realms, and the moment they landed, it felt like it.

The air reeked of sulfur and smoke, and the sky was a fiery, ash-colored haze. The gravity pressed harder here, and every step cost a little more than the last.

Barry's jacket reacted instantly, wrapping him in something transparent that took the worst of the strain.

"Compress," he said, and the suit melted against his skin until it was nearly invisible.

Thor stared, eyes wide. "What was that?"

"Nothing. Just a space suit for everything."

"Space suit?"

Barry didn't answer. He looked around instead, Thor following him toward the far shore. At the edge of a lake, he crouched and picked up a single scale. It was warm and faintly vibrating, and it matched nothing in any book he'd read. He turned it over for a moment, pocketed it, then tapped his waist. Thor watched the engineer's jacket reconfigure itself again, mid-motion.

A small hammer now hung from Barry's belt, its head glowing with the same blue-white current that ran through Mjolnir.

Thor's eyes narrowed. The hammer was tiny, barely bigger than a palm, and yet Mjolnir had stirred against his shoulder, the way it did when lightning was close. Stronger? No. Just different, and Thor couldn't put a name to how.

He almost asked. Then he decided to let it go, for now.

Barry shifted the jacket again. The inner lining rearranged itself to hold a set of calibration rods, a micro-emitter, and a folded resonance cage no bigger than his closed fist.

Thor's voice dropped. "You've been busy. I don't remember you having those tools before."

"Ha! Spoken like someone who was ordered to keep me in check. And yes, I've been busy. I need to get all this done and reti—" He stopped.

"What?"

"Nothing. Just keep watch. I don't plan on getting killed by some fantasy alien mush."

Black glass stretched out in every direction, rivers of cooled lava glowing orange through the rock. Barry's System kept feeding him readings: spikes in ambient temperature, stress along the far ridgeline. But they all came down to one number. The lake's surface output was rising and rising and rising.

Thor leaned on his hammer. "What are you actually after, engineer? I get the feeling it's more than just some mineral."

"I'm after... independence," Barry said at last, still looking around.

"Hmm?"

Barry stood and turned to face him. "I was going to keep this from you, but I see you as a friend, Thor." He tapped Thor's massive arm. "In plain words, which you can understand, I want something of my own."

Thor frowned. "Your own what?"

"A gate. One I can use to get around."

"You mean the Bifrost? You can't build a Bifrost." He stopped short, his ears refusing to take in what this mortal had just said.

"Good. I don't want one." Barry pointed back at the scorch mark where the Bifrost had set them down. "Too religious a name. I want the failure. Let's call it that."

"The failure?"

"The error that makes the hole. The Bifrost is a controlled failure of space. It only fails when Odin says so, and it takes Heimdall to run it. I want one I can trigger on my own schedule."

Thor stared at him. "You say that like you're talking about building a shed."

"A shed doesn't need dimensional stabilization. But good, you seem to understand where I'm coming from."

"Somehow, that sounds worse."

"The principle is the same."

"No. It absolutely is not." Thor shook his head. "And you'd still need something Odinforce, which defeats the whole point of independence."

"Exactly. That's why we're here."

"Huh! I thought we came for the bridge mineral."

"That too."

"Barry," Thor grunted.

"Yeah, yeah. We'll get to it." Barry looked at the scorch mark again. "You want to know why? Ten years I spent building things for other people. I'm done waiting for someone else to decide whether I get to use what I built."

Thor didn't answer right away. He studied Barry, a man who clearly carried more than he let on. "Then we'd best find what you came for," he said at last.

The stone under their feet began to glow.

Thin orange lines crept through the cracks in the black rock and spread outward like veins. The temperature kept climbing. Barry's suit held, but the number in the corner of his vision ticked up a degree every few steps. Then the System started throwing warnings across his vision, each one more aggressive than the last.

[WARNING: ENVIRONMENTAL CONDITIONS UNSUITABLE FOR STANDARD MATERIAL FABRICATION.]

Barry ignored them as long as he could, but something out here wanted his attention, and badly.

Then the readings stopped making sense. The lake's output was still rising, but its surface temperature was dropping. Barry tapped the side of his head as if that would help.

"Thor, I think the planet's about to explode, or some freakish heat-related fantasy creature is on its way."

The calibration rods in his jacket began to hum. The micro-emitter locked onto a rhythm he hadn't set, and the little hammer on his belt flickered in time with it. He cut the emitter's power. It kept pulsing.

Under the gauze, his knuckles throbbed. Harder this time.

[Foreign interference intensity: increasing.]

Thor felt it too. His body, which never gave up so much as a drop of sweat, was suddenly drenched.

"Barry..."

"Hmm."

Silence.

"I think we need to g—"

The ground ahead split open with a deep, grinding roar, revealing a vast valley buried beneath the mountain. At the center of it, on a ridge of black stone ringed by fire, something moved. Something with horns. Many, many horns.

Then a dragon's head uncoiled.

It rose out of the valley, bigger than the workshop they'd left behind. Thor's hand tightened around Mjolnir.

"We really need to go."

Barry didn't answer. His System was screaming, and not over the size, or the heat, or the output. He stared at the readings, and his expression slowly changed.

"That's impossible."

Thor glanced at him. "What?"

Barry pointed at the dragon. "It's putting out the same energy signature as the Bifrost."

The dragon opened one enormous eye. The scale in Barry's pocket went hot. The System flashed red.

[WARNING.]

[Unknown energy architecture detected.]

[Similarity to Bifrost: 87.4%.]

Barry forgot about the bridge. Forgot about Thor, about getting home, about the hand throbbing under the gauze. He smiled.

"Thor."

"What?"

Barry slowly reached for his notebook.

"I think I found the last ingredient."
