The day of the festival.

It had crept up quietly. The past couple of days after the assassin attack had been slow but steady. Veronica kept her movements subtle, letting herself blend in just enough not to rouse suspicion.

She visited the ruins yesterday, alone. The cultists were long gone, of course. Nothing left but the faint scent of scorched soil and the lingering memory of demonic residue clinging to the air like stale breath. She’d walked the ritual site twice, careful with her steps, but there had been little to learn beyond what she already knew.

If the Ashen Covenant knew she’d been there, they hadn’t acted on it. Then again, reconnaissance and tracking were never her strengths. No matter how long she stared at the footprints in the dirt or the burn patterns carved into the stones, no clarity came, only guesswork.

For most of the past two days, she weaved through Greystone, trying to gather information.

She visited shops, passed by the mines, and even browsed the old church archives for a time. She made a mental note to start paying closer attention to the townsfolk—their faces, builds, and voices.

If any of them matched the silhouettes she saw performing the summoning—well, it wasn’t obvious. No matching limps, no scars she recognized.

Perhaps those particular cultists hadn’t been residents at all.

Near noon, there was a person of interest she had spoken to. A member of House Ronswick. However, they seemed completely innocent. They were just here early to enjoy the town and to inform the Baron of any accommodations they might require beforehand. Things like food allergies or when to prepare for the envoy’s arrival.

She tried to pester them about who the envoy was, but they wouldn’t budge.

Practically—it was a dead end. The cultists just hid themselves too cleanly. And with the envoy’s arrival, there was nothing else she could do to stop them in advance. There wasn’t enough time.

But that also provided her an opportunity.

If the Covenant had people inside Greystone, then the envoy’s arrival was the moment they would use them. Too many eyes would be turned toward the square; too many guards distracted by the ceremony. It was the perfect time to move unseen.

But that meant it was also the perfect time to catch them in the act.

And to do that, she’d need the help of a few people.

Stalls lined the main square, decorated with bright cloth and faded streamers. Lanterns were strung between rooftops. A long banquet table—gaudy and expensive-looking—had been laid out the night before, and this morning, half the town was already buzzing around it, polishing silver and setting up trays. All of the miners had been given the day off, their pickaxes replaced with paintbrushes and garden shears as they helped adorn the streets with flowers and banners.

All of it felt just a bit too excessive.

At first, Veronica had assumed it was mere theatrics for a noble’s arrival—Welterman trying to play the part of a generous host. But after a little digging, she’d discovered there was more to it.

The Ronswick envoy wasn’t just passing through. They were bringing something with them. A donation, apparently. Payment. Tribute. Whatever it was, it was part of a private deal with the baron, and the town had been ordered to celebrate as if it were a gift from the gods.

Veronica stood just outside the square, watching the early stream of townspeople rush about with baskets and ribbons. The sun was barely over the rooftops, and already the scent of roasted nuts and honeyed bread wafted through the air.

Veronica dusted her palms together, then reached for the next garland of dried lilies.

“Hold it up just a bit higher, dear,” came the voice beside her. “There—that’s perfect.”

She stepped back, letting Mrs. Sena admire the floral drape from the cobblestone path. The vegetable vendor, dressed in a thick apron with streaks of pollen on her sleeves, offered a satisfied hum. Her cheeks were already flushed from exertion, her graying hair tied back with a green ribbon that matched the faded color of her stall’s awning.

Veronica found herself enjoying the rhythm of the work. Simple. Repetitive. Tangible. She didn’t necessarily enjoy the labor, but getting to move around and do normal things for once felt satisfying.

“Hard to believe we’ll have a noble in town today,” Sena said, setting her basket down and pulling out another coil of wildflowers. “Proper nobility, I mean. Not just messengers or coin-counters.”

Veronica handed her the loop of binding twine. “You mean the Ronswicks?”

“Aye.” Sena nodded. “They have frequent dealings with Baron Welterman, coming and going, but this time’s different. They’re donating. Big money, too—enough to change things ’round here.”

Veronica adjusted the last loop, letting it rest across the beam of the merchant’s house. The scent of crushed herbs clung to her fingers.

Nearby, she heard some middle-aged people talking.

“About time some coin came back to us.”

“You know he’s gonna pocket most of it anyway.”

Veronica raised a brow. She turned toward Sena. “What kind of changes?”

“Oh, plenty. Pavement on the east road, for starters. Some proper lamps, not those fire-blasted torches we have to relight every day. Tools for the mines, too—new harnesses, sharper blades. Even the kids’ll feel it once the school’s got better windows.”

“And this is all thanks to the baron?” Veronica asked, glancing sideways at the men who were walking away, grumbling.

“So they say. Welterman’s the one who negotiated the deal. Got the Ronswicks to part with their coin for our sake.” Sena leaned back, one hand on her hip, watching the workers file through the square. “Can’t say I like his charm, but credit where it’s due.”

Veronica offered a polite nod but said nothing. Her gaze drifted toward the square, where the energy in town had grown brighter with each passing hour. Tables creaked across the plaza, chairs were being set, and plates arranged in neat rows, all through the joint efforts of the people of Greystone.

It wasn’t all just manpower making it happen, however.

Stones shifted all around.

The earth itself rose in a gentle slope, rippling like a slow tide as it carved into a rounded platform. Debris swept neatly to the side. A large plank of polished wood floated across the open space, guided by invisible hands.

At the center of it all stood Elise.

Her hand moved through the air in slow, deliberate patterns. Thin veins of light traced her fingertips, mana responding not just to command but to nuance. The platform finished settling into place as if it had always belonged there.

Veronica watched closely.

That level of control wasn’t easy. At least, not for a mere second tier. It was sculpted intent and not simply manipulation. While Veronica’s Path of Ruin demanded overwhelming energy stabilized by sheer will, Sculpting was something else entirely. It relied on discipline. The ability to shift mana like clay. Perhaps the girl’s second path was the Path of Focus, though she wasn’t certain.

Veronica folded her arms, just for a moment, as she watched the platform lock into place and Elise move onto the next area.

“Ahh, now that’s a real mage,” Sena said with a smile, shielding her eyes from the sun as she looked out. “Does things the clean way. Not all fire and fury.”

Veronica allowed herself a faint smirk. Sena most likely wasn’t privy to all the details that had happened in the square with the mercenaries. Different rumors had been going around—Veronica obliterating the gang, the guards saving a damsel in distress—the actual news was all very messy. Unless you were there in the square to witness it firsthand, no one knew what she had actually done.

Most people now knew there was a mage in town now, but not everyone necessarily knew it was her. Either that, or they didn’t particularly care.

“Different schools of thought,” Veronica said with a smile.

The garlands were finished. The square was almost ready. And the day had only just begun.

When the time came, Veronica left the square and made her way to the edge of the open space she called a park.

The benches there were familiar now. Weathered wood, soft sun, the chatter of townsfolk passing by just far enough not to intrude or bother her. She liked that. The rhythm of this space. A quiet, living bubble.

She sat down; a paper-wrapped roll rested in her hand, still warm from the baker’s oven. The top had been split open and glazed with cream sugar, soft and sticky enough to cling to her fingers.

Beside it sat a cup of tart lemonade.

A shadow dropped onto the bench across from her.

Finn landed with a quiet thump, his legs tucked beneath him, looking far too pleased with himself.

Veronica opened one eye. “Did you do what I told you?”

“Yep,” Finn said, puffing out his chest. “Got it all done. Well, mostly all done.”

Her eye narrowed.

He quickly raised both hands. “Not because I messed up. Just because you asked for a lot. I went around and got some of the other kids to help hang the window chimes with those small wires lying on the ground all around the edge of town. Houses near the outer roads, the fence gaps, the places by the old sheds. Anywhere people don’t really look.”

Veronica took another bite of her roll. “And the branches?”

“Did those too,” he said. “I spread them around some of the narrow paths between alleys and near the town gates. Dry ones, like you asked. But I couldn’t get everywhere yet.”

“That’s fine,” Veronica said. “You don’t need to cover every path.”

Finn tilted his head slightly. “I don’t?”

“No. I have Old Thom helping out too, covering areas that you haven’t.”

“Old Thom? Why him?”

Veronica washed down the sweetness with a sip of lemonade. The tartness cut through the glaze, both refreshing and sharp. “Because unlike most people in this town, I’m inclined to believe he’s trustworthy enough,” she said. “He’s the only one who spoke out about the cultists, even when everyone laughed at him for it.”

Finn’s face shifted slightly. He looked away, swinging one leg beneath the bench. “Yeah. Guess that makes sense.” He leaned forward, lowering his voice. “But why exactly did you need us to do all that? I get the chimes, sort of. Festival stuff. But the branches? Are you trying to trip someone?”

Veronica tapped a finger against the side of her cup. “It’s a surveillance system.”

“A what?”

“With those chimes and branches, I’ll be able to know whenever a cultist decides to make a move. If they happen to leave or enter town during the festival.”

Finn frowned slightly. “Stepping on a branch isn’t that loud. Unless you’re standing right there. Most people are going to be in the town square. How are you going to hear someone stepping on a branch from so far away?”

“Don’t worry about it. I have my ways.”

He looked skeptical. “That’s a cool line,” he said, his face deadpan.

Veronica rolled her eyes.

She leaned back again, her gaze drifting toward the square. The platform had finished taking shape. Banners were rising. People were starting to gather. The Ronswicks would arrive at any second.

The festival had begun.

And so had the clock.
