CHAPTER 25

“Every field operative will encounter the question: ‘Why didn’t you warn us?’ The answer - that warning without context produces panic and panic produces death - will not satisfy the person asking. Accept this. The work was never designed to be understood by the people it protects.” - Codex of the Long Watch, Operational Directive

They knew the restaurant was empty even before going in.

The barricade had been dismantled. The tables that had blocked the entrance were stacked neatly against the interior wall and the chairs pushed in. Someone had taken the time to leave the space orderly. That wasn’t a panicked abandonment.

Donna went through the door with an axe held low and checked the kitchen, the back-office, and the storeroom. The body near the counter was still there but the survivors were gone. And Emma was gone with them.

“The walk-in fridge was cleared out; it was not ransacked in panic. The perishables were taken, the dry goods left. Knife block is missing the three I gave them. The first aid kit from the back office is gone too. This looks like a planned evacuation.”

Steve was looking at something near the till. He called out, “Come have a look at this.”

Someone had used a knife or something similar to scratch words on the counter.

EM

ARSENAL

ARMY CAMP

An arrow next to the letters pointed north-west. The letters were hurried but deep enough that nobody could mistake them for accidental scratches.

Donna touched the mark, her fingertips resting on the scratched counter. Arthur could see worry change to relief then back to worry on her face. EM was what Donna called Emma.

“She evacuated,” Donna said finally. “And she did it properly. Took food, weapons, and medical supplies. Then left a destination. She’s smart and probably has the restaurant staff with her.”

“She knew you’d come back,” Arthur said.

Donna nodded and checked the street through the front window, already mapping their route.

“The stadium is about six kilometres. Moorgate, Old Street, then north through Islington.”

“Six kilometres there and six back if we plan on stepping onto the platform,” Steve said.

“Do you have somewhere more important to be?”

“Of course not, I’m just noting it for efficient planning.”

“Then why are we still standing here?” she said, turning towards the door. “Let’s go.”

“Since when did the Emirates turn into an army camp?” Arthur asked, as they stepped outside.

“Not a terrible idea. Controlled space, controlled access points. If they have set up some camp, they could have done a lot worse than a stadium.” Donna said.

They went north through Moorgate and Old Street. Curtains shifted as they passed. People watched from upper-floor windows and rooftops, some holding tools or lengths of timber.

Outside a shuttered off-licence, two mutated rats lay in the road. One had been crushed beneath a paving slab. The other had a kitchen knife buried beneath its jaw. A broken broom handle lay nearby, dark with blood.

“Oi,” a man called, leaning from the first floor of a terraced house with a cricket bat tucked beneath one arm. “You lot come from the City?”

“Yes,” Arthur called back.

“How bad is it?”

“Pretty damn bad. Passable if you stay together.”

The man looked at their weapons. “Where you lot heading?”

“Arsenal. The Emirates. Got told army got a camp there.”

“Heard that myself. Take Upper Street and keep off Essex Road. Rats have got into the shops.”

“Upper Street. Thanks.”

The man nodded and pulled his head inside. Arthur heard him calling for someone to get their bags before the window closed.

As they continued north Arthur noticed more signs of life. Chalk arrows marked buildings with water. Bedding hung from balconies. Two men stood watch on the roof of a school, raising a hand as the party passed below.

London was wounded, but it was fighting back.

The stadium was visible long before they reached it.

The silver curve of the Emirates dominated the skyline, a massive bowl of steel and glass rising over the terraces. Smoke climbed from somewhere inside the ground in several thin columns.

The military perimeter began two streets out.

Buses had been parked across the wider approaches with their tyres deflated. Crowd-control barriers funnelled anyone arriving towards the southern entrance. Soldiers in camouflage watched from behind concrete planters and overturned maintenance vehicles.

The closer they came, the more organised it became. Coloured signs divided the approach into ARRIVALS, MEDICAL and SUPPLIES. Names were scribbled in marker pen on sheets of plywood near the entrance, arranged alphabetically. People stood around searching for family.

A corporal at the inner barrier looked at the Shield, Donna’s axe and the Reacher resting across Steve’s shoulder.

“Evening,” he said. “Weapons don’t go beyond the red barriers.”

“We’re looking for someone,” Donna said.

“So is everybody else. Weapons cage is over there.” He said, pointing towards what used to be a ticket office. Racks had been constructed inside from scaffolding poles. Axes, hammers, kitchen knives and improvised spears stood under the watch of two soldiers.

Donna looked at the cage, then towards the hundreds of civilians moving through the stadium concourse.

“I’ll keep the shield,” Arthur said.

The corporal shook his head. “If you can hit somebody with it, it’s a weapon.”

“It’s defensive.”

“So’s a brick wall until it falls on you. It stays this side of the barrier.”

“I’ll stay with the gear,” Arthur said, looking at Donna and Steve. “You two go inside.”

Donna studied the approaches, the soldiers and the controlled flow through the turnstiles. Apparently satisfied, she unhooked the axe and handed it to Arthur and joined the crowd. Steve handed the Reacher and hurried after.

Arthur carried the weapons to the designated area but stopped short of placing them in the rack.

“I’m staying with these.”

One of the soldiers guarding the cage looked at the faint blue sheen on the weapons.

“Suit yourself. Stay behind the white line and don’t start waving anything about. We’ve had enough excitement.”

Arthur rested the Bullguard against a concrete bollard and settled in to wait. He had been waiting about twenty minutes when an officer walked up.

He was in his mid-fifties, broad through the shoulders with a day’s growth of grey beard and a paper clipboard tucked beneath one arm. HARGREAVES was stitched above his breast pocket. The crown on his rank slide marked him as a major.

“Now then. Sergeant says you’re the fellow who brought a medieval armoury to my gate.” His Yorkshire had been softened by years in the Army but still there.

“Three weapons and a shield.”

“Aye. That would be the armoury.”

Major Hargreaves examined the marks across the Bullguard. His attention moving to the dried material caught along its rim and then to the Conduit-Edge Axe.

“You’ve been fighting.”

“Yes.”

He drew a pencil from behind his ear and turned to a fresh sheet on the clipboard.

“What have you seen?”

“How much do you already know?”

“Plenty of stories. Not much I’d trust. Don’t give me the grand version. Give me whatever keeps my lads alive.”

“Animals are changing. We –a few foxes, we have seen them working together and testing position before committing. We’ve also encountered other things that weren’t …….animals.”

“What sort of things?”

“Ether seems to be mutating what it finds, even if the thing is not an animal.”

Hargreaves’s pencil paused.

“We’ve had three reports of those. Thought the first bloke had taken a knock to the head. Second one was a paramedic, so that made it less convenient. Anything else?”

“The animals are also getting much stronger.”

“Aye. Seen that. But so are we.”

Hargreaves tapped the edge of the shield with his pencil.

“And how did you manage to do this?”

Arthur told him about the cores and how they can be used for crafting.

“Bin lid, eh? Impressive. So, you have seen the blue writing as well?”

“Everyone alive has seen it, I think,” Arthur said. “Anyone killing anything is absorbing Ether that makes you stronger.”

The major absorbed that without visible surprise.

“Thought it might be summat like that. World doesn’t generally hand out numbers unless it expects somebody to keep score.”

“Start small, rats and things like that. Groups of five, open ground with room to disengage. And don’t –do not pursue anything into a building, yeah.”

The Major gave a dry humourless snort. “I’ve been teaching CQB since before you were in long trousers, lad. We’ll manage.”

“You looking to join the group inside?”

“No.”

“Shame. I’ve got a stadium full of civilians, soldiers with equipment that’s becoming less reliable by the hour and reports that half of London’s wildlife has developed ambitions.”

“We have somewhere we need to go.”

“Aye. Reckon you do.”

Hargreaves closed the clipboard. “Right lad, if you find anything else useful, bring it back.”

“That I can do.” Arthur nodded, shaking the Major’s hands.

Donna and Steve emerged after another hour or so.

Steve looked relieved. Donna looked composed, but her eyes were slightly red. She crossed directly to the weapons and lifted the axe.

“And?” Arthur asked.

“She is fine. Unhurt, and all six from the restaurant made it here.”

She checked the axe head and returned it to her shoulder.

“They are working in the field kitchens. Emma is helping coordinate civilian stores and family registration.”

“She’s staying? I thought she would be coming with us.” Arthur asked.

“Don’t be daft. There are walls, trained personnel, medical staff and nearly two thousand civilians. She has food, water and people who need her.” Donna said, tightening the axe strap. “We are walking towards an alien transit platform and god knows what then, I’m not dragging my sister into that.”

Arthur looked at her for a long time as she checked and rechecked her equipment.

“You could stay with her, you know. If you wanted to.” He said finally.

“Don’t be daft.” She said and turned towards the street.

They left the stadium by the southern checkpoint and headed back towards the city.

***

They headed south towards the river. The transit chamber was waiting beneath Temple Church, but Donna had already laid out the problem while they walked. They shouldn’t step onto an alien transit platform without supplies, without preparation and without knowing if there was food or water on the other side.

“We need supplies before we do anything else,” she said. “Water. Food. Medical kit. Proper packs. We’re running on fumes and memory of a protein bar.”

The change and the tragedy were all around them as they walked back.

They found a body on Cannon Street. A woman in her mid-forties, lying in a doorway with her left leg torn off. The wound was ragged and they could see teeth marks in the exposed bone. Something had fed on her and moved on.

A second was on a side street near Blackfriars. It was a young man, in running gear. He hadn’t been fast enough for whatever had caught up to him.

Donna catalogued each body with professional detachment without commenting on any of it. Steve was having a more difficult time of it. His jaw clenching and unclenching repeatedly.

“The feeding pattern indicates territorial range establishment. Consistent with early-stage apex behaviour observed in comparable Ether-fauna ecosystems. The creatures are selecting fixed hunting corridors.” Archie said.

Donna glanced at Archie who was hovering beside Arthur’s shoulder. “Hunting corridors also means they’re predictable.”

“Predictable is not synonymous with safe.”

They had walked another hundred metres or so when Steve smelled food.

“Smoke,” he said, stopping. “No. Cooking. Someone’s cooking.”

Arthur smelled it a moment later. Something savoury on the air, tinned soup, maybe, or beans heated over an open flame. It was the smell of civilisation, incongruous and specific, drifting from somewhere south of the main road. His stomach growled.

They followed it and the smell led them off Blackfriars Road and down a residential side street to a five-storey block of flats. It was council-built, in drab 1960s concrete, the sort of utilitarian housing that Arthur’s brain classified as structurally sound if aesthetically bankrupt.

The ground-floor windows were boarded with furniture, and the main entrance was barricaded with a shipping pallet and two filing cabinets. A man sat on the first-floor balcony watching the street with a length of scaffolding pipe across his knees.

The courtyard beyond the barricade was visible through the gaps, a small, paved space between the building’s wings, sheltered on three sides by the block itself. Fires burned in metal drums and tarpaulins were strung between balconies.

The man on the balcony called down. “You lot lost?”

“Looking for supplies. We can trade.” Arthur said.

“Trade what?”

“Knowledge. About the creatures. About how to fight them.”

The man looked down at the Bullguard Shield and the faint blue glow on its surface. And then at Donna’s Conduit-Edge Axe resting casually in her hands.

“Wait there. Don’t get no funny ideas.”

Two minutes later, the pallet shifted and a large man in his fifties squeezed through. He had what Arthur liked to think of as the pub-landlord build. He was the kind of person who’d fallen into authority because he was large, practical and loud.

“I’m Geoff. You don’t look like the usual strays.”

“I hope not. We’re passing through.”

“That thing’s glowing.” Geoff said, pointing at the shield again.

“Yes.”

“Why?”

“That’s a long answer. Mind if we come in?” Arthur asked.

Geoff thought for a second then let them through. The courtyard was more organised than the exterior suggested. Zones were chalked on the concrete: FOOD, WATER, MEDICAL, SLEEP. The medical zone was only a stretched tarpaulin over two camping mattresses, but it was something.

The food zone had folding tables covered in tins arranged by type. This was the organisational logic of a man who’d run a pub and understood that people behaved better when systems were visible and made sense.

Books stood upright on a plank near the medical area, propped between two bricks. There was a first aid manual, a camping primer and a guide to edible plants in the British Isles. Each one had a consultation slip tucked in the front cover with columns for date, name, and duration of use.

"Some woman from the university," Geoff said, catching Arthur's look. "Turned up yesterday and set all that up. Says the books stay on the shelf. Consultation only. She’s coming back tomorrow to check them slips."

Steve's jaw tightened with amusement and he looked away.

“How many have you got in here?” Arthur asked.

“Had seventy of us after the first night,” Geoff said. “Some went. Some...” He didn’t finish. “Down to sixty-two now. Mostly locals. Nobody’s got a clue what’s happening, mind. Plenty of theories, though, each dafter than the last.”

“What’s yours?” Steve asked.

“Chemical weapon. Government test went tits up.”

“Chemical weapons won’t—" Steve started. Donna looked at him and he closed his mouth.

Arthur got working on the barricade, mostly out of habit. He repositioned the filing cabinets as the primary barrier and reinforced the pallet with a wardrobe that three of Geoff’s people helped carry from a ground-floor flat.

Geoff watched with his arms folded. “Ran a pub for twenty years, mate. I know how to keep people out.”

“You kept drunks out. Things coming through there won’t be drunks.”

Geoff considered that, nodded and then helped with the wardrobe.

Donna assessed the watch rotations and found three blind spots. She fixed them without framing it as criticism and gave some general security tips.

A woman sat apart, holding a child, a girl of four or five, limp against her chest. The child was flushed with her breathing shallow. Though the child didn’t look in obvious discomfort.

“How long?” Arthur asked crouching next to them.

“She’s had a temperature since the sky changed. She won’t eat.” It was the controlled voice of someone holding together by refusing to acknowledge what might happen if she didn’t. “A few other smaller children are the same. There aren’t any doctors.”

Arthur looked at Steve. Steve gave a small nod and drifted around the corner of the building, out of earshot. Donna shifted position to block the approach.

“Archie. The child. Is the fever dangerous?”

“Juvenile Nadi Matrix integration produces elevated thermoregulatory response,” Archie said. “The adaptation is biological. Not pathological. The fever will resolve within thirty-six to forty-eight hours.”

He paused then continued in a quieter register, “Children adapt fast. They are more resilient than your species typically assumes.”

Steve returned and the relief on his face told Arthur what he needed to know.

“We’ve seen this before. Changes are happening to everyone, adults process it one way. Children run fevers. Their bodies adapt fast and the adaptation looks like illness. The fever will break, there is nothing to worry about,” he said to the mother crouching beside Arthur.

“How do you know?”

He didn’t of course, not for sure. He’d seen exactly zero other children since Day Zero. And the information came from a floating alien construct whose reliability was approximately the same as everything else in this new world: unknown, untested, and unfortunately the only option available. But the alternative was leaving a terrified mother with no answer at all, and the alternative was worse than the risk.

“Because it isn’t different for children,” Steve said smiling up at her. “The adaptation is the same as what we are going through. The fever is her body catching up to it.”

The woman looked at her daughter. The girl’s flush was tinged with violet and not the red of infection.

“She’ll be all right?”

“She’ll be all right.”

Arthur was about to ask Geoff about packing supplies when Donna’s head snapped towards the courtyard entrance.

“Movement. Five. Armed.”

Arthur looked at the five walking through the section of the barricade Geoff’s men were working on with the swagger of people who expected the men to make room for them.

“Not this lot again.” Geoff said with a grunt.

“Again?” Donna said.

“Third time, this. They come, they take. We don’t have enough people to –"

“You do now,” Arthur said, adjusting the Bullguard Shield. “Stay behind us.”
