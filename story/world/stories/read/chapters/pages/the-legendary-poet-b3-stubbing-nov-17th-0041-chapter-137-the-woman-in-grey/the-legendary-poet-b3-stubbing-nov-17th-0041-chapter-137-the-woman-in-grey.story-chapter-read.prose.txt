The Galleries let him out, and one moment he stood in the white Drafting Room with his hand on the desk, the next the world folded shut behind him and he was standing in the hidden lane in the Artisan's Quarter, surrounded by several hundred paintings that were all pretending they had never moved.

He gave the nearest one a flat look. A pastoral scene, sheep, a hill, a suspiciously innocent shepherd. It held perfectly still.

"Sure," he told it. "We believe you."

The lane was empty. Fading light came down through the gap between the buildings in a single pale slab, and dust hung in it. Somewhere beyond the mouth of the lane, Korros was doing its usual impression of an orchestra tuning forever.

First problem.

He looked down at himself. Burgundy beret with the plume. Charcoal longcoat with the silver filigree. The black boots, the jagged void-dark shortsword at his hip with its vein of crimson. A very specific silhouette, and thanks to the world announcement and the forums, a very documented one. He ran the inventory in his head and came up with the answer he already knew. Zero spare gear.

Unequipping was technically an option. He pictured it: a man walking through the Artisan's Quarter in system-default underwear, holding a legendary sword.

That draws more eyes, and worse ones.

He sighed, adjusted the beret, and walked.

Field test it is. If I get mobbed, I get mobbed. At least I'll learn the aggro radius of the idiots who decide to do so.

Forge lanes rang their tuned hammers, the pigment street steamed in six colors, and player traffic flowed thick through all of it, clumped around vendor stalls and the commission board. Kage kept his pace even and his eyes forward, like he was somewhere specific to be, which in his experience was the closest thing to invisibility that money couldn't buy.

Two players by a dye stall glanced at him. One elbowed the other. He caught a fragment of it as he passed, "...coat, look at the coat, I'm telling you that's the guy from the...", and then the crowd noise ate the rest and nobody followed him. A few murmurs drifted over a cluster of newbies near the glass lane: did the raid party get ANYTHING good or was it all lore stuff. Another one, farther off: my guildmate swears the Poet is actually an NPC.

Confusion was free camouflage.

So far, recognizable meant stared at, and stared at meant nothing. Good. That held exactly as long as nobody with an agenda decided to test it, and the big guilds all had agendas, but that was tomorrow's tax. Today had its own.

While his feet handled the route, he pulled up the one thing from the raid he still hadn't opened. It had been sitting in his inventory since the Curator's chamber, heavy in the specific way Synesthesia made important things heavy.

[Ink of Origin]

Quality: Mythic

Type: Artisan Reagent / Quill Ammunition

Volume: 3/3 Uses

Effect:

[The First Word]: Increases the overall potency of Written Verses by 100%. A statement written in this ink is woven into reality's original text rather than imposed upon it. The world does not resist the edit, because the world remembers it as having always been true.

Description: Before the ink, there was a thought. Before the thought, there was this.

He stopped walking for a second. A porter with a crate of glassware swore and flowed around him.

The world remembers it as having always been true.

He read it again, slower, and the Operator and the Prodigy had, for once, the same reaction, which was a low whistle.

Three uses. Three sentences that reality would treat as retroactive fact. His brain immediately started doing what it always did with a resource, which was to try to spend it, and the obvious slot presented itself within seconds: Narrative Forging. The whole thing, currently, had a known weakness: the Anchor. And here was a substance whose entire function was to make the world stop arguing.

It's an Anchor. Or better than one.

Which was exactly the problem. He had a Mythic conceptual material sitting in his bag, the Maker's Abandoned Breath, an entire dead god's exhale distilled into a reagent, and now a Mythic ink to write it with, and the correct pairing was so obvious it was practically flashing. Mythic soul, Mythic medium. The bottleneck was the third component. The Vessel. Pouring that combination into some vendor-trash base item would be like commissioning a cathedral and building it on a raft.

Not yet. The vessel has to be worth the sentence.

He closed the panel and kept walking. Somewhere behind his sternum, the part of him that had stood inside the First Maker's memory and watched a crown shatter wanted to take the bottle out and just hold it. He told that part of him to get in line. It had been getting bolder lately, and worse, it had been getting results, which made it very hard to manage.

The Promenade eroded into the lower city the same way it had yesterday, marble to limestone to soot, and he used the walk to reopen the commission file in his head, because the deadline had gone from comfortable to insulting while he'd been busy inheriting a dungeon.

The job. One introductory poem, spoken before a Guild panel, for a slum painter's debut. No Keywords, no Forms, no system grading. Pen, ink, paper, and whatever's left of my actual brain.

He had the ink. He had the folio. He'd had, at one point, two days.

And the client. Lady Elara Vane, the Iron Matriarch, a woman assembled out of protocol and black silk, who spoke about Lyra like a line item and then said it must be honest like the word cost her something. Who claimed she'd only reviewed the work through intermediaries and then described it the way you describe a wound. She paints as though the canvas wronged her in a past life. Who'd been asked a simple binary question about outcomes and answered, you assume I am measuring the outcome, and for one frame, one single dropped frame, something had moved under the permafrost.

Do not make her sound like a beggar. Force them to open the gates.

Patrons said convince. Investors said persuade. Force was a word you used about gates that had been shut on someone specific.

He'd filed all of it at the time, the way he filed everything, tagged inconsistent client behavior, possible leverage and shelved. The shelf had been rattling quietly ever since.

The Greywater checkpoint had the same two constables, and this time the broad one just looked at the coat, looked at the face under the beret, and stepped aside before Kage even reached for the sealed letter. "Fourth Bend's still where you left it," the man said.

The Ward hit him with its wall of sound and cabbage water and life, and he took the left at the tannery, followed the smell of the worse tannery, and came up the crooked lane to Studio 6. The door still wore its archaeology of handprints. The palm print in burnt sienna by the handle had been joined by a new one in a violent teal.

He knocked.

Footsteps, fast, something clattering. The door swung open, and Lyra stood in it with a palette knife in one hand and an expression already loaded.

"You're late."

"I know."

"No. No, I don't think you do." She pointed the knife at him, and a bead of grey paint dropped off its tip onto the threshold, joining several hundred ancestors. "The Salon is tomorrow. Four in the afternoon. You were supposed, no, ought to, come back earlier today, look at the piece, and go do whatever poets do, and instead I've been standing here finishing it and wondering if the word-monkey got stabbed in an alley, which, honestly, fine, but before the debut is bad timing." She stopped. Her eyes did their quick melt-through-your-skull pass. "Can you even write it in time? An actual piece? Or am I walking into that room with three sentences you scribbled on the carriage ride over?"

"I'll manage."

"He'll manage." She said it to the ceiling, to the general concept of ceilings, and stepped back, swinging the door wide with her hip. "In. Don't touch anything wet, which today is everything."

The studio smelled exactly as before, turpentine and linseed and lived-in air, but the geography had shifted. Canvases had been moved, restacked, cleared away from the window, and the easel had been turned to face the door, so that the painting was the first thing anyone entering would see.

The cloth was off.

"There," Lyra said. Her voice changed on the word, dropped its volume and most of its armor. "That's the piece."

Kage looked, and for the first three seconds it was just data.

A pale grey field, fog or morning or unprimed nothing, and a figure half-dissolved into it, tall, cloaked, indistinct at every edge. The single point of extreme clarity, her signature grammar, sat where the hands were: white gloves, rendered with obsessive, almost forensic precision, every seam and crease and tension line of the fabric, reaching down toward the bottom of the frame. Offering something. A stick of charcoal, held out to a small, dirty palm that entered the composition from below the frame's edge, a child's hand, open.

The face was a blur.

His eye went to it the way her paintings steered the eye, and instead of the expected cruel clarity he found layers. Scraped-down layers, repainted, scraped again, so many passes that the canvas itself had developed texture under the final coat, ridges and scar tissue, a topography of attempts. She hadn't left the face vague. She had fought for it, over and over, for what looked like years, and lost every time, and the final version wore all the defeats at once.

The Operator ran the assessment. Technically superb. Compositionally consistent with her portfolio. Thematically an outlier. Every other canvas in this room was rage. The starving dog, the self-mutilating bird, the smiling merchant with the rotting fruit. This was something else, and the something else was the problem, because he could feel the file resisting its own label. He reached for sentimental and the word slid off. He reached for unfinished and that slid off too, because the scar tissue was the finish.

Longing, the other part of him said, unasked. It's longing. She's been reaching for that face her whole life and it keeps refusing to arrive.

And underneath all of it, faint, the Synesthesia hummed. A low vibration off the canvas, the resonance the interface reserved for objects with weight in the story of the world, the same shiver he'd learned to trust, slowly. A slum painting on a secondhand stretcher was buzzing like a historical artifact.

He should have moved on to logistics. Portfolio order, panel names, timing. That was the efficient path, and he was aware of choosing the other one.

"Who is she?"

Lyra's shoulders came up. "Nobody. It's a composition."

"The gloves have more rendering hours than most portraits I've seen... anywhere."

"Well, you must not have seen many portraits then."

Kage gestured towards the painting's upper part, "And the face has been painted at least a dozen times."

"Closer to forty." It came out before Lyra could stop it, and she scowled at herself, set the palette knife down on the worktable with exaggerated care, and stood there a second, visibly negotiating with her own reluctance. When she spoke again it was faster, flatter. "I… listen, I don't even know why I'm telling you this. I was six. Maybe seven. Alley off Mire Street, I was drawing on the flagstones with a burnt stick because that's what you draw with when you're seven in Greywater. And there was a woman. Grey traveling cloak, hood up, never got close, never gave a name. White gloves. Clean ones." She laughed, one short note. "You understand what clean white gloves look like down here? It was like seeing a ghost. Ghosts are more plausible."

Her hands had started moving while she talked, like they usually moved, and one of them was doing something specific. Her thumb dragged hard across her opposite palm, pressing, wiping, scrubbing at the dried paint crusted there, again and again, an absent motion her eyes took no part in.

Kage's attention snagged on it and held.

"She crouched down," Lyra went on, looking at the painting now instead of him, "and she put a stick of charcoal in my hand. Real charcoal, you understand? Artist's charcoal, wrapped in paper. And a few sheets of actual paper, which was worth more than everything my… pack, owned. And she said," her voice shifted, unconsciously mimicking a register that was lower, more precise, more placed, "don't let them make you neat. And then she was gone. Gone, like that." Her eyes seemed to unfocus. "I turned around to show somebody the charcoal and when I turned back the alley was empty." She shrugged, clarity coming back, aggressive, defensive. "That's it. That's the whole thing. Stupid, right? I've been trying to get her face right ever since and I can't, because I was seven and I only kept the gloves and the voice. It's embarrassing. It's the softest thing I've ever made and I'm hanging it in front of the Gilded Brush Guild because apparently I hate myself. Say something poet-ish."

Kage watched her.

Press, drag. Press, drag. Her thumb worked across her palm, grinding at paint that had been dry for hours, and he had watched that exact motion before, the same pressure, the same rhythm, the same total unawareness, performed in white gloves against the rim of a porcelain saucer in a room where nothing was permitted to be out of place.

Coincidence. He set the label on it firmly. A painter wipes paint off her hands. A noblewoman wipes nothing off porcelain. Half the people in this city have restless hands. It's a tic. Tics converge. This is pattern-matching noise, the same part of my brain that sees faces in wood grain.

The label sat there for approximately two seconds and then slid off the shelf.

Because the shelf already held other things, and now, without his permission, they were reorganizing themselves. The lowest drawer handle in Vane's study, the only imperfect object in a surgically perfect room, worn dark, a residue in the joint the color of charcoal. The paintings in her halls, dozens of them, expensive living enchantments every single one locked in a three-second loop, a collection he had privately tagged a collection of cages and moved past. I have reviewed samples. Through intermediaries. Spoken by a woman who then described the work with the specificity of a bruise. Force them to open the gates. It must be honest. You assume I am measuring the outcome. A patron with three unissued Tokens and no discernible affection for anything, spending resources for years on one slum painter she had allegedly never met, insisting her name stay out of it.

Don't let them make you neat, said the woman in grey.

Do not make her sound like a beggar, said Lady Vane.

Kage looked up from Lyra's hands to Lyra's face, and the ice-blue eyes looked back at him, fierce and melting where the other pair had been fierce and frozen, and the temperature of the thought he was refusing to think dropped straight through the floor.

"What," Lyra said.

"The gloves." His voice came out level. His hand, he noticed distantly, had curled at his side, and the thumb was pressing into the fingers, and he made it stop. "Were they..."

Were they seamed at the wrist. Was she tall. Did she smell like nothing at all, like distilled water, like a room where nothing is allowed to live.

"...in the painting from memory, or reference?"

"Memory." She squinted at him. "Everything's from memory. That's the entire problem. Why?"

"Detail work like that reads differently to a panel if it's observed." A real answer, a true one even. He turned back to the canvas because it was easier than her eyes, and he stood in front of the blurred, scarred, forty-times-defeated face, and the Synesthesia hummed its low note, and he understood with complete clarity that he was going to say absolutely nothing. Here, in this room, to this woman, with the file at maybe thirty percent confidence and a debut tomorrow afternoon and a client who owned every bottle of ink in the city. He knew nothing. He suspected something enormous, and suspicion was a debt he had no intention of taking out loud.

"I have what I need," he said.

"That's it? You stare at it for five minutes, interrogate me about gloves, and leave?"

"I write better away from wet paint." He moved to the door, then stopped with his hand on the frame, on the palm print in burnt sienna. "The piece is good. Better than everything else in this room."

Lyra opened her mouth, closed it, and settled on scowling, which he was beginning to understand was her word for thank you.

Outside, the lane was warm and loud and reeked of the worse tannery, and Kage stood in it for a moment, letting the Ward's noise pour over him while the pieces sat in his head, refusing to unassemble.
