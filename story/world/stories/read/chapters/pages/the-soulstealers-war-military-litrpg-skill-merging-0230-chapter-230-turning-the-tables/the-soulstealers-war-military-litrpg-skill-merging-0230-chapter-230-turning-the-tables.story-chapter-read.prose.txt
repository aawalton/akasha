I knelt in the bushes beside the roadway, clutching my spear tight. In the distance, horses’ hooves clomped on a mud and gravel trail through the forest, wagon wheels rumbled, and the soft voices of guards casually whispered through the woods, unaware of what awaited them.

I glanced to the left. Anchor waited with an arrow nocked and his bow half drawn. On the right, Brawl held up a chopped tree. He hugged it while staying hidden in a clump of especially tall ferns, ready to drop it at any moment.

As for Sage? I couldn’t see her, but with my perception Skill, I could sense her. She waited in the bushes, fifty paces away from us. Morph stood at the ready with her, though I only sensed them as one being nowadays.

The approaching convoy’s rumbles turned wooden when it reached a bridge. It was almost here.

Yeah, almost might have been a bit of a stretch. My regular, physical senses were much better nowadays than a mortal man’s, so I could hear for much farther. I still couldn’t see it.

But crossing the bridge meant they were close. We’d scoped out the land before setting up our ambush, and we’d done our best to memorize as much of it as we could.

We were about a day’s trek north of Steelshore. Here, the land was still cracked. Deep valleys and crags separated chunks of land, and isolated forests grew on top of them. They were mostly pine trees, but there were a few lime-green aspens and oaks scattered amongst them.

It smelled fresh. Birds chirped, and mid-summer sun dappled through the forest.

It had been a few months since I had fought the Warlord and escaped Steelshore, and this was the closest we’d ever come to the city.

“My lord,” a voice whispered from the opposite side of the road.

I hesitated for a second. When I heard people say ‘my lord,’ I was still used to them trying to call for Lord Ceonwin.

But Lord Ceonwin wasn’t here, and that was meant for me—the true and technically exiled Ealdorman of this domain.

“What is it?” I whispered back. The approaching convoy was getting louder, and we wouldn’t have much time.

“I can sense Path-walkers. At least five of them, all Titaniums.”

I tilted my head. “Anchor?”

“He’s right,” Anchor replied. “You did say there would only be Steel-tier Dupes, at best.”

Without being able to see across the road, I couldn’t pick out who exactly had tried to reach out to me. My guys were dupes as well, and sappers, but they weren’t a squad that I had hung around regularly. The other squads that remained in southern Gate were busy executing other raids on the Warlord’s men, or helping hold the line at Hyrdford.

I said, “Keep quiet. We’re all stronger than Titaniums, now. They may have brought extra protection, but it won’t be enough.”

I leaned forward, careful to keep my armour in the shade so the sun wouldn’t glint off it. Then, I pulled my helmet on. Its plume swayed in the breeze, but I stayed low enough that its shock of bright orange wouldn’t ruin the ambush.

Anchor and Brawl already had their helmets on, and they gave me a nod to indicate they were ready.

By now, the convoy’s horses had stepped onto solid ground again. They turned a corner, coming into view as their wagons drove single-file into the trap. The guards were a mixture of regular Dupes, Steelshore militia, and cultists.

The cultists were the Titaniums—they wore black robes and flaunted their bald, rune-etched scalps proudly as they marched alongside the convoy.

Cultists would be a bit bigger of a problem. They usually had abilities that let them punch above their weight, which would put them roughly on par with the average sapper—we were Silver-tier. We had to take them out fast, before they could do any real damage.

Two horses drew each wagon. They were open-backed carts, and crates and barrels jostled around. Weapons clanked inside the crates, elixir vials clinked, and chainmail armour rustled.

Once the first wagon passed in front of me, I whispered, “Brawl, now.”

He pushed over his tree, dropping it in front of the first horses. Its three-foot-thick trunk wasn’t going to let anyone through any time soon. With a thud, the tree fell into place. An explosion of pine needles and dirt splashed up around it, and the convoy guards all drew their weapons, shouting and turning around.

The horses pulling the wagons reared up and kicked, and one fell on its back. The enemy Dupes took a protective formation, while the cultists rushed to the back to try and usher the convoy away from the road.

Sage was waiting for them.

A wall of vines rose behind the convoy, knitting into a tight fence, then hardening. Bark formed over the vines, protecting them from the cultists.

The cultists probably could have chopped through, given enough time, I wasn’t going to give them a chance to react.

Jumping out of cover, I leapt up on top of the tree we’d cut, then pointed my spear at the convoy.

“We are men of Gate!” I shouted. “If you don’t harm us, we won’t harm you!”

Except maybe the cultists—knowing how they gained power, there was no way I’d let them escape justice. But for the time being, if surrender made my life easier, I would let them. We only had my squad and another, and there was no need to endanger their lives if I didn’t have to.

But chances were, the cultists weren’t going to surrender anyway.

“We are here for your goods,” I said. “If we can earn your allegiance as well, we would take it.”

Most of the militia stared at me, pausing mid-act. They, being mortal men, couldn’t march with armour on. They wore light tunics and gambesons, and their weapons still hung from sheaths at their hips, or were slung over their backs.

One, however, drew a bow and loosed its arrow at me. My arm whipped into place, raising my enhanced shield. It didn’t even bite into the wood-and-rawhide face of the shield. It left a nick on the paint, at best, before bouncing off and clattering away into the woods.

“Do you think you could get lucky?” I asked. I crouched down, facing them. “I’ve got seven other men in the woods. They’re equipped just as well as me. You can barely scratch our skin, let alone our armour. There’s no getting lucky. This is your last chance. Working with them”—I motioned to the cultists—“is never going to be as good as working with me. We could use people like you to help contain the orcs.”

“And that’s why you fight your own countrymen?” one of the militiamen farther back snarled.

“I am Sir Levi, rightful Ealdorman of this domain, unjustly removed from power by the Warlord,” I replied. “He is trading all your souls to maintain his power. I would let you fight back.”

“Enough!” a cultist shouted, marching back to the front to face me. He drew a rusty sword from his hip, then flourished it. “You’re an imposter. Sir Levi is dead! He has been replaced.”

I pulled off my glove, then held it up to the rays of light shining through the branches overhead. I clenched my fingers together, and the golden teardrop shape of the Royal Thegns appeared on the palm of my hand, shining in glowing amber. “So long as the royal family hasn’t stripped my thegnage from me, I am still in charge. You’ve all heard of the convoy raids, haven’t you? That was me.”

“Kill him!” the cultist yelled, pointing his blade at me.

I closed my fist. “Anchor, now.”

He blasted an enhanced arrow through the first cultist’s head. One moment, the man was standing, and the next, he laid on his side, eyes glazed over, arrow sprouting from the side of his skull.

The rest of my squad emerged from the woods, and on the other side, the second squad jumped down, their weapons drawn.

I triggered my second Art—[Will to Power]. My voice boomed, and all of the nearby militiamen and Dupes’ gazes snapped to me. Their eyes turned directly to me.

A link existed between our minds, now. A spiderweb of invisible strands reached between me and them.

[Will to Power], from what I understood, was a memory-based Art. I hadn’t figured out all the intricacies, but I could gain the enraptured attention of all who I targeted by grasping at their most recent memories, and creating new ones exactly the way I wanted. It cost twenty Presence to use, so it wasn’t cheap, and it was best saved for large crowds.

With it, I could also transmit memories.

I transmitted a memory of the past few months since I had fought the Warlord, sending them images of the other convoys we’d raided, or the many cultists we’d slain and hideouts of theirs across the countryside that we had burnt.

Most importantly, they understood my intent to do it again.

I couldn’t compel them to do anything. But I could show them I was telling the truth. Was it worth twenty Presence?

Maybe when I actually figured out everything that the Skill did, or merged it with some of the other memory-based Skills I had. I knew I’d saved those for a reason.

But right now, I couldn’t afford to get distracted, nor to dilute the message I’d just sent them.

“Throw down your weapons if you wish to live!” I ordered, then leapt.

Using my gravity Art, I launched myself up over the first wagon, then dragged myself down toward the second cultist. He hadn’t drawn a weapon, but the runes on his scalp were glowing, and bloody tears leaked out the corners of his eyes. The last thing I wanted was to let him draw maegen from the surrounding militiamen and use them to increase his strength.

I impacted him with a thwump. He crumpled beneath me. Dust exploded beneath his body, and the force created a shallow crater beneath him. My resonance Skill sharpened my spearhead and pierced through the cultist’s skull. It knocked him to the ground, and my spear pierced into the mud, delving a foot deep before finally stopping. The cultist was dead on the spot.

There was only one last cultist, but Brawl and a swordsman from the other other squad were on him. The cultist wielded a mace, and he was fast, but against two Silvers, there was nothing he could do. In a matter of seconds, Brawl knocked the man’s mace out of his hand with his flail, and the swordsman cut the cultist’s throat.

Anchor picked off a militiaman with a regular arrow as the man tried to charge me from behind. It was still strong enough to pin the man to the wagon beside me.

I ripped my spear out of the ground, then used it to finish off the pinned man.

It left only a few foolish dupes and a cluster of militiamen—only a quarter of the total convoy guards.

“You’ll never get away with this, soulstealer,” one of the Dupes snarled.

I sighed. “Let’s make this quick.”
