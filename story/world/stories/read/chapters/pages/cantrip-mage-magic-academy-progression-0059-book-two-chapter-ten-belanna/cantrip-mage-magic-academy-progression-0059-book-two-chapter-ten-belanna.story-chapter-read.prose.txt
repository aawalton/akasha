The rest of the week was a whirlwind.

Between prepping everything she, Colin, and Roth would need to hunt an unknown monster through the Spine Mountains, trying and failing to earn Belanna’s trust, and her classes, Eola had no time at all to work on assembling Varin’s notebook. That annoyed Atta to no end. “You need to focus on what’s important. This patrol doesn’t matter. The notebook does,” she hissed.

Eola ignored her. The notebook sat in a stack on her desk, a few pages half-translated—and what she’d learned was interesting—if so highly incomplete she couldn’t make heads or tails of it at all. But both Roth and Colin were counting on her, and the upcoming patrol wouldn’t wait. Varin, however, wasn’t going anywhere.

And then there was Fourth-Day’s Spellsword Tactics.

Maestro Yarrowbloom was in charge that day. He quickly grouped the handful of would-be spellswords into groups of four and set them to dueling each other. “No holds barred. Any spells you know, and tricks you can pull—it’s all acceptable today. Don’t worry about the Taron-Li’s sanctity. You’re learning to fight for your lives.”

Eola, of course, ended up with Catrine first.

Last term, she’d dueled with Catrine, and it had been closer than either Eola or the noblewoman wanted to admit. They’d been a fairly even match with the smallsword, and with enough research, Eola had been able to more or less neutralize Catrine’s scent magic. But that had been out in the hedge maze, where Catrine could go all-out.

Here, in the gymnasium, surrounded by classmates and professors, she couldn’t use her most potent weapons—and Eola punished her for that.

Eola had been putting in work, and she’d fought Eddarn and his book monster familiar. Compared to them, Catrine’s swordplay skills were almost childish. When she tried to turn the corner by casting First Order magic, Eola was ready for that, too; she pressed her attack, starting a Cutting mark before stopping it just a single line from being finished. Catrine’s spell was too complicated to finish while under attack, and in the gymnasium, there was nowhere to break contact with Eola.

She didn’t have to use her magic to stop Catrine’s. She could simply lunge and thrust until her opponent had to stop casting and focus on defending herself.

All her fights went that way; as best she could see it, she was in the upper quarter of spellswords, or perhaps a little higher.

And she rode that feeling the whole rest of the week, all through her attempts to source more parts for her Ensorcellment project and during the increasingly dry lectures on Old Alemic’s roots and conjugations.

And then, almost before Eola could blink, Fifth-Day arrived, and she found herself once again at the South Varin’s Town Stables—this time with a letter in hand.

They’d gotten lucky, and a village had sent a request for help. Instead of a full patrol, they’d have a specific target.

To the Librarian-Knights,

The village of Shimmer Grove requests assistance.

For the last three weeks, a monster has been stalking our goat pens. At first, none of the village adults had seen it, and we thought it was just another story told by Tarn. But Ragon saw it yesterday, and the number of does and kids that have gone missing is alarming. Shimmer Grove won’t survive the winter with it hunting our livelihoods, and even if we do, it’ll do enough damage that we won’t recover next summer.

The monster is as long as a house, but thin. Its coloration is white with pale blue markings on its fur, like the ice in the never-melting valley. It moves on dozens of legs, and its eyes resemble those of a spider.

We eagerly await your apprentices’ intervention.

Elder Toma Lerak of Shimmer Grove

As Roth hoisted Belanna’s pack saddle onto the mule—while the beast itself stared dourly at Eola, much to her consternation—she read over the letter. “It’s standard form for a request for assistance, according to the lectures we’ve been through. Information about what it hunts, where it’s been seen, what it looks like, and the stakes. But…”

“But what?” Roth asked. “Colin, help me with the tent.”

“Just one tent?” Colin asked.

“We’re sharing a two-person tent, yes. It’s colder than Y’aer’s tomb in the mountains, even this time of year.”

“Won’t that be tight? And what about privacy for Eola?”

“You can carry a second tent if you want, but after your first fall night in the Spines, we’ll all be packed in and getting to know each other pretty well, regardless. Just wait until winter sets in.”

Eola cleared her throat, trying to force down a blush. “I have no idea what the monster is. It could be a Winterstalk. That’s my first guess. But it could also easily be a Srilla. The two require very different strategies; a Winterstalk is pretty much just a beast, while a Srilla could be almost as smart as Lannith. We need more information.”

Roth and Colin finished hoisting the sack of waxed cloth and thick wooden poles onto Belanna’s back. The mule didn’t seem to mind the weight. As Roth worked the straps tight around the assembled saddlebags and tightly wrapped sacks, Colin grinned. His face was also red, and the grin looked a little forced. “I’ll talk to the Shimmer Grove folks when we get there. We can brainstorm questions on the way.”

“We’re done packing. Let’s get moving.” Roth gave the mule’s lead a little flick as his songbird flitted over to land between the gray animal’s long ears. Atop the pack, Nera and Atta faced off less than a foot from one another, the cat’s blue fur on end and the weasel chirping loudly. “And you two, get along or get away from each other, Y’aer take you.”

And so, Eola’s first patrol began.

In the little more than a year since she’d arrived at Varin’s Academy, Eola hadn’t left the round granite walls that protected both the school and the village that shared its name. For that entire year, she’d contented herself with the library, books, and unraveling the mysteries of magic—and truthfully, she could easily have stayed there forever. But there was something about the world beyond that reminded her of…

Not her parents. Her uncle. She’d hunted trogs with him in the woods by Greenarbor, and she’d learned a little about the world beyond cities’ walls. The Spine Mountains were nothing like the pine-and-aspen forests she’d helped him hunt in, though.

By the time they started up the first hairpin-tight switchback and entered the hanging mountain valley that, according to their map, protected Shimmer Grove from the worst of winter’s fury, she’d put on a coat below the thick Varin’s Academy winter cloak, shoved heavy mittens over her hands, and was still freezing. Every tree had been covered by a thick layer of snow that had blown in almost perfectly sideways the night before, and icicles hung from the branches. Some of those were longer than Eola’s smallsword, and occasionally, one crashed down onto the road to shatter into a thousand shards.

And aside from the musky smell of Belanna as she climbed the dirt path at a steady, unforgiving clip, the only thing Eola’s nose could pick up was the sheer cold. It was so cold that ice formed on her lashes and clung to the edges of her nostrils. The snowball fight with Patrice last year had been enjoyable, but part of why she’d had so much fun was that she knew there’d be a hot meal and a fire to come inside to after. This cold just lasted and lasted, digging through Eola’s clothes and skin until it chilled her spine.

“People live here?” Colin asked breathlessly as they neared the top of the pass and looked down at the highest trees below.

“This is a nice summer day in my village,” Roth said. His voice had taken on a wistful tone that meshed poorly with his short, angry-sounding voice.

“Sounds awful.”

“Best days of my life,” Roth replied. He pointed to a dozen thin columns of smoke in the distance. “That’s the village. Let’s go.”

Eola consulted the map, fumbling with the paper, then slowly nodded. “Two more miles. We can be there before lunch.”

“Don’t get your hopes up. We packed our own,” Colin said. He put an arm around Eola, and they walked together. It was…nice…to have someone to warm her up a little.

Maybe that was why Roth had only packed the one, too-small tent.

The village of Shimmer Grove was tucked under a towering cliff. A couloir full of snow loomed over it to the north. Even this early, it was already full of snow, and Roth shook his head. “If they don’t have protection spells, that avalanche will level their whole village before Y’aer’s Day.” Eola stared up at the cliffside and the sheer mass of snow sitting above the village, and she couldn’t help but agree.

Not that Shimmer Grove was much of a village. Back home in Greenarbor, Eola had been used to a proper town, if somewhat of a backwater compared to the big southern cities. Even Varin’s Town had cobbled stones and enough streets that they had to have signs at the intersections. But Shimmer Grove was nothing but a three-street crossroad with a dozen or so steep-roofed buildings. Stone first floors gave way to wood-framed second stories, and piles of snow surrounded each building on each side, reaching almost as tall as Colin already.

At the village’s center, a sign hung outside of a small, single-story building. Unlike the others, its roof was relatively flat, and someone had clearly shoveled snow off of it recently. More importantly, a sign hung from a rusted length of chain.

“The Lonely Pine. Sounds like a tavern,” Roth said. “Go in and see what you can learn.”

“They have taverns here?” Eola’s stomach rumbled.

Roth rolled his eyes. “Don’t get your hopes up. Taverns in villages like this serve nothing but watered-down beer and gruel. You’re better off with the stuff we packed.”

“Besides, this village is already hurting without us eating them out of house and home,” Colin added—somewhat unhelpfully, in Eola’s opinion.

Still, they left Belanna tied to a post outside and, while Roth brushed the snow off of her, Eola and Colin went inside.

A tavern was a tavern, no matter whether the counter was in the center of Greenarbor, a cliffside in Highstone, or the middle of a freezing vale in the Spine Mountains. Eola relaxed a little as the scent of a never-ending pot of stew filled her nose, melting the ice that had built up over the last few hours. It fought with the stale, bitter stink of spilled ale—both familiar smells from the Chubby Saber. As for the bar itself, it was…

Well, small was a charitable way to put it. A single table with three chairs—one of which was missing a leg. Two stools up against the counter. Both were empty, which was a surprise. A single, thin-cheeked bartender cleaning a cup with a cloth that looked like it hadn’t seen soap in weeks. Eola stared for a few seconds, then shook her head slightly as she recalculated just how hungry she really was.

Then the barkeep noticed them. His eyes narrowed, and he reached for a pair of spectacles. “Oh. Varin’s colors. You’re the apprentices I wrote for, then?”

Eola tried to decide what to say, but before she could, Colin reached into her pocket—somewhat rudely, but she wasn’t about to say anything—and plucked the letter free. “Yes. You would be…Elder Lerak? We’re here about the monster you’ve had trouble with. Lieutenant-Librarian Fringe sent us.”

“No idea who that is, but if you’re here to handle that…thing…you’re a week too late. It killed one of the shepherds. Herder Ginger. She was a little orange-yellow gnome, been in the village for decades.”

“Y’aer’s curse,” Eola muttered. Colin reached over and slid his mitten into hers, and she squeezed it tight.

“We don’t blame you,” Elder Lerak said in a voice that very much blamed the academy, Lieutenant-Librarian Fringe, Eola and Colin, and Y’aer themselves.

“Right.” Colin cleared his throat. “We’ll take care of it tonight. In the meantime, we’ve got a few questions. First, where is everyone?”

“After Ginger got et, we couldn’t leave people out there alone. They’re all watching. Meanwhile, the cursed beast keeps right on hunting, like there aren’t even shepherds.”

Eola closed her eyes. “Can you tell us about the monster? Anything you can think of, no matter how important you think it is. We’ll get rid of it—and we’ll do it today. But we need to know what we’re up against first.”

Then she sat on the barstool and listened as the village elder talked and talked. He told them how the monster slid through the snow on dozens of legs. How its fur seemed to pick up ice and snow, making it all but invisible in the fields until it took a goat. How it never took more than one, and never hunted twice in one day. Its size, tracks, the smell it left behind in its wake—like a ferret’s musk, but much, much stronger.

At that, Eola raised her hand. “That’s everything we need. You’re dealing with a Winterstalk, and we’ll handle it. Tonight.”

~Roth Gerr~

Roth had a lot on his mind.

Eola, for one. She hadn’t said as much, but he understood. She wasn’t entirely comfortable with sharing the tiny tent he’d packed with him. With Colin? Sure. They probably hadn’t even had a proper kiss, but Roth wasn’t stupid; the two of them were both just too shy and stupid to do what needed to be done. But with him, too? There was no way a decent young woman would be caught dead tenting up with him.

And that was a shame, because Roth had absolutely no interest in freezing to death tonight.

Back in his village, he’d always been cold. That was just how it was. Music had helped, but it didn’t create warmth. Joy, yes. But not warmth. For that, you needed a fire—or another body.

Nathien had been that person, and more. The elf had worked a piece of cedar on a lathe, slowly narrowing it down into a tube a bit thicker than his thumb. He’d let Roth watch, but hadn’t explained any of the steps, or what he was making. Every finger-hole had been painstakingly drilled with a drill made of hardwood spun between two strings—a long, slow process that filled their tent with a slight, cloying smell of smoke. And when the time came, Nathien had crashed through the pond’s ice to find a suitable reed, then spent almost three hours shivering and pressed against Roth’s body until he warmed up.

He’d always had Nathien for that job, and Nathien had always had him.

Then he hadn’t. When House Andrese showed up in his village, he’d lost his closest friend. The person he’d clung to in the cold nights up high in the mountains, keeping watch over the thick-wooled sheep or hunting the Y’aer-cursed monsters that stalked their herds. The person who, without a musical bone in his body, had carved a flute so beautifully tuned that Roth couldn’t play it without thinking about him.

Nathien’s flute sat on his desk, in his room. It was the only sentimental object he owned. And maybe his dad had been right. Maybe Roth should have been a hunter and a knife-fighter, and not a musician. Maybe it would all have been easier. He’d still have the stupid flute in one piece, and he could play it where no one could hear him.

He kicked the post Belanna was tied to. Ice cracked across its surface, and a little bit fell off and crinkled against his boot.

“Y’aer’s curse.”

He’d have to talk to them both. Set a few things straight and see how that landed with Eola—and with Colin.

Roth paused. Then he poured a double-handful of oats into a feed bag and strapped it around Belanna’s mouth. “Eat up, Bells.”

Then he slowly ran the brush through his mule’s mane, across her shoulders and flank, and down the thick, gray hair that covered her legs. He’d have to have that talk with them, and he’d have to have it soon.

And he desperately didn’t want to.
