And so Lao Bushi came to his greatest challenge! Greater than the dark of the forest, greater than joining the peoples in alliance, greater than the hateful beasts that came before! The other beasts saw this greater monster and ran, scampering in fear as cowardly predators must. The villagers heard its great footsteps coming, and knew their end to be approaching!

But Lao Bushi was as clever as he was brave, and he was as brave as he was strong, and he was a very clever man indeed. So when he saw the beast coming, he went to the highest point of the hill, and he cut his chest to bleed himself. The smell of the magic fruit in his blood woke its hunger terribly, so much so that it leapt beyond the other villages, right to the hero of the hills and valleys. It sought to eat him up in a single bite, to swallow whole the hero of the peoples, but Lao Bushi was so brave that he stood still, and his body matched the strength of his heart. So great was their battle that it lasted ten days and ten nights, and their blood flooded down the hillside as they fought each other, blessing the fields with fresh life and the peoples with new strength.

Until at last, on that final day and in that final hour, Lao Bushi took hold of the whole of his courage, the whole of his wit, the whole of his strength, and struck a blow with all his might! So great was the blow that the beast was crushed to the earth, and the hill itself broke apart, driven by the impact straight into the depths of the earth. The beast lay cracked and broken, and its blood flooded the crater of its body and the hill, but Lao Bushi had used up every part of himself for that last, strongest strike, and as the beast and the hill fell deep into the earth, he fell with them.

Even still, at the final moment, the great hero gave one final gift to the peoples of the hills and valleys. His blood joined with that of the beast, and as they both lay in that final resting place, the victory of the hero was affirmed, as a great flood filled the darkness left behind in the wake of his battle. And so, the people of the hill became known as the people of the Lake, which blessed us all with the bounty we all have now, and the homes we have built along its edge!

– The second part of the tale of Lao Bushi, performed during the festival of the Lake

It is with a mixture of relief and tremendous dread that the sounds of children screaming can be heard.

Raika is there only a little faster than someone in her too-mortal condition should be, though she walks exactly the same way she normally does. There’s a part of her already preparing to react, preparing some of those stranger parts of herself to act, but Chen Tai is not hiding, and when Raika catches sight of her, the relief only multiplies.

There is a creature almost the size of a dog, if a dog were really long and had way too many legs, and if its face was more of a mix between a cat and a big chunky reptile. Still, the way it moves is familiar, its open mouth letting a tongue lol out, forked and bopping children on the forehead as she runs past them. The kids, most of them, at least, are all shrieking in joy, chasing desperately after the spirit beast in their midst, playing a game of tag that winds up and down the main street of Laketown.

“Careful, Chen Tai!” Raika calls, still feeling worried enough to comment. “Be gentle!”

“I’m so gentle-kind, master! Watch!”

Ah. Exactly the word a parent wants to hear.

Chen Tai does a backflip, her spin tumbling like a snakes as she flashes from fur to scales, her front feet landing on the wall of a home and then launching her forward off of it. She corkscrews through the air, letting out a yipping yowl as she does, flying over the heads of kids that leap up to try and chase her.

She lands on a five or six different paws, ready to dash forward again–

Except that one child, slightly older and slightly faster than the others, manages to grab the back end of her long, slinky body.

Raika grabs Chen Tai by the back of her skull, her staff clattering to the ground at her side, her disciple’s fanged mouth less than a foot away from the kid.

“Chen Tai. What did I just say?”

Her disciple lets out a little whimper, in almost the exact same tone as the kid whose life probably just flashed before his eyes. Before she needs to step in, Wei Zin has already arrived, patting the kid on the shoulder and emitting an aura of calm (notably without letting any Qi escape his body, which she’s proud of him for). They separate the two kids, with Chen Tai wrapping her noodle-body around Raika’s arm and shoulder as she keeps hold of her disciple by the back of the skull.

“If you’re going to play with the kids, you have to be nice. Gentle. Like you’re playing with a baby.”

“Hey, I’m not a baby!” yells one of the other kids. Most of them didn’t get too close a look at how fast Chen Tai reacted, nor how sharp her teeth, and are more grumpy at their play being interrupted than anything.

“Yeah! We almost caught her! And she’s so fluffy!”

Kid speak for “how dare you interrupt!”.

“Sorry, master,” Chen Tai grumbles, wriggling but keeping herself from trying to squirm away from Raika’s grip. “I’ll do better next time.”

Raika brings her unruly charge close to her face, staring into her eyes.

One thing that’s easier with Chen Tai is that she doesn’t lie. She can, Raika knows that for a fact, but the Spirit Beast masquerading as a human doesn’t see a need to, especially not with important things. If she says she will do better, then she has every intent, awareness, and will necessary to do better.

“I… believe I would not enjoy being surrounded by children yelling, master. Too many reminders of my time in Singheart, I’m afraid.”

He sighs, starting to walk after the parade of kids chasing their new local spirit beast, shaking his head.

“Will that be alright?” comes a voice behind her and Wei Zin.

Raika nods, groaning a bit as she picks up her walking staff again. Turning, she looks directly into Lai Shu’s eyes, the mother worriedly looking at the strange new creature in their midst, in spite of its size and relative adorableness.

She lowers herself just a bit, slightly angling a knee and leaning forward so as to lean towards Lai Shu and not over her. She looks her neighbor in the eye as she speaks, offering every bit of sincerity, and even going so far as to add Intent to her words.

“No harm shall come to your children while I am here,” she says, and the weight of her speech is enough to make it nearly empirical.

It doesn’t take Qi senses to feel the statement’s meaning, its heft on reality. Those who do have them can feel the slightest ripple moving through the world, while Lai Shu herself gets the full blast of the full words, spoken directly to her.

She takes a slow, deep inhale. Then nods.

Raika has lived a short walk from Lai Shu’s house for five years. She was there when Lai Mi got sick, and helped to heal her. She was there when the two of them had to volunteer, together, to help rescue some of the other villagers from a sinkhole that turned to a muddy flood over a chunk of the fields. Five festivals they’ve known each other, this being the sixth, and all their time together, never once has Raika ever lied.

Five years is a long time. Five years is no time at all. Not enough time to stop being an outsider, but enough time to start being something more.

Lai Shu nods again, giving a smile to reassure herself and Raika both, and then nods a third time.

Lai Shu nods, then smiles wider. “My husband has a jar of pickled peppers he’s been saving up that he’s brought out. The smell alone is enough to make me cry, but maybe your girl will like them?”

Raika sniffs the air once, then twice. “Oh, wow. That is pickled. I’d love to see her try it, actually, and she’d love the challenge.”

He doesn’t follow it up. The sun has started nearing the horizon, though it is not yet dusk, and that means that there’s alcohol coming out. There was alcohol available beforehand, of course, it is a town of fishermen, after all, but now the good stuff has started to emerge, bottles of rice wine of a dozen different family varieties being passed around and shared. Raika heads around their group, the bottle on her hip going temporarily forgotten as she gives space to her perhaps most neglected student.

“I watched everyone die, and now I’m here, in this place that killed them, with these people who didn’t. And I don’t know if they deserve my… my hate.”

Internally, she lets out a sigh of relief. Externally, she gives no sign at all, but of the things he would be wondering about, that’s the better one.

He’s a good egg, no doubt about that. Has been since she met him, and the time she spent with him in Singheart before and after her Death has only reaffirmed that. It was far, far too much to ever put on his shoulders, but he took on the burden anyways, kept the city together and its people from drowning in the politics of greater monsters almost on his own. He lied, stole, cheated, manipulated, convinced and threatened and played every side, until Nascent Soul Realm and Warrior Realm entities that flocked to the multicultural metropolis powered by her flesh had no choice but to work in its benefit.

Wei Zin is better than she is at dealing with people. When she calls him a political animal, it is not an insult, not even an observation, but a compliment. He is a predator in those waters, and if she ever had to go up against him in that environment, she’s pretty sure he could run circles around her.

For months he, a kid barely in Core Formation Realm, still in his early twenties, out-played monsters a millennia his senior who could wipe him out with barely a thought. For years after, as she rebuilt herself and helped where she could, he continued to lead the charge, keeping dozens of tribes, sentient spirit beasts, old monsters and new, from overtaking each other or even attacking each other.

If he was uncertain that these people deserved to live in retaliation for the loss of his family, his culture, his whole tribe, then…

Well. She swore oaths, and she has ideals, but stopping him would have been difficult. In terms of what he can do, yes, in terms of who he can wield, absolutely, but ethically, even.

When one has suffered so much at the hands of so many, what right does a stranger have to tell them what to do with that suffering?

She does her best. She will continue to do her best, to guide him, support him, to keep making the best decisions she can alongside her ideals, alongside her hope for a better world. But she’s not the only one that’s fighting, and her ideals aren’t the only ones.
