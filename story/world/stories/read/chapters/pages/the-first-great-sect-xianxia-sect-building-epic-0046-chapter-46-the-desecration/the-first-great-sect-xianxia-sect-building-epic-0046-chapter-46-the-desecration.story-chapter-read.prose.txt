“And what shall this captain do if you do not return in a reasonable time?” Captain Yao asked upon hearing of his lord’s plan to follow a shaman, the ancestral enemy of his lord’s family, into a wilderness that was potentially infested by bandits.

Liao Hua, the lord in question, shrugged, ignored her aching head, and said, “Kill all the mortals.”

Shaman Yan, the practitioner of the heretical arts of Wuyu, gasped, whirling to glare at Hua. “You can’t—”

“Hold them accountable for your actions? I can and I will. Yours is a lineage of betrayal and deception. If you intend to fool me or trick me, the people you care most for will die.”

“I rather enjoy seeing your look of horror,” said Liao Weiji, who would be following his lord, eagerly looking forward to doing as his ancestors did and killing a shaman.

“You wouldn’t,” the shaman said staring at the steely-eyed captain who very much would kill a refugee convoy at his lord’s direction. “You’re monsters.”

“And you are a shaman,” Weiji spat back with the same inflexion. “Now, how long did you say this would take?”

The Shaman answered. Hua found she disagreed. “We can reach it before sundown if we move quick.”

“That’s a fast speed for a mortal,” her cousin said.

“Shamans aren’t quite mortal, now are they. Closer to demons, animals if we’re being kind,” Hua said with all the vitriol her hangover gave her. Weiji laughed, watching the shaman with a violent intent.

Realising that all those around her wished to see her dead, Wu Yan made her escape.

They moved with great swiftness. The journey would be through the forest, following a route with few paths that a horse could safely follow at any speed. Hua felt painfully slow in her reactions, nearly stumbling over roots and slamming into tree trunks. Partly the hangover but also her struggle with Qi reinforcement after her near-deviations. Her injured arm was making it difficult to adjust, her instinct to grab branches as support and adjust direction by pushing off with her dominant arm working against her.

The shaman kept up well, pushed to her absolute limit by the pace Hua and Weiji casually kept. Tempered in body, as Hua had expected. Shamans were strange entities, with stranger powers, and Hua was curious to know what made them work.

Two hours in, Hua called a break so the shaman could be watered and so Hua could handle her hangover better. She was starting to suspect something in her breakfast was off, because her nausea had only grown worse.

“This Lord finds himself rather curious,” Hua said when Shaman Yan had recovered and was ready to head on. “And he would have answers if you wish his assistance.”

“Duty not enough for Liao? How far you all have fallen.”

“One must know what cause they work towards to fulfil their duty,” Hua said, clipping her sharp words till they were blades. “And I do not know who you serve, Shaman. Your people once served eastern invaders. How can I know you don’t serve foreign masters once more?”

“Only foreign master we know is called Liao. Liao who leaves us abandoned. Liao who be watching us die and be doing nothing. The lie called Liao is all we got. All we ever had.”

“Your dramatics are needless. I would have an answer to a rather simple question. Where have you been sourcing your supplies?”

“Supplies? Where we getting food is what you don’t trust? May as well say you don’t trust us not dying.”

“Indeed. Based on your explanations, you have been on the run for weeks if not months, and some of your members for far longer. You had no time to organise your flight and have been making it up as you go along. I have seen no outposts or villages for you to have traded with. And so, I am left to wonder why you haven’t all fallen over dead from starvation. Why do you have strong spirits and medication to treat your injuries? And your silver—don’t look surprised, hiding it doesn’t change how loudly silver taels jangle. You carry silver and not enough grain that I saw. So, explain.”

Shaman Yan shot Hua a hateful look, one matched by Weiji’s surprise. It intensified when Hua refused to follow, leaning against a tree trunk. Mercifully cool, Hua closed her eyes, imitating relaxation when all she wanted was to be sick.

“Coaltown. That’s where we get all our things from. Get all our aid from there. Silver and grain and meat and the kind of medicines I can’t make. All that stuff, the stuff that lets us do the living, we get from there.”

“Curious. How exactly? The Mayor rejected you. You most certainly would have been hung if you walked in there looking the way you do. So, one of your people petitioned him, many of them perhaps, but whatever they could carry on their backs wouldn’t be enough for your numbers.”

“The head of the mining union is an old friend of mine. Grew up together but he went for the money. I went my own way. When I called, he answered. Been helping us since to get us supplies.”

Weiji hummed. “Where do the Mayor and Justice fit into this?”

“Those eunuchs don’t. If they knew anything about this, then they’d have rope around my friend’s neck faster than you bring out the lightning. Probably the same rope that took their manhood,” she added lowly.

“For working with a shaman or for another reason?”

“The politics of Coaltown don’t much make sense to me. Never did. He just told me that his boss man Huang would get him killed if he was caught up in any of this. And there’d be no bringing it to the Mayor because they’re all in cahoots. A whole lotta corruption there.”

Her cousin threw his hands up. “A bandit army led by a Cultivator, a corrupt Justice and Mayor, a miner and an evil boss. I suppose we would be the benevolent Cultivators—no, those were the Jolly Rangers and they’re probably in a mass grave. We’re the distant force that arrives at the end of the story and brings peace by executing everyone involved. How much more convoluted will this get?”

“Our lives are no story. Easy from your distant city, maybe, pretending that our lives don’t have no meaning, that they be thrown away easy. But hear me proper: we endure, Liao or no.”

Whenever she became heated, her accent deepened, and Hua found it near-incomprehensible. Slamming her forehead into Weiji’s back hurt less than deciphering the shaman’s words.

“Despite all these forces, you somehow manage to communicate and receive supplies from this miner. A singular miner. How did you manage that feat of logistics?” Hua asked. The Shaman scowled. “You can answer, or the people waiting for you can die. It makes little difference to me.”

“There’s a smuggler we work through.”

“Ah, now the criminals appear. This tale would not be complete without them.” Hua stopped and glared at her cousin. He stepped back, raising his hands. “Apologies. Please, continue your sordid explanation of the criminals you work with, preferably every detail you know about them.”

“You two are very young,” said the young shaman who could be no older than Weiji. “Thought it was just the hair and the Cultivation that made you feel like a young one. But you both… how is you the lord Liao so young? Where is your father?”

“Shaman, you go beyond yourself. Do not give me reason to kill you.”

“Grew up with stories of the Liao Clan, horror stories the lot of them. What your kind did to me and mine. And now my enemies ain’t any older than I.” The Shaman shook her head. “Sailor Kong, he ses the old smuggling routes. Through the marshes and streams. Too small for any big barge but a boat with a few days’ worth of food and some silver can make it.”

Is the Sealord involved in this as well? No smuggling occurred without his permission. More and more threads were being added to make a tapestry beyond Hua’s view. Threading through it would be like telling colour purely from touch.

“And you can’t use the roads because of the bandits.”

“No, but they’re less a bother than the mayor’s patrols. He doesn’t let anything get out. Doesn’t let his workers escape.”

Odd. A city mayor wasn’t meant to be a great landowner. That wasn’t a title of nobility. A lord owned the land, and servants rented it through their labour in the form of their taxed crops. It was collected and flowed up to the Liao Clan who was the highest lord of the region.

But that wasn’t important. Liaojiangkou hadn’t been affected by this apparent lawlessness. Had it occurred recently or was it a concerted effort?

“Kong sounds foreign,” Weiji pointed out. Hua made a sound. It wasn’t that foreign. Weiji was just being paranoid again.

“Maybe. But blood be mixing in this province. Met me children born of Jurchen and Goryeo. Some even carrying names from the Eastern Sanctuaries. Knew me more than one Isshin in my time. After that blade saint, yeah.”

“You’re well read to know these places or these people. Not what I would expect from a peasant or shaman. Your old masters, yes, but not the rest.”

“We had to know. They might give us hearth and home when your clan wars sweep on by and devastate the lands again.”

“Neither of us was born during the last one, so let us not pretend you have any idea what those were like,” Hua snapped out, the sharp snap of her teeth clacking adding to her irritation. “They would all kill you without remorse.”

“Same as you. Same people, same mistakes, same bullshit. Always mine getting killed the most.”

And, well, that was true.

Steaming, stewing in anger and old resentments, they made the long journey, each step colder than the last, to a field of horrors.

It was part of a farming commune, the village they reached.

Built overlooking a small reservoir fed by a small river from the north, it was lush and green. Along the river grew plum trees in great numbers, stripped of their leafy summer coats by the early fall. The early winter had swept through this village as well, frost already forming in the fading sunlight.

Perhaps that was why the bodies were so well preserved.

Unclothed corpses hung where they were tied to the trees by rope around their ankles. Swaying in the wind, swish-swash, death on the breeze. The shaman was frozen more solid than the corpses that had been picked at by carrion. Strips of flesh hanging as surely as the corpses they were attached to. How soft she was to find this disheartening. Far, far worse occurred all the time.

“I knew something terrible had happened, but I didn’t expect this,” Shaman Yan said softly, her hands covering her mouth. “No one deserves this.”

They searched the village and found no one still living with any of the clay and thatch huts. Explored the central gathering building and found signs of blood and violence. If the great patch of frozen crimson was to be believed, a mass execution had occurred here.

“What would make them behave like this? No animal would act like this.”

“People are singular in their creativity,” Weiji said softly as they were examining one of the hanging bodies.

The shaman said, “Someone went out of their way to impale these people. Seen demons less evil.”

“And to castrate them,” Hua added with a frown. They had nothing dangling from their exposed crotches. It was a gruesome sight, one infested by flies and maggots. “That’s needless even for a bandit making a show of terror.”

“So why are their heads missing? What madness could Kang be doing with them?”

Yes, that was the other thing. Each corpse had been beheaded. The cuts varied, some messy, some clean, all old.

Hua pointed where the fields laid fallow. “Probably for that field of skulls.”
