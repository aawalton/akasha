Once we took the first three steps down, the world simply… vanished.

Not in a jolting, violent way. More like drifting into a veil. The bioluminescent greens blurred, smeared, then folded inward until they were nothing but a wash of cascading light. It swallowed sound first—V’s muttering, Ten’s bickering, Fallias’s tail tapping lightly against the ground—and then shapes. Then even the weight of their presences fell away.

I was alone.

Suspended.

Sightless.

Weightless.

A falling dream without the falling.

Then—very suddenly—not alone.

The light dimmed into a soft grey glow, like dawn trying to remember itself. And there, standing in the near-distance, was a young man.

No—not young. Ageless. But shaped like someone in the early bloom of adulthood.

His clothing was the first thing I saw: a snow-white robe, drenched in stains of red so deep they looked painted on. The colors shouldn’t have complemented each other, but somehow they did, like brutality arranged so carefully it became art.

He held a small silver platter in one hand and plucked a grape from it with the other. His posture—relaxed but heartbreakingly lonely—made him seem less like an executioner and more like a poet abandoned by his own verses.

His expression was tranquil. Somber. Tired in the way only immortals ever looked in stories.

It did not match the blood.

It did not match the silence.

It did not match this realm.

Everything about him was wrong.

And off-putting.

And deeply intriguing.

“Greetings. Salutations. Hello,” he said with a gentle dip of his head, as if trying on each greeting to see which fit best. He chewed the grape thoughtfully. “How doth do you fare on this day?”

His voice was soft. Musical. Old-fashioned without being antiquated. Like someone imitating archaic speech but not fully committing.

It took me a second to find my own voice.

“Uh—normally…” I cleared my throat, steadying myself. “Normally you don’t start by separating someone from their team.”

He blinked, slowly, almost theatrically.

“And two,” I continued, “if you’re planning on a formal introduction, you start with your name.”

“Yes,” he said, as if I’d said something profound. “Names. So terribly important to you.”

He ate another grape.

“This one is called Sadrin,” he announced with a fluid, practiced cadence. “Sadrin is my name. You may call me Sadrin.”

I stared for a second, trying to catalog him.

He was tall—but not imposing. Lithe—but not fragile. His hair hung in straight, pale strands like strands of spun frost. His eyes were a soft, muted gold, unfocused yet razor sharp at the same time, as if he existed both here and in a thought far away.

Everything about him screamed Dominus. Or something close enough to count.

I swallowed.

“Okay…” I said carefully. “Sadrin. Where exactly am I?”

“In between,” he answered with a sigh that seemed to carry centuries. “But only for a moment. You came to me. I came to you.” He gestured vaguely at the shimmering nothing around us. “Thus, I brought you into the Between so there would be fewer… complications.”

“That doesn’t answer anything.”

“That answers the things that require answering.”

Of course he said that.

Dominus-types loved riddling nonsense.

He popped another grape into his mouth, chewed, and tilted his head at me thoughtfully.

“You are Alexander Duarte,” he said. Not asked. Declared. “Walker. Visitor. Rabbit child of two worlds. We have observed you.”

My blood hand froze in place. “…Who exactly is we?”

He ignored the question entirely.

“In truth,” he continued gently, “I have been waiting for you. You and your companions—yes, yes, I know their steps and scents and fears. But you most of all.”

He stepped closer, and the ground firmed underneath my feet automatically, as if reality wanted to make the approach more manageable.

“You touched the conduit,” Sadrin said. He smiled—small, fragile, a little sad. “And the forest touched back.”

“So that thing was alive?”

“All things here are alive,” he murmured. “Some more than others.”

“That’s not creepy. At all.”

To my surprise, he huffed a quiet, almost embarrassed laugh. “Apologies. I forget the sensibilities of your kind.”

“Why am I here?” I asked.

He finally stopped walking, standing just outside arm’s reach. “To speak. To greet. To understand one another before the others return.”

“Return?” I frowned. “They’re not hurt, are they?”

“No,” he soothed. “Merely traveling a different… rhythm.”

“You separated us.”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

He plucked another grape, held it between his fingers, and let it drop. It didn’t fall. It simply dissolved into light.

“Because,” Sadrin said with a soft tilt of his head, “your mind presented fewer risks. The others would have… reacted poorly to the Between. You… adapt.”

“Adapt is a nice way of saying I’m already used to weird.”

“Yes. That.” He smiled faintly.

I crossed my arms. “And what do you want with me? With us? With this Gate?”

For the first time, something shifted behind his eyes—an echo of a weight he didn’t normally let surface.

“We want contact,” he said. “Not conflict.”

That… was reassuring. Sort of.

“Sadrin,” I said slowly, “are you the Dominus of this realm?”

A pause.

He looked at me—really looked at me. Not the casual glance you give a stranger, nor the suspicious once-over you’d give a potential threat. No, this was the kind of gaze that seemed to peel apart the layers of a person, sift through the pieces, and weigh which ones mattered.

The air around us reacted first.

A faint vibration rolled through the clearing, like a plucked string from some unseen instrument. A hum—too resonant to be mere sound, too delicate to be called power—passed between us, rippling over my skin in a way that made every instinct in my body straighten, bristle, prepare.

Then, in a voice soft enough to be a whisper yet heavy enough to feel like a verdict, he answered:

“No. But I am their herald.”

Herald. Not Dominus. Not ruler. A messenger. An extension of someone who was not present.

And for a realm like this—an entire forest suspended in glowing bioluminescence, watched by unseen eyes yet devoid of fauna—that single word felt too small and too massive at the same time.

I forced myself to swallow and speak, keeping my tone as level as possible.

“Okay. A herald.” I crossed my arms out of habit, not confidence. “So tell me, Sadrin. What is it you want with me— with us?”

He tilted his head, expression still somewhere between serene and bored, and plucked another grape from the ivory-white bowl balanced on his knee. He ate it delicately, like someone imitating courtly manners rather than possessing them.

“Haven’t I already answered that?” he said, sounding genuinely confused. “We want contact. Not conflict. We would rather you not burn your way through the Nexus when you could simply… walk through it.”

“There's that word again,” I muttered. “Nexus. You used it when talking about the forest earlier. I assumed you meant this place.”

“Indeed,” he said with a gentle nod. “Correct. You are absolutely right.”

The hum in the air faded, replaced by the distant, rhythmic pulse of light from the bioluminescent canopy above. For a moment, I could almost pretend this was just a conversation in a strange clearing with a stranger in stranger clothes.

Almost.

“What,” I asked carefully, “is a herald in this case, Sadrin?”

He inhaled slowly through his nose, exhaled as though considering how simple or complex he wanted the answer to be. Then he tapped one finger against his chest.

“I speak for the Dominus of this realm as its… janitor?” He frowned. “Caretaker?” Frowned deeper. “Maintenance?”

He waved his free hand dismissively.

“Something along those lines. I manage what remains functioning. I monitor what has failed. I tend to what still breathes, and I archive what does not.”

A chill crawled its way up my spine, and not because of his tone.

Because of what he said next, so casually that it almost didn’t register:

“Sadly, Astratius has been absent for… a long time.”

Astratius. The Dominus. The one who should be watching us, judging us, deciding whether we were Visitors or Others.

And they were absent.

Not “sleeping.” Not “indisposed.” Not “meditating in a starfield for a year or two,” as Dominus sometimes did.

Just… absent.

My stomach tightened.

“How long,” I asked, “is a long time?”

Sadrin shrugged, elegant but helpless.

“I do not know time as you do. But the Nexus has weathered thirty-two full decays since he last spoke.”

Decay. Not a word anyone used lightly.

“And,” he added, popping another grape into his mouth, “I have not personally seen him since before the last collapse of the Northern Veins. So. A while.”

A Dominus going missing was never a good sign. A Dominus going missing long enough for the realm itself to rot in cycles? That was cause for the Walkers, the Guardians, and maybe even the Council of Twelve to start sharpening knives and drafting emergency protocols.

And here we were—my team—standing on the threshold of a realm whose ruler had effectively ghosted it.

“So who’s running things, then?” I asked.

Sadrin smiled, soft and almost apologetic.

“I suppose… I am.”

That hum of dread in my chest returned.

“And the Nexus?” I said. “You said it earlier—something about not burning our way through it.”

Sadrin nodded. “It is the heart and skeleton of Astratius’s domain. A living intersection of paths. A forest grown from concepts rather than seeds. Every tree is a conduit. Every lichen is a memory. Every root is a vein.”

He looked past me, at something I couldn’t see.

“When Astratius was present, it thrived. Now… it endures.”

I thought of the forest outside—the oppressive humidity, the lack of fauna, the bioluminescent glow that shouldn’t work as lighting but somehow did. And most of all—

The feeling of being watched.

“Is the Nexus watching us?”

“Yes,” Sadrin said instantly. “It observes anything foreign. It is wary. But it does not strike unless commanded.” He paused. “I have not commanded it.”

That was… mildly reassuring.

“So,” I said, drawing in a slow, steadying breath, “what exactly happens now?”

Sadrin stretched out one hand, palm up, as though inviting me to place something in it.

“Now?” he said gently. “Now we speak. You answer some questions. I answer yours. Dialogue, not hostility.”

“And my team?” I asked.

“They are safe,” he replied, a touch of annoyance entering his tone. “Separated only so you and I can converse without the clutter of six overlapping anxieties.”

“…Overlapping what?”

He smirked. “Your companions are very loud in their thoughts.”

I stiffened. “You’re reading—”

“No. I am listening,” he corrected, tapping his temple. “Difference.”

I didn’t like that answer. Not at all.

But I forced my shoulders to relax and exhaled slowly through my nose, the way Morgan taught me years ago when dealing with interrogation pressure.

“Then ask,” I said. “Let’s start.”
