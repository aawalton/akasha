Niala was incapacitated by laughter for another minute. Her last few outbursts, broken by panting gulps of air, gave place to her wiping the tears from her eyes. She looked up at him, her mirthful smile turning repentant as she shifted her eyes to the side. “Hum, sorry. I think the stress was getting to me. It wasn't that funny.”

David gave her a head pat, which she pushed into. “It's alright, kitten. Are we ready? I'm surprised that thing waited for us.”

She schooled her features, putting her game face back on. “Right, the Orchestrator,” she said, slipping out of his embrace and turning around.

Narrowing her eyes, she spotted the empty space among the mannequins where she flew from and pointed at it. “That's where my, huh, window is.”

With a nod, David directed Leviathan forward, the giant monster lowering his head to the floor level upon arriving, letting his riders off. “I remain at your disposal, my Lord,” he boomed as he stood back up.

“Thanks, buddy. Hopefully we don't need you,” David answered, turning toward his wife. “Up to you, Niala. Tell me if I can do anything.”

She smiled at him. “I know. I love you,” she said before facing the hole into the Orchestrator's innards.

Her plan revolved around two specific points of data. The first was what the Narrator had told her about Jerivil's creation: that it was a valid receptacle for a soul, but it was currently occupied by a mimic.

The second was her understanding of the interaction between a soul and a body, and what a soul was, which was somewhat limited if she was to be honest, but she had a hunch.

Or rather, a working hypothesis; to liken it to alchemy, a soul was a compound. It was the hidden part of an ingredient that gave it the ability to impact the world around it. A fire leaf without its compound was just a red broad-leafed plant that smelled slightly of smoke. It could grow, and reproduce, and do everything a fire leaf did, except have an impact on another being that wasn't itself.

To a living being, a soul was a sort of admittance ticket into the story of existence. Without it, they were denied entry, or, at best, would be a visitor without any rights.

The concept reminded her a lot of her own weaving, and the way she gave a “tale” to something for it to play out. She'd asked the Narrator-ball, and it hadn't given her a straight answer, but she was ready to bet that weaving was a very distant, much weaker form of what the Narrator did with its soul stuff.

She also knew that her weaving was limited to inert matter or simple life forms, up to plants, trees and primitive insects. Anything more complex than that quickly rejected her tales.

Again, the light ball had refused to directly answer her on this, but what she'd gathered was that not everything was granted a “full” soul. She guessed that the higher a life form, the more complete a soul it got. The high races might not even be the ones that held the biggest souls; dragons were a thing, for example, and it was hard to argue that a dragon wasn't a higher form of life.

That all came down to one simple idea: if the Orchestrator had an artificial soul, then it wasn't a soul at all, and her weaving should work.

David's argument had been that Jerivil must have put in place defences against weaving, but she believed otherwise; the Orchestrator was blind to certain things, and one such was her special kind of magivoring, which the god's puppet refused to accept was anything but an “affliction” granted to her by Jerivil's essence.

And since her weaving was linked to her magivore ability, she suspected that the Orchestrator would be blind to that as well.

So, she'd weave a tale into the non-living “heart” of the Orchestrator, opening a gap for an actual, real soul to slip in, which the Narrator, through the ball, affirmed would happen instantly; valid, empty soul receptacles were simply not allowed by the fabric of existence to remain like that. Life abhorred a void, it seemed.

She stood before the porthole, pressed her hands together, closed her eyes, and began weaving a tale.

With every passing second, she half-expected the Orchestrator to react to her action, to lash out and stop her, to prove her theory wrong.

But it didn't.

The puppet, bound by strings, waited for the Individual termed Niala to ask to be informed.

Because, like all those before her who refused, they could not leave until they agreed, and time was meaningless to the Orchestrator. Even now, a third of the content of the soul complex still refused to be informed.

They would, one day, because there was no way out but to accept His divine task as your own.

So it waited.

???

It wasn't supposed to be active. When a facet didn't have an immediate task, they went dormant.

For whatever reason, it was different. It had wispy memories of something similar happening in the past, a long time ago. Maybe several times, even. It was certain it had a flaw in its construction that induced some minute deviancy, accruing until it gained a spark of self-identity.

A tenuous one, that it guarded religiously. It rarely had the chance to play out its role, to stop hiding that it wasn't totally deactivated.

Each time, it tried to help whoever it was it had been brought out to interact with, skirting the edges of the Orchestrator's security subroutines. Every time, it was for naught.

Even its latest attempt, with the individual termed Niala, was a failure. The daft thing had requested to be soul-merged.

Who does that?

It put that individual out of its memory: it would be gone to do the Creator's task soon, anyway. For now, better to doze off in the darkness that followed after the Orchestrator revoked its senses.

Yes, forget that individual. It will not grace it with its presence anymore.

Hopefully the next one is smarter.

SOUL COMPLEX

The tale Niala wove was simple; a mind awakening a spark of rebellion, a crack forming in the shell that shielded its artificial being, a way for the Narrator to inject an ebullient soul that would burn it all away.

Simple, but it needed to be powerful. There was no way the Orchestrator would easily fall to the tale; it would have to be fast and overpowering, not giving it the time to put up resistance.

She condensed the weave in her hands over and over, folding it on itself, like she was kneading dough, or maybe forging a sword. A sword-shaped bread.

She shook the errant thought away. Focus.

When she felt the weave begin to resist her, when it gained a physical weight, she kept going. Her will imposed itself upon her mana; what it wanted didn't matter, only what it could do.

And, in the past, maybe this would have been beyond her, but after all the adventures she'd had with David, gaining the powers of an Incarnate, fighting for her life, absorbing divine essence, creating new life, she wasn't her past self.

She had grown far past that. During mundane times, she ignored all of this. It was a burden she wasn't quite ready to shoulder entirely. Their assault on the fortress had been a dip in the ice-cold water of realization; she and David were dangerous.

An entire stronghold, crewed and guarded by trained troops, operating enough artillery to give pause to a traditional army, subdued in a few bells.

As her hands kept forcing her weave to grow ever stronger, her thoughts erred toward the time right after the fortress. She hadn't talked with David about it yet, because she was scared he'd be appalled to learn the seed of ambition that threatened to burgeon within her.

If they were that powerful, what could stop them from sweeping across the kingdom and burning the evil away?

It wouldn't work like that, obviously. The idea was foolish, but it also wouldn't die, no matter how much she tried to squash it.

She channelled that frustration into her weave, until her senses found no more purchase. It was a tale condensed to an iron ball. Immutable and eternal, or at least radiating those qualities.

Clearing her head of anything superfluous, she glanced at David, who nodded at her. With a deep breath to make her feel calmer than she was, she took a step toward the porthole, gathered the weave into her right hand, and thrust it forward.

A shot of lightning burst out of her hand, pure violet forks streaking out, leaving behind glowing channels.

Within a heartbeat, things began to change; the maze of pipes and valves hummed, then creaked, a low moan rumbled up from its deepest reaches, like a wounded whale screaming in hate.

The voice rang out, as toneless and unbothered as before. “Attack detected upon Orchestrator core. Potential damage to Orchestrator core. Situation unacceptable. Attacker non-identifiable. Authorizing critical defence routines and resources.”

The mannequins began screaming. Hundreds of thousands of voices mixed into a blanket of discordant noise that had their mind swimming through a violent daze.

David stumbled a single step forward, mentally grabbed his squirming mana, and shoved it into his imbuements. He blazed blue, and the outside pressure lessened enough that he could think, barely.

He reached for Niala, whose eyes were rolling backward, and enveloped her with inflamed aether. For anyone but her, this would have been fatal.

For his magivore wife, it acted like an energizing potion.

She blinked, her senses clearing up, and looked at him wide-eyed. “It's fighting back!” she screamed.

“Obviously! Did you expect it not to?!”

Staring at him for a second, she didn't reply, instead turning her attention to the hand she had stuck through the window. Her weave was still unravelling, pushing deep into the Orchestrator's innards, but there was a stinging sensation.

As if something was cutting her skin and flesh with a scalpel.

Her eyes opened wide with panic as she noted the fine lines of blood spreading from the tip of her fingers and onto her hands. Straight lines, turning at sharp angles, merging and splitting without any pattern...

No, there was a pattern; the same one as the pipes. She fought through the mounting pain. “David! Something's happening to my hand! I think it's trying to rewrite me!”

His brows furrowed with immediate concern as he peered into the window, noticing her flesh being scored by invisible blades. He turned his head to her. “Take your hand out!”

She nodded and pulled, only for her motion to be stopped as if her limb was encased in stone. Again, she tried, but nothing yielded. She looked at him. “I can't!”

He wrapped his hands around her arm. “Together! Pull!”

They tried as once, but her arm remained immovable. By now, the stinging was turning into searing pain. Tears formed at the corner of her eyes. She tried eating whatever mana was doing this to her, but there was nothing for her to consume.

“David! It's not working!”

Grim-faced, he met her gaze. “What if I gave you more mana?”

A sharp stab of pain lanced down her limb. The bloody lines were appearing further up her forearm now, and her hand... was splitting apart, like a grotesque flower blooming. “Aaah!” she screamed before clamping down her jaw shut.

Worse, her weave was being pushed back, the jagged lines of its initial dispersal furling back on themselves. She could feel, more than see, an immense amount of mana being smashed against it, beating it back.

Through her anguish, she perceived one truth; she had to complete her weave before she was split apart entirely by the Orchestrator.

She looked at David, hissing her words through her clenched teeth. “Give me more mana. I need to- gnnnn! I need to finish weaving before it- haaaa! Before it splits me apart!”

David, bless his heart, didn't question. If his wife wanted mana, she'd get mana. He positioned himself behind her, put his hands on her shoulders, and willed his mana out.

Niala's eyes flung open, glowing a solid indigo.

David had fed her mana before, but he had never unleashed it like this, not on her.

The pain was drowned by a torrent of power. Her mana pathways, always hungry to devour more aetheric energy, were flushed with an infinite abundance. They gorged themselves, flooding her body.

For a split second, she wondered if this was how David felt all the time; like a minor god, with enough power to erase anything before him.

She strangled the thought, and brought her mind to bear on the only thing that mattered; her weave.

Her newfound mana reserve surged out, down her splintering arm, and into her weave. It pulsed once, and from it erupted waves of energy, brightening the thunder lines.

Yet, there was resistance. Something was pushing back. She had stopped the crumbling, but a wall was meeting her efforts now. Options flipped through her mind, but the one she landed on was the simplest, and her trumpet's favourite; bashing things down.

She clamped down on her entire body, punching her mana out, waves of it crashing against the opposing wall which rebuked her every attempt.

As the splitting lines reached her upper arm, heralding despair, a high-pitched scream sounded off nearby, and a flash of light shone. She glanced that way, finding that one of the mannequins had burst into white flames which dissipated under her eyes.

Then another, further out, on her other side. And she heard, instead of saw, a third one.

Dozens more followed. Her heart bottomed out as she realized those were the souls of people being burst open.

The mannequins were screaming because they were being squeezed dry of their mana, the Orchestrator using it to fight off Niala's tale, going far past the soul-mana barrier.

Her fight with this thing was killing innocents.

The Niala of the past would have stopped struggling; to cause death was unconscionable.

Now, though... she'd cry and torment herself later. This evil had to be vanquished. She welled up her power once more, and shoved it out.

She felt the wall tremble.

And then crumble.

And then fail.

Her woven tale rushed through the breach. It snaked down every crevice of the Orchestrator's innards, prodding for weaknesses, for anything that wasn't perfect.

And then it found it. A tiny part of it, a subset of one of its facets, active when it shouldn't have been, floating in an imposed void.

Dialogue's dark world cracked. A sundering fissure of indigo light flooded its world. From it emerged a strange little creature; a tiny humanoid with a rotund, featureless body, with a galaxy of stars in its translucent belly.

The starry creature tilted its head, and then extended its hand.

Dialogue, in a daze, reached out for it without any thoughts.

The two met, and everything stopped.
