After a few grueling, exhausting minutes for Sigurd, the elder dwarf finished measuring the man and led him back out to the lobby, thereafter offering him a cup of cold water. Sigurd voraciously gulped down the refreshing beverage and asked for seconds and thirds. Once re-composed, his thoughts returned to him.

“I can’t impose,” he barely managed to word.

“Nonsense,” Zhelyazko snapped back. “If I can’t repay you for what you’ve done, then I’ll offer you my life, for real. I’m in your debt, and you won’t budge, so I propose that you and me be business partners from now on. Anything you want, whether you need anything fixed or crafted, ask and you’ll get it, free of charge. And I won’t take ‘no’ for an answer!”

Feeling backed into a corner, it was Sigurd’s turn to begrudgingly accept the blacksmith’s terms.

“Now, as for your sword. As soon as it’s ready I’ll have someone deliver it.

Zhelyazko’s transactional comment brought Sigurd back into focus, “Oh good. So roughly how many days will I be waiting?”

“Days?” the dwarf asked, confounded. He scoffed and added, “It’ll be done in a few hours.”

“Hours?!” Sigurd mimicked his confusion.

“But of course!” Zhelyazko pumped out his chest, proudly boasting, “I’m a master blacksmith, the best this side of the Vierinkivyo. This is nothin’ for me.”

“A few hours then… until I go back to using my sword,” the way Sigurd delivered the follow-up sounded sad, almost disappointed.

“I promise my handiwork will make it so your sword never breaks again!”

“It’s not that I doubt you,” Sigurd sighed. “It’s that I yearn to fight in a different way. One not constrained to the blade, but it’s not possible. And it’s less to do with the art and more with me.”

“Well, if it’s something that can be crafted, I’d love to give it a shot,” Zhelyazko replied, taking a seat at the front desk.

Sigurd approached the desk from the other side, and – recalling his past indiscretions – carefully chose his words, “It’s not so much a matter of crafting, as it is of the internal mechanisms that may make it an impossibility, but it came to me in a dream: a handheld, portable device capable of firing metal pellets at high speed. It was called… a gun.”

At the mention of the word, Zhelyazko’s eyebrows twitched; his expression softened, seeming humbled. “Like Gunnhildr,” he whispered. “That’s no coincidence. Do you recall what it looked like in the dream?”

“I do,” Sigurd nodded slowly. The dwarf handed him a sheet of paper, a plume, and a bottle of ink. He dipped the tip of the plume in the ink, but before pressing in onto the paper he had a moralistic thought.

‘Wait a minute, what am I doing?’ for once, Joyce weighed the consequences of her actions. ‘If I draw a firearm and he makes it, what then? Something so sophisticated would surely upend battles in this medieval world. It would permanently change how wars are fought, and scar the people in a never-ending cycle of deterrence-seeking.

‘Am I prepared to assume such a responsibility? Is it even right for me to selfishly and absentmindedly ask for such a thing? Earth became the hellhole it is because of firearms. Do I really want to dramatically alter this world’s playing field? Do I dare continue here?’

But just as easily as those thoughts intruded into her mind, a similarly self-serving thought took its place, ‘Well, even assuming he could make it, I doubt the concept of gunpowder exists here. And magic would only be a bad substitute.

‘And besides, worst case scenario is I have a gun and non one else does. I deserve a little fun too, after what that shitty goddess put me through!’

Putting aside his momentary hesitation, Sigurd finally pressed the plume unto the paper, thereafter tracing the lines from memory. He was no artist, and excused himself as such, “You may not even understand what I’m about to draw. This will probably come out very crude.”

“That’s fine,” the elder dwarf replied, inviting his granddaughter from her chair to observe as well. “I’ll work with whatever you give me.”

Despite Sigurd’s terrible posture and freehand skills, he carefully tried to recreate his favorite handgun on paper. An artist he was not, but using the knowledge Joyce had obtained from her life as a soldier, the drawing’s intricate details spoke volumes. After all, part of any soldier’s training was to know the ins and outs of most basic firearms, from the internal mechanisms to the external parts.

While he traced the strokes – trying desperately to instill on the paper his vision – Sigurd inquired, “You mentioned Gunnhildr before. What is that?”

“It’s not a what, it’s a who,” Pikku replied in her grandfather’s stead. “She’s a warrior of legend in our clan, revered as the concept of war and resilience.”

Zhelyazko then interjected, “It’s said that she comes to brave warriors in dreams and inspires them to do their best, through ancient charms and whispers.” He paused and observed the drawing take shape. Captivated by what he saw, he added, “I think that’s what happened here, Sigurd. It’s the reason why this thing is called a ‘gun’, most surely. She gave it her name.”

Inspired, Sigurd’s crooked lines took shape in the form of the barrel, the sights, the muzzle, the hammer, and the grip. He also detailed the magazine, both as whole and individually. He even drew the bullets. All of it was detailed in his mind, imprinted from hours upon hours of disarming and rearming that gun of his in a past life, his metal baby.

In the end, Sigurd drew more than just what he set to, in a fleeting and fantastical hope that he could get what he wanted. The final product on that sheet of paper seemed more like a crudely-drawn diagram.

“So this is it,” he said, putting down the plume. “I don’t think it can be done. There’s lots of things here I can’t even begin to draw; a lot of engineering that needs to take place.”

“Be that as it may, you’ve intrigued me!” Zhelyazko took the drawing and scanned it. “I’ll see what I can do about this, even if all I make is the frame. For now, I’ve kept you long enough and I need to finish my work.”

Sigurd agreed with the notion and departed the blacksmith’s workshop. The moon began to rise at that point and he quickly returned to the inn. True to his words, Zhelyazko had the fixed blade and the custom armor sent later that evening.

Content with how the day went, the daring rescue, and that his sword was finally fixed, Sigurd was ready to face on even more trials and meet even more people. Yet the best part about the adventure that day, was that, unknowingly, Sigurd had made a life-long friend.
