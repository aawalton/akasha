“I understand you are building your own spear techniques,” Moonhand said. We stood in a sparring pit, isolated from the first pit. “As you should, being a Dupe.” He gave his own spear a whirl, slicing its glowing head through the sand. It left a perfect line in the sand. I didn’t know if it was possible, but I thought I heard a screech, as if he’d sliced some of the grains perfectly in half. “The best I can do is teach you a variety of styles, and hope that you deploy them in the Labyrinth. Turn them into Skills, parcel up the memories, and draw on them.”

“Sir, I thought you said not to think about what we were doing…” I said. I raised my shield and spear into a ready position.

“Hm.” Moonhand snorted. The snowflakes whirled in front of his nose. “I’m far more interested in the result. Your Focus is high. You can think about every single muscle that twitches, if you practice enough, and not worry about lagging behind in a fight. You are not every Dupe.”

I chewed my lip. “I suppose…”

“Once you parcel up your memories, it will be much easier. Now, begin. Throw everything you have at me.”

I circled, until I realized I was putting myself in the same position Squirrel had been in earlier. It wasn’t ideal against someone like Moonhand.

I stopped, then inched forward, spear raised. I tested him with a few small swipes, which he quickly deflected. He retaliated with a lunge, striking my shield and making me stumble to the side.

At first, I glanced down at my shield, making sure he hadn’t cut a hole right in the middle of it, but he’d flipped his spear over at some point, and I hadn’t even noticed. He’d hit me with the blunt end.

I approached again, jabbing three more times, aiming at his silver shield, but each time, he pushed them away with his spear.

I’d land at least one more strike on his shield today. That much, I promised myself.

Moonhand turned the fight back on me. He moved to the offensive, lunging and swiping, and moving me around the pit on a whim. Disappointment filled his eyes. I wasn’t holding my ground. I’d lost the initiative, and just resigned myself to defeat?

No, no I hadn’t. He hadn’t said anything about using our Arts or not.

I used my Art high up in the air above us, not affecting anything except the snow, and envisioned what was about to happen next. If I attacked, where would my spear go? I knew more than enough about Moonhand to use this against him.

When I had a plan, I enacted it.

I ducked to the side instead of trying to block with my shield, then leaned in. Feinting, I pretended as if I was going to bash him with my shield, as if I was about to do anything to land a hit, then instead hooked my spear around the back of my shield and pulled it down. His grip didn’t break, but it exposed him.

I whirled my spear around, trying to hit him with the tip, but he was still fast enough to deflect it, then hit me with the haft and push me back.

“Good,” he said. “Aiming for a strategic victory instead of a prideful revenge hit. You would’ve at least made me bleed—had you been faster and more confident in yourself. You didn’t trust your own plan?”

“I will keep practicing, sir,” I said.

“Keep attacking me, then. You already know wisps of the Falling Leaf style—I can see that in how you fight—but you could stand to learn its sister style, the Rising Leaf style. Moreover, you must learn to integrate your Arts into your strikes better. You wasted it up in the air? Why? Did you not know how to use it, or was that just instinct?”

“I suppose both, sir.”

“At least we know what to work on. Very well. Attack me again. This time, begin lower. Start slow, and work up to your finale.”

In the evening, I returned to the smithy again for my meeting with Drywsone. My arms and hands were bruised—mostly from Moonhand striking me, not the other Dupes—and my legs were stiffening up, but I still hobbled over to the counter.

“The dust will soak into the armour,” Drywsone said. “It will make an excellent final tempering ingredient, so long as you can get the dragon’s core crystal.”

It separates them from the rest of the Labyrinth and resets their goal, in combination with the hairs in its mouth.”

At the end of the week, during our next Labyrinth run, I did my very best to use Moonhand’s combat suggestions.

The sooner I could turn them into a Skill, the better.

We didn’t go after the Topaz Dragon yet. I wasn’t going to do that until we were absolutely ready. If it was anything like the level guardian, we needed a little more practice before we got there. I wanted to ensure my attributes were at least close to Gold-tier. My Presence would need time to catch up, but the others would go faster.

Besides, today, we needed other ingredients.

“Anchor has the list Drywsone made,” I said.

“Rawhide and some sort of maegen-infused wood,” Anchor said. “Preferably some kind of sap that he can use as glue.”

“I’ve got the sap part covered,” I replied. “He can have some of my alchemical solution. The wood itself, though? That’s the hardest part.”

“I believe I know a monster,” Sage said. “Once again, follow me.”

Today, instead of racing to the edge of the city, we approached the city center. It was difficult to place, but the lava canals all met up together at the center. The actual densest part of the city was located off-center on one of the canal’s spokes. We crossed over a few bridges and jumped over a deep gorge that led to an even deeper (but unpopulated) lava pool.

Along the way, we slew a Stingbreath and a Flameknuckle, not to mention any skeletons that tried to intercept us. It was becoming just another routine, but using them to help boost the danger helped boost my Skills. I practiced my Perception Skill, feeling for the arcane weight of the monsters.

It was hard to put an actual value to their level. It wasn’t like a magic tag appeared above their head, telling me their tier. However, comparing their weight to each other, to my squadmates, and then to what Sage told me, I was able to get a sense of how heavy each tier felt.

Heavy wasn’t the right way of putting it, though. It was more like pressure. Irons inflicted a pressure that made it feel like you were plugging your nose. With Steels, it felt like you had a nasty head cold. With Titaniums, it was like swimming to the bottom of a deep pool. With Steels, it was like swimming to the bottom of that same deep pool, except you had a stuffed head at the same time, making the pressure infinitely worse. As for Golds? If a giant wrapped his hands around your head and squished, that was what it felt like.

The problem was, the pressures changed the farther away you were. If I used the Skill and the monster was behind a wall, a faint ripple ran through my senses. Something was there, though I couldn’t explain how I knew. The world didn’t change, but on the other hand, it was obvious that something was there. I could practically envision an outline of the monster through the wall.

That meant my Perception Skill had hit Apprentice-tier.

Along the way, I also practiced my memory palace formation. I drew on the sphere, trying to picture it, and at the same time, where I planned to put my memories.

Today, I nearly sealed up the sphere. It was more than halfway envisioned, and once I finished envisioning it, hopefully it would stay. It was something elastic. I just needed to pull it the last little distance, and get it over the hook.

And that required an extra push of willpower that my body couldn’t muster without feeling like I was about to split in half.

The Labyrinth and its monsters just weren’t enough of a push.

Finally, we arrived at an oval track in the city center. It looked like a running track crossed with the Colosseum. Marble walls nearly fifteen storeys tall wrapped around in a circle. We passed through a hallway at its base, arriving on a soft plane of black sand. A boulevard ran down the center, and a single tree grew in its very center.

From here, it didn’t look unusual. It was the shape of an old yew tree, and it had no leaves. Fair enough, it was the winter, and this thing had been underground for centuries.

But why the hell was there a tree here at all?

“An elder forest golem found its way in here,” Sage said.

I glanced at Morph. “And…we’re alright with killing it?”

“Morph is a tame, more modern breed. This one is not. It is necessary, and we need the wood,” Sage replied. “It is only a Silver. It should not be a problem.”

“How did you know this was here?” Anchor asked.

“I overheard the other druids speaking about it,” Sage replied. “It spooked a squad when they broke in. Apparently, this is a ‘broiled’ variant. It is slower, but it hits harder, and its bark has been tempered. Its wood is flexible, but incredibly durable.”

“Let’s make this quick,” I said. “We still need to find some sort of rawhide alternative, too.”

We rushed in, maintaining our formation. Already, the extra combat practice came in handy. As we approached the tree, it grumbled. Its trunks groaned, and its bark cracked. It hoisted itself up on its roots as if they were legs, then lumbered toward us. Each of its branches was a mace, and it swung them toward us.

Though it turned its trunk slowly, it had a massive wingspan, and the branches at its ends whistled past my face faster than an arrow.

I stayed low, using my shield to deflect what I could. Brawl swung his flail with near-perfect precision, and Anchor deflected using his bow. That couldn’t be good for it—to use it as a melee weapon—but apparently, Moonhand had given him a few tricks to help it last longer. Sage was faster at using Morph to create protective shields above and beside her, and she extended them beyond to protect the rest of us as needed. Since we stuck closer together, she didn’t have to reach as far.

We fought our way to the center of the lumbering forest golem. I made sure to practice the Rising Leaf style, employing its swooshing movements to deflect branches, and using the edges and wings of the speartip to break branches.

In combination, I practiced timing it with my Art. I could pull something down quickly, ramming it right into my spear to help shear it off quickly, then triggering the Art a second time elsewhere to immediately cancel the well, allowing me to pass through it unscathed.

When we reached the core, I shouted, “Where do we hit it?”

“Aim for the exact same place you hit Aelfwine’s Birch with the catapults, if you remember,” Sage said. “Proportionally, of course.”

Bark protected the tree’s core, and it lashed out at us with its root-y legs. Brawl protected us as I swapped to my crossbow and took aim. “At the same time, Anchor!” I called. “Now!”

We both launched an arrow at the same place, and the bark shattered. Anchor’s arrow punched a deep hole into it, and I followed up by charging the same place with my spear. I jumped, without even relying on my Art to fling myself, then stabbed my spear into the hole.

With my resonance Skill, I pierced through something hard. A black crystal broke under my spearhead’s jab, and the tree stopped moving with a sudden lurch.

Climbing back down to the ground, I turned to the squad and motioned to the tree. “Take what we can.

“We should try to chop it up into a few easily-carryable pieces, at least,” Anchor said.

“Trunks, brother. If we cut it into rings, I can manage,” Brawl replied.

“After this, we’re still gonna go after one more monster,” I said. “Something with a hide. Don’t fill your arms too much. But after that, it should be everything we’ll need.”
