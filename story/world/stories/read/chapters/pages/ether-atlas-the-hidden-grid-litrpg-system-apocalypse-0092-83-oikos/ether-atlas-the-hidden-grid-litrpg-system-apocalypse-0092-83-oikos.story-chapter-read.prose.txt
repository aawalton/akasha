Chapter 83

We have all been walking toward coordinates since Day Zero, nodes and temples and monuments, and not one of them has been a home. Sarah has one. A place that remembers her and where the cutlery has always been in the same drawer. I am an archaeologist. My whole career is based on that everyone, eventually, had a before. It is strange to walk beside someone returning to hers while it is still warm. — The Ether Atlas: Field Notes of Steven Parr, Athens

The coil closed before Arthur fully understood that it had landed.

It came down across his shoulders out of the pine canopy, a weight like a sandbag thrown off a roof, and then the weight became a band and the band drew tight across his chest in one smooth pull. His arms went to his sides, the shield held uselessly against his thighs.

The pressure was even along his ribs like a strap tensioned by a winch, and it was climbing. His next breath was shallow. The one after it did not come at all.

His ribcage was a frame, and the frame was being asked to carry a load it had not been built for, and the load was reaching a number past which a structure simply goes. Then the primitive part of his being took over. The animal part. The part of him that had stopped calculating and only wanted air.

Somewhere past the roaring in his ears he heard Donna.

“Snake. On Arthur. More in the trees.”

Archie was inside his head, the voice arriving without sound, and it made Arthur’s eyes alert for a second. “Constrictor morphology, Ether-saturated scales and muscles. It will not stop until your thorax fails. You have perhaps eleven seconds of useful consciousness.”

He manoeuvred the fingers of one hand around a loop of the body where it crossed his sternum and pulled, and the muscle under the scales did not give at all. It was like trying to bend a mooring line by hand.

Then Sarah’s hand came past his shoulder and touched the snake.

She had not planned it. He understood that afterwards, when his brain was not starved of oxygen. In the moment there was only her palm on the scales, and then the jolt.

He felt it faintly through the snake. The coil that had been tightening with such terrible patience went loose for a breath, and the creature lost track of what it had been doing.

Arthur did the one thing the slack allowed. He got the shield arm free, dropped his weight and threw the loosened coil off over his head.

It hit the dirt, rolled and tried to rise back up, the head weaving on a body that was not yet answering it.

Behind him Sarah caught herself on a pine, her hand not quite steady. “It worked,” she said, sounding as surprised as the snake looked.

Arthur brought the hammer down before the thing stabilised. The head turned at the last instant and the blow landed across the thick neck, and the iron sank into Ether-dense muscle and stopped. The creature spasmed.

He hauled the hammer free and struck again. It took two more after that before the weaving stopped. The hammer had killed larger things in fewer blows than this.

Donna had taken the second snake already. The lattice was bright across her throat and the sides of her face, Threshold Drive running, and she moved through the dark between the trees faster than the snake could read her. The axe came down on the spine behind the skull. It bit and then caught, the muscle closing around steel.

She wrenched it loose with a noise of disgust and struck again at the same seam. The snake hissed and darted for her head. With her technique active she was miles faster. She sidestepped and swung the axe in an upward arc, aiming for the vulnerable belly. It took her two more strikes to kill it.

She let go of Threshold Drive and cursed. “The damn scales are too thick.”

Steve was at the back with the rod in the two-handed grip, the air at the rod tip flickering between heat-shimmer and a rim of frost. The third snake was coming low and fast along the ground toward Donna’s blind side. Convergence left the rod and reached it before it could bite down on Donna’s leg. Its locomotion fell apart.

By the time she turned, the creature was steaming and icing over. It lay there spasming at her feet, its belly up. Donna took the invitation and went about the butchery with grim determination. It took her four strokes even through the belly to sever the head.

Arthur saw the fourth gather on a low branch and lunge, the long body launching at his chest, and this one was a strike. A strike had force in it and force was a thing he could use.

The shield came up as he activated Counterforce Plant. The snake hit it with a loud thud, and the impact went into the reinforced lattice instead of through him. A charge sat stored in his arms, waiting to be unleashed. Arthur did just that.

He swung the hammer and let all of it go at once.

The iron came down at the base of the skull with everything the snake had spent on him riding on top of his own force. Half the skull caved in and the thing lunged again with half its skull missing. Arthur barely managed to bring up his shield in time.

A second later a fire lance from Steve burned through what remained of the brain and the snake went still.

“You cannot be serious,” Arthur muttered, looking at the dead snake at his feet.

The woods were quiet now. Arthur stood with a hand against a trunk and worked at breathing, which had become a bit of a project.

Sarah was at his side. Her palm was flat on his chest over the sternum. “Rib,” she said. “Eighth, left side. Cracked, not displaced. The muscle under it is bruised.” Warmth spread under his shirt and the worst of the grinding eased. “That is the most I am doing on our feet. The rest when we get to the house.”

“Thank you.”

Sarah went about checking for cores and found two. She parted the skin, muscle and tissue with a surgeon’s precision, which in her case was not merely a metaphor. She tossed both the cores to Steve, who placed them in his core pouch with a grin. The pouch had residents again.

Donna was examining her axe. She held the rolled edge up to what little light there was.

“We won that on techniques and three strokes a snake,” she said. “Not on steel. The axe and the hammer are finished. They are lagging behind. The damage output will be a problem if these snakes are any indication of what we will face here.” She slid the blade back into her belt. “We need new gear before something comes at us that needs raw damage to put down.”

“Ether-soaked materials,” Steve said. “Anything that has sat in this much Ether for a month is past Refined. We have carried London hardware into Athens.”

They found the road lower down, where the pines thinned and the ground gave over to cut stone. It ran dead straight between two walls, the blocks fitted close. It pointed downhill at the dark sheet of the Aegean.

The party started down the road, the walls offering a measure of security.

“This is the old road to the port,” Steve said. “They walled it on both sides, the whole length, six or seven kilometres, so you could move between the city and the harbour without ever being in the open.”

He was quiet for a few steps, then gave in to his inner demon.

“Pericles started it after the Persians came through. The entire point was that as long as Athens controlled her harbour, it could be surrounded on land and food would still get into the city through this road.”

“Steve,” Donna said from the rear. “Is any of that going to stop a snake.”

“No.”

“Then walk.”

He did as he was told. But Arthur watched him slide the stub of pencil out and make a mark in the ledger without breaking stride, as he marked everything now, and heard him say, half to himself, “Walls on both sides, though. Nothing flanks us in a corridor. That is worth knowing.”

“Listen to you,” Sarah said over her shoulder. “Sharing the apocalypse with Donna has made you a tactician.”

“I can break down at least two dozen historical tactical battles.”

“Those who can’t do, teach,” Donna replied.

“The actual phrase that the Irish playwright wrote was ‘He who can does; he who cannot, teaches’. Completely different meaning,” Steve shot back.

“Also, if Mrs Hadley hears you say that, you will be in a world of trouble.”

“Then it’s a good thing she is back in London,” Donna replied with a smile.

“Isn’t she the librarian you guys met?” Sarah asked.

“Yes. The one who scolded Donna like she was a little girl,” Steve said, enjoying every minute of it.

“Steve, your tactical brain should tell you not to piss off the woman with the axe. I would thump you over the head before you can say Mrs Hadley.”

It got a laugh out of all of them as they continued down the ancient walled road.

“How far to your mother’s?” Steve asked after walking for about half an hour.

“Forty minutes, give or take.”

“Archie. Anything alive between here and the water? Things that might want to eat us?”

“Three heat signatures, four hundred metres downslope and west. Their posture and movement are consistent with humans who want to be discreet. They are unlikely to try to eat you. I cannot speak to their other intentions.”

Donna was past Sarah and at the front before the sentence finished, without seeming to hurry.

“Down,” she said, and the party crouched into the lee of the western wall where the cut threw a deeper shadow.

Three men, and then a fourth, moved along a track where the walls broke for an old gateway. These were not civilians, though the distinction mattered less and less in this world. They moved in a deliberate spacing, covering sight lines and each other’s flanks. They carried their weapons like men who had been taught how.

“Those are not regular survivors,” Donna murmured. “Survivors clump. That is a patrol. Somebody is running this city, and they have enough men to run patrols at night.”

She waited until the spacing carried them past the gateway and behind the far wall, counted something under her breath and moved the party across the open stretch in one quiet rush to the next band of shadow.

The walls let them out near the bottom where the ground levelled off. The harbour at that hour was peaceful and quiet. A few boats knocked against their moorings. The sea lapped in a soothing murmur.

Sarah’s pace changed as soon as they reached the harbour, her feet following a known path down to people she hoped were safe.

She led them off the main road and down a narrow lane lined with flat-roofed, low-slung houses and walled courtyards. Stopping at a gate, she gestured toward a sprawling residence that was somewhere between a villa and a mansion. A lemon tree and a pomegranate tree stood like sentinels near the property line, while mature mulberries dotted the rest of the spacious yard.

The gate was a vibrant yellow. Sarah undid the top latch and pushed it open, motioning the party inside. The sound of the gate opening stirred the house and a light came on in a window.

Sarah crossed the yard alone, the others hanging back just inside the yard gate. As she raised her hand to knock, the door opened, and the sound of a plate shattering echoed in the quiet night.

Sarah’s mother stood in the doorway, her fingers clutching air as she stared at the daughter she had not seen since before the world ended, who was now standing in the dark of the yard with three armed strangers at the gate.

Nobody moved to pick up the pieces.
