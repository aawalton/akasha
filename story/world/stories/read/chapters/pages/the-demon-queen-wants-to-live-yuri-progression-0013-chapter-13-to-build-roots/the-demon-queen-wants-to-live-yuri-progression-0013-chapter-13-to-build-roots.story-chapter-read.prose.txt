Time passed. It had now been two days since the Inking. My marks had grown, just barely. The twin Sparks were larger now, and their once clear edges were less defined. They did not grow past this point, no matter how I tried, and I certainly tried.

They stayed sparks. Neither the Requiem nor the Cradle had become the Line I had seen on Marsh's arm. Even this much was fast progress for people of this time, I was told. It took most dedicated users months before their Sparks grew, if they ever grew at all.

I trained every moment I could. I trained at dawn first, and then at dusk. And between the two I was forced into a routine I had not chosen, filled with people who would not leave me alone.  Today, the village had found a new way to torment me.

This latest torment took place in Martha's kitchen. I stood there with my arms folded, watching the old woman work.

Her hands moved through dough with speed I would not have expected of her. Every motion was practiced, and the practice I knew well. It was the kind that only came from doing something ten thousand times.

"Now it's your turn, Lily."

"I did not agree to this." And do not call me that. I almost said it. It would have been the fiftieth time. The woman would not have listened, and so I didn't bother.

"Your friend said you'd be happy to learn." Martha smiled without looking up. "She was very insistent."

Ash -that treacherous wench. I would deal with her later.

The dough faced me. Of all the challenges I had ever gone through, this was the lowest of all of them. I reached out and did as the woman had done. The dough was cool and slightly damp to the touch. My fingers tore right through it.

The woman did not sigh or even frown. She moved in beside me, and now her hands guided mine. "Listen dear. You have to go softer. It's not fighting you. You can be gentle with it."

She stepped back, and let me make the next attempt on my own. There was still too much force. The dough bunched and split under my fingers. It was like trying to work mud that wouldn't take.

"Gotta let your palm do the work," Martha said. "Just your palm. Push gently with it, then ya fold here, and then you turn. Do that a few times till it's done. That's all there is to it."

I pushed. The dough slid off the table and found the floor. Martha picked it up, brushed it off, set it back down, and during this she did not say a thing. I would have -my patience with my subordinates had always been thin.

On my new attempt, the dough held. It moved sluggishly but it did not tear. I pushed, folded and turned, just as the woman had done, and with each repetition the motion became less alien. It did not become natural, it never would.

The door opened. Ash stepped inside and moved to fill a cup from a small jug. Sweat covered the Hero's clothes even as it still ran down her brow. Her work had started far before mine.

Martha handed her a cup without looking away from my dough. "Here you go love."

Ash stopped mid step. She stopped for half a second, perhaps less. Her hand tightened on the cup and something in her face twisted before it settled into a smile -a faint one. She took the water and left. I watched her go.

"What was that?" I asked.

"What was what, dear?"

I frowned, turned the question over. I could not recognize its shape.

The bread came out terrible. One side was the color of ash while the other was barely cooked at all. It sat on the table between us. Martha cut a piece, ate it, and chewed thoughtfully. "Not bad for a first go," she said.

I had heard many lies in my time. That one would have made my Court Whisperer weep with admiration. I ate a piece myself and it was dreadful, dense enough that it might have served as a weapon. I ate all of it. It was still better fare than I was used to, and I had made it, with no powers at all.

The woman was watching me. And hers was an expression I was beginning to recognize, but not understand. It was the same one she wore when she refilled my bowl without being asked. The one I still had no name for.

I sat outside at my usual spot, by the low wall.

The afternoon had just begun. I was pushing mana through both Sparks simultaneously -Requiem on a stone with my right, and Cradle on a dead twig with my left. Days of this, and the motions had become almost comfortable. It was rather convenient to have the power to make and unmake. I moved the twig over to my right, the stone to my left and began again. I suspected this would not do anything, but I did it anyway.

Martha came out a few minutes later. I thought she came to speak to me. Instead, she sat nearby, on a stool she had brought from inside, and began shelling a basket of dry beans. I had done the task myself. It was the worst of my small torments.

We sat in silence for some time. I did not mind it. Her silences were different from most. They did not always demand filling.

"Sara's been making something new," Martha said. Not to me, exactly -to the air between us. "Saw her down by the river this morning with a whole handful of clay." I said nothing. "She started that after Edrin passed last winter." Martha cracked another shell. "Think it keeps her hands busy."

"And who is this Edrin?"

"Her father," she said, still shelling. There was no change in the rhythm at all.

I said nothing. I did not know what I could say.

"Used to follow him everywhere, that girl. He taught her to work the clay and they made pots for the whole village. He wasn't much good at it neither." She smiled at something I could not see. "She used to make 'em too, with him. Never did again after he died."

And then she had made one for me. Just because she'd thought I was pretty. No, maybe it had been more than that. Something inside of me twisted and pulled. I moved the stone to my right, the twig to my left.

"Doesn't have a mother neither," Martha volunteered without being asked.

"What? I have seen her with my own eyes." She had been with the girl at the Inking. I had seen her.

"Oh, you mean Tamara," Martha said, still shelling. "She's the girl's aunt. The mother died in childbirth. Now...now we all take turns caring for her, helps split the load. The girl doesn't really take to many, but we try."

Martha kept shelling. She did not seem to expect a response. That was good, because I did not have one. The stone beneath my right hand cracked. The twig beneath my left grew another bud.

It was mid-afternoon when I saw Ash walk toward the hut she had been avoiding for days. I almost missed it. I had been focused on the marks, running through another cycle of my new powers. When I looked up, she was already halfway there.

Her stride was different -slower and more deliberate than I was used to seeing. I watched from my spot. Ash reached the foot of the hut. The mother was there, as she always was now. The woman looked up and there was a moment where her face tightened.

Ash sat down on the ground in front of her. She stared at the mother, who held her son. She did not do anything besides sit there. If she spoke, I could not hear.

I watched from across the village. She was a Hero stripped of her power, and she chose to sit in the dirt beside a broken child. She had no power to fix him. She could not bring back whatever the boy had lost when those black veins had crawled up his arm. All she could do was sit there. What was the purpose of it?

They stayed like that for a long time. Distantly, I saw the mother sit up a little taller. More distantly, I realized I had forgotten to train my marks altogether.

Ash came back later. She moved to walk past me and our eyes met. Days ago, I had stood outside a door and heard her weep. The question was still there. It sat like a lump in my throat. I held the Hero's gaze a moment longer than I needed to.

She held mine back.

The day  drew on. In the afternoon, I caught the Hero helping patch the roof of a hut near the center of the square. A small crowd had gathered to watch. Ash's strength made the work easy. Perhaps the villagers found it entertaining.

I had come to sit nearby, under a large tree. One needed small changes like this, if only to freshen the mind. That was why I was here.

"That girl's worth three men!" someone called out.

"Five, at least!" another voice.

"Let me see those marks of hers! Sovereign, wasn't it? Can't believe I wasn't here for-"

The Hero paused. And then her shoulders climbed, and something in her smile went tight.

She kept working, but more stiffly than she had been. I wondered how many of these people were here for those marks, and not for the work at all. There was a particular hunger power always inspired in those that had very little of it. I had seen it often.

There was a whisper of mana and something shifted, so faintly I would not have noticed if I hadn't been sitting so close. I inspected Ash and didn't see anything at all. Not until I looked. The Sparks on her forearm had faded. My eyes trailed over them, almost wanted to slide right off without finding purchase.

The man mid sentence lost his train of thought. The woman beside him turned to say something to her neighbor and stopped. The gathered eyes drifted. Slowly, so slowly I would not have noticed had I not already been watching.

"Need to get the stew done."

"She's just a hard worker."

Ash exhaled and her shoulders dropped. She kept working, and nobody stared. The crowd dispersed.

That had to be her mark -her Cypher mark. It seemed to have done more than just hide her marks from the people watching. They truly had been there only to gawk at them, or they would not have left.

It was natural that I could still make them out. My mind was too sharp to fall for such a petty illusion, as diminished as I was. Or perhaps it was something else. I did not examine that thought. It was just one more thing I filed away. The list was growing, and by now it was getting long enough to warrant its own library.

The sun moved through the sky and the light dimmed. Evening approached.

I sat near Tom, who was sorting goods from his last trip -the one where he had found us. He was arranging jars and sacks in the space beside the cart. I had not decided to take a place near his cart. My feet had simply carried me here.

I made no conversation. But Tom saw me and talked, because that was what Tom did. "- takes four days to Koralis and back, and even that's if the roads are clear but they're never clear, are they? There always ends up bein' something. Last month it was mud, so much mud the cart couldn't move. Month before that? Those damn wolves -same ones you fought. Now there's something new. Heard the eastern roads got beasts too, and more frightening ones than wolves, so that's another detour. What in the hell's is happening to the world late-"

And as he talked, he loaded the cart. He placed a sack of grain at the bottom, then stacked two crates of jars on top, then wedged a bundle of cloth in the gap beside them.

One thing was bothering me. "You are loading that like an idiot."

Tom paused his rambling. "Pardon lass?"

Now that I had spoken, there was no reason not to continue. I pointed. "See there. You are loading the grain at the bottom, and yet grain absorbs moisture. The bottom is where rain collects and pools. Doubtless you lose a portion of your stock to dampness every trip. Keep the grains at the top, so the air can reach them." I gestured at the cloth next. "And clearly those should go at the very bottom, for they can be dried out later with no loss at all. And those jars," I pointed again. "They should sit in the center for balance, not stacked where a single bump in the road will topple them."

There was silence. Tom looked at me. He looked back at the cart as if he was seeing it for the first time. "Those are not the only flaws. See there. It is uneven on that side. You have likely been loading heavier goods on the left. Now the frame has warped towards it. If you only distribute the weight evenly, the cart will last twice as long and won't ride like some demonic beast." And it had ridden like a demonic beast. Sleep on it had been hard to find.

Tom stared at me and then at the cart and then back at me. I had only told him because of the unpleasant journey he had subjected me to.

"How do you know all that?" he asked slowly.

"I managed armies," I said. "Compared to that, your operation is one man and a cart. It is not difficult."

Tom looked at me for a moment longer as he scratched his chin. Then, without a word, he began to unload and restack. He asked two questions while he worked, and I answered both. I realized in the middle of the second answer that he was looking at me the way his wife Martha did.

"You're a sharp one, Lily," he said.

I let the comment pass. Correcting it would be to acknowledge I had heard it.

Time passed, and before long, it was dusk. I went looking for Ash. Martha had sent me on an errand to find her. I should have refused on principle, but the old woman had a way of asking that made refusal feel like more effort than compliance. Ash was not in the village. Martha had told me where she would be. In a clearing past the low wall, on the village's left side. There, I found her past the treeline.

Ash stood there, alone. Next to her stood several stones of varying sizes. The smallest of them was no larger than my fist. The largest of them came up to my knees. Ash knelt beside the largest stone. She pressed the tip of her Hero's Sword to its surface and drew it gently across the stone.

And it came apart in two halves, with no sound I could hear and no force I could see. The halves were cut so smooth I could see reflections. I watched from the edge of the clearing. She had not seen me. Many of the other stones bore those same perfect cuts. I moved closer and a damned twig cracked beneath my foot.

Ash turned at the sound. She saw me and straightened even as something crossed her face. "Training like a thief in the night. How very unlike a Hero." I said.

Ash took a second to answer. And when she spoke, her voice was hard. "I didn't want to worry anyone."

It tasted like a lie, and a poor one at that. Something in me wanted to press for the truth. Instead, I sat down at the edge of the clearing, not far away from her. She watched me. She watched me for some time. Perhaps she waited for me to say something. I would not. After a long moment, Ash turned back to a stone, pressed her blade to its surface, and traced another line.

The stone split again. Ash was already moving for yet another one.

The two of us stayed like that until Martha's voice found us through the trees. She was calling us for supper.

There was a large fire in the village square. One so bright it made the fire Ash had built in the forest seem small. The fire wasn't planned. Someone had kindled it for warmth. A woman had brought out her pot and then Martha had brought hers, then Tom had dragged a bench over, and then another, and eventually the rest of the village had joined in.

I stood at the edge of the square, close enough to feel the fire's warmth on my skin. Far enough that I was not part of it. The flame crackled and threw shadows that danced along the walls of the huts.

Tom was arguing over the fire's crackling with another man about the proper way to roast meat. Someone else was arguing with another over the proper way to skin a boar. Women talked in hushed whispers over things I could only guess at. Children ran between the adults' legs, chasing each other the way children always seemed to.

A melody began to play over the talking and the crackling. One of the men had brought a string instrument, and he sat and played it from atop an overturned cart. The sound was strangled, and the melody wandered in directions that even I, who knew nothing of human music, suspected were not intentional.

His strangled music did not matter. A woman began to hum along, then another, and then a few of the children.

Ash sat far closer to the fire than I, amongst the other villagers. She had found her place easily, and now she ate and talked to the woman beside her. When one of the men said something to her I could not hear, she laughed. The firelight caught the marks on her arms. Few people looked at them now and she looked happy. I wondered if this was what she had wanted all along.

Occasionally I would catch a furtive glance from someone in my direction, gone almost as quick as it had come. Whatever glue joined Ash to this village, it was clear I did not quite share it.

A hand found mine.

I looked down. A little girl stood beside me: Sara. She took my hand and held it, her small fingers wrapping around two of mine. Her grip was warm and firm. She had been wandering close to me for some time now. I had not said anything then.

I did not pull away and did not move closer. I just stood there with a child's hand in mine, watching the fire. The hand felt warmer by far.

The terrible music played. I had heard the warhorns of my horde. I had heard the deathsongs of the Elven people, which were said to be the most beautiful sounds in existence. I had heard a Goddess sing, once, in the days before I had killed her and her brother. I did not leave.

My gaze found Ash across the fire. Her eyes were searching for mine and hers was a strange look -not one between a Hero and a Demon Queen. Not one between a person and someone who had killed their friend. Not one between friends either, I think.

I held the look and did not know what to do with it. I held it anyway. Sara's hand squeezed mine. It was small and warm. The Cradle mark twitched and stirred.

Nearby, two of the children from the ceremony were sitting cross legged by the fire's edge. The boy who had received his Somatic mark was showing it to one of the older children, holding his arm out proudly.

"What was it like?" the older boy asked.

"It tickled! And then I felt really strong." He flexed a small arm. The dull Spark on his wrist didn't even glow. "Makes me feel really strong! Mummy says it might be something else, but I'm sure!"

People did not stand in that darkness and see that screen as I and Ash had. I had asked the questions as subtly as I could, and for them, it was a trance they woke from, their new marks on their arms. Moreover, the villagers I had asked had told me they only vaguely knew what their marks did, barely knew what they were. That was a small advantage I had -I, who had seen them spelled out for me. The thought didn't matter right now. Sara's hand was still in mine.

The warmth seeped up my arm, up my shoulders. It swirled and eventually came to settle somewhere around my chest.

I had once thought that Hamel was nothing. It was still nothing. For one night, it was enough. The fire crackled and the music played. The stars sat above us in a sky so vast it made even me feel small.

The sound came from the north. It came as a rumble that I felt in my chest. The music stopped and the man's fingers froze on the strings. Every head in the square turned. Sara's hand tightened in mine. The sound was closer when it came next. The third time it came, it was close enough that the lanterns shook.

The fire popped and a child began to cry. The night was no longer enough.
