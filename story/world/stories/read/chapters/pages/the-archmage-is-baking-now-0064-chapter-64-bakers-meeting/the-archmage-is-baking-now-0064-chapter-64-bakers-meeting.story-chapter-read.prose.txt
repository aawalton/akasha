The problem of the buns solved itself around noon. Dave walked in, spotted the basket, and moved to grab it.

"Are you going to take all of those again?" Jane asked.

"Yes."

"Dave." She put her hands on her hips. "As much as I don’t like to waste food, I can't always give away all the buns for free just because they didn't sell. I have places to donate them, if it comes down to that."

He laughed. Then, reaching into his bag, he drew out a handful of coins and put them down on the counter.

Jane stared at the coins. The pile was more than big enough to buy the whole basket. "Are you buying them all for yourself? Where did you get the coin?"

"No. It's the Underbridge Market." Dave tucked the basket under his arm. "I took the last batch there yesterday and handed them out for free. Today I stopped in, and they told me to go get more. They like them. They're talking about putting in a regular order."

Jane stared at him. "Was it that easy? Just handing them out?"

"It was this time." Dave shrugged. "And they're paying me courier rates to pick them up, so don't worry about me."

"I won’t.” Jane scraped up her pile of coins and put it in the register. “I think you’ve earned my trust when it comes to knowing your business.”

"Good. Make the same amount tomorrow. Like I said, you'll need them."

He was out the door before she could say anything else. This was a new Dave, and the new Dave didn't waste time.

The rest of the afternoon passed quickly. Jane sold the last of her regular stock to a few late customers, then began the process of closing up shop. It took longer than usual, mostly because she had been lax on cleanup the past few days. Actually sweeping and scrubbing the shop to where it was supposed to be, instead of just looking that way, was a lengthy but necessary task.

She was just hanging up her apron when someone knocked on her door.

Jane opened the door to find an older woman standing just outside. She recognized the woman as Lynn. This was one of the bakers Jane had taught to make keln all those months ago, after learning the recipe from the shade of the baker who’d introduced it to Glenfall. Since that day, Lynn and Jane had met a few times at the market, never talking much beyond a polite greeting and a bit of baking chat.

"Oh, good. I caught you." Lynn wiped her forehead with the back of her hand. "You're the last baker on my list, and I was worried I might miss you."

"Miss me for what?"

"The bakers are meeting. Can you come?"

Jane blinked. "All the bakers in town?"

"Most of them." Lynn was already glancing back down the street, clearly in a hurry. "Everyone we could find, anyway. I caught all of the ones I was supposed to tell."

A vague sense of worry gripped Jane. Visions of flour shortages danced in her head as she tried to imagine what force could make every baker in town need to confer with the others immediately.

“Why?” she asked. “I mean, for what reason?”

Lynn shrugged. "I don't know. I just offered to help gather everyone. Someone will explain when we get there, I'm sure."

"Of course. When is the meeting?"

"Now."

Before Jane could get her bearings, Lynette had taken her by the arm and was more or less shooing her out of her own shop. Jane barely had time to grab her keys and lock the door before she was being hustled down the street.

"Is something wrong?" Jane asked as they walked. "You seem like you're in a hurry."

"Everyone's already there by now. I’m curious, you know. I don’t want to miss anything."

The walk was short, only a quarter of the way around the lake to a spot near the incoming river. The bakers had gathered in an old warehouse with a few tables and chairs dragged in, which Jane supposed could qualify as a ‘meeting hall.’

"That's everyone,” Lynn announced once she’d hurried them both inside. Then she went to take a seat at the back, satisfied she had done her job as an organizer.

Jane found an empty chair near the door and sat down. She counted nearly twenty people, which was most of the bakers in Glenfall. She knew all but a couple by sight, and most by name.

A man at the front of the room stood up. Jane recognized him as Eric, one of the older and more established bakers in town. His shop had been open for decades, passed down from his father before him. Jane was a relative infant in comparison. If anyone could be named as the leader of Glenfall’s bakers, it was him.

"Thank you all for coming," Eric said. "I know this is unusual, and I apologize for the short notice. But something has come up that affects all of us, and I thought it best we discuss it together."

Jane tensed. She had felt as if something was about to happen, and now something was. Whether or not this was what she’d been sensing was yet to be seen, but this was clearly not nothing.

She felt her hands creep down to grip the side of her chair, as if to keep herself from floating off or running away.

“Now, don’t look like that. It’s not a bad thing.” Eric smiled and held up a parchment. “I’ve just received this from the town council, and they asked me to tell everyone about it. The king and his family are coming here in a week or so. To vacation, I suppose.”

The room exploded with questions, loud and chaotic enough that Jane couldn’t pick out any one of them. Eric lifted calming hands over the crowd and called for order. It took him almost a full minute to get it, but eventually, the room did calm down enough for him to continue.

“Now, now. I’m not sure why he’s coming, but there’s no indication that it’s a negative sort of thing. The king does travel, you know. He’s never come here, but as far as the town council is concerned, our only worry should be making sure we put our best foot forward. I agree with them.”

“But what does that mean?” shouted a worried auntie-type from the back. “I mean it. I don’t understand what’s being asked.”

Eric nodded. “I’m getting to that. The council has decided to institute a new festival, a sort of celebration of the town’s productivity. A few weeks from now, we’ll gather several bakers to show their wares, alongside every other kind of work our town produces. The smiths and craftspeople will be doing the same kind of thing, as will the town’s artists and chefs. Everyone, really.”

“And we all participate?” the same woman called. “I’m hardly ready for something like that.”

“You don’t have to participate if you don’t want to,” Eric replied. “In fact, we won’t have room for everyone. It’s a showcase, you see. The council asked us to determine among ourselves who to send, and what to show. I figured anyone who wanted to could make some show pieces, and we’ll judge them amongst ourselves before picking our delegates.”

Murmurs rippled around the room. Nobody seemed opposed to the idea, which Jane understood. In fact, she shared the sentiment. If the king was going to come inspect their town, then the bakers would not let themselves be found wanting.

It took only a few minutes to pound out the simple details. Any baker who wanted to participate would return to the ‘meeting hall’ on the next rest day, in three days’ time, bringing a few examples of the best baking they could do. All assembled bakers would sample everyone’s work and make a decision. In the end, they would select three bakers whose work represented the absolute best their town could offer.

Jane left the building with her head spinning. She hardly noticed where the other bakers went. Though she had been doing well enough as a baker, her success had been helped along by the fact that her shop was much closer to some customers than others. She had been living in a situation where ‘adequate’ was often enough. Now she was faced with a new scenario, one in which adjectives like ‘best’ came into play.

She was confident enough in her own suitability for her job as it stood, but much less sure that she could hold her own among the town’s finest, especially when they were working with the king himself in mind.

Still, she found herself smiling. If baking had become a bit ‘same old, same old’ recently, here was the fix. She might not win, but she liked the idea of competing again. Competition had always been a part of her life at the academy. At least, before she so hopelessly outpaced all the other students that there wasn’t any point in it anymore.

Let’s do this, she thought, her smile widening until she was grinning like a child. But what could I possibly make that’s fit for a king?

—

When Bella found her late that night, all of Jane's cleaning had been ruined. Her kitchen was a disaster again. Every available surface was covered with bowls and pans and ingredients in various states of preparation. Jane’s arms were floury up to her elbows as she stood at the counter, working on three different recipes at once.

"I've never seen you flustered like this," Bella said from the doorway. “It’s fun.”

"I get flustered all the time."

"Not like this." Bella stepped inside and looked around at the chaos. "This is a new variety of flustered. It has a distinct flavor."

Jane didn't stop working. She had half a dozen of her favorite recipes going, all the particularly decent things she had developed over the course of her time as a baker. The burnt-top bread was rising in one corner. A batch of keln flatbread was ready for baking. Three different variations of her new meat buns sat cooling on a rack, waiting to be tasted and judged.

"It's the king," Jane explained. "He's used to the very best. And even if he didn't care about food at all, the queen and the prince will be coming. One of them might actually taste what I make. I can’t approach this casually."

"I understand. I’ve already been thinking about what kind of breakfast a royal might like. I hadn't heard the prince was coming, though." Bella leaned against the doorframe. "He's supposed to be handsome, isn't he? That's what I've heard."

"Sure. I've heard that too." Jane punched down a ball of dough with more force than was strictly necessary. "But I have enough handsome in my life right now. I'd much rather he was a starving bridge troll. Hungry enough to enjoy my food, rather than good-looking, if I had to pick."

Bella laughed. "Fair enough. So how do you rate your chances? Of actually winning a spot at the main table, I mean."

Jane stopped working and stared at the mess around her. "I don't know. I'm a new baker. You can only get so good so fast, and even then, I have no idea what to make. Everyone else has been doing this for decades. I don’t even know how to start to compete with that."

"That's easy." Bella pushed off from the doorframe and walked over to examine the cooling buns. "You make your specialties. The things you're known for. The burnt-top bread, the keln, and these buns, if you can get them perfected in time."

"The buns aren't even really selling yet.”

"They're selling in the Underbridge Market, Brit tells me. That counts." Bella picked up one of the buns and took a bite. "And they’re good. Besides, there's no point in competing on boring fundamentals. Every baker in town can make a decent loaf of bread. What they can’t do is be you. Show them who you are."

Jane considered that. It felt right almost immediately. Trying to out-bake the old masters was a losing game. But if she focused on the things that made her different, she’d at least be competing on her own terms.

"You're right," she said.

"I usually am." Bella finished the bun and brushed crumbs off her fingers. "Now get to baking. I’ll get to cooking. I’ll give you my professional opinions, and you can give me yours. That’s another edge we can count on, and I want to use it."
