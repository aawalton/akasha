The Breeder heard Saint Mercy’s Widow die through several hundred meters of seawater, and for one second, his mind refused to accept the sound.

But then the sound came to him again as a long, distorted vibration through the glass dome and the reinforced metal ribs around it, a deep underwater groan that rolled across the laboratory. He was hunched over by the extraction console at the time, shoulders rounded, one hand adjusting a pressure sequence while the other worked through the arithmetic for the extraction chambers when the sound stopped both his hands. His head turned slowly. His eyes climbed toward the glass ceiling—toward the black seawater and the pale, distant smear of daylight high above—and then his face drained of what little color it had left.

… What?

He’d heard the same groan yesterday when he carved open the leviathan’s back and implanted the Myrmur into its spine.He remembered the exact pitch of it when the parasite first latched onto its spinal tract. He remembered how the eel had convulsed against six restraints hard enough to bend two of them, how the first layers of its carapace had pushed through its skin in wet black plates, and how beautifully the mutation had stabilized once the host’s own bioarcanic density began feeding it. It’d been a magnificent specimen—his most powerful artificial Myrmur Host yet—but now it was dying?

How?

His thumb found his teeth before he noticed he’d raised his hand, and he started biting his nails as he continued looking up at the dome.

The Myrmur dying wasn’t the real problem. The real problem was how quickly it’d died. He turned from the dome and looked around the laboratory with a rising pressure behind his eyes. The ten giant tanks stood around him in a wide ring, each one reinforced with black iron braces and filled almost to the upper markings with thick, pulsating bioarcanic mass. The substance shifted slowly behind the curved glass under dull crimson lamps, dark and viscous and precious beyond any sensible measure. Nine months of work sat there. Nine months of implantations, controlled releases, maturation cycles, retrievals—nine months of turning Wraithpier into one enormous growth chamber and then harvesting the results when they became useful. And he’d thought the S-Rank Blight-Class Myrmur would buy him at least two more days to complete the extraction procedures, but…

It’d barely been a day since he released it into Wraithpier.

Oh, this is bad.

Very, very bad.

His teeth moved from one nail to the next, his breaths shortening. Gael Halloway and the Heartcord Clinic had clearly become much stronger than they’d been in Bleakhearth if they managed to deal with his masterpiece so quickly, and now that they’d taken it down, there was nothing stopping them from enlisting the Fishermen’s help to sail north of the ward and storm his lab. Pale, the Fishermen might even be glad to sail them north of the ward. Those fish-obsessed maniacs respected only those who could bring down the toughest of leviathans, and he’d served Saint Window’s Mercy up to Gael on a platter to slaughter.

For all he knew, the Heartcord Clinic was already on their way here, and by that, he meant right fucking now.

He abandoned the extraction calculations immediately. There was no more time. If he stayed here to finish the procedure, they’d be able to intercept him, and if they intercepted him here, inside this underwater lab, there’d be no second retreat. Bleakhearth, at the very least, offered tons of tunnels, decoys, and layers of expendable bodies. Where in the pale was he going to go in this place that offered only glass domes and seawater in every direction?

So he changed procedures. His hands moved fast across the control console, flipping guarded switches and opening a panel he’d hoped never to use. He entered the laboratory self-destruction sequence, disengaged the safeties on the lower ballast chambers, and activated the thermal overload process around the storage ring. Red lamps clicked on one by one along the walls. Somewhere beneath the floor, an old mechanical bell began tolling at slow intervals, and all across the underwater laboratory, more bells began to chime in an alarm-like fashion.

Two hours. Roughly. Two hours until pressure destabilization, chemical ignition, tank rupture, and structural collapse destroyed everything important enough to matter. And he had to do this. If the tanks of bioarcanic mass were to fall into the Heartcord Clinic’s hands, well, not only would they be able to mass-distribute the biomass to all of their gangsters for points—supercharging their system-enhanced strength and mutations—but Gael would most likely figure out what he and Benedict were trying to do with the biomass in the first place as well. That was a no go. If they wanted to make their ‘miracle’ come to life, the Heartcord Clinic absolutely could not be allowed to prepare for it.

But as the Breeder prepared to run and head for one of the many evacuation pods scattered across the lab, his eyes settled on the tanks of bioarcanic mass once more.

The red emergency lights reflected across the glass, and for a moment, his mind stopped racing in panic.

… Nine months, huh?

He walked towards the nearest tank and placed one hand against it. The bioarcanic mass behind the glass shifted faintly, groaning and gurgling almost like it was alive.

If he destroyed these tanks now, then everything he’d done in the nine months since he arrived in Wraithpier would be a complete waste. Every Myrmur he raised in these labs, every artificial Myrmur Host he’d created under this dome, and every corpse he retrieved and harvested and grinded to a pulp to place inside these tanks—all of it would become heat and debris at the bottom of the sea. And still he was sure Benedict would tell him to leave it all behind. Benedict would tell him to prioritize his own life and start collecting bioarcanic mass all over again in a new lab in a new location, but… that wasn’t going to work again, was it?

Not anymore.

The Heartcord Clinic was too large now. They had influence everywhere in Bharncair, and if not the Saint’s Hands in Blightmarch, then the Fishermen in Wraithpier, the Steelborn in Ironwych, and the Three-Faces in Bleakhearth. They had gangsters, chefs, merchants, physicians, smugglers, children, and even ravens serving as their eyes and ears. Even if he were to escape today and head to any one of his many dozens of reserve laboratories scattered across Bharncair—and he had to be in Bharncair in order to collect bioarcanic mass efficiently—what, exactly, was stopping the Heartcord Clinic from locating him even quicker next time? And the next time after that? And then the next time after that?

Benedict’s part of the work could be done elsewhere. His had to be done in Bharncair, and as long as he was in Bharncair, he’d never get a third chance to collect bioarcanic mass again.

… He lifted his head again and looked up through the glass dome.

The surface of the sea was far, far away, but still there were pale traces of blue-tinted sunlight filtering through.

Now that he thought about it, Caria had always preferred her sunlight green-tinted than blue-tinted. ‘Blue’s too damp’, she used to say. And she used to complain incessantly about Benedict’s habit of forgetting meals, about his own habit of forgetting sleep, and about the both of them arguing over their specimen notes until morning. She used to stand between them with stained gloves and tired eyes, passing tools back and forth, correcting labels, reorganizing tables neither man had asked her to touch, and… he could still remember a younger Benedict on the other side of a dissecting bench, hair messier, face thinner, but his eyes bright with the irritating arrogance of a brilliant apprentice who truly believed he could one day harness the power of Myrmurs for humanity.

He missed that Benedict.

And he missed Caria, too.

What would he not give to see his daughter and son-in-law smiling together again?

His shoulders began to shake as he laughed—once under his breath, then again, again, and harder again.

“...Oh, fuck it,” he whispered.

As the self-destruction countdown continued tolling around him, he wiped one eye with the back of his hand and looked at the tanks again.

How many people had he already killed? How many had he already drugged, cut open, released, broken, refined, and used as growth mediums for his Myrmurs? Oh, if the inquisitors of the Church were correct and the dead crossed the pale only to be sorted into seven different hells, then his place in the deepest pits of hell had already been reserved decades ago. Perhaps there was even a little brass plaque waiting for him.

He started cackling as he pulled a lever and entered the cancellation code for the self-destruction procedure, resuming the extraction procedure for the ten giant tanks.

The warning bells stopped, and the red lamps flickered as transfer valves opened and pressure lines hissed anew. The enormous tanks shuddered as the pump network came alive beneath them, but unlike the self-destruction procedure, which would take only around an hour to complete, the extraction procedure would take roughly six more hours. Six hours to finish stabilizing the bioarcanic mass and preparing the tanks for liftoff—and by then, Gael and the Heartcord Clinic would almost certainly be here.

If he could just stall the clinic for a little longer, he’d be able to get these ten tanks to Benedict, and that’d be the end of his part in their ‘miracle’.

That’d be his pride as a researcher.

Exhaling softly, he rested both palms against the edge of the control board and stared up through the dome one last time, smiling faintly.

“... This is foolish, Benedict,” he said softly. “Completely illogical. You would tell me so yourself, wouldn’t you? You would tell me to abandon the work, preserve the mind that created it, and begin again with cleaner conditions. Very sensible. Very proper of you, I might add.”

His smile became smaller.

“But I do miss it.

“The old house we used to share.

“The smell of preservatives we used on our food.

“Caria at the end of the table, you on the other, and me in the back of the kitchen…

“We were happier then, weren’t we?”

With a small shake of his head, he turned to face the shadows lurking in the corners of his dome, all of them silent and waiting for his command. His expression softened once more.

“My pride and joy, all of you,” he whispered. “Buy me six more hours, and I promise I’ll die with the rest of you.”

None of his artificial Myrmur Hosts answered.

They didn’t need to.

One by one, they slinked into the shadows of the laboratory, moving towards the pressure locks, the stairwells, the corridors, and taking their defensive positions without hesitation.

He grinned, cracking his neck as he whirled to start slamming buttons and pulling levers on the extraction console.

Come on, Heartcord Clinic.

I’ll show you the march of inevitable scientific progress.
