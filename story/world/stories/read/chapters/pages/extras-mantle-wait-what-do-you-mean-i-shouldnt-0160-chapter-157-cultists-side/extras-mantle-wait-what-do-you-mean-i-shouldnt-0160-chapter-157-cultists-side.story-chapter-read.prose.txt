Xerxes had never in his life been so certain of anything as he stood at the head of the table in the undercroft beneath the cathedral.

The seven Favoured Ones who remained to him knelt in a half-circle at its foot, and not one of them would meet his eyes. Xerxes huffed with pride. That was correct; finally the blind flock was seeing what their lord actually wanted.

A week ago they had knelt to Vakan and pretended not to notice that the man heard nothing when he prayed, that he mouthed the rites like a merchant counting coin, called it devotion, and led the flock further from the Lord with every order he gave.

A week ago Xerxes had been just a servant to Vakan…But now today… Vakan was inside him.

He could still feel the Lord Ranker’s spark pulsing with his heart… vast, and wrong, and beautiful, a fire built for a body three ranks larger than the one it had been poured into. It burned him from the inside every hour of every day, and his chest ached around it the way a socket aches around a bone set wrong, and he had begun, to his shame, to love the pain a little, because the pain was proof.

That he was doing what his lord wanted, and that’s all that mattered to him.

When the voice had come to him in the healing chamber—warm and low and patient as a father at a bedside—and told him that the crippled leader was no longer a vessel but a resource, that a spark left in a broken man was a spark wasted, that the flock could not afford waste with four days left.

Xerxes had, despite loathing the man… asked if there was another way. And the voice had said, there is always another way, my son, and this is the one that saves the most of my children, and Xerxes had understood.

So he had gone to the tank and pulled the body of Hand Vankan out by the hair, and he had consumed every part of the man. His lord’s blessing soothed his soul and made all his discomfort go away.

"Favoured One." The eldest of the kneeling seven spoke, a woman with ritual scars down both arms, her aura pulled flat with fear. "The disposition."

Xerxes wiped the black from his cheeks with the back of one hand and looked at the map.

Vienna, rendered in salt and blood on the tabletop. The bastion sat in the west, a dull grey stone the acolytes had carved to scale, and east of it ran the long throat of the Ossuary Mile and the Sanguine Wall beyond, marked in fresh crimson. Precinct. Cathedral. And below the cathedral, unmarked because no map was permitted to show it, the garden, and the pond, and the thing that hung in the dark on iron chains, growing.

"Tell me what these new soldiers are," Xerxes said.

"Conscripts, Favoured One." The scarred woman recited it like scripture, because to her it was. "Scryed and counted for six days. One hundred to the surface, never more after they started losing the numbers against our shades and monsters… most likely due to inexperience. And so after that they had made the decision to send only Overmortals to the surface. And that’s not all, my lord… we are certain those overmortals are forcefully elevated. General auras, no attunement, no depth. Frightened bakers in matching coats." A flicker of contempt she could not quite hide. "The bastion has emptied its cellars. They put clerks in armour and drill them in the one working they can hold. It is not an army, and it will come apart the moment a real thing touches it."

Xerxes nodded slowly. It matched what his scouts had reported from the rooftops, what the scryers said, what his own eyes told him each time he watched the recordings.

And they are wrong, said the small cold part of him that the spark had not yet burned away. Something in that is wrong.

It is because we haven't seen a glimpse of Starkiller and his companion. He reasoned… painfully.

Across the last week, he had learned the difference between the doubt in his own skull and the word of his Lord, and he had learned to strangle the doubt the instant it spoke. That was what discipline was. It still felt, every single time, like drowning something small that had come to him for help.

"And the threats," he said. "Name them. Only the ones that matter."

"Three, Favoured One." The woman lifted three scarred fingers, one for each name she meant to give him. "The crimson-eyed doorman. Commander Mathew of the bastion. And the Star Killer." Her voice thinned on the last name.

"Three powerhouses?" Xerxes murmured.

"Yes, my lord," she agreed. "We haven’t seen a single underlord presence from the bastion apart from these.”

It was a good assessment.

"Position the Favoured against those three," Xerxes said. "The rest to the wall and the precinct. Feed the Warrens. Nothing crosses the causeway that we did not send." He pressed two fingers to his sternum, to the turning fire there, and bowed his head. "And pray. The Lord is close, brothers and sisters. Four days. I have heard Him say it. Four days, and the darkness He promised comes up out of the ground and walks."

They pressed their foreheads to the stone.

And Xerxes wept, and was radiant, and told them the truth exactly as it had been told to him, which was the most terrible thing about him. The thing none of them would understand until it was far too late. He was not lying. He believed every word. And he had simply never once been told the truth by the thing he believed.

Somewhere in the underground crypt of the Cathedral, in a room that smelled of rain and rot and hot copper, Hand Vella was humming, and she was so very, very happy.

She worked at the long table under a rack of blue essence-lamps, and around her the workshop settled and creaked—jars of preserved things easing on their shelves, coils of synthetic tendon swaying on their hooks, the half-built and the half-unmade laid out across a dozen slabs. She loved them all—which is to say completely, and with no interest whatsoever in what they might want.

And then there was the thing on the centre slab. Oh.

The thing on the centre slab was her masterwork, and it was finished, and finishing something always left her a little sad and a great deal proud, all at once, like a mother at a door.

She set down her essence-welder and stepped back to see it whole.

It had been three things once. She kept the seams, because the seams were the honest part. Down the left side, collarbone to hip, ran the body of a man who had been an Order V garrison captain until a week ago. Old but still had very good dense muscles, a heart that could carry a lord's load, the raw tonnage the piece needed. Threaded up through it, wired into the spine and the skull and the long bones, were the erased channels of a Child of Silence, resurrected out of the dark meat the Lord had gifted her. That was the aim of the thing: the killer's patience, the auraless stillness, the way it would sit in a doorway for three days and never once blink.

And the face. The right side of the face, and the right hand, and the memory sewn in behind them.

The face had belonged to a man named Hobbs.

She had not known him, and had not needed to. The bastion had known him… had loved him, from the fragments she'd combed out of the captured minds. Their old commander and teacher, the man who came before the one with the eye. She had gone hunting through her stolen memories for the shape of a wound in the enemy's command, and she had found this one, worn smooth by grief and never healed, and she had thought, with a small private ache of something she mistook for tenderness: oh, I can build a key for that.

She had taught it one word. It had taken days. A resurrected thing does not want to speak; it wants to be still. She was patient, though… patient and fond both, and she had all the time in the world until the Convergence, and eventually the seams had learned to move together and make a sound.

"Go on, my darling," Vella crooned, and reached out to stroke the cheek that had been Hobbs' cheek. "For me. Just the once. Show me you remember."

The thing on the slab opened its mismatched eyes.

Its left was the garrison captain's, dull and dead. The right had been Hobbs'. And the right eye moved— found her, focused, softened into something that was almost a person, almost a man waking from a nap in a chair by a fire—and the mouth that had been Hobbs' mouth curved into the small crooked smile.

"Matty."

Vella clapped both hands over her mouth and made a small sound of pure delight.

"Perfect." The word leaked out between her fingers. "Oh, that's—that is perfect. He'll break like a dropped cup. He won't even lift his hand, the poor thing." She leaned in close, nose to nose with the dead-and-living face, her crimson eyes wide and shining. "You're going to make a widower of that whole city, aren't you? Yes. Yes, you are. Such good work. Such good seams."

The thing said "Matty" again, because it was the only thing it knew, and it smiled, and behind the right eye, in a place with no mouth left to it, something that had been Hobbs screamed and screamed and could not reach its own face.

Vella did not hear that part. She never heard that part. She was already turning away, already humming, already reaching for the next slab, because the Convergence was four days out and a good craftswoman never let a deadline catch her idle.

Hierarch Malachar knelt at the edge of the pond and scattered feed across the black water where two fish circled beneath the surface.

"There," he murmured, as the white fish rose to take a crumb. "Thou art calmer today. Good. The noise above unsettles thee, I know. It will not be long now."

He tipped his head, as if listening to something two floors up, and shaped his throat, and when he spoke again the voice that came out of him belonged to someone else entirely. It was warm, and low, and patient, and it carried the enormous aching tenderness of a father who has watched a child suffer and cannot yet explain why the suffering was necessary. It was the voice he poured, drop by drop, into the ear of the weeping zealot in the undercroft, every time the zealot knelt and begged to be told what to do.

"Four days, my son," Malachar said, in the Darkened One's voice, to no one, practising. "You have done so well. Hold the wall. Feed the deep. Trust me a little longer."

He let the borrowed warmth drain out of his throat and clicked his tongue at his own performance, mildly, the way a violinist frowns at a sour note only he can hear.

Xerxes truly believed he served a god. That was the beauty of him… a man with a real and living faith, poured into a vessel with no god at the bottom of it. Vakan had heard nothing and known it, and so Vakan had schemed, and doubted, and hedged, and made himself useless.

Xerxes heard, and thought the hearing was the Darkened One, and so Xerxes would do anything. Walk into anything. Consume anyone. And never once suspect that the tender father-voice in his skull belonged to the thing feeding fish in the dark beneath his feet.

Ventriloquism, Malachar thought, was so badly underrated as an art. Everyone admired the puppet. No one ever thought to ask why the man behind it had bothered to learn to throw his voice in the first place.

He dropped another crumb and watched the black fish take it from underneath.

What he told the water now he would tell no one else in the world, because there was no one alive he trusted with it, and because saying it aloud—even to two blind fish—was the single indulgence he still allowed himself. He had loved nothing in a very long time. This was as close as he came.

"They think I am defending it," he said softly. "The wall. The ritual. My poor weeping Xerxes and his four days. They think the Hierarch has come to watch the great working done." He smiled, and it was not a kind thing, and the luminous fungus on the walls dimmed a shade away from it. "I have come to watch it break."

Above him, in the deep, the nascent seed of the Convergence pulsed on its chains, a wet and slow and terrible heartbeat, growing.

"A working of this size cannot simply stop," he told the fish. "It gathers. It aims. If it completes, the singularity feeds the Darkened One's incarnation, my brothers get their little apocalypse, and I am one more devout servant in the crowd, cheering." He trailed a pale finger through the water. "But if it fails—if some clever animal breaks the nexus at the crest, with all that death already gathered and nowhere left to go—then the singularity does not disperse. It redirects, and falls, screaming, into the nearest thing shaped to receive it."

"Into the seed," he said. "My seed. The one already sunk into the bones of this place, already fed, already waiting. They would hand me a god and never know they had done it, and thank me for my counsel with their last breath."

The white fish circled up. Malachar fed it, and was quiet for a while, and enjoyed the quiet, because it would be the last of it for four days.

His cult's assessment sat on the table in the undercroft. Three threats. The doorman, the Commander, the Star Killer. A fine assessment, drawn from good observation and clean logic, and blind in the one place that mattered, because it counted power, and power was the least interesting thing on that field.

The thing that interested Malachar could be neither scryed nor counted. It was the bend in the weave... the place where the futures he cast forward went soft and refused to resolve, where his seers brought him only noise, where the probabilities had been rotting for a fortnight in a widening stain.

And the stain had its origin not in an Overlord, nor an Underlord, nor a walking legend, but in one small, cracked, borrowed soul that the whole world insisted on filing.

Once, for three heartbeats, he had felt an outer being bloom inside the bastion. He had felt the fate-stain spread out from it, and underneath both, distant and unmistakable, the specific and blasphemous signature of something rooting into a god-working from the inside.

His cult saw a clever aide. Malachar saw the only genuinely unpredictable object within a hundred miles, and it had a name, and he had been turning that name over in the dark for some time now, the way the black fish turned a crumb, unhurried, from underneath.

He rose, at last. Behind him, a low crystal seat pushed itself up out of the cavern floor, and two figures knelt at the garden's edge who had not been there a moment before and had made no sound in arriving.

Children of Silence. Two of the four that remained to him.

They did not speak… they never did. They only waited to be aimed.

Malachar took a last crumb of feed between finger and thumb, and looked at it, and did not drop it yet.

"There is a boy in the bastion," he said, and his voice had gone flat and enormous and entirely his own now; the grandfather and the fury both folded away. "You have him filed as an aide to the Commander. You are wrong, and it is not your fault; you were told to count strength, and he has almost none, and that is precisely why he is the most dangerous living thing this side of the sea." He turned his head, and the two grey figures pressed their foreheads to the moss. "The others may die as they fall. The Star Killer is for me to take… the doorman, the Commander… take them if the chance is clean, spend nothing chasing them if it is not. None of them matter. When the crest comes, everything that is not mine dies anyway."

He held the crumb over the black water.

"But this one you bring me. Not his corpse. Not his spark. Him. Whole, and breathing, and able to answer a question… because I find I should very much like to ask it of him in person, before the end."

"Jin Winters," Malachar said, and dropped the crumb into the water, and watched the ripple spread out flat and perfect across the still black pond. "Do bring him to me whole when the war starts. I should like to ask him what it is like… being the only free animal in the pen… or at least believing in that."

The black fish took the crumb from underneath, the way it always did.

And the two grey shadows were already gone.
