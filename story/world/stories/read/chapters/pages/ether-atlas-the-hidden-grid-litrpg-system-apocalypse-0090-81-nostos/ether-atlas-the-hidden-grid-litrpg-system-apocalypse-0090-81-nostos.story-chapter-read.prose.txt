Chapter 81

"A ruin lies about itself. A column on a hill could be a temple, a tomb, or a folly some lord threw up in 1840 to feel old. You never name a site from the skyline." -  The Ether Atlas: Field Notes of Steven Parr

Sarah was on her feet before Donna had finished the sentence.

“I think we all could use some good news.” She had her pack half onto one shoulder already. “My bar for good news has gone down dramatically since the system paid us a visit. Now a good night’s sleep is good news.”

“Oh, you’ll like this one.” Donna stayed where she was, one shoulder against the curve of the passage, and let her wait a moment longer than was kind.

“I climbed out into a square,” she said. “It’s all old stone and broken columns up there. The whole place has gone to grass and rubble. But it’s quiet. No patrols, no lights, nothing moving but birds.” She paused for effect.

“Donna, seriously!” Sarah said.

Donna laughed and continued, “There is a hill above, on which is a rock. A big flat-topped rock with a ruin on top of it. Columns that show up on postcards.”

Sarah froze. “The Acropolis,” she said.

“That’s the one.”

Sarah let out a breath, and a wide smile cracked her face. A small tear trickled down from the corner of her eye. Arthur suspected it was relief. The chances of the portal taking Sarah back to Greece were too small to bother calculating. Donna took a step towards her and squeezed her shoulder, but before Sarah could say anything, Steve set his pencil down on the dais with a small click and cleared his throat.

“No,” he said. “Sorry. No. Before anyone cries.”

“Steve.” There was a warning in it.

“Donna, I love you, I do, but I am not betting four lives on your tourism.” He was already getting up, brushing the dust off his knees. “You saw a flat rock with a Doric ruin on it and a field full of broken columns. Do you have any idea how many places that describes? Agrigento. Segesta. Paestum. Half the bootheel of Italy. There is a temple in Sicily that has fooled better-read people than you.”

Donna looked at him. “It’s the Acropolis.”

He was at the mouth of the passage now. “Ten minutes. If I’m wrong, you can mock me for the rest of the trip.”

“I’m going to do that anyway.”

“Then I’ve nothing to lose.”

He went up the passage at something close to a jog, as Arthur’s eyes followed him, a wry smile on his lips. Donna raised her eyebrows.

“Greece is something of hallowed ground for a guy like Steve. Right now he is praying to all the classical gods that you are right,” Arthur said.

Sarah sat back down on the edge of the dais, slowly. She had an unsure look on her face. Excitement mixed with anxiety. Arthur lowered himself beside her. He was not built for saying the thing she needed to hear, so he put himself within reach of her hands and left it at that.

“If you are right,” she said, to no one in particular, “we’re an hour’s walk from my mother’s kitchen.”

“And if I’m wrong?”

“Then we’re in Sicily and I’m going to kill you,” she said with a small laugh. “You are not, though. I know that rock. I’ve known it my whole life.”

Steve was back inside ten minutes. They heard his feet on the stone first, quick and then slowing, and he came round the curve of the passage and stopped at the bottom of it without a word.

The silence went on a moment too long.

“Well?” Donna said.

“It’s the Acropolis.” He said it almost gently.

Sarah’s hand went to her mouth.

He came down off the last step, and his hands were already moving, the lecture reflex taking him over. “You never call a site off one building. That’s the beginner’s mistake. One broken temple looks much like the next broken temple. It’s how people talk themselves into swearing Segesta is the Parthenon and get laughed out of the room.”

“Before we make plans, are you a hundred percent sure?” Arthur said.

Steve started counting it off on his fingers.

“The temple is sitting precisely where it is supposed to sit. The gateway’s beside it, on the proper side.”

He held up his third finger.

“And off the flank there’s a small porch with the roof held up on the heads of six carved women. The caryatids.” He looked at Sarah. “You cannot fake those. Nobody on earth is faking those.”

“There’s a bald little hill below the rock where the old court used to meet. That’s Areopagus.” His hand came down. “It’s Athens, Sarah. We came up underneath Athens.”

“So basically you are saying the exact same thing that I said, but with seven hundred more words and ten minutes later,” Donna said.

“Yes, but I’m saying the exact same thing using evidence.”

“Git,” Donna murmured.

Sarah had been quiet for the duration, absorbing the fact that a fold in space had led her back home. Then she was on her feet.

“Right.” The brisk doctor came back into her voice. “Then we are not sleeping in a hole tonight.”

“Sarah,” Arthur started.

“My family’s in Piraeus.” She was not really listening to him. “Our house is at the port, the far side of the city. It’s about ten kilometres on foot. We could be there in a little over an hour.”

She shifted her pack on her shoulders.

“My mother. My brother, his wife, the two little ones. My grandmother.” The names came out of her one at a time, each one set down with care. “A proper house, with a yard, and a well my grandfather dug that has never once run dry in forty years.”

“Sarah, slow down.”

“There are beds, Arthur.”

She turned and looked round at all of them, and what was in her eyes was honest and simple.

“Do you understand what I’m telling you? Real beds. A mattress. A door that locks from the inside. My mother in the doorway telling me I’ve gone too thin and putting a plate in front of me whether I want it or not.”

She gave a small laugh, with half a sob caught in it.

“I can’t remember the last time I slept in a bed.”

“It’s a good idea,” Donna said.

Coming from her, that was close to a blessing, and Sarah took it as one.

“More than good. A house full of family that is part of the city. Walls. A door. A base off the street.” Donna pushed up off the wall. “But it is also why we don’t go strolling up to Sarah’s family. If we go there, we don’t want to drag suspicion along with us.”

Arthur was nodding.

The customs house was still fresh in his mind. The fifty men. The summons with no reason attached to it, and the cart ride down to Mdina not knowing what waited at the end of it.

“Donna is correct,” he said. “We find out who’s running things before anyone finds out we’re here. Nothing about portals. Nothing about nodes.” He glanced at Archie’s slow-turning facets. “And nothing about him. Not until we know the ground.”

“So we build the story before we walk out. The areas looked clear. I don’t think we will run into anybody around the Acropolis,” Donna said.

“We create covers, and we stick to them. No deviating. No improvising.”

She sat down on the edge of the dais, and the rest of them drifted into a loose ring around her, and the triangle turned slowly over their heads.

“First rule.” She held up one finger. “We lie as little as we possibly can.”

“That sounds backwards,” Steve said.

“It isn’t. Every made-up detail is one more thing four people have to remember.” She shook her head. “The best cover is mostly true with the dangerous parts cut out. So we start from what’s real, and we trim it down.”

“Real names, then,” Arthur said.

“Yes, real names,” she agreed. “No databases anymore to run our names through. And thankfully none of us is called something ridiculous like Tarquin Blackadder.”

“I wouldn’t mind Blackadder, truth be told. Arthur Blackadder, sounds badass.”

She nodded at Sarah.

“She will probably get recognised in Piraeus. The first time somebody calls out Sarah and she doesn’t turn round, we’re finished on the spot. We’re exactly who we are.” A small shrug. “We just aren’t going to say what we are.”

Steve frowned at that.

“I’d argue the opposite, for me.” He turned the pencil over in his fingers. “If there’s anyone up there who knows the field, an archaeologist turning up the same week the world ended is exactly the coincidence that earns you a second conversation. The kind you don’t walk away from. I could be a teacher. Something grey.”

The triangle made one slow turn.

“You would be caught inside the day,” Archie said.

Steve looked up at him. “Thank you.”

“It is not an insult. It is a forecast.”

“You examined four tool markings on the walls when Donna was scouting. You have redrawn and cross-referenced the portal Seeder symbols against the ones you had copied in Marseille and Malta.”

Archie sounded, for lack of a better term, more human since his fourth-tier unlock. He continued, “A man who can read Greek letters and then claims he teaches geography is more memorable, not less.”

Steve opened his mouth.

“Conceal the profession, and every reflex you own becomes evidence against you,” Archie went on, over him. “Keep it, and the same reflexes become character.”

Donna pointed at the triangle. “He’s right. Lean in. You’re an archaeologist. Embrace your boring self.”

“I am not boring.”

“Your cover story practically writes itself.” Donna grinned. “You’re between digs. More interested in your lunch than in glory. And nobody on earth is frightened of a tired academic.”

“I resent how fast you put that together.”

“What do we tell them we’re here for,” Sarah said, crouching back into the ring. “That’s the real question. Four of us, three not Greek, in Athens when the lights went out. Why?”

“Because you brought us,” Donna said. “And that part is nearly true.”

She ticked it off slowly, so they would remember.

“You’re the one with family here. You ran yourself into the ground with MSF, so you took leave at the start of the summer and talked three sad British friends into a holiday round the Mediterranean.”

“That tracks,” Steve admitted.

“Marseille first. Down to Malta. And then over to Greece to see your mother.” Donna looked round at them. “We flew into Athens. We were out at the coast the day it happened. We’ve been holed up with Sarah’s family in Piraeus ever since, sitting tight like everyone else, and we’re only now coming up for air.”

“Every place on the list is real and in the right order,” Steve said, half admiring it despite himself. “The dates won’t track against a flight. Thankfully for us, there are no flight records anymore.”

“That’s the whole reason to pick it.”

“People won’t believe we were holed up once they get a look at our equipment,” Arthur said.

Donna stayed silent for a bit, nodding her head.

“You are right. We need to layer the equipment in. In any case, I don’t think we will pass as hapless tourists with what we have seen and done. Near-death situations change a person’s bearings.”

“Okay, so everything stays the same, except that holed-up part. We were not with Sarah’s family. We were out somewhere when the system hit. Sarah, any close-by tourist towns?”

“Nafplio,” Sarah said without hesitation. “It’s a charming seaside town. Quite romantic, and it’s connected to Athens by land.”

“Good. So we went to Nafplio and got stuck there. We have been fighting and surviving like everybody else. And we have finally managed to get back to Athens.” Donna nodded to herself. “That should explain the equipment.”

“There are Mycenae, Epidaurus and Olympia pretty close by as well,” Steve said. “I think they make better tourist destinations.”

“Not for our cover story. Nafplio is perfect, now that I think about it.”

“Why is that?” Sarah asked. “Mycenae is quite popular, actually.”

“I think a charming seaside town fits a couple better,” Donna said.

“Say what now,” Arthur said, looking up from his pack.

“We need a stronger reason why Sarah brought us over to her family. Four friends on a holiday is fine, right up until someone watches us for a minute. Especially the two of you.”

Sarah had found something fascinating to look at on the floor, and she shifted away from Arthur on the dais.

“You’re together. A couple. Have been a while.” Donna kept her voice flat and reasonable, which only made it worse. “Sarah’s brought her partner home to meet the family. The most ordinary reason in the world for a man to cross a continent.”

“We could just be friends,” Arthur said weakly. “I think you are reading too much into gestures.”

“You could. But it won’t be believable,” Steve said, and started rummaging through his pack.

“Fine.” Sarah cleared her throat and went looking for the doctor in her. “Together. Yes. Fine. It’s operationally sound.”

“It’s operationally sound,” Donna agreed, dead flat, and looked back down at her hands before her face could betray her.

Arthur let himself look at the side of Sarah’s face a moment longer than he needed to. Then he looked away, and filed the whole thing somewhere to deal with later. Possibly never.

Donna looked back up and took charge of the conversation once more.

“I’m myself. A security consultant Sarah met during her MSF tour of Nigeria. We have kept in touch ever since. I let her rope me into her holiday scheme.”

Donna looked at Steve. “Actually, you are the wrinkle here. Why would an MSF doctor be friends with a relic worshipper?”

“Sarah, have you worked in Egypt?” Steve asked, letting Donna’s jibe slide.

“Very briefly, at Aswan. I was mostly in Sudan, Ethiopia and Côte d’Ivoire,” Sarah answered.

“Ha!” Steve barked a laugh and looked at Donna. “Luxor.”

“Early in 2021, they found a complete ancient industrial city buried under the sand near Luxor. It is over 3,000 years old,” he said.

“I was in Luxor, consulting on the dig. Aswan is close enough. I met Sarah when she came to Luxor to visit the city,” Steve said carefully, putting some cheese and a few olives on a piece of bread.

“Well, that isn’t half bad. Fine, then. We have our covers in place.”

Donna looked over at Sarah and said to the group, “We shouldn’t involve her family too much. But we will have to tell them some of the truth.”

Sarah nodded. “They will need to play along if someone comes asking about us. Especially the neighbours. When did we get to Athens, when did we leave for Nafplio. Those sorts of questions.”

“We will give them an overview behind closed doors. We just keep the dangerous truths to ourselves,” Donna agreed.

“Last thing, and then we move.” Donna looked at Archie.

“Archie. The trick with the voice in our heads. You can talk to us and nobody else hears a thing.”

“I can converse telepathically with one of you, or all of you, seen or unseen,” Archie said. “I have explained this.”

“You have. But here is the problematic part.” She leaned in over her knees. “We can’t do it back. You drop a warning into my skull in the middle of a packed square, and I can’t think of a reply back to you. I have to say it. Out loud. To thin air.”

She spread her hands.

“And worse than that, unless I use your name, you’d never know if I’m talking to you or to the man selling fish.”

“People will either think we are crazy, or worse, that we have some unusual powers.” Arthur nodded slowly. “Any chance to pass by without notice goes if people suspect we have more up our sleeve than we are letting on.”

“So we need a flag,” Donna said. “One of us says a word, and the next thing out of our mouth is for you, not for the room.”

“A sound protocol. Name the word.”

“Man, if we had some fresh tomatoes, this would make a nice bruschetta,” Steve interrupted, taking a bite.

Donna took just long enough to enjoy what she was about to say.

“Tomato.”

“It’s perfect.” She was completely serious, which made it funnier. “Nobody ever clocks it. You can drop it into any sentence on earth.”

“Do you have any tomatoes? Mind the tomatoes there. That man’s got a face like a tomato. It never sounds like code, because it’s a damn dull word.”

Steve motioned with a finger while he was chewing.

“No, Steve.” She held a hand up against the objection. “We are never going to need to say the word tomato to a real greengrocer about a real tomato in the exact same second one of us also needs to talk to Archie.”

Steve dropped his finger.

“It is,” Archie said, after a pause that carried an immense quantity of reluctance for a shape with no face to carry it on, “operationally sound.”

“That’s three times today somebody’s hidden behind that phrase,” Sarah said.

“I’ve been operational longer than you can comprehend,” Archie said, “and I have been assigned a vegetable.”

“It’ll grow on you.”

All of them laughed.

“With covers. Say it like you believe it, and most people won’t question it.” Donna nodded at Sarah. “You do the talking wherever you can. It is your city, your language and your knowledge. The rest of us stay behind you and keep it dull.”

The party checked and fastened their packs one last time, then stood up facing the exit.

Sarah went first through the passage, and the three followed her.

They came out into a wide broken square held in a bowl of low hills, the Aegean sun bathing it in streaks of gold. Arthur’s eyes lingered on a small near-perfect temple off to one side that had somehow kept its roof through all of it.

Nothing moved in the square but the birds, as Donna had promised, swifts cutting the gold above the ruins and screaming as they hunted the last of the light. They looked slightly mutated but not dangerous.

“Welcome to Greece,” Sarah said, beaming. “Let’s go and surprise my mother.”

FIELD NOTES OF STEVEN PARR, IN COLLABORATION WITH ARCHIE

(I am not a botanist. The subject is harmless enough that Archie has agreed to supply the parts I would otherwise invent.)

Athens.

THE LEAST DRAMATIC WORD IN THE LANGUAGE

Donna chose our distress word for being the dullest in the language. So, in fairness to the tomato, here is everything it has been accused of.

It is not European, to begin with. It came up out of the lower Andes, was already being grown in Mexico by the time anyone nearby was writing things down, and reached the Old World in the hold of a Spanish ship in the 1500s, under the Aztec name tomatl. After that, nobody could agree on what they were holding.

The Italians, working from the early yellow ones, called it pomo d’oro, the golden apple. The French called it the pomme d’amour, the love apple, and served it to stir the blood, on the theory that it was kin to the mandrake. The botanists, squinting at the same plant, settled on the wolf peach, after a German notion that things in the nightshade family were used to summon werewolves.

One fruit became food, aphrodisiac, and werewolf bait, depending entirely on which border you were standing behind.

Then it got worse. Because it is a nightshade, Europe decided it was poison. The rich ate off pewter, pewter is heavy with lead, the tomato is acidic enough to draw that lead out, and so the aristocracy quietly poisoned themselves at supper and blamed the reddest thing on the plate. The poor, eating off wood, ate tomatoes happily for two centuries and wondered what the fuss was about. That’s my theory on the poisonous bit.

The matter was supposedly settled in 1820 by one Robert Gibbon Johnson, who ate a whole basket on the courthouse steps in Salem, New Jersey, before a crowd gathered to watch him die. He did not die. The tale is almost certainly invented, which is exactly why I am keeping it.

England, of course, grew it as a garden ornament and declined to eat it on the grounds that it stank.

It ends in court. In 1893 the Supreme Court of the United States ruled that the tomato, a fruit by every botanical measure, is in law a vegetable, so that the importers could be taxed.

✎ Donna picked it because it the word was not dramatic. The least dramatic word we own has been a love charm, a werewolf lure, a murder suspect, and a defendant before the Supreme Court. We choose better than we know.
