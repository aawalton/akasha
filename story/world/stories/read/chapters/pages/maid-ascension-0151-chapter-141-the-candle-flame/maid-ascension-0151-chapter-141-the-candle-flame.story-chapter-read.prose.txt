Lira stood still between the dark shelves, holding the candle in her hand with her face perfectly blank. She let the silence stretch a little longer, since the person who spoke first usually gave away more than the one who waited. But the mage just kept smiling at her, refusing to say anything further.

“I am sorry, my lord,” she said at last. “What do you mean by Archmage?”

“I mean someone who can craft a completely original spell without the need for a primer,” the court mage said casually, as if he were commenting on the weather. His smile did not move an inch as he waited for her reaction.

She gave him none. After Aric’s little trap, she was not going to be fooled again, especially since Aric had been subtle, while this was anything but...

“I think you have mistaken me for someone else, my lord. I am only the library caretaker.”

“Of course you are.” He smiled at her. “Relax and take a seat. You have nothing to fear from me.” He waved a hand toward her chair.

“You haven’t been caught or anything. In fact, no one is even really here.” His edges blurred as he spoke, the red and gold of his coat running together until there was no man left, only a tall column of living flame standing where he had been. It licked at the air without scorching the floor or the nearest shelf, and the voice that came out of it crackled but was otherwise unchanged.

“Edward Emberborn has never been here, you understand.” The flame winked at her. “At this very moment, the court mage is having a late dinner with the royal family, discussing the death of Kavard and what it entails. While you are talking to a candle flame.”

Lira blinked. She sat down in her chair slowly, since refusing would have looked worse than obeying. As she did, she wondered what exactly she was looking at. Her danger sense had stayed quiet the whole time, which meant the thing was harmless, or at least not actively intending to harm her. She couldn’t help but stare at it. It was some form of simulacrum magic, she guessed, a body made of flame that was tied to the real man somewhere else. He could probably see and speak through it in the same way her horn and funnel spells let her listen through a sheet of paper. It was her linked spell, only ten times more complex.

She suddenly felt very small and very inexperienced.

She wanted to cast Mage Sight to see how it worked. She wanted to ask about the magical word he had used to craft it, what tier it was, and whether he drew runes with the flames. But she didn’t do or say anything because she was supposed to be just a scared maid.

“I know that you will deny anything I say until the day you die,” the flame went on, “and that you will never be honest with me, so you can keep quiet and listen to the fire crackle.”

Lira folded her hands in her lap, trying to look meek, which wasn’t hard since she was genuinely terrified.

“I became aware that a maid could cast magic the day she created her wind spell for the first time. It used so much mana that even I took notice of it, all the way from the other side of the palace. So I took a peek with a little magic, and I found her collapsed in the middle of that drafty library with every window open, in the heart of winter.”

That early? If this was true, and Lira doubted the court mage had any reason to lie, it meant she had been caught from the start.

“I sent the footman Marco with a few old books to dump in the old library, and he found the maid and carried her out before she froze to death.”

Lira kept her face still, though her mind went back to that day on its own. She remembered the fever and the headache, Celene scolding her, and Marco telling her he had only come by to drop off some old books and had been lucky to find her. She had counted herself lucky and moved on.

That was stupid of her. She should have investigated who had sent Marco and, upon learning it was the court mage, fled immediately.

For all her paranoia, how could she have missed this?

“Once the library was empty,” the living flame continued, “I went in myself. I suspected that some primers had been sent there by accident, which was to be expected after years of dumping old books in the library. My theory was that the maid had found one and imprinted it by mistake. No big deal. A little warning about the dangers of magic, and then I would send her to work directly under Aric. A good lad, that boy, and the only royal who might make some good use of a magical maid.”

That didn’t sound so bad. Here she had thought she would be executed over her first imprint. Then again, a single cantrip didn’t make a mage.

“But imagine my surprise when I found the little alcove,” said Emberborn, “only to find a fire primer, a water primer, and a book on martial arts open to a chapter on meditation.”

He paused for a bit as the flame crackled. “And there, in the middle of it all, was an attempt at making a wind primer. A ridiculous attempt, I might add, since true primers are magical works built on the concept of Transference, not just a mix of natural philosophy and poetry.”

Lira forced herself not to blush. Back then, she had known nothing about magic and had been treading blindly. Still, a thought flooded her mind. He had not mentioned her father’s lexicon. Had he somehow missed it?

She tried to remember if it had been there that day.

She was pretty sure it had been, under her grand-uncle’s calligraphy book. She had used it to get the word for wind, Riad, and then buried the table under wind references as she created her pseudo-primer. She didn’t say anything. Better not to volunteer any information.

“But the attempt wasn’t so ridiculous, was it?” he said, sounding almost proud. “After all, the maid collapsed casting a wind spell, not a fire spell or a water spell. She had, in fact, taken the first step toward becoming an Archmage, something real mages dedicate their lives to achieving.”

“Assuming all that was true, why didn’t you reveal yourself to that maid?” Lira asked. “Offer her an apprenticeship or something.”

“It was definitely tempting,” he said with a nod, “but I couldn’t.”

“Why not?”

“For me to explain that, we need to talk about Mage Guild politics. What do you know about the Guild?” asked the flame.

“It’s a world-spanning organization that controls how magic is used,” said Lira.

“World-spanning,” he scoffed. “There is nothing world-spanning about the Mage Guild. Every local branch is its own little organization. The only thing they share is their charter, the one with the objective of restoring magic to what it used to be, back in the lost age of magic.”

“So what? You figured that, left alone, the maid might somehow be the one to do it, to bring magic back?”

Emberborn shrugged. Or rather, the flame did. She forced herself to keep that image in her mind for plausible deniability.

“To risk sounding like a zealot, I am part of a sect called the Guardians of the Unbound, who believe that change will come from outside the guild’s threaded path. After all, the Restorationists have spent a thousand years trying and failing to fix the world.”

“Guardians of the Unbound? Restorationists?” asked Lira.

“Two competing sects within the Mage Guild. We are the minority, and with magic restricted in most countries, we were beginning to lose hope. So imagine my surprise when not one but two Unbound Archmages appeared within the span of five years. Here in the kingdom of Halthar, of all places.”

“Two Archmages?” Was he talking about Harwin and his coin magic? Or maybe it was Kelroth?

The flame facsimile smiled. “Yes. Four years ago, glowing magical coins flooded the market, and investigating them revealed an anti-theft enchantment. A new spell, one the Guild had never seen before. Someone outside the Guild structure had somehow managed to create a completely original spell.”

Lira stayed quiet, and he went on.

“And now the maid in the library.” He kept referring to her in the third person for some reason, even though, at this point, he had shown that he knew so much that Lira doubted she could deny it any further. “She has shown a great deal of growth, too. From what I gathered, she has recreated at least twenty spells, some mental spells, something good enough to fool a Truth Stone, I think, at least one divination spell, and then a few runic spells, which I suspect she picked up after watching the heating plate get created; Ignition, warmth, and dust retrieval. I think that’s when the maid found her niche and started to specialize in creating runic spells.”

Lira almost reacted to that. He was way off, since she had imprinted at least sixty spells. Was he not keeping a close eye on her?

“More importantly, after Samy and Nabi delivered the books for beginner mages to the library, the maid was able to create a mana obfuscation spell, probably a runic version too, that stopped me from sensing her casting in the library completely. I was honestly baffled by that. She was able to do it using books that were meant for children.”

He had also only mentioned the Mana Obfuscation spell, not the Mana Stabilisation spell. She always stacked them on top of each other, so it was possible he had missed the second one.

“If you have been watching said maid for this long without interfering, why now?” Lira asked instead. “Why reveal yourself?”

She needed to push the conversation forward; otherwise, she might accidentally reveal something she shouldn’t. She doubted he knew about Thadwick, Salvek, or Hesta, so she guessed from the timing that the only thing he knew about was Kavard.

Emberborn smiled. “Because the maid had finally created something completely original as well, not a recreation of a common wind manipulation spell or a mana obfuscation enchantment, but something completely new.”

“What?”

“You know, the court mage’s role in this palace is to teach the palace children, activate heating and water purification runes, and be the last line of defence if the castle is threatened. You see, I am not a diviner. I am a fire mage. I can’t tell what spell the maid had created, only the weave of disturbance she used to draw before she learned Mana Obfuscation and blocked my sight.”

“The only divination spells I know are fire-related,” he continued. “It wasn’t up to me to investigate Kavard’s death, but I did it anyway out of curiosity, using a facsimile like this one. Imagine my surprise when my fire divination revealed that a fire spell had been cast in that bathroom.”

He paused dramatically. “One I didn’t know, and I have imprinted more than a hundred fire spells.”

“Why do you suspect that a maid killed the crown prince?” Lira tried not to panic.

“Oh, don’t be coy,” the flame said with a smirk. “Remember, no one is here, and no one is accusing anyone.”

Lira wasn’t sure what he was getting at, what the play was here, or what he wanted from her.

“The death of a prince is a serious matter,” she said.

“My sect recommends that we do not interfere against an Unbound mage,” he said. “I would still have intervened if the maid had targeted Lyth or Aric, or even King Argon. But it is just Kavard. Who cares about him anyway? The man would have destroyed the kingdom in a decade. I also heard that he threatened the maid, so it was probably self-defense. No, what I care about is the spell that was used. It was runic in nature, using ink and paper as a medium, which was bizarre for a fire enchantment, though I think it also had some wind connotations.”

Seriously, was he insane? Did he not care that Lira had killed the prince?

“This is all the information I could gather on the spell,” he continued. “I couldn’t tell what it does. So, do you think the maid would want to trade me the spell?”

Lira hesitated. She was technically at his mercy. He knew she had killed the prince, and he could accuse her, and it would be his word against hers. He also had enough proof to bury her, the existence of primers in the library being one example. So she should probably comply and plan her exit strategy.

“Trade you it in exchange for what?” Lira could have asked for her life in exchange, but she decided against it. It was better to see if he would threaten her for the information he wanted first.

“How about a hint on what the other Unbound Archmage is doing?”

Lira blinked at that.

She wasn’t expecting this… She considered the offer for a long moment.

“The maid calls it the Canary spell,” she finally said, admitting for the first time that she could do magic, even if it was in a third person, something she hadn’t told Pallan, Maren, or Celene, and something she had decided against revealing to Aric. “The idea came to her from a book that explained how miners take small birds with them into coal mines. It turns good air into bad air by burning it. The spell doesn’t produce an open flame; it just alters the air, resulting in a slow, unnoticeable suffocation. It uses Kisar. Riad. Fila. Terassana. Kurur. making it a runic spell, as you guessed.”

“Absolutely brilliant,” he said, sounding like a proud teacher. “It is such a simple concept that I believe I should be able to recreate it.”

He extended his hand over her journal, the same journal she used to keep the hint to restore her memory.

“Here is the hint.” He burned a rune into the page, one that looked like a spider web. “This is Kalma. It means karma. Are you familiar with the concept of karma?”

Lira nodded. She had read enough philosophy and books about the soul to have encountered the concept a few times.

“I wonder if a spell to see karma could be created from this?”

“Aren’t your expectations a bit too high?”

“This is why I said a hint,” the flame said with a smile. “And remember, I was never here. You have just been talking to a candle flame.”

He disappeared in a burst of sparks, leaving Lira confused, baffled, terrified, and really in the mood to pack up her stuff and run.
