The third-floor executive chamber of the Black Ink Consortium’s Jining headquarters was quiet, save for the faint hiss of burning incense. In the center of the room was an ostentatious rosewood table, elegantly carved and inlaid with gold. The shaggy rug beneath it looked imported and expensive, as did the opulently framed paintings hanging from the walls.

Around the edges of the oversized, oval table sat a panel of six managing directors. The topic of the day was the recent news from their ‘investments’ in Laiwu.

“The report has been verified,” a dry voice from the far side of the table began. “The Deer Valley mine has been lost. And the damages don’t stop there. Our analysts estimate Iron Creek will also be back in Tai-shan hands, likely within the next two weeks.”

“How did this happen?” a voice from several seats down asked, its owner drumming his fingers on the table. “What changed?”

“It seems a fledgling dragon appeared: the Tai-shan Young Master. He is apparently not just a formidable warrior, but also a peerless strategist. He outmaneuvered Wi Mo, executed him on the field, and paraded his head around on a pike.”

A collective rustle of silk echoed through the room as several of the directors shifted in their seats.

“We committed thirty thousand silver taels to arming that excursion,” a director near the window noted. “Is that entire investment now lost?”

“Likely. As is what we paid to lease the siege ballista for them, and more,” another voice chimed in from the shadows. “We will have to compensate our contact at the Armory for its full value now. Unfortunately, no one – including us – offers insurance coverage on Imperial war machines.”

An uncomfortable silence settled over the board.

“Five years,” the first voice muttered. “Our goal was clear: absorption and liquidation of one of the Ten Great Sects. Pressure the Tai-shan’s finances until their infrastructure collapses – and then scoop up the wreckage at rock bottom prices. But if they secure those timberlands and ore veins, along with the tribute they are still receiving from Jinan and Tai’an, they will be able to weather the storm.”

“Then we play our trump card,” a voice near the edge proposed. “It may feel uncomfortable working with an unorthodox sect, but there is no stronger mercenary force anywhere near Shandong.”

The room grew noticeably colder. A teacup settled onto its saucer with a sharp clack.

“Have you lost your mind?” an elderly director asked, adjusting his spectacles. “Do you have any idea how much they cost? Even for top-level martial artists, their fees are exorbitant. And their reputation is atrocious.”

“We lack the capital,” a woman’s voice interjected bluntly. “Our reserves may feel like they are sufficient. But supporting the Meng-shan in this war has been costly, and purchasing those highway toll rights put a strain on our liquidity after that disastrous bidding war.”

“And our troubles do not end there,” another voice chimed in, tossing a sealed red document onto the center of the table. “Censor Guan issued a formal order yesterday morning. A substantial portion of our non-liquid assets in the capital have been frozen, pending an audit over archival discrepancies. We are facing immense back taxes and potential fines.”

“It’s not like the war is lost. Our position in Shandong is still quite strong,” the voice near the window interjected softly. “And our leverage against the Tai-shan’s primary ally in the region remains firm; their leadership understands what will happen if they cross us. When we ask, they will fall into line. Which means we can still claim the main prize.”

“We cannot afford them,” the woman insisted. “It’s too risky. Just their baseline rate would drain our remaining reserves within a few months.”

“Not if we structure the contract correctly. What say we offer a modest base retainer, but tie most of their compensation to incentives. Successful capture of military targets results in a bonus. The costs of employing them will then be directly offset by revenue from the assets they seize.”

The group of directors fell silent, mentally running the numbers and considering that plan.

“If they fail to secure the province,” the elderly director warned, “the financial fallout will be severe.”

“If we withdraw now, the capital we already invested is lost completely,” the voice near the window replied. “We cannot afford to retreat.”

One by one, the managing directors around the rosewood table nodded. A murmur of quiet approval rippled around the table.

“That incentive structure for the mercenaries is viable,” the woman admitted after a long pause. “It limits our downside risk. And provides an opportunity to rebuild our reserves in-between campaigns, as needed.”

“We have reached a critical point,” the first director concluded, writing out directions for the managers beneath them on a nearby scroll. Once finished, he picked up an official stone seal and pressed it into a tin of red cinnabar paste. He stamped the bottom of the scroll with a flourish.

“Issue the contract,” he declared.

“Send in the Fellfang Brigade.”
