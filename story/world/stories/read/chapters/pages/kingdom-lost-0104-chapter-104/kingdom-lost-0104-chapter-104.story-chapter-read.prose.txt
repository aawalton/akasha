Chapter 104

It broke with the sound of a leather cord cutting through air, a clean fast hiss above and behind him, and before he had located it the cord was around his throat and biting. He could feel the sharp pain of the constriction of the whip tightening around his neck. The rage in his chest turned to fear as the helplessness of his situation became clear.

The knife fell out of his hand.

He heard it hit the dirt. He did not see it. His hands came up to his throat without his telling them to, and his fingers found the leather and got under it for a moment and then slipped, because the wrap was pulling tighter through them, and the air at the top of his chest was suddenly thinner than it had been a heartbeat before. He turned his head.

He could not turn his body. The line of the cord ran out behind him in a straight pull and would not let him face the thing at the other end, but he got his head around enough to see, and behind him stood a woman.

This was no Hoshin.

She had short dark hair and a southern look to her face and she held the handle of the whip in a steady two-handed grip, and her eyes on him were not angry eyes, they were the eyes of a person doing a piece of work. Past her shoulder, at the edge of the square, his two Clawborn had been pulled off their horses. They were on their knees in the dirt with their hands behind them, and around each of them stood five soldiers with drawn swords, and behind the soldiers stood a tall man with a long blade resting easy at his shoulder and dull patient eyes, and the swords at the necks of his Clawborn did not waver.

He had not heard them come out of the huts.

He tried to speak. The air was not there. He pulled against the line and the line pulled back and he went down to one knee, then to both, and then the woman with the whip stepped back and put her weight into the line and he went all the way down onto his back in the dirt of the square with his head twisted toward her.

He looked up at her.

"Now," she said, "you are the slave."

He understood the words. He understood her face. The hot stone behind his eyes did not understand any of it, but the rest of him did, and he saw the morning for what it had always been going to be from the moment he had ridden through the gate at the working pace and seen the empty mat. He had not known then. He knew now.

He tried again to speak.

He had air for nothing.

The tall man with the easy blade moved past the woman without looking at Korr-Va, and Korr-Va's horse came with him, the reins gathered short in the man's free hand. The horse did not fight. The man brought it to where the woman stood and she did something at the saddle without taking her eyes off Korr-Va, and Korr-Va felt the line at his throat go from her hand to the horse, and the weight of it changed.

Tama was walking toward them.

She did not hurry. She crossed the square with her hands open at her sides and her face very calm, and she stopped beside the horse, and the woman with the whip turned the horse so that its head pointed toward the back fence of the village and gave Tama a single small nod.

Tama looked down at Korr-Va.

"NOW YOU LEARN!"

The flat of her hand struck the horse's haunch with a sound that cracked through the square, and the animal jumped forward into a startled gallop, and the line at Korr-Va's throat went tight, and the ground came up under his shoulders and his back and the back of his head, and the village dragged past him in a blur of dirt and feet and the sky tilted overhead.

He could not breathe.

He could not see clearly.

The horse ran the length of the square to the back fence and turned at the fence the way a horse turns when it has nowhere else to go, and it came back the way it had come at the same hard frightened gallop, and Korr-Va was dragged the second length on his back with the leather burning at his throat and the dirt grinding into him through his armor and the breath he did not have somehow getting smaller.

At the gate the tall man caught the reins.

The horse stopped.

Korr-Va lay in the dirt.

He could not breathe and he could not move and the world above him had gone to a narrow tunnel with a grey ring at its edge, and through the tunnel he saw Tama walk toward him with the long knife in her hand. His knife. She held it the way a woman holds a thing she has not held before, awkward at the grip but firm enough, and she stopped over him and looked down into his face.

"You no learn fast enough."

She knelt.

She set the point of the blade against the place where his armor ended below the line of the wrap at his throat, the soft place at the hollow of his collar where the leather and the plate did not meet, and she pushed.

The blade went in.

He felt it go in. He felt the heat of it and the cold of it and the way it traveled through him, and his body tried to gasp and could not gasp, and a sound came out of him that was not a word and not quite a breath, and Tama leaned on the handle until the blade was as deep as it would go, and then she let go of it.

She stood up.

She stepped back.

Korr-Va lay in the dirt of the Hoshin square with the leather at his throat and the knife in his collar and the grey ring of the tunnel closing around the sky above him, and the last clear thing he saw before the ring closed all the way was Tama's face. She was looking at her hands. Her hands were shaking. And then her shoulders began to shake too, and she stumbled back a step, and she looked at her hands again and a small breath of a smile moved across her mouth for the count of one beat before the tears came, and the tears ran down her cheeks in two clean lines, and the old healer was already moving toward her across the square with her thin arms open.

The ring closed.

The sky went.

Riley stood at the edge of the open ground and watched.

The old healer reached Tama and put her arms around her, and the other Hoshin came in around them in a quiet ring, hands going to Tama's back and to her shoulders, and Tama let herself be held. She did not collapse. She stood inside the ring and shook, and the people of her village held her up the way they had held each other up for as long as they had been a village.

Thorne walked up behind Riley's left shoulder.

For a moment neither of them spoke.

"What about the other two," Thorne said.

"Take them," Riley said. "They will die, but we will get information out of them first."

Thorne nodded once and went to do it. The soldiers at the gate moved without needing the order spoken twice. The two Clawborn were stripped of their weapons where they knelt, and their hands were bound behind them at the wrists with the short leather cord Riley's soldiers had ready in their belts, and they were brought to their feet and walked toward the gate.

"Prepare to leave," Riley said. "Mount the Clawborn tied to their horses. Recall all our soldiers who are gathering to help the Hoshin pack to leave."

The soldiers moved.

“Thorne,” Riley called to him to return. “I have a special assignment for you. Find a small basket and…” Riley leaned in and whispered into Thorne's ear, once she was done he looked at her for a moment then with a single nod, he took his leave.

Riley walked across the square to where Tama was standing. The old healer had stepped back. Tama had wiped her face with the back of one hand and the marks of the tears were drying on her cheeks, and she had folded her hands again in front of her at her waist, and she was breathing in the slow measured way of a person who has just put a weight down and is waiting to find out what their body feels like without it.

She looked at Riley.

"Is it over?"

Riley shook her head once.

"No," she said. "Now it begins."
