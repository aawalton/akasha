[Time]: Autumn Semester, Day 9 — 7:30 PM[Location]: White City South · Century Window Sky-Island Archipelago · The Cloud River

The Century Window Archipelago did not float. It flowed.

A river of condensed cloud wound through the airspace south of the White City, threading a few dozen shattered islands onto one luminous ribbon, and the whole arrangement drifted at the pace of something that had never in its life been late for anything. Yenna had picked the venue, and made the reservation.

They ate on a boat.

The skiff ran seven meters by three, single triangular sail, and the deck was a masterclass in premium spatial economy: an open grill glowing with banked alchemical coals at the stern, a long driftwood table at the bow, and sofa seating deep enough to swallow a witch to the ribs, the entire arrangement sheltered under a rune-stitched canvas parasol the size of a small roof. A waiter in a crisp vest tapped a brass node at the bow and stepped back. A [Mage Hand] bloomed in the air, hauled the star-iron anchor out of the cloud-tide, and took the rigging in hand. The sail cracked taut. The skiff slid off the pier and out onto the river, and below them the city lights came up through the mist like a drowned galaxy.

Hathaway ordered a pound of sea dragon, two pounds of White City lobster, a pound of dragon-turtle, two pounds of Holheim cherry-blossom clams, a pound of Ghost Sea scallops, and a roasted-charcoal green tea.

Then she sat back against the cushions and began fighting a war she was losing on every front.

Ninety-nine.

Her internal telemetry had been throwing confetti for six consecutive hours and showed no sign of running out of confetti. Heidi Lucent handed out academic validation at roughly the frequency of a total solar eclipse, and Hathaway had walked out of that hall with a ninety-nine. Yes, she had dropped a point. Who cared about the point. She was second in the entire cohort, and first place belonged to her girlfriend, and losing to your own girlfriend was not a defeat, it was a household asset.

She wanted to stand on the table. She wanted to propose a toast to open-source development.

Across the driftwood, Surtrina and Spectra were generating a localized weather event.

Surtrina was slumped so far into the cushions she had achieved a reclining position that no furniture designer had intended, arms crossed, tail-flame guttering. Beside her Spectra sat upright and radiated the ambient temperature of a well-kept morgue. The combined pressure differential over that half of the table was pulling smoke off the grill sideways.

Hathaway picked up her tea and drank it with the ceramic rim positioned to block the lower half of her face. She bit the inside of her cheek. She turned and watched the [Mage Hand] work the rigging with the deep, unblinking fascination of a woman auditing a load-bearing structure.

The rigging ran out of structural interest at about the same rate her composure did, so she turned to her left.

"Yenna. How did you actually get us this table?"

Yenna looked up from her plate, and both golden ears came forward, level and attentive and entirely under management.

"My aunt supplies their scallop beds," she said. "I sent a note."

"On a list that runs in seasons."

"They were very kind about it."

A supplier, a family connection, a mechanism, and a courtesy. Four facts, and between them not one gram of how.

She was in no condition to open a ticket, and drew breath to ask about the coals instead.

"Stop it," Surtrina said, golden eyes cutting through the grill smoke. "Just say it. What did you get."

Hathaway coughed into the cup and pressed a hand to her sternum. "It hardly seems appropriate to discuss grades at a time like—"

"I am not so petty that I need you faking a funeral over a barbecue pit." Surtrina rolled her eyes with enough force to be audible. "It's ruining my appetite. Spit it out."

Hathaway set the cup down and smoothed her napkin.

"Ninety-nine."

Surtrina took a skewer off the waiter's tray without breaking eye contact, tore a chunk off with her fangs, and chewed it like it owed her money.

"Ten points higher than me," she said around the meat. She swallowed hard. The embers in her hair flared once, and she lifted her chin. "It won't be like this next time."

Hathaway's humility slipped its leash and ran off into the clouds.

"Next time is next time," she said, leaning forward onto her elbows.

Surtrina's face went perfectly flat. She picked up a pair of tongs, lifted an enormous Ghost Sea scallop off the coals, shell and all, and set it down in front of Hathaway hard enough to rattle the table.

"Eat. Apparently not even food can plug your mouth."

Yenna's knife paused halfway through a serving of cheese-stewed dragon brain. Both golden ears gave one small, involuntary twitch.

"I received a ninety-four," she offered.

Surtrina clutched her chest and sagged backward into the cushions. "Congratulations," she wheezed, entirely drained of life force.

Spectra finished a cut of roasted sea dragon, set her fork down without a sound, and reached into her robes for the silver-banded pipe. She tapped the bowl, drew, and let a ribbon of sugarcane mist out into the sea breeze.

"I was also given a ninety-four."

Surtrina choked.

She turned her head and looked at the pale, shadowed line of Spectra's profile, and the conflict that came across her face was genuinely spectacular. Hathaway watched an operating system crash in real time.

Yenna's ninety-four is a triumph of standard coursework. Spectra's ninety-four is a formal notice from the Tenth Seat reading: your mathematics is flawless, and you are still living in your mother's basement.

And there was no protocol on the server for consoling a woman whose catastrophe outranked your own best effort by five points.

Mercifully, Spectra spared her the attempt. She turned her hollow gaze across the table to the aristocrat who had spent the entire meal quietly adjusting the grill temperature so that nobody's food burned while they were busy being miserable.

"And you?"

Victoria turned a slice of abalone with silver chopsticks, her posture unbroken.

"One hundred."

Something brittle and complicated moved through the fog around Spectra's face. She held the pipe at her lips a moment without drawing on it.

"I see," she murmured. She was quiet for a long, weighted second, and then she dipped her head a fraction. "You deserve it."

Victoria looked at her, and the glacial perfection in the pale blue eyes came down by a degree, just enough to be human.

"You will find your own answer."

Surtrina let out a long breath. She uncoiled her heavy scaled tail from around her own waist and dropped it onto the cushion beside Spectra's hip, where the banked abyssal heat of it pushed back the cold the ghost witch carried everywhere she went.

Spectra looked down at the tail. The corner of her pale mouth lifted, very slightly, and stayed there.

[Time]: Autumn Semester, Day 10 — 2:00 PM[Location]: West Campus · Yggdrasil Academy · Potions Hall & Compounding Dispensary

The morning Botany block passed in a haze of humidity wards and toxic spore harvesting that Hathaway's brain logged, compressed, and declined to render.

The afternoon put them back in the ozone-scrubbed cold of the dispensary, and the syllabus had moved on from experimental fox mutations to the bedrock of the entire A2 curriculum: the [Empower Spell Potion]. The standard upper-tier crucible output. Grind the aether-root at step six, purge the salamander blood at nineteen, hold the thermal gradient for forty minutes without blinking at thirty-one, and if you could not clear all sixty-two under pressure you did not sit the final. Roxanne walked them through every failure node with the joyless mechanical patience of a metronome, and Hathaway turned in an acceptable glowing blue vial four minutes before the bell.

She had one glove off when the flat voice cut through the ventilation hum.

"Hathaway. Surtrina. Remain at your stations."

Her heart performed a hard reboot.

Nobody in the history of education has ever been kept back for a good reason. Did I sign the safety waiver in the wrong column? Did she see me pour the excess salamander blood down the sink?

The rest of the class filed out and offered them nothing but the silent, unanimous pity of people who had gotten away with it.

Roxanne waited for the doors to seal. Then she came down the aisle, black mourning velvet swallowing the light, the lace bonnet giving away nothing, and stopped at their bench to draw out a leather-bound ledger and a silver quill.

"Supplementary inquiry," she stated. "Regarding the unstable Foxification matrix. Your results were anomalous and require documentation."

Hathaway's shoulders dropped three inches. A bug report.

"Onset interval between ingestion and appendage manifestation."

"Three seconds, Professor."

"Trigger threshold for involuntary caudal movement."

"Any active calculation. Anything at all. Square roots did it."

The quill scratched, and the next question arrived. Then the one after that, and the one after that, and it went on with the hyper-focused, entirely hostility-free pedantry of a database administrator running a manual audit at the end of a long shift. Roxanne wanted nerve density at the base of the spine. She wanted the lag between motor command and physical response, in tenths, and she asked for it with a face so blank that Hathaway spent several seconds uncertain whether the woman was breathing.

The sterile cold, the metronome cadence, the sheer relentless granularity of it. Hathaway's eyelids began losing a war against gravity. It was the precise, inescapable, soul-flattening drowsiness of the back row of an advanced theoretical mathematics lecture immediately after a heavy lunch, and she swayed on her feet and answered a question about capillary dilation without any clear memory of hearing it.

"Documented." The silver eyes shifted. "Surtrina. The Balor variant. Quantify the degradation in physical output across the affected period, as a percentage of your own baseline."

Surtrina, who had a considerably higher tolerance for tedium and a considerably lower one for being talked to, began rattling off telemetry with annoyed precision. The quill kept pace in clean shorthand. Ten more minutes went by.

Hathaway was approximately three seconds from a standing coma when Roxanne spoke again, without looking up from the ledger, in the exact same dead, clinical, wax-cylinder monotone she had been using since the doors closed.

"Does she still like to smoke sugarcane fog?"

Hathaway's spine snapped straight.

Ice water went through her nervous system and vaporized the entire afternoon's accumulated fatigue in a single microsecond. Still. Her threat arrays came up hot and screaming. That question already knows the answer to nine other questions.

Surtrina, who possessed many gifts and no subtext decryption whatsoever, did not even blink.

"Oh, yeah, she loves it," she said, leaning back against the bench and crossing her arms. "She bought a whole new silver-banded pipe for it over the break. Down at the port in Casendiara. The shopkeeper tried to upcharge her and she just stared until the woman found her a twenty percent discount without being asked. She started on the standard green-cane blend, went through a couple of the sealed vials looking for something better, tried an iced-mint one last week and hated it, went straight back to the original."

Hathaway stood beside her with her mouth slightly open.

Surtrina. Her social engineering thread was on its feet and shrieking into a void. Surtrina, you are dumping the entire user log to an unverified endpoint.

"Did she mention," Roxanne said, and the flat voice came down by a fraction of a decibel, "whether she prefers the vapor chilled or at ambient temperature?"

"Chilled. Always chills the stem first."

Roxanne wrote.

Surtrina's last answer had run six words. The quill kept going for the better part of two minutes.

Then Roxanne drew one clean line under it and closed the book.

"The inquiry is concluded. You are dismissed."

She turned and drifted out through the side door, and the heavy panel clicked shut behind her, and the dispensary was suddenly very white and very quiet.

Surtrina stood there. She blinked at the door and scratched the back of her head. Somewhere in the machinery, a cog engaged.

"Wait," she said. "She just wanted to ask about Spectra, didn't she."

Hathaway opened her mouth. She closed it.

She had perhaps forty different things to say and every one of them died in transit, because she could not work out how to file what she had just watched.

What is this, she thought, pressing both palms against her eyes. What is this crushing sensation of checking in on a lonely elderly relative. She moved across a continent, she took a post teaching a class her own daughter tested out of, and she just spent half an hour building a twenty-item questionnaire so she could ask a stranger what flavor her kid smokes.

"So," Hathaway said carefully. "Do we tell Spectra."

Surtrina looked at the door, and Hathaway looked at Surtrina.

They thought about the frost that had crawled across an inkwell yesterday morning. They thought about a ninety-four. They thought about a woman who had spent a week hiring strategic deterrents to cut one specific person out of her own architecture, and about what it would do to her to learn that the person had never once stopped taking notes.

"No," Surtrina said. "We absolutely never mention this again."

"Agreed."

Two undisclosed contributors in as many days. Her developer brain moved the incident into a directory that was starting to require an index of its own. At this rate I'm going to need version control for the things I'm not allowed to say.

At dinner that evening Spectra brought the pipe out over dessert. She chilled the stem first, then filled her end of the table with sweet sugarcane fog.

Across from her, Hathaway did not look at Surtrina, and Surtrina did not look at Hathaway, and neither of them said a word, and Spectra ate her pudding in peace.
