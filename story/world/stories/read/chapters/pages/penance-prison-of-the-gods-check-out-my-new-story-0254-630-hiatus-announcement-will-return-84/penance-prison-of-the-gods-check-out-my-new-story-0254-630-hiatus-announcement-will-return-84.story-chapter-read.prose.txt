The silence didn’t feel like peace. It felt like breath held too long, like a string pulled tight across the chest, waiting to snap. The air was thick with the echo of what we’d done and what had almost been done to us.

Jessel knelt near the edge of the shattered arena, her knees brushing glass dust. One hand braced on the floor, the other still faintly glowing from the spell that had pierced the Trickster’s chest. Her lips moved slightly, whispering something I couldn’t hear, perhaps a prayer or a spell she didn’t know she’d started. She wasn’t bleeding, not visibly, but the look in her eyes said she wasn’t okay.

She didn’t look up when I approached, but she spoke, voice low and distant.

“I saw myself die.”

I crouched beside her, careful not to disturb the blood-crusted glass. “One of the reflections?”

She nodded slowly. “It was me . Same braid. Same robes. Same scar on my wrist. But older. Tired. And she was screaming. Just… screaming. Like something inside her had come loose. Her mouth kept moving, but no sound came out. And I didn’t revive, I didn’t come back—”

She shivered, arms wrapping around her ribs.

“—She looked like me, but hollow. Like there was nothing left inside.”

I didn’t answer right away. My own reflection had stayed intact, but I remembered the one that lagged behind by half a second. The one that blinked when I didn’t. The one that smiled just a little too long.

“That’s how the place gets to you,” I said quietly. “It doesn’t just copy your shape. It copies the parts you don’t want to admit are real. Just part of the bag of tricks that trickster used.”

Jessel’s hands had gone still on her lap, fingers tangled in her prayer string. “It didn’t feel like a lie. It felt like a warning.”

I watched her a moment longer. Then said, “Maybe it was.”

She looked up, surprised. Probably expected me to tell her she was safe, that it wasn’t real, that the dungeon plays tricks. But that wasn’t the truth—not here. Not in this place.

“I’m still here,” she whispered.

“You are,” I said. “So hold onto that.”

A shard of broken thread drifted nearby and caught the light—briefly flickering like a line of fire before settling back to dead silk.

Jessel blinked, then straightened, slowly pulling herself upright. She looked thinner somehow. Smaller. But her eyes were clearer.

“That thing didn’t kill me,” she said, more to herself than to me. “And that version of me isn’t all I am.”

“No,” I said. “It isn’t.”

She turned toward the far end of the chamber, where Fen was still scouting the dais. Her fingers wrapped once more around the prayer string, then relaxed.

Fen stood with her back to the largest broken mirror, her spear reversed and resting tip-down like a cane. She was breathing through her nose, slow and steady, her face blank in a way that meant she wasn’t. Her boots crunched softly as she shifted, ichor crusted to one thigh. Her blade dripped black against the tile, and the fact she hadn’t cleaned it yet told me more than words would have.

I turned a slow circle. Threads hung loose from the ceiling like snapped nerves, drifting in air that didn’t move. The reflections were real now, mimicking us perfectly without any of the old delays, warping, or wrong smiles. The mirrors were still cracked, but they were just glass again: sharp, broken, and reflecting light instead of intent. The Trickster and its hidden selves were gone, leaving only wreckage, residue, and the three of us still standing. I lowered my staff.

“Ready to move forward?” I asked, though I already knew.

Jessel looked up, startled like I’d pulled her from underwater. “Y-yeah,” she said. “I think so.”

Fen didn’t answer right away. She wiped her blade on the hem of her coat, eyes scanning the ceiling like she didn’t trust the quiet. “For now,” she muttered.

I walked over to one of the larger mirrors and stared at my reflection. It stared back with the same staff, the same blood under the chin, and the same tight jaw and narrowed eyes. It was tired and undeniably real.

{That’s what you think.}

I didn’t flinch. “Still talking?”

{You’ll miss me when it’s just silence.}

“Doubt it.”

Behind me, Jessel was standing as Fen sheathed her blade with a sharp click. There was no sign of Halver, not even a blood trail. We had no clue which shard of the dungeon had pulled him through or if we would even find a path to him. We were all that was left.

I clenched my jaw and looked at the wreckage again. Threads curled like dead veins while the air carried the sour-metal stink of raw mana and dead monsters. My back ached and my knuckles were split. This room hadn’t wanted to kill us; it wanted to see what we’d become. I turned from the glass.

“Let’s loot the place,” I said. “Then we go back to the Bazaar. The others should know to

meet back there.”

No one argued. We moved into the debris, and it felt as though what remained of the mirrors still watched us go.

We didn’t expect much, but the reality was worse than I thought. The loot chamber was massive, wide enough to fit a battalion and high enough to echo if you breathed too hard, but once the dust settled and our nerves stopped twitching, it became clear how little there was left to take. The room held no treasure piles or glowing chests, only debris: clippings of thread, broken glass, and blood in too many shades to name.

Fen nudged a shattered mirror with the butt of her spear. “Nothing here but pieces.”

I crouched beside one of the thread pillars, brushing away grit and shards to inspect the base. Something metallic caught my eye. I reached in carefully, my fingers sliding along cold stone, and pulled out a small, plain silver ring that had no runes or weight to it.

I held it up. Jessel squinted. “Is it enchanted?”

I shook my head. “Dead. Might’ve been a trinket once.” I pocketed it anyway, because sometimes broken things could be fixed.

Across the chamber, Fen moved toward a slightly raised platform. It looked like a decorative dais at first, just a mirrored plinth cracked down the center, but as she ran a hand along its edge, a faint shimmer pulsed beneath her palm.

“There’s a seam here,” she said.

I joined her and traced it with my thumb. The magic was dormant but not gone. I pressed in gently, like I would with a mimic’s jaw, and the panel clicked. A small compartment hissed open, barely wider than a helmet, revealing a cache no larger than a merchant’s coinbox.

Inside was a rolled-up strip of aged silk, two bone coins marked with strange carvings, unlike any of the coins I’d picked up before now, a vial of cloudy amber liquid, and tucked beneath it all, wedged between layers of false glass, was the aerlyntium humming faintly with power. I didn’t smile; I just reached in and took it. It was warm in my hand, almost too warm.

Inside was a rolled-up strip of aged silk, two bone coins marked with strange carvings—unlike any of the coins I’d picked up before now—a vial of cloudy amber liquid, and tucked beneath it all, wedged between layers of false glass, was the Aerlyntium, humming faintly with power.

I didn’t smile. I just reached in and took it.

It was warm in my hand. Almost too warm.

The shard pulsed once—then again, sharper—and the hum grew into a low, resonant note that vibrated through my bones. Before I could speak, light spilled from the crystal in thin threads, webbing outward like veins through the room.

Glass lifted off the floor. Threads snapped and curled in midair. Blood, debris, and shattered fragments evaporated, not in flame or force, but like dust sucked into a vacuum. The chamber cleaned itself with eerie precision, the remains of the Trickster vanishing without sound.

A symbol lit up across the Aerlyntium’s surface: a triangular lattice, pulsing faintly.

{Error: Insufficient organic matter. Request incomplete. Please provide biomass.}

The glow faded. The room went still again.

Jessel leaned closer, brow furrowed. “What does that mean?”

I turned the shard over in my palm once more, then slipped it into my belt pouch. “Means I need to clear a bunch of mobs.”

Fen raised an eyebrow.

“And,” I said, exhaling slowly, “I know just the room for it.”
