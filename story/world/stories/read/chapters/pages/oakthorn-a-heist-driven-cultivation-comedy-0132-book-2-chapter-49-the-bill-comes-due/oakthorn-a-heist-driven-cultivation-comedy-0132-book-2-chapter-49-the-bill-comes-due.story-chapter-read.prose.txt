The Soulkeeper’s Crown pulsed against Cade’s palm like a corrupted heartbeat as he placed it on the polished ebony conference table. Emerald light leaked through the cracks between his fingers, casting sickly shadows across his face that made him look like he’d aged a decade in the few days since they’d left.

Jameson’s eyes widened a fraction—the draugr equivalent of gasping in shock.

“You actually succeeded,” the crime lord said, reaching for the crown with barely restrained hunger. “Astrid insisted you would, but...”

“But it seems your crew wasn’t so good at keeping their hands off of an ancient shiny object,” Cade finished, arching an eyebrow. “Funny how corpses tend to underperform in the artifact retrieval department.”

Jameson’s hand froze halfway to the crown. “Bernard is dead?”

“Very,” Cade confirmed. “Along with the rest of the Stone Britches. Though to be fair, the blame lies more with Bernard’s decision to accessorize with forbidden headwear than with us.” He gestured to the pulsing crown. “I’d recommend a display case for this thing. Preferably one that locks from the outside. Oh, and a sign that says: Caution. This will possess you with a Progenitor’s grumpy ass.”

Cade knew he was pushing his luck. He knew he should’ve striven for decorum, but right then, after all they’d endured, he just couldn’t muster the patience.

The journey back through the Blightmarshes had been a nightmare of mud, monsters, and misery, punctuated by brief intervals of dread. Astrid and her draugr contingent had met them at the swamp’s edge, their normally expressionless gray faces betraying something like surprise when they saw the bedraggled band of thieves stumbling toward them.

“You look like you’ve been through all four hells,” she’d said by way of greeting. “And emerged with a souvenir.”

Now, three days later, cleaned up and wearing clothes that cost more than their previous collective wardrobes, Cade and his crew stood in the ornate chamber while Jameson examined their prize. The crown gleamed malevolently, its emerald glow seeming to bend the light around it in ways that made Cade’s newly-gold-ranked core itch uncomfortably.

“The terms of our arrangement will be honored in full,” Jameson declared, finally picking up the crown with reverent hands. “The Clover Clan rewards success most generously.”

“Especially when said success involves Bernard’s demise,” Astrid murmured, a hint of genuine pleasure coloring her otherwise impassive face.

“Speaking of rewards,” Cade ventured, forcing nonchalance into his voice, “when do we get the grand tour of the Clover Clan’s legendary secret handshakes and whatnot? Being hunted pariahs is getting old, and I’d like to cash in on that protection you promised.”

“All in good time,” Jameson replied, not taking his eyes off the crown. “Tonight, we celebrate. Tomorrow, we initiate.”

And celebrate they did.

The Clover Trading Company’s headquarters transformed into a scene of debauchery that would make even the most dissolute nobility of Elysia blush. Cade had seen extravagance before—hell, he’d stolen from it often enough—but draugr revelry reached levels that redefined excess.

Crystal chandeliers hung from vaulted ceilings like frozen waterfalls, casting prismatic light across marble floors inlaid with precious metals. Tables groaned beneath exotic foods arranged in architectural displays that seemed to defy gravity. Servants glided through the throng bearing trays of drinks that smoked, bubbled, or glowed depending on their contents.

Music filled the air from an orchestra whose instruments seemed partially made of bone, producing sounds that wormed their way into Cade’s mind and painted colors behind his eyelids when he blinked. Dancers moved with inhuman grace, their movements suggesting they might not be entirely bound by the same physical laws as everyone else. Or bone structure.

Through it all, draugr aristocracy mingled and preened, their gray skin adorned with some jewels pressed directly into their flesh rather than merely worn on it. They observed Cade’s crew with the calculated interest of collectors examining rare specimens.

“Is anyone else feeling a bit like the main course at a banquet?” Gavin muttered, tugging at the high collar of his borrowed finery.

“Don’t be ridiculous,” Elena replied, scanning the room with professional interest. “We’re clearly the entertainment.”

“Speak for yourself,” Jer quipped, already halfway through his third glass of something that shimmered like liquid moonlight. “I’m just here for the free drinks.”

Cade drifted through the celebration, accepting congratulations from faces he’d never remember and names he couldn’t pronounce. The crown had been secured in a special display at the far end of the hall, surrounded by wards that made his eyes water when he looked at them directly. Draugr security guards stood at attention nearby, their gray skin etched with sigils that pulsed in time with the crown’s emerald glow.

“Admiring our security measures?” Astrid appeared at his elbow, offering a crystal flute filled with something that looked suspiciously like liquid gold.

“Just calculating how long it would take me to steal it back,” Cade replied honestly, accepting the drink with a caution born of experience.

Astrid’s laugh sounded like wind chimes made of icicles. “I’ve always appreciated that about you, Stormhollow. No pretense.”

“Life’s too short for bullshit,” Cade agreed, taking a tentative sip. The liquid tasted like summer sunlight distilled through honey, with an afterburn that suggested it could probably strip paint off walls. “Well, my life is, anyway. You people have time to spare.”

“Time,” Astrid mused, her black eyes reflecting the dancing lights overhead, “is both our greatest luxury and our heaviest chain.”

Before Cade could pursue that particular philosophical rabbit hole, he felt a prickling sensation at the base of his skull—a warning sign he’d learned never to ignore. Someone was watching him with intent that went beyond casual interest.

He turned slowly, scanning the crowd until his gaze settled on an elderly draugr standing apart from the revelry. Unlike the ostentatious display of wealth that draped the other undead aristocrats, this one wore simple clothes of impeccable quality—the kind of understated elegance that didn’t need to scream its value.

His gray skin was weathered like ancient parchment, creased with lines that suggested he’d been old before most civilizations had been young. But it was his eyes that caught Cade’s attention—they were milky with age and yet somehow sharper than anyone else in the room.

And they were fixed directly on him.

“Who’s the walking history book?” Cade asked Astrid quietly.

Her expression shifted subtly, something like respect mingling with wariness. “That,” she replied in a low voice, “is my grandfather.”

“I thought Jameson was the head honcho around here.”

“Father manages our operations,” Astrid explained. “Grandfather oversees... broader concerns.”

“How broad are we talking?”

Before Astrid could answer, Jameson materialized beside them with the silent efficiency of shadow given form.

“My master wishes to speak with you,” he informed Cade, his tone making it clear this wasn’t a request. “Privately.”

Cade raised an eyebrow. “Your master? I thought you were the big bad wolf in this particular fable.”

“There are always bigger wolves,” Jameson replied, his gaze drifting meaningfully toward the ancient draugr. “Some have been hunting since before the forest had a name.”

With that cryptic statement, Jameson melted back into the crowd, leaving Cade with the distinct impression he’d just been summoned to an audience he couldn’t refuse.

“Heading into what might be a trap,” he projected to his crew through their mental link. “If I’m not back in twenty minutes, feel free to stage a dramatic rescue. Explosions are optional but encouraged.”

“As if we’d miss the chance to blow something up,” came Elena’s dry response. “Want me to follow at a distance?”

“No, let’s not antagonize our hosts just yet. Besides, I’m armed with exceptional charm and rakish good looks.”

“So completely defenseless, then,” Nora interjected. “We’ll be ready.”

Cade severed the connection and began making his way toward the ancient draugr, who watched his approach with the patience of mountains. Astrid remained behind, her eyes tracking his progress with an expression Cade couldn’t quite decipher.

As he drew closer, Cade felt an odd sensation—a resonance with something deep within his core, like recognizing a melody played in a key he’d never heard before.

The elderly draugr inclined his head in greeting. “Son of Ruin,” he acknowledged, his voice surprisingly warm for someone who looked like they’d been mummified several centuries ago. “Walk with me.”

Without waiting for a response, the ancient being turned and moved toward a hidden door partially concealed behind heavy draperies. Cade followed, mentally mapping their route through the labyrinthine corridors of the Clover Trading Company’s headquarters.

“I am called Gerald,” the draugr offered as they walked.

Cade nearly stumbled.

Gerald? The ancient, power-radiating being who’d been watching him like he was a particularly interesting insect was named... Gerald? He bit the inside of his cheek to keep from laughing.

Someone clearly had a wildly inconsistent sense of dramatic flair when naming Progenitor children. Mordred for one brother, Gerald for the other. There was a story there, and probably not a flattering one for Gerald’s parents.

“Cade Stormhollow,” he replied once he’d composed himself. “But you already knew that.”

“I know many things about you,” Gerald agreed, leading him into a modest study lined with books that, unlike many noble libraries, showed clear signs of having been read. A fire crackled in a hearth of green-veined marble, casting dancing shadows across walls hung with maps of places Cade didn’t recognize.

“That’s not at all unsettling,” Cade muttered, taking the seat Gerald gestured toward.

“Mortals find comfort in ignorance,” Gerald observed, settling into his own chair with the careful precision of someone who’d learned to move delicately in a world more fragile than himself. “Knowledge is often uncomfortable.”

“Says the man who apparently has a library worth of it.” Cade gestured to the book-lined walls. “You didn’t bring me here to discuss your reading habits, I’m guessing.”

“Direct. Good.” Gerald’s smile revealed teeth that had never been human. “I’ve found that immortality tends to encourage circuitous conversation. A bad habit when dealing with those whose lifespans are so...” he paused, searching for the right word, “...abbreviated.”

“Always happy to help with the eternal’s time management,” Cade replied dryly. “What can I do for you, Gerald?”

“You can accept my thanks,” the ancient draugr said, his tone shifting to something almost gentle. “For my brother.”

Cade blinked, momentarily thrown. “Your brother?”

“The essence trapped within the crown you recovered,” Gerald clarified. “Mordred.”

The young thief was glad he was seated.

The pieces clicked into place with a jarring suddenness, leaving him to reel in their implications. At least this mind-shattering tidbit answered one nagging question he’d had. The Clover Clan knew exactly what they were asking his crew to steal.

Bastards. A little warning would’ve gone a long way.

“You’re a Progenitor.” It wasn’t a question.

“Was. Is. Will be.” Gerald waved a weathered hand dismissively. “Language struggles with concepts that exist outside its temporal constraints.”

Cade leaned back, mind racing to process everything. “So the Clover Clan is what, a family business in the most literal sense? A Progenitor dynasty of crime lords?”

“We prefer to think of ourselves as specialists in acquisition and redistribution,” Gerald replied with a hint of amusement. “But essentially, yes. My descendants have carried on the traditions I established long ago, though few retain more than a shadow of their Progenitor heritage.”

“And the whole draugr thing?”

“A consequence of our nature. Progenitors aren’t defined merely by our racial traits rooted in our blood—what truly sets us apart is that we’re born without cores.”

“Without cores?” Cade couldn’t hide his surprise. “But how do you—”

“Wield power?” Gerald finished for him. “We can’t cultivate magic in the way you mortals do. That is why we’ve worked so hard on developing incredible enchantments, wards, and weapons. It’s to compensate for a world driven by magic we can never access ourselves.”

The revelation reshaped Cade’s understanding of everything they’d encountered since arriving in Honor’s Light. The intricate transportation system. The powerful wards. The mechanical marvels that operated without obvious magical input.

“So you wanted the crown because Mordred is family,” Cade said slowly. “That’s why the Clover Clan went after it.”

Gerald’s expression darkened. “Honor—the false god, not the true one—used the crown to imprison my brother’s consciousness after the Culling War. The ‘quarantine’ around the Blightmarshes was never about protecting the world from monsters. It was about keeping everyone away from the truth buried beneath that cathedral.”

“And what truth would that be?” Cade asked, unable to keep the edge from his voice.

Gerald studied him for a long moment, as if weighing how much to reveal. Finally, he rose and moved to a cabinet near the fireplace. “Before I answer that, tell me what you saw when you touched the tree.”

Cade stiffened. “How did you—”

“Know?” Gerald withdrew a rolled parchment from the cabinet. “The tree’s power rippled across the ley lines. Those sensitive to such things felt it awaken. What I don’t know is what you saw.”

Cade hesitated, then decided truth might yield more than deception. “I saw colored streams—ley lines, I’m guessing. The tree was connected to them, but there were these thorns...” He paused, remembering the wrongness of it. “They weren’t part of the tree. They were parasites, corrupting it, forcing it into something it wasn’t meant to be.”

Gerald’s eyes widened fractionally—the first genuine surprise Cade had seen on the ancient being’s face. “You saw clearly, then. Better than most.”

“What exactly did I see?”

“The truth that the gods have hidden for millennia.” Gerald unrolled the parchment on a small table between them. “That the divine thrones they sit upon were corrupted from their original purpose. That the Oakthorn Thrones are not natural creations—they are parasitic constructs designed to siphon power from the world’s natural energy pathways.”

Cade stared at the unrolled parchment, his breath catching in his throat. It was a map—not just any map, but one whose markings seemed to burn themselves into his vision with familiar fire.

“This is—”

“The map to Destruction’s domain,” Gerald confirmed. “Her original domain, not the scattered fragments that survived her demise. We are not blind, Mr. Stormhollow. We saw you ogling it when you first visited our humble halls.”

Cade’s core hummed in response to the familiar markings, the fragment of divine essence embedded within it resonating with the power contained in the ancient parchment.

“Why show me this?” Cade asked, forcing himself to look away from the map that called to something primal within him. “Why tell me any of this?”

Gerald’s weathered face crinkled in what might have been amusement. “Because my granddaughter was right about you.”

“Astrid?” Cade couldn’t keep the disbelief from his voice at his admission.

“She witnessed your performances in the Tournament of Life. She saw how you consistently chose your companions over personal gain.” Gerald traced a finger along the map’s edge. “My granddaughter believed that mortals can be trusted when they value each other above power.”

“While Jameson thought differently,” Cade guessed, one of his fingers playing with the edge of his seat.

“He believed that mortals could only be trusted when their ambitions were known and could be manipulated. Hence, Bernard and his crew.”

“So we were all just pieces in a family argument,” Cade concluded, unsurprised but still irritated. “Cosmic test subjects to settle a bet about mortal trustworthiness. I’m honored.”

He wasn’t honored.

“It was a wager between myself and my descendants,” Gerald confirmed without apology. “I believed mortals couldn’t be trusted with certain truths. They believed otherwise, though they differed on the type of mortal worthy of that trust.”

“And now?” Cade asked.

“Now I find myself in the uncomfortable position of admitting I was wrong.” Gerald held out the map. “Some mortals can indeed be trusted.”

Cade stared at the offered parchment, suspicion battling with his desperate need to snatch it from where it lay. “What’s the catch? Because I’ve been around long enough to know there’s always a catch.”

“Knowledge has its own price,” Gerald replied, his gaze sharpening. “Should you follow this map, you will discover truths that may shake whatever faith you have left in the cosmic order. You may find that everything you believed about gods and mortals and the nature of power itself is built upon foundations of sand.”

“Been there, done that,” Cade muttered, though inwardly he felt a chill at the warning. “What’s one more existential crisis at this point?”

“Such bravado,” Gerald observed with something like fondness. “Youth is so resilient.” His expression sobered. “There is one more thing you should know. I’ve marked a location of particular significance—the resting place of a Lost Throne.”

Cade raised an eyebrow. “Another throne? I thought we established those were bad news.”

“This one is different.” Gerald’s voice dropped lower, forcing Cade to lean in despite himself. “It belonged to a god that neither we Progenitors nor the current pantheon had any hand in destroying. It simply... disappeared, along with its deity.”

“Which god?” Cade asked, feeling tension coil in his gut.

Gerald’s eyes seemed to peer through him rather than at him. “That is for you to discover. Though I suspect, given your... unique circumstances, you already carry a piece of the answer within you.”

Cade thought of the chaos magic that had emerged alongside his destruction core—the crimson energy that had allowed him to free the oak tree from its parasitic thorns. The power that seemed to predate even the gods themselves.

Before he could press further, a soft chime sounded from somewhere within the room.

“Ah,” Gerald sighed, “it seems my presence is required elsewhere. Immortality does not exempt one from social obligations, unfortunately.”

He offered the rolled map to Cade once more. “Knowledge is a double-edged sword, Son of Ruin. It can free you or destroy you. The choice of how to wield it remains yours.”

Cade accepted the map, feeling its weight in his hands—physical and metaphorical.

Before he could stop himself, he called out to the undead Progenitor. “Why not claim this Lost Throne yourself? If it’s so special?”

Gerald’s laugh held the bitterness of millennia. “As I said, we draugr are born without cores. The Thrones respond only to those with the capacity to channel their power.” He moved toward the door, then paused. “Besides, some powers are best left untouched by those with too long a memory and too many regrets. My brother made that wretched thing. I think it’s best it’s gone before we figure out how to safely awaken him. Good luck, Stormhollow. May it prove to be less of a curse to you than it was to me.”

With that cryptic statement, Gerald nodded to Cade and left the room, leaving him alone with the map and a head full of questions that threatened to overflow.

“Okay,” Cade breathed out, staring off into nothing. “Shit.”

They’d actually done it. They got the crown, membership into the biggest crime syndicate in the world, and the map to Destruction’s Domain. Which, now that he knew more about the Thrones the gods all seemed to possess, was far more valuable than he could have ever imagined before.

He continued to stare into the emerald fire for several more minutes, his mind unable to slow down. But the swirling thoughts and emotions finally reached a fever pitch, and he stood suddenly, his gold-ranked body making the movement remarkably smooth.

“I need a drink,” he announced to the room. It didn’t respond.

After securing the priceless artifact in an inner pocket, Cade made his way back toward the main hall, the celebration still in full swing. His crew seemed to have adapted to the opulence with varying degrees of success—Gavin was chatting animatedly with a group of youthful draugr nobles, Jer was balancing upside down on one hand atop a fountain to the delight of onlookers, and Elena was cataloguing each of the jewels the draugrs wore while pretending to admire the architecture.

Nora and Evie had staked out a quieter corner, and the paladin’s watchful gaze never strayed far from the siren as she swayed to the otherworldly music. Orro and Rayka stood close enough that their shoulders brushed, and they seemed to be speaking in low voices while scanning the room with the ingrained caution of those who’d survived by anticipating danger.

And Chubbs—

Cade froze, his heart skipping several beats.

The chubby toddler he’d come to expect was gone. In her place, seated at a table surrounded by fascinated draugr children, was a young girl—perhaps eleven or twelve years old—with the same golden curls and azure eyes, but in a form that had aged years in the span of hours. Her face had lost its baby fat since their fight with Bernard, and her features sharpened into something more defined, more recognizable.

She was in the middle of telling a story, her animated hands painting pictures in the air as her audience leaned forward in rapt attention. Bunny curled around her feet, his eyes shifting between contentment and wariness as he kept watch over his transformed charge.

Halfway through a particularly dramatic gesture, she looked up and met Cade’s somber gaze. Her hands faltered for a moment, her smile diminishing slightly before she quickly looked away.

Cade made his way toward her, heart hammering against his ribs. The draugr children scattered at his approach, melting into the crowd with the unnerving efficiency of their kind.

“Chubbs?” he managed, the name suddenly seeming inappropriate for this transformed child.

“Hello, Cade,” she replied, her voice higher than her adult form but lower than her toddler self—a strange middle ground that sent chills down his spine. “I was wondering when you’d come over.”

She rose from her seat, moving with a grace her toddler self had never possessed, and gestured toward a secluded balcony overlooking the glittering lights of Honor’s Light. Cade followed, mind reeling as he tried to process this new development on top of everything else Gerald had revealed.

The night air outside was crisp with approaching autumn, stars gleaming like scattered diamonds above the city’s warm glow. Chubbs—though he supposed he’d need to reconsider that name soon—leaned against the stone balustrade, her small hands gripping the edge as she gazed out at the vista.

“Nice view,” Cade offered lamely, joining her at the railing.

“It’s beautiful,” she agreed, her voice soft. “So many lights. So many lives. I feel like I could count them all now, if I wanted to.”

Cade studied her profile, seeing echoes of her divine self in the angled cheekbones and the set of her jaw. “So, how exactly did it happen? How did you...?”

“Grow?” She glanced at him, something ancient flickering behind her childlike eyes. “It started in the crypt, when that thing—when Mordred—called me by my true name. Creation.” She said the word carefully, as if testing how it felt in her mouth. “But when you nearly died to protect me…” The young woman let out a shuddering breath. “Something broke loose inside me then. And it’s been growing ever since.”

“Do you remember? Everything, I mean?” Cade asked gently.

“Not everything,” she admitted with a shrug that made her curls bob. “Pieces. Fragments. Like a mosaic with half the tiles missing. But enough.” Her gaze sharpened as she turned to face him fully. “Enough to know that you lied to me.”

The accusation hung between them, sharp and undeniable.

“I did,” Cade acknowledged, seeing no point in further deception. “I won’t insult you by claiming I did it for your own good, but... I was trying to protect you. I now see how insulting that is—to claim I know your strengths better than you do.”

“I appreciate that clarification, Cade. But tell me this. What did you think you were protecting me from? From myself?” There was a bittersweet wisdom in her expression that no child should possess. “Or from what I represent?”

“Both, maybe,” Cade admitted. “I didn’t know what would happen if you remembered too soon. I still don’t.”

She turned back to the view, and was silent for a long moment. The distant sounds of celebration filtered through the air, incongruously festive against the weight of their conversation.

“I forgive you,” she said finally, the words so quiet Cade almost missed them.

“What?”

“I forgive you for lying to me,” she repeated, still staring out at the distant lights. “I understand why you did it. You thought you were protecting me. Maybe you were.”

Relief flooded through him, warm and unexpected. “Thank you.”

“But,” she continued, her voice cooling slightly, “I don’t think I can trust you again. Not for a while.”

The relief curdled, leaving behind a hollow ache. “I understand.”

“Do you?” She finally looked at him again, and Cade was struck by how ancient her eyes seemed in her young face. “Trust is like glass, Cade. Once broken, it can be repaired, but the cracks remain visible if you look closely enough.”

“I’m not sure the Chubbs I knew last week could have come up with that metaphor,” Cade observed, attempting a smile that felt brittle on his face.

“I’ve always been this person,” she replied, a flicker of her old mischief surfacing briefly. “I just couldn’t express it properly before. It’s like...” she searched for the right comparison, “like having thoughts too big for the words you know.”

They stood in silence for a moment, the sounds of celebration muffled by distance and heavy curtains.

“What happens now?” Cade finally asked.

“Now,” she said, pushing away from the balustrade, “we continue our journey to my sister. But things will be different. I’ll be different.”

“You already are,” he observed softly.

“Yes.” She stepped away, moonlight catching in her golden curls and casting her face in silver shadows. “I need time to understand what I’m becoming. What I’ve been.”

“I’ll help however I can,” Cade promised.

“I know you will.” Her smile was sad and knowing. “Just remember what you learned. You don’t know people better than they do. It’s time to stop pretending otherwise, Cade. It’s time you saw all of us more clearly.”

With that, she walked past him, back toward the celebration and their waiting companions. Cade remained on the balcony, the map to Destruction’s domain burning like a coal in his pocket, watching her go with the distinct feeling that he’d gained and lost something precious in the same moment.

A cool breeze ruffled his hair, carrying the scent of autumn and change. Somewhere in the distance, a clock chimed midnight, the sound ringing with finality.

And with its echoing chime, a beginning was disguised as an ending.
