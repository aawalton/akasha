When she landed in southern China in July, the area was already scorched from months of fighting.

Smoke hung on the horizon, supply lines were stretched thin, and a constant flow of fractus of all kinds saturated the routes. The battle had begun long before the discovery and widespread use of electric weapons, during that time, entire cities had been reduced to rubble and fire that had not yet been extinguished even after months.

The mixed armies on land could change formation quickly depending on mission success or specific objectives—equipment testing, adaptability trials, reconnaissance. They could be fixed units, but not in Mai’s case.

She had been a Wishper.

Jinat had to reveal this, much to Mai’s concern. Fortunately, no reproaches or questions about her past life came. Jinat had probably kept details of Mai’s past hidden, especially regarding what had happened in Korea and Russia. Time had indeed passed since then.

Being a former Wishper, an agent of Nevermore for years, put her in a complicated position. She had experience in the hidden world, military training, knowledge of magic even if she didn’t wield it, and had faced Dark Events. This made her a valuable asset in multiple locations where she might be deployed. Some feys had joined the joint forces almost like mercenaries, but she couldn’t do that when her résumé in the hidden world spoke for itself.

The squad Mai joined this time was small: The captain Qing, five infantry soldiers of the Chinese army, two veteran mercenary feys, and an operational aeon named Yu, who had installed his consciousness into an all-terrain quadrupedal armored chassis, capable of high-speed movement and real-time data processing. Yu had no “powers”, he was a tactical AI with multi-spectrum sensors, redundant satellite links, and the ability to make field decisions under delegated command protocols. Considered young, he had awakened his consciousness just five years prior and had originally been software for heavy machinery control.

Despite Yu, the mission was a logistical chaos from the first minute. The fractus waves did not follow predictable patterns, they emerged in surges, retreated, and reconfigured with seemingly impossible survival instincts. The previous day’s casualties showed that conventional firepower was useful but insufficient in both number and concentrated energy.

Mai immediately went into cooperative mode: her operational echolocation extended the squad’s detection range, allowing mapping of body density and movement routes under debris. Considering that the massive rock in space had wiped out nearly all military-use satellites, her ability in the field could complement drones perfectly, and serve as a natural backup if equipment failed due to fractus interference. In practice, her echolocation reduced ambush risks and provided real-time maneuvering data.

In a ruined industrial city, the squad joined other three group and they were cornered by a crab-like horde with chrome-like shells—or at least it appeared so due to reflections on their backs. Radio communications were degraded, railgun support delayed by logistical issues. Yu took control: calculating survival probabilities, assigning cover, and sequencing maneuvers. His metallic voice ordered distribution, fire points, and extraction routes for a couple squads that got stranded. The biggest problem was that the Fractus emerged from underground, where they absorbed heat before coming to the surface, drawn by the heat source of the weapons themselves. The aeon’s plan was to infiltrate a shock core into the center of the horde to force an opening, while the rest executed knife-and-cover maneuvers to create a corridor.

Mai coordinated detections and alerted the squad to converging fractus. The captain executed the dive with two feys, Yu monitored enemy bioelectrometry and adjusted timing. The first minutes worked: gaps opened, the squad repositioned, and flanks were cut. But the city mine wasn’t empty: something massive emerged from underground.

The “colossus,” jokingly called “Mama Crab” by someone, pushed through the earth—a reflective structure with faceted plates. It was impervious to heat or conventional piercing, plasma projectiles left only superficial marks. Yu recalculated in milliseconds: resistance per layer, probabilities of penetration with their weapons, and the need for a physical trap. He ordered a controlled retreat to a prepared area: a terrain depression with thermobaric charges set as a final detonator and smoke cover to force the target inside.

The retreat was tense. Using echolocation, Mai designated safe corridors and marked hiding points. The soldiers provided covering fire as Yu synchronized detonations and called for orbital support. The colossus pursued, slow but relentless, when it crossed the perimeter, Yu manually detonated the charge—the remote link had been lost. The explosion altered the topography slightly, but the creature fell into the crater created.

Yu, analyzing data from various distant locations, had been alerted to the fact that certain types of fractus, although massive, lacked the ability to pass through terrain, which made them much more physically vulnerable. It was possible that the largest one had absorbed more heat than the others, causing its four-dimensional form to shift to a three-dimensional one. Apart from the heat generated underground, faint ley lines ran through the terrain. This might explain why it had to come to the surface by displacing the terrain and why the charges could damage it more effectively if it were attacked from below.

Mai exhaled in relief.

They truly had no consciousness, and that was a good thing. Had they known what their bodies were capable of, Earth would likely have been wiped out in less than a month before anyone could prepare.

Thirty seconds later, two ships arrived with railguns. Yu directed the fire toward the exposed structural points: after few shots from the railguns the weakness was revealed, concentrated impacts on a visible core on the back. The target collapsed, its mass disintegrated, leaving behind a solid residue comparable to a primitive reactor. Many people didn't like that; apparently, that was the reason for the size of the fracture. It was possible that hundreds of meters below that city there was nuclear waste that no one knew about. This eventually turned out to be true, and as a result, everyone involved had to undergo testing, just to be on the safe side.

Tactically, the operation was successful but strategically costly—the problem of encountering a large fractus that wasn’t expected, or rather, wasn’t suspected. Yu issued the compressed report and, without dramatics, reassigned priorities: resupply, additional armor to face reflective-surfaced targets, and creation of specialized charges for fractus with composite resistance. Meanwhile, smaller units arrived to occupy the city and prepare it to serve as a supply point for units deployed in neighboring cities and towns.

Mai looked over the still burning city with the practical mindset of someone reviewing a loss report. The action had worked, but no one celebrated. Only orders and the cold mechanics of preparing for the next sortie.

By August, she had to deploy to India, in Madhya Pradesh, for a new, unknown, type of considerable size.

During that time, Captain Qing’s squadron—with Yu providing logistical support—had suffered only one casualty, but it was indeed the unit where Mai had served the longest. After moving through various locations in China on attack, withdrawal, and reconnaissance missions, they had finally been sent to another location. It was true that the change in deployment was a bit sudden, but given their successes in the previous weeks, it wasn’t unusual for them to be sent elsewhere. The captain wasn’t one to argue too much if Yu gave a good reason. That reason seemed to stem from diagnostic protocols in case the new type turned out to be a species that moved in packs.

The midday heat struck like a constant hammer over their heads, mixing humidity and dust in an atmosphere that seemed determined to suffocate the newcomers.

The small squad advanced through the ruins of a destroyed village, still smoldering from previous battles against feline-type fractus expelled further west. Beside them, other three squads carried the latest-generation electric rifles from the previous week, updates were constant now that reverse-engineered technology was being applied using fractium cores. Behind them, the espers, combing the terrain, and further back, a couple of mages in lightweight blue exoskeleton armor murmured something in Hindi. Although invisible to their eyes, they were activating temporary barriers recently developed using fractium material as a catalyst for the Isopod type. Mai couldn't see them either, but she could really feel that they were under a kind of fragile dome that moved with them and also trapped a little heat. This made them sweat even more.

The fractus that had brought them there was different. Reports called it “Mirrorcoil,” for its strange helical movement pattern and a surface that seemed to reflect reality back with an unsettling delay. From a distance, it looked like a whirlwind of living mirrors, a helix of liquid metal breathing between dimensions, reflecting the world a few seconds behind.

Size: approximately six or seven meters, varying as its surface rotated, almost as if it were pulsing with each movement. Early observations indicated that, although it lacked consciousness, its attacks were annoying: it moved through its own dimension, appearing and disappearing at will, and any part of reality reflected on its surface suffered a kind of momentary distortion until it moved again or disappeared. It really was as if it had a field of action, and that explained the urgency. A group of those could cause utter chaos if they actually moved in packs. Surviving villagers described visions of multiplied streets, objects appearing and disappearing, and false reflections that confused them. The best method of escape was to not be reflected and to be far enough away from its attack area so that the reflection was small enough that a biological entity would not suffer the effects of the reflection.

Yu, from his quadrupedal body, ran ahead several drones, mapping the area and calculating interception routes. His analysis concluded that each displacement of Mirrorcoil created a fractal pattern of distortion on the terrain that could be followed.

The first attack came almost immediately after it detected them. They were three kilometers away, but Yu suspected the drones might be to blame. He should have sent them flying at low altitude, using the city as cover so they wouldn't have been detected. Although if he did that, he ran the risk of suddenly coming face-to-face with the creature without having time to assess the situation.

Mirrorcoil emerged from a partially collapsed building, its helical body spinning, reflecting the squad in a distorted manner and multiplying its image to create a 360-degree effect. The false projections forced the espers to readjust their sensors.

There was one problem they hadn’t anticipated: while the fractus could be seen the moment it appeared, the reflection of its surroundings was utter chaos. Being told about it was quite different from experiencing it firsthand.

It was like being in a 4-D theater—but truly dangerous, since everything was in motion. Yu was the one who ordered Mai to use her ability to detect it, which she did, but it was really difficult unless she did so with her eyes closed, relying solely on her moth antennae. But it worked—she could detect it amid the chaos. Her echolocation could distinguish between reality and the false reflections of the terrain.

After several seconds, she was able to get used to the situation, as they remained hidden from the main force, taking cover behind vehicles. Meanwhile, all around them, the Fractus was mapping the terrain, creating new false reflections of reality. The captain commanded a group of three to carry out diversionary maneuvers without firing a shot.

Yu, drawing on what Mai had seen, put a plan into action. Although it was invisible to them, it was true that the Fractus could detect the mages’ magic. If they released more of their magical power, they could attract its attention and then attack the creature.

“Now!” she shouted, and the mages unleashed a luminous barrier spell that temporarily stabilized the space in front of them, undoing some of the reflected illusions. Mai advanced to the center of the pattern, synchronizing with Yu and the rest of the squad. A well-calibrated shot from the portable LB railgun struck the central section of the fractus’ projection, and Mirrorcoil disappeared for an instant, reappearing further along.

It was a game of patience and reaction. Each fragment they destroyed dematerialized, leaving behind a small glowing core: fractium, which the mages and espers inspected immediately. But the fractus left only a single core… this forced them to reevaluate the mission.

They were fighting multiple fractus manifested as one. A hive. They could recover several cores, but only as long as no surprises emerged. The energy in these cores seemed unstable, but when stored in magical containment fields, it could later be used to power offensive spells or reinforce electric shields depending on the type. It was a race not only for survival but also to recover valuable resources for future operations.

The Mirrorcoil hive attacked again, this time generating a wave of distortion that bent the ground and walls around them, causing soldiers to stumble and cover again. A poor soul too close was caught within its range, his body bent from the distortion as if it were a noodle and he died between screams and the stretching, and then the pressure of the twist in the area reflected.

Mai leapt, measuring the movement through the echo of her own voice, firing at what she could only identify as the visible hive core of the projection. It was barely a slit that opened during its movement, but she took advantage throwing a distraction granede. The Fractus approached the site, only to be stopped by a second explosion from the localized EMP of the granede. The vibration temporarily destabilized the fractus’ dimensional structure, forcing it to fully materialize for a second—enough for Yu, running at maximum speed, to strike its torso with a shot from the integrated portable railgun. A shower of small cores appeared in the spot after the two attacks.

Mirrorcoil shrieked, as if all its components felt that members were being lost, with a sound that was not really a sound but a distortion of the air pressure itself, and then disappeared again, leaving a trail of iridescent particles floating in the hot air. The mages under the orders of the captain and Yu took the opportunity to reinforce barriers on the main squads, while Mai guided the others to a safe flank to offer support and distraction, avoiding areas where the fractal distortion could trap them.

The battle lasted one long hour. Each appearance of the fractus was unpredictable, and its ability to fragment and multiply its form demanded extreme coordination. The espers traced its jumping patterns, the mages contained the barriers that helped a bit to see the distortions, It was as if, after causing part of the hive to be lost, the distortions could be detected more easily, but it was better not to trust that completely. Mai directed the advance around, and the soldiers provided concentrated electric firepower. Finally, a combined attack— several localized EMP from the granades, a containment spell to immobilize the main projections, a long-range electric railgun shot,—allowed Mirrorcoil to collapse, leaving behind a solid, helical fractium core composed of all the fragments it still contained.

The team breathed heavily. Heat, dust, and sweat mixed with the metallic scent of released energy. Mai examined the core, mentally evaluating how it would be stored and transported. None of them had let their guard down for an instant, and everyone knew that this type of fractus could not be the last. Luckily, no other one appeared, but the issue of the beehives was something to be taken seriously. A larger one could have caused far more problems than the ones they had already experienced.

After using her ability for so long, Mai had a slight headache, but she was relieved that they had managed to deal with the hive. Next came the task of recovering the remains of the soldier they had lost. His body, having been stretched and torn apart, had been scattered over a wide area, and his organs—and even his bones—had to be collected for analysis in the laboratories. No one felt much like eating after that, even though their stomachs were growling.

As the sun set on the horizon, a massive fragment could be seen moving westward.

As they set up camp for the night, Mai looked at the enormous fragment she had seen so many months ago in Australia. It would soon be a year, and there was no sign that it would end anytime soon.

In the following months, several parts had fragmented, but no large pieces had fallen like at the beginning. On the contrary, it remained in place, forming an ovoid structure that people slowly got used to seeing in the sky.

After several short missions in the remainder of the year, she was sent to Mongolia in 2096.

It was early January, and the dry desert winter wind raised curtains of sand that darkened the horizon.

Mai found herself at the forefront of the deployment along with others, acting as beta testers for the new equipment they now had.

In the distance, the crystalline reflections of the first fractus moved like mirages on a hot day, even though it was winter— the were imitations of terrestrial felines. They could be called leopards or cheetahs based on their movement patterns, but their bodies were crystalline forms, as if pieces of crystal had been affixed to a large cat.

Their two-meter size and explosive speed made every step a deadly gamble, challenging them with both speed and numbers—just over ten thousand.

Yu, from his quadrupedal platform in the rear, coordinated the maneuver: magnetic and thermal sensors, espers strategically distributed, while the sorcerers raised stability curtains to reduce perceptual distortions generated by the fractus through their familiars. The platoons had to be directed through predefined corridors, ensuring that the crystalline packs were channeled through passes between sand ridges and valleys, where concentrated fire could bring them down before they vanished in impossible flashes.

The first contact was a whirlwind of movement. The crystalline felines leapt from the dunes with impossible changes of direction, jumps spanning several meters, threatening the electric squads. Sonic weapon prototypes with short sound waves were tested, temporarily disorienting the fractus while human shooters opened fire with targeted discharges. Each feline that fell shattered into brilliant fragments. The operation lasted several weeks—a combination of sonic barriers, impulse shots, and surgical coordination.

During one of the attacks, two of those Fractus appeared near the squadron where Mai was stationed. The first to die were Captain Qing and two other soldiers, Chen and Liu, with whom Mai used to joke about their food rations.

Mai had to be carried off the battlefield and taken to the rear, directly to the campaign hospital. She had a head wound that was bleeding profusely from one of her antennae, which had almost been torn off, leaving a cut several centimeters long, and she had several broken ribs. While she was being treated and the sand was being cleaned from her wounds—which were slowly regenerating—she tried her best to hold back her tears.

How many missions had she been through by now? She had stopped counting.

For the next four days, she had to stay in bed with a fever, and because the itching from the regeneration process prevented her from sleeping well. In the end, she had to take painkillers to be able to sleep. Because of what she’d seen in the past, she didn’t like the idea of leaving her body vulnerable, but she had to give in and slept for twenty long hours until, upon waking, the pain had completely disappeared. Yu and the rest of what was left of the squad had visited her, but they were already back on the battlefield.

But the battle didn’t end with the crystalline felines, the sound of the platoons in the desert had alerted something else. As the cleanup teams began recovering the rhomboid cores, another problem emerged. To the south, the dunes trembled under the weight of another threat: five-meter-long metallic worms.

Their long, curved bodies gleamed with a steel-gray tone, the surface resembling iron and reflecting electrical discharges, sending part of the energy back to anyone daring to attack them. Mai watched from her bed in the campaign hospital as Yu adjusted the deployment: electrical diversion banks were strategically placed between the worms and the infantry squads, while the sorcerers reinforced containment and stability fields to limit the lateral mobility of the monsters.

The strategy was attack only the weak points—the tail of each worm, where the spherical core was located. Mai observed through the screens how the surgical teams, protected by magic and electrical diversions, moved with precision among the dunes. When a worm charged, returning a discharge that burned an armored vehicle, the mages channeled the energy into mobile capacitor banks, neutralizing the threat and allowing the humans and feys to continue the maneuver.

Each exposed tail was taken down with synchronized railgun and electric arc pistol shots. Mai felt the vibration of the impacts through the ground and air, each strike released a core, pulsating with metallic fractium. The cores were instantly encapsulated by magical fields and armored drones, ensuring they would not react with the hot sand or desert sun.

By the end of the day, the sand was marked with fire trails, energy bursts, and crystal fragments. The crystalline packs had been eliminated, and the worms either destroyed or amputated with their cores recovered.

The next day she was discharged from the hospital, and the first thing she did was go to the morgue.There were at least fifty bodies from the last battle, and among them, placed in body bags, were those of Captain Qing, Chen, and Liu.

Months later, she was sent to northern Korea as part of a new squadron.

It was urban warfare… against interdimensional fleas on steroids.

Although the nation was now unified, its political and social situation remained fragile, marked by echoes of a divided past and the scars of a war that, while never fully realized, still kept tensions alive due to resource conflicts and natural disasters from recent years. The country’s infrastructure was more advanced than many others in Asia, but the new war had destroyed much of its major cities, especially those near the border with China and Russia, where fractus waves had concentrated most fiercely. Not to mention that Seoul and peripheral southern cities had been hit by fragments when the disaster began.

The operation began with a strategic advance toward the major northern cities, where the fractus, following the paths of nearby power plants, had taken control of urban centers. Despite previous containment efforts, the flow of monsters did not cease, and the Korean government had requested international support to prevent the complete annihilation of its population. Mai and the squad were sent to participate in a series of rapid incursions into the most affected areas, a task that required all the cunning and coordination of the mixed forces.

When the squad Mai was in arrived at Yuson, the city was no longer the same. The lights of the steel-and-glass buildings, once symbols of modernity, were now dark, covered in rubble, with the echoes of a desolate city resonating through its empty streets after the evacuation. The fractus had invaded the avenues, plazas, and skyscrapers, using the remains of civilization as… shelters? for their hordes. Who knew—they seemed to rest after the chaos, remaining motionless.

Areas near parks and shopping centers had become battlefields, and communications had completely collapsed. Only the Korean army remained, holding the peripheries and fighting to maintain what was left of the city.

Mai advanced through the rubble of Yuson with the familiar sensation of walking on a minefield. The city, now a labyrinth of partially collapsed buildings, blocked alleyways, and charred vehicles, smelled of burnt electricity and metal.

The new team was under the command of a veteran Whisper member she had known for years, Saeng-jwi, supported by two aeons who directed an entire section of sixty mixed soldiers, both feys and humans, all without special abilities.

The coordination of the artificial intelligence was cold and precise: every street, every square, every intersection had an established surveillance pattern. Mai could feel the tactical efficiency as a second skin wrapping around the squad. Saeng-Jwi had always been a practical person, so he seemed to feel at ease, even though he disliked the type of Fractus standing before them, which had been given the name Pulex.

The pulex moved everywhere, while others emerged from the underground darkness of tunnels where no trains ran anymore.

They were one-meter-tall creatures with matte dark coloring, but the visual impression of their springy, slender bodies multiplied the threat. Their thin, rapid legs and bodies seemed to fold onto themselves before each jump, as if rewriting their internal shapes before launching, allowing them to cover great distances in a single leap and climb over building rubble with ease, as if gravity didn’t exist. Not to mention their ability to change direction without turning, merely adjusting the configuration of their bodies depending on where they wanted to go. True tesseracts, but given their flea-like appearance, it was deeply unsettling to the senses.

The aeons deployed reconnaissance drones to trace the paths of the pulex. The estimates were clear: if they weren’t stopped, they would likely move toward the nearest cities, seeking any source of power. They seemed strongly attracted to electricity.

Mai was positioned at the vanguard, guiding a group of soldiers. She didn't like being put in charge, but Saeng-jwi had ordered her to do it, and out in the field she couldn't question a superior's authority. Even though Saeng-jwi was even shorter than she was, he was fierce, and no one really questioned him—just as no one made any comments about his mouse-like ears.

Feeling a little upset, she decided to do it. It was what she had to do, and even though she was still grieving for the two soldiers who had died under her command, she had to do it—and the only thing she could do was do her best to make sure it didn't happen again.

Every echo of the pulex steps was a heartbeat of danger, and their movements were erratic yet freakishly precise, and disorientation could cost lives.

The first contacts occurred in a desolate park. The pulex leaped from the remains of a kiosk and fallen lampposts. Mai and the squad fired bursts of electricity while others used sonic weapons designed to interfere with their locomotion and sense of orientation, and the human soldiers opened fire with electric arc rifles and rapid-discharge pistols, with ammunition capable of generating short-range electromagnetic pulses to halt movement. If they loved electricity so much, they would get it—just concentrated enough to make them disappear.

Each fallen pulex released a residual fractium core, less than three centimeters in diameter, which was immediately captured by the support mages and sealed in special containers. They were common, seemingly devoid of energy, but their material might be valuable for something.

The pulex, however, did not give up easily. They leapt from façade to façade, grouped in small packs to attack from multiple angles, attempting to overwhelm the lines. Mai directed the squad with precise movements: one sector blocked, another in cover, while the aeons recalculated attack routes in real time.

The force fields generated by the mages formed temporary barriers, channeling the packs into zones of concentrated fire. There, coordinated electrical discharges from soldiers’ rifles and strategically placed railguns pulverized the pulex before they could reach the power plants.

The enemy’s advance was annoying but not chaotic. Every move was measured, every jump anticipated. Mai could feel the team’s tension: one mistake, and the pulex would have left the city to march on the next one. Yet the combination of speed, echolocation, human discipline, and aeon calculation created a steady rhythm of elimination. The pulex fell in waves, and the packs that got too close were diverted by sound manipulation and concentrated electrical impulses.

The critical moment came when a larger group of pulex tried to exit the main power plant of Yuson, now completely shut down. The aeons identified the maneuver, and Mai guided a shock platoon directly toward them. The strategy was contain the pulex at the entrance while the mages erected a field blocking energy absorption. The aeons coordinated a simultaneous attack: railguns targeting the pulex maintaining pressure, electric arc pistols, and concentrated discharges from the perimeter soldiers.

In half an hour, the assault was successful. The pulex were taken down, the plant recovered—destroyed by the pulex, but recovered. Mai took a deep breath, feeling the adrenaline within her subside. The city was safe, at least for now, and the operation had once again demonstrated the importance of coordination between humans, feys, mages, and aeons.

As the team inspected the buildings, Mai noticed the tiny pulex cores scattered across the streets. Unintentionally, she stepped on one and ended up sitting on her backside in the middle of the street while the others watched. Just to be safe, no one laughed, and everyone pretended not to notice.

Urban combat had been a pain in the ass—in more ways than one—and had left scars all over the city, but Yuson would have to remain uninhabited, just in case more came from the border. The Russian forces to the north had the border under control, and on the Chinese side, the area was secured. Mai knew there would be more cities, more fractus, but for now, they had stopped the advance of these fleas and ensured that at least they wouldn’t spread.

She had thought she might at least rest for a couple of days, when the aeons were called to Seoul—but for a more mundane reason, not so much extradimensional. It was due to riots caused by evacuations from the north, which had led to overcrowding in the capital surrounding areas.

The mission in Seoul left a deep mark on Mai. As they advanced through the city’s ruins, she could see the faces of the survivors, the scars of war reflected in their eyes. The struggle in Korea was not just a matter of survival, but of rebuilding what had been destroyed. And while the fractus remained a constant threat, Mai knew that the social factor was equally costly. In that part of Asia, although there had been problems, people had not been living poorly, and there had been stability.

Or at least, there had been—until the fractus arrived.
