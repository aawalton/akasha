The heat shimmered on the horizon, a rippling mirage that turned the barren dunes into a surreal, endless ocean of gold. The oppressive sun burned high above as Sigurd urged his horse forward, the animal’s hooves kicking up fine sand with every step. He squinted under the brim of his helmet, his thoughts a jumbled mess of frustration and determination.

The Guildmaster’s orders still rang in his ears. "Evacuate Grimroost? Not on my watch," Sigurd had said, his voice firm enough to silence the bustling chaos of the adventurer’s hall. The guildmaster, a stout man with a perpetually furrowed brow, had sighed deeply, torn between his duty to the townsfolk and the confidence Sigurd exuded. In the end, Sigurd had won out.

Now, with the fate of the town resting squarely on his shoulders, he couldn’t afford to fail.

“Why doesn’t Grimroost have an emergency plan?” he muttered, his voice lost in the desert wind. “‘Oh, let’s just wing it,’ they said. ‘Earth dragons don’t come around that often,’ they said.” He shook his head, gripping the reins tighter as his horse’s ears flicked nervously.

The desert stretched endlessly before him, a harsh yet strangely beautiful landscape. Unlike the typical barren wastelands depicted in stories, this desert bore the striking features of the Sonoran terrain. Towering saguaro cacti reached toward the sky, their arms twisted and gnarled like ancient guardians of the sands. Red rock formations jutted out of the earth in jagged spires, their surfaces etched with layers of geological history. Canyons sliced through the desert, their shadowy depths providing brief respite from the unrelenting sun. Every so often, Sigurd caught a glimpse of a desert fox darting between the rocks or a hawk circling high above, its sharp cry piercing the dry air.

The abandoned mines were still a few miles out, their location marked on his map by an ominous “X.” A fitting symbol, Sigurd thought grimly, for the trouble he was about to face.

Sigurd’s horse crested a small hill, and he spotted something unusual in the distance. A figure huddled under the sparse shade of a jagged rock formation, flailing their arms in frustration. As he approached, the scene came into sharper focus. A woman, her wild hair tied in a messy knot, was arguing with a scroll that flapped stubbornly in the desert breeze.

The woman was a peculiar sight. Though she couldn’t have been older than her late twenties, she was small and slight, giving her the appearance of a teenager. Her delicate frame was draped in a tattered yet intricately embroidered robe, dark and adorned with glowing green runes that pulsed faintly with magical energy. Her pointed ears peeked out from beneath her oversized, crooked witch’s hat, which flopped slightly as she moved. Her hair was a deep teal, unkempt but striking, cascading in loose waves that framed her sharp, angular features. Her skin had a greenish-gray hue, hinting at halfling heritage, which added to her unique and otherworldly appearance. Her wide, piercing pink eyes were alight with a mix of irritation and determination, their color almost too vivid to be natural. She clutched a gnarled wooden staff in one hand, its surface covered in strange carvings and a glowing green orb embedded at the top.

“‘Compatible with all pyromancies,’ my ass!” she spat, shaking the parchment. “This ink won’t hold a spell past two uses, let alone one! Who writes this garbage?”

Sigurd slowed his horse, watching with a mix of curiosity and disbelief.

“Excuse me,” he called out. “Do you need help?”

The woman’s head snapped up, her sharp eyes narrowing as she took in his armor and weaponry. With a dismissive wave, she barked, “No, thanks. I’m sure you’re another one of those ‘helpful’ adventurers who think they’re God’s gift to magic users. I’m fine.”

Sigurd raised an eyebrow, dismounting with practiced ease. His imposing presence loomed over her as he stepped closer.

“You don’t look fine,” he said flatly, nodding toward the array of broken tools and scrolls scattered around her.

The woman’s defiance faltered. “Ugh, fine,” she said, brushing sand off her robes. “I’m Ada Lamarr, a frustrated witch, a dubious expert in magical disasters, and – apparently – a desert nomad now.” She gestured to the chaos around her. “I’m here for ore. You know, to craft tools that don’t explode on first use. But noooo, the universe has other plans.”

“Sounds like you’ve had a rough day.”

“A rough life,” Ada corrected, crossing her arms. Her voice rose in pitch as she launched into a tirade. “Do you have any idea how useless most magical tools are? I worked in a magic tools shop for a few years, and I don’t trust any of that stuff. Who the hell knows what components are in the ashes? Half the time, it’s ground-up scraps of failed spells or who knows what else. And don’t even get me started on scrolls! Those things are disasters waiting to happen. Ever wonder why they’re so cheap? Because they’re held together by the magical equivalent of duck tape.”

She started pacing, waving her arms as she continued. “I was in the circle that developed Alston’s Divine Circle of Teleportation back at the Royal Magic Academy. You know, the teleportation spell everyone uses? Yeah, I helped create it. But do you know what happened? The headmaster published it without us finalizing the equations! There are corner cases so nasty they make me shudder. I just take ships now. Way safer.”

Sigurd stared at her, half-amused and half-concerned. “Sounds like you’ve seen a lot.”

“Oh, you have no idea,” Ada huffed. “Even the private ventures are worse. They’re completely unregulated. Do you know how many houses I’ve seen burned down because someone decided to save a few gold on crafting components? It’s madness! And when I tried to submit my thesis about standardizing magical tools, do you know what they did? They laughed! Called me crazy and delirious. But tell me, is it crazy to suggest that maybe, just maybe, we should have some basic safety rules for enchanted artifacts?”

“Sounds reasonable to me,” Sigurd said, his smirk growing.

“Exactly!” Ada threw her hands up. “But nooo, ‘It’s tradition!’ they said. ‘We can’t regulate magic!’ Meanwhile, people are out there blowing themselves up with faulty staves and cursed amulets. It’s a miracle anyone survives magic school these days.”

Sigurd’s smirk turned into a chuckle. “Well, I’m glad you’re here to keep things interesting.”

Ada glared at him, though there was a hint of a smile tugging at her lips. “Interesting? I’ll show you interesting when I fix this mess.”
