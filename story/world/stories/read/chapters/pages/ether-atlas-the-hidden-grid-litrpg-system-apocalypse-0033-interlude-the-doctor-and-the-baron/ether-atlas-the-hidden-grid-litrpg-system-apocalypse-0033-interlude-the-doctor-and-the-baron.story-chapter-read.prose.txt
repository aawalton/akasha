Interlude

“The Grid is not a gift. To see the Grid is to lose the comfort of the lie.”  - Codex of the Long Watch, Founding Tenet

The power died at 16:07 when Sarah Vassilou was elbow-deep in a man's abdomen.

The man had been carried in with what Sarah's estimated was a perforated bowel and a lacerated liver, both caused by something that had punched through his midsection with a force and pattern that didn't match any trauma she'd seen in seven years of MSF field work. She'd seen machete wounds in South Sudan, propeller injuries from migrant boats in the Mediterranean. She'd never seen puncture marks arranged in a radial pattern like the points of a compass.

For a brief moment she saw some sort of writing, but she shook her head and concentrated again. It was probably stress.

The generators should have kicked in but they didn't. Mater Dei Hospital went dark, and the ventilator keeping the patient in Bay three alive stopped breathing for him.

The emergency torches were dead too and so were the phones "Open the windows –and someone find a lighter or something," she said and kept working. The same way she'd kept working in a surgical tent in Juba when the generators had failed during a mortar barrage. You didn't stop because the lights went out. You stopped when the patient died or you fixed them. Those were the two exits.

Then the man died. It was morbid to think of it as anti-climactic but one moment he was breathing under her hands and the next moment he wasn’t. It was the most sudden passing she had ever seen.

And twenty-five per cent of the hospital went with him.

Sarah counted the dead the way she'd been trained to count them in mass casualty events. You did it quickly, accurately and without pausing. Seventeen deaths in her ward alone. Spread among patients, staff, and visitors. There was no seeming pattern to it except that the blue lattice appeared on some and not others, and the ones without it were the ones who collapsed.

The first night she worked eleven hours straight. With no power, no running water after the first hour and no communication with anyone outside the building. The violet sky outside the windows provided enough light to work by during the day, and at night they used torches, candles and the faint blue glow that came from the lattice under the survivors' skin.

By dawn of the second day, she'd triaged every patient in her section, stabilised what she could with diminishing supplies, and accepted that the hospital was no longer a hospital. It was a building full of hurt people with no infrastructure to help them. And she had no idea what was happening, a few doctors had gone out to get help but hadn’t returned.

Then the creatures appeared in the car park.

She heard a scream and then the sound of something heavy hitting a car. She ran to the window and saw two creatures in the car park. They looked like dogs but weren’t and they were circling a man on the ground who was waving a tyre iron about. His blood was bright against the tarmac, reflecting the sky above.

Sarah was outside in a flash, her MSF training kicking in. When someone is bleeding, you go to them, you don't assess from the window.

The creatures scattered when she came through the doors and she thought belatedly that running in without support was foolish of her. Then to her relief she saw an orderly coming out behind her. The man’s arm was torn open from elbow to wrist, the brachial artery nicked and blood pulsed out in bright rhythmic jets.

She clamped the artery with her fingers putting direct pressure above the wound. She'd done this a few times to shrapnel wounds. She applied pressure to the wound while a voice in her head told her to: hold it, hold it, hold it.

And the Ether answered.

The response came to her mind’s urging. Energy moved from somewhere inside her chest, down through her arms, out through her fingers, and into the wound beneath her hands. The arterial jets of blood lost their urgency and the pressure dropped from lethal to manageable. The tissue under her fingertips warmed, changing in front of her eyes, the wound's edges drawing fractionally closer.

Inside ten minutes, the blood flow was down to a seep. In fifteen, the seep was a weep. Her ten years of medicine told her that what she was seeing, what she was doing was impossible. Tissue that should have needed weeks of careful recovery was condensing that timeline into hours.

Her hands came away trembling. She applied a pressure bandage with shaking fingers and walked inside on unsteady feet.

She had been noticing things that seemed impossible over the last two days. People talking about blue boxes and wounded healing quicker. She had noticed the blue lattice on her skin as well and her training was telling her to do it again and confirm the result.

So, she did. On the next patient. And the next. She set the bone of a compound fracture a nurse had suffered, held her hands on the splint for twenty minutes, and felt the bone begin to knit at a rate that should have taken weeks.

Sustained contact for forty-five minutes stabilised a case of internal bleeding. The damaged vessels repairing from inside.

Fifteen minutes of contact on a child with burns had the blistered skin calmed and the tissue begin the repair cycle.

Each time, the acceleration worked. And after each acceleration she had to pay a price. She felt hollowed out after each session, as though the energy came from a reservoir that refilled slowly and emptied fast. After two patients, she would start to shake. After five, she could barely stand.

But the results were visible. The nurse was walking with a limp by the next morning and the man from the car park was using his arm by evening. Word spread through the hospital and the surrounding area, patients who should have been bedridden for weeks were sitting up, eating, moving.

II.

The Baron's compound had been a warehouse complex before the sky changed. It was a fishing warehouse that had morphed into a guarded complex. Now there were men with weapons on the roof, and the fishing boats were pulled in tight against the dock.

Noel Zammit had not panicked on Day Zero. Panic was for people who needed the world to make sense before they could act. The Baron had spent twenty years operating in a world that didn't make sense. Customs officials who could be bought, coastguards who could be avoided, and partners who could be trusted exactly as far as the next deal. When the sky turned violet and the power died and people started dropping, the Baron assessed, planned, adapted, and gave orders.

Secure the compound. Account for the men left standing. Inventory supplies. Establish a perimeter. It was the same protocol he'd run during two police raids and a rival crew's attempted takeover of his dock. The context was new, but the response was not.

By the end of Day One, he had forty-seven people behind his walls. His crew of thirty-two men and women who'd been running Mediterranean contraband for him, plus fifteen civilians who'd found the compound and been allowed in because the Baron understood that controlled population was a resource. People who owed you for shelter worked harder than people you had to force.

The creatures arrived on the second night. There were four of them the same chitin-plated things that every survivor group on the island was learning to fear. They came over the south wall where the razor wire had been pulled down during a restoration.

The Baron's crew fought them with boat hooks, machetes, and a shotgun that Marco had kept in his cabin. Two creatures were killed. One of the Baron's men took a claw across his thigh that had opened the muscle to the bone. The other two creatures fled.

Blue text appeared in his vision and the Baron stared at it.

THREAT NEUTRALISED

ETHER ABSORBED: 52

INTEGRATION: 10 / 119

Attributes Available: 3

"Baron." Said Enzo. He was twenty-three, the youngest of the crew. He'd been staring at the notification with a bright smile and manic eyes. "Baron, this is a level-up system."

"A what?"

"Like a game. Elden Ring, Skyrim." Enzo was talking in a rush, the excitement pushing the words out before he could organise them. "You kill things, you get experience. Enough experience, you level up. You get stronger. The blue text is tracking it - Integration is XP. The number after the slash is the threshold. When we hit it, we level. Like we did just now."

The Baron looked at the dead creatures on the ground. Then he turned at Enzo and said "Mela, explain it again. Slowly."

Enzo explained. While Baron did not pay attention to all gaming terms he did understand the principle. And the principle was simple, violence produced power. Killing creatures made you stronger using these attribute points. The more you killed, the faster you grew. The blue boxes were not only tracking it, but they were also rewarding it.

The Baron walked to the edge of the dock and looked out across the Mediterranean. The island was small and the creatures were everywhere. And every other survivor group was hiding behind whatever walls they could find, waiting for rescue that wasn't coming.

"Enzo. The creatures out there. How many would you say?"

"A few dozen? Maybe more around the temples. The old sites are crawling with them, something about those locations draws them in."

"And every one is worth experience? This integration?"

"If we can kill them, yes."

The Baron was quiet for a long moment. He was running the same calculation he'd run a thousand times in the smuggling trade: risk, reward, investment, return. And this math told him that Enzo might have given him information that very few had access to. Afterall, you would have to kill one of these to get a blue box.

"So, while everyone else hides, we hunt. And every day the gap between us and them gets wider."

He turned to his crew, he had thirty-two fighters, weapons, walls, boats, and a young man who understood the system because he'd spent his adolescence preparing for the apocalypse without knowing it.

"Listen, we're going to the temples at first light. The creatures want to fight. So do we."

He smiled up at the sky. It was the smile of a man who'd just realised that the rules of the old world were gone and the world had just come round to his way of doing business."

He came to the hospital five days later with four of his crew, all of them moving with the confidence of men who'd been fighting daily and could feel the difference in their bodies. The Baron walked through the hospital's barricaded entrance with the easy authority of a man visiting a property he was considering acquiring.

He walked calmly through the hospital with a bleeding arm. There was a gash across his forearm wrapped in a strip of boat canvas that was soaked through. One of his crew had a similar wound across his shoulder. They'd been fighting near the temples. The creatures were getting larger and more aggressive, but the experience was more too, and the Baron had decided that acceptable casualties were part of the cost of accelerated growth.

Sarah was in the emergency bay when he appeared. She'd been working for fourteen hours. Three patients that morning and two the previous night, she'd lost one because the damage had been too extensive and her reserves had been near empty. She was running on the professional momentum that MSF had welded into her.

"Doctor." The Baron's spoke when she was done. His voice was pleasant. Warm, even. The Maltese-accented English had the practised smoothness of a man who'd done business across multiple countries. "I've heard you can do something remarkable."

Sarah looked at his arm and the blood. Her hands unwrapped and examined the wound on autopilot. “Sit down. The laceration's deep but the tendons are intact."

She put her hands on the wound and the ether moved with familiar warmth. The bleeding slowed and the wound edges drew closer. She held contact for ten minutes, feeling the tissue repair accelerate beneath her fingers. When she pulled away, the wound was half the size, and it would be fully sealed by morning. A week's recovery compressed into hours.

The Baron flexed his arm and tested the range. "How many people can you treat in a day?"

The question sounded clinical and curious and Sarah answered it the way she'd answered a hundred administrative questions during MSF rotations.

"Depends on severity. Minor wounds, I can accelerate maybe eight before I'm spent. Ten, fifteen minutes on each. Major trauma, two or three, and they need hours of sustained contact. I'm not performing miracles. I'm speeding up what the body is already doing. The patients still need rest, food, fluids. The healing is faster, not free."

The Baron nodded and looked around the emergency bay. There was no power, no water. Supplies dwindling. Six staff trying to serve forty patients in a building that was becoming less defensible by the day.

"Doctor, how long can you keep this up …..here? I’m sure you realise this is untenable”

She didn't have an answer. The hospital was dying. She’d stayed where the patients were and she’d been watching it die for four days. Treating the symptoms while the structure collapsed around her.

The Baron smiled and leaned over. The posture of a man presenting a business proposal over coffee.

"I have a compound on the south coast. It has walls, armed fighters and food supply for months. We've been clearing creatures around the temples and getting stronger every day, but my people get hurt in the field. A man lost a hand yesterday because the wound turned septic before we could close it." He paused and let the image settle. "You're running a hospital in a building with no power, no water, and hardly any defence against the creatures. I'm not asking you to stop what you're doing. I'm offering you a better place to do it."

"My patients -"

"Bring them. All of them. And your staff. And every piece of equipment you think is worth carrying. Beds, medicine, surgical tools, bandages, whatever you see fit."

"The way I see it, Doctor,” he continued after adjusting his position in the chair “You're treating the people of Malta from a building that can't protect them. I'm offering you one that can. You keep treating civilians and my people join the queue like everyone else. Your mission doesn't change just your location. The people would start showing up at the compound instead of here for treatment. I’ll spread the word."

The Baron spread his hands.

“And I help the people of Malta by helping you. It is a new world, Doctor; we have to stand together”

Sarah looked at his crew. They were armed and healthy and stood with confidence. The hospital was falling apart, and she knew she would have to abandon it sooner or later.

"This isn't a military arrangement. I'm a doctor. I treat whoever needs it most, in the order they need it. Your people don't jump the queue because they're yours or because you think I owe you a debt."

"Le, mela, le. I wouldn't dream of it." The Baron's expression was sincere. It was the most dangerous expression he owned. "Treatment is your department, Doctor. I know nothing about it and wouldn't presume to interfere with medical decisions."

To Sarah the math looked simple and convenient. Defensible walls plus medical supplies plus continued civilian access equalled more lives saved. When the field hospital is indefensible, you relocate to where the security is. This was just sound operational logic.

"When?" she said.

"As soon as possible, no reason to tempt fate, eh?” the Baron smiled. “Tomorrow morning. I’ll send men to help with the move."

The Baron stood and shook her hand. His grip was firm and dry and held hers for exactly the right amount of time. Then he turned and walked away.

Standing in a hospital that couldn't keep its patients alive, the Baron's offer was the most rational thing she'd heard since the sky turned violet.

She went to find the charge nurse. There was packing to do.
