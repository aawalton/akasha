Chapter 129

The sky over the road was the same iron it had been since dawn, only darker where it moved. The cloud had come down over the tops of the western trees so that the tallest trunks vanished into it and reappeared as the wind pushed the cover thin and then closed it again. The wind did not gust. It ran steady out of the north, cold along the shoulder of the road, cold on the back of Thorne's neck under the wet weight of his cloak. It bent the tops of the pines the same way each time. It pushed the rain sideways in long slow sheets that fell out of the sky at an angle and struck the road at a slant and ran off the crown of it in twin thin streams into the ruts on either side.

The rain smelt of cold earth and horse and, further off where the trees crowded thickest, of pine sap opened up by the wet. The road smelt of mud that had been turned over and turned over again by wheels and hooves through the morning. When the wind shifted it carried a thinner smell out of the west, of leaves rotted down through last year and the year before, and Thorne read it without thinking, the way he read every wind he rode into.

The horse's breath came up in a white curl and broke against Thorne's chest as he leaned forward. The hooves came down in a hard even four-beat that threw wet clumps of the road up behind him. The shovel he had tied to the saddle by the shaft shifted with the run, the head knocking softly against the leather of his pack at each stride, a dull metal note under the drumming of the rain on his hood.

He rode hard. The furthest lookout in the chain was better than an hour from the depot at a walk. He meant to be there in less than half of that.

The road climbed and dropped, climbed and dropped. On the tops he could see the wet green fall away to the north for a long stretch before the next rise cut the view. In the dips the road ran between banks of low brush and standing pools and the rain sound closed in around him.

He came over the last low rise before the lookout and saw the horse.

It stood at the base of the small hill where the road bent up over the crest, tied by a long lead to the trunk of a bush at the bottom. Its head was down against the rain. Its coat was dark with wet. It stood the way a horse stands when a hand has told it to and no one has come back to say otherwise. Thorne slowed as he came up on it. He drew rein a few paces short and dismounted quietly and tied his own horse to the same bush on the far side, so the two beasts had space between them.

He went up the hill on foot. He kept low as he climbed. The slope was not steep but the grass was slick and he set his boots down flat to keep from slipping. Halfway up he could already see where the lookout would be. The crest of the hill carried a thin line of bushes along its northern lip and a man crouched behind them would have a clean view of the road running north for a long measure before the road bent into the trees.

The bushes moved before Thorne reached them. The lookout came up on one knee with his hand already going for the bow at his side, and stopped when he saw Thorne's face. His shoulders came down. He let out a breath. The bow settled back beside him on the wet grass with the arrows in their loose sheaf next to it. He was young. His armour was the new plate the smith had turned out the week before, the buckles still bright where they had not yet had time to dull. His cheeks were red with cold. He smiled and gave Thorne a small nod as Thorne crouched down beside him at the lip of the bushes.

Thorne set a hand on his shoulder in return and did not speak for a moment. He looked out at what the lookout was looking at. The road ran north from the foot of the hill, straight for a good stretch, and then bent east into a stand of trees and was gone. Nothing moved on it. The rain sheeted across it. Two crows sat in the top of a tree a long bowshot down the verge and did nothing.

"From Riley," Thorne said.

The lookout turned his head. He did not speak. He waited.

Thorne kept his voice low.

"If you see anything more than a single wagon and two guards, you do not fight. You slow it. Any way you can that does not put you in front of them. A broken trace. A lame horse. A tree across the road. She wants minutes. Every one you can buy her."

The lookout's eyes moved once as he took it, and settled.

"A single wagon and two guards," Thorne said, "you let through. The moment you see anything more than that, you send word back down the chain, and then you do the work. Word first. Then the work."

"Word first," the lookout said. "Then the work."

The lookout stared down at the wet grass between his knees. He held there a beat. He was thinking it through, then he lifted his head and looked back out at the road.

The crows in the top of the tree came off the branch together. They came off all at once, four of them, not two, breaking upward in a hard flurry that carried them south along the verge, calling once and then falling silent. Thorne's eyes tracked them and went back to the tree they had left and then further, past the tree, along the road, to the place where the road bent east into the stand.

Something moved at the bend. Not clearly. A darkening in the grey of the rain where the road came out of the trees, the way a piece of the horizon thickens before a column comes up onto it. The lookout saw it in the same moment. His breath caught. His eyes widened. Thorne felt his own widen too.

Thorne turned to the lookout.

"Let's go."

He almost didn’t have to say it. With Thorne there they could work first, and if time was an issue then Thorne would carry the word and the lookout could do the work to slow whatever was coming.

They went down the hill together, fast and low. At the bottom Thorne cut his horse's shovel free of the saddle in one movement and the lookout pulled a second shovel from where it had been strapped along his own horse's flank, and they walked out onto the road side by side with the tools across their shoulders.

The wheel tracks were easy to find. Countless passes had cut them into the road in two clear parallel ruts, both a hand's width deep in places, both running in a narrow band that hardly wandered from one rise to the next. Wagons of the same width had come down this road again and again. What the first wheel had cut, every wheel after had cut deeper.

They chose their spot. A flat stretch a stone's throw north of the base of the hill, out of the sightline of anything coming around the bend, but before the road climbed again.

Thorne took the western rut. The lookout took the eastern. Thorne set the blade of his shovel down at the near edge of the rut and drove it in with his boot. The wet earth gave with a soft thick sound, half a suck and half a tear. He worked the shaft, lifted with his leg rather than his back the way he had learnt to lift, and threw the first spadeful clear of the road into the grass. The lookout was doing the same on his side of the road, and the two shovels made the same wet slap and lift, out of time at first and then falling into a rhythm on their own. The rain ran off the brim of Thorne's hood and dripped from the end of his nose and into the hole as he worked. His hands on the shaft were cold and did not slip.

The pot hole came out in a long shallow trough half an arm deep and about an arm across, cut into the rut on Thorne's side to the width of a wheel. He tested it once with the flat of the shovel to see the ground would not fall in on its own, and it held.

The lookout had his the same size a step behind Thorne's, on the opposite rut. He nodded when he looked up. Thorne nodded back.

They went to the verge and gathered armfuls of bush and dead tree leaves out of the wet undergrowth, and brought them back and dropped them into the holes. Loose, not packed. Enough to fill the space to the top and no more. Then they scraped a thin covering of mud back over the leaves with the sides of their shovels and smoothed it flat with the backs of the blades, until the surface of the covered hole read from a step away as an unbroken stretch of the rut.

Not packed tightly enough. Not built to bear a load. Only enough to look like road.

Thorne stood back. He looked at it.

"Move up," he said.

They walked a few strides further along the road toward the bend. There they set the second pot hole in the opposite rut, the eastern one this time, so that the two traps sat offset, a wheel-sinking hole on the right of the wagon, then a wheel-sinking hole on the left a few paces ahead. A wagon coming down at the road's normal pace would drop the near-side wheel first and the driver would haul on the reins to correct, and the far-side wheel would go a stride later.

They dug the second the same as the first. Boot to blade, lift with the leg, throw clear. The rain did not let up. The wind ran steady down the road at their backs and pushed the sheet of it across the worksite. Their breath came white. The lookout's plate was dark with wet at the shoulders where the rain had run down under his hood and past his collar. He did not slow.

They filled the second hole with leaf and bush. They covered it with the thin mud coat and smoothed it flat.

Thorne stood back a second time and read the road. From where they stood, both holes were invisible. He turned to the lookout.

"The very first sign of them in the distance," he said, "you go. Not the second sign. The first. You take your horse and you ride back to the next outlook in the chain and you pass the word.”

The lookout did not answer. He looked down the road at the bend where there had been movement. He looked back at Thorne. His jaw set. His eyes were steady on Thorne's, and there was nothing in them of the young man who had smiled up at Thorne from behind the bushes on the crest of the hill. It was the look of a soldier who had heard an order and made it his.

He nodded once. Thorne looked past his shoulder, back along the road. It ran south from where they stood in a straight line for a good measure, clean and open, and then dropped away into a stand of trees where the road bent and was hidden. A rider going south from this spot would be in the open only until the trees took him. Anything coming down from the north around the bend would not see him past that point. It was as good a run for a scout as this stretch of road offered.

Thorne clapped the lookout on the shoulder.

"For the glory."

"For the glory," the lookout said.

Thorne walked back to his horse. He untied the lead from the bush, gathered the reins, and set his foot in the stirrup and swung up. The horse threw its head against the rain and snorted, a hard wet sound out of both nostrils, and stamped once in the mud. Thorne turned it to the south.

He touched it into a gallop. The hooves came down heavy in the wet road and threw mud up in wide arcs behind him, dark clods that hung in the rain a moment and then fell back into the ruts. The sound of the run carried further than the sound of the rain, four beats close together against the road, and the shovel head knocked at the pack behind him in time with the stride until it settled and went quiet under the rhythm.

The pot holes took the rain and lay in wait.
