[Time]: 1978

The summoning circle was a mathematical miracle that had absolutely no right to exist on this side of the dimensional veil.

Lavinia Habsburg, Heir of a dynasty that had been quietly bleeding relevance for forty years, had expected slumming. She had crossed the dimensional threshold into a barren, low-magic backwater, a plane where the local mages chanted for half a minute just to produce a spark, and arrived wreathed in the traditional abyssal glamour: obsidian horns, a cloak that swallowed light at the edges, eyes burning an appropriate demonic coal. You wore the demon suit. You extracted the contract. You walked away. When the world eventually collapsed under the weight of its own desperation, the Abyss took the historical blame. It was a time-honored Witch tradition.

The spatial smoke cleared. She was standing in the center of a cavernous imperial throne room.

Beneath her boots, mapped in crushed silver and liquid mercury, recursive fractal matrices held the spatial tension of the dimensional tear with the razor-sharp precision of high-tier astromancy. Lavinia's scholarly instinct activated: the specific, itching helplessness her species experienced in the presence of an unanswered question. This world has a hidden inheritance, she reasoned, staring at the flawless geometry. Some Witch passed through here once and left fragments behind. No mortal could plot spatial tension with this degree of elegance.

"The translation of the spatial coordinates was tedious," said a voice from the far end of the hall. "The historical texts I acquired were severely decayed. I had to manually derive the missing planar variables to stabilize the gate."

Lavinia looked up. Sitting upon a throne of smelted iron, resting her chin against her knuckles with the unhurried quality of a sovereign who had been waiting at moderate interest for some time, was a girl. Dark-haired, pale, impossibly young even by mortal standards. A single beauty mark beneath her right eye. A face of geometric precision that did not belong in a universe governed by random biological chance. She wore the regalia of an empire as a skin she had long since grown bored of.

There were no fragments, and no inheritance.

The understanding arrived in a cold, dismantling wave. This mortal child had sifted through a thousand years of contradictory, superstitious historical garbage and reverse-engineered, from inference alone, a circle that read as the work of a peer.

The girl's eyes were golden and completely still. She looked like a mortal, she breathed like a mortal, but staring into those depths, Lavinia felt the unmistakable prickle of recognizing her own kind in entirely the wrong place.

A piece of heavy parchment drifted from the dais, gliding down to rest on the marble before Lavinia. She read it.

The definitions were hermetic. When mortals attempted to barter with the beyond, they inevitably committed the same errors: they offered their souls, a currency entirely useless to Witches and fit only for demons and devils, while vastly underestimating the worth of the dirt beneath their feet. No souls. No abstract devotion. What it offered was tangible: mineral veins, endemic flora, pristine geological samples, and exclusive harvesting rights to her empire's raw, breathing talent.

The girl clearly did not know the precise exchange rate of a miracle in the Inner Sea. So she had done the only sensible thing: identified the correct currencies, acknowledged the gap in her pricing, and erred loudly in the direction of generosity. There was no seam to weaponize.

Lavinia looked at the Emperor on the throne. "What is it you actually want?"

The girl's gaze shifted, slow and even. "I would like to know how to become one of you." A polite pause, the kind that implied several moves had already been thought through. "If you do not mind telling me your race's true name."

Lavinia went still. "How did you see through the glamour?"

The girl's head tilted fractionally. "Demons," she said, her tone conversational, "aren't necessarily as evil as you."

Lavinia swallowed a sudden, complicated knot of insulted pride and deeply uncomfortable validation. The script is entirely wrong.

Lavinia exhaled. The obsidian horns dissolved into the air. The cloak that had swallowed light fell away, and she stood in her immaculate Habsburg coat, a pureblood aristocrat of the Inner Sea, with the performance gone and nothing left to perform. "We are Witches."

"Witches," the girl repeated, tasting the syllables.

Lavinia flicked her wrist. Space warped, and a slender glass vial appeared in her palm, filled with viscous, shifting crimson, a slow inner pulse of light moving through it like something alive and impatient. She closed the distance in three steps, planted one hand on the armrest of the iron throne, and leaned down until the vial hung between them at eye level, trapping the girl against her own seat.

"This is Witch's Blood," Lavinia announced, her voice rising to its full theatrical cruelty. "Consume it, and it will attempt to rewrite your mortal architecture into something better. There is approximately a ten percent probability that it succeeds." Her smile sharpened into something structurally unhinged. "If it fails, your biology collapses. You dissolve into slime: living or dead, the fluid will remain highly active. You will have your immortality. We Witches have no use for the unlucky."

The girl looked at the vial and did not waver. One second passed.

"Is this how your recruitment ceremonies typically proceed?" she asked.

Lavinia clicked her tongue. "How else?"

The girl studied the crimson pulse in the glass. "A ten percent success rate. For a talent your people supposedly desire, that strikes me as an appallingly low yield." She lifted her gaze, cold and precise. "What is the price for a one hundred percent success rate?"

Lavinia's manic smile evaporated. "A barren, low-magic plane like this does not have the capital to afford that price."

The girl's brow furrowed, a flicker of genuine confusion crossing her perfect features. "I was under the impression that the most valuable thing in this world was me."

The air pressure dropped as Lavinia's mana surfaced involuntarily, a High Witch's weight pressing into the stone walls.

"Have you looked at the night sky?" Lavinia said, her voice dropping into the register she reserved for things that needed to be crushed. "However many stars burn up there, that is how many geniuses the Inner Sea holds. One to every world, from its first light to its last. A mortal standing on a dying rock: what makes you think you have the leverage to negotiate with me?"

The girl didn't blink, and something lit quietly in her golden eyes. "Ah," she said softly. "So your world is called the Inner Sea."

Lavinia's breath caught, and she straightened, taking one step back as a cold, slow understanding arrived in pieces. She replayed the last ninety seconds: The mortal had walked her through a prepared sequence of provocations, extracted the name of the Witch civilization from the most aggressive sentence Lavinia had produced, and looked entirely satisfied about it.

Lavinia, Heir of House Habsburg, closed her mouth and said nothing.

The transformation chamber tasted of ozone and ruptured physics.

Lavinia was running. The report had reached her terminal minutes ago. She threw the oak doors open and stopped.

The newly born Witch sat on the edge of a velvet chaise at the back of the room. Her complexion had shifted, taking on the translucent, porcelain quality that Witch physiology produced after conversion. The black had gone out of her hair entirely: what fell across her shoulders was silver, and her own mana rendered a faint gold wash through it wherever the light moved. Her long lashes cast shadows down her cheeks.

By every known metric of Witch biology, a convert should have been a tempest. She should have been testing the limits of her new mana pool, setting the curtains on fire, laughing at the sudden, godlike expansion of her senses. New Witches were, as a rule, a spectacular disaster for any enclosed space they occupied in the first hours.

Irene sat in absolute silence, staring down at her open, empty palm.

Her expression was a profound, immovable ennui: the specific, hollow exhaustion of a creature whose desires had been met too completely, a vessel filled so rapidly and with such overwhelming density that the very act of possessing had become a tedious chore.

Lavinia took one step into the room, and the mana density pressed against the walls and warped the ambient light at the edges of the space, bending it like heat over stone. She was standing in the presence of something barely an hour old, and she understood with the cold, unsparing certainty of someone staring at a leviathan: she had already been completely, irreversibly eclipsed.

Irene heard her footsteps and looked up, and the ennui dissolved in an instant. A soft, breathtaking smile spread across her face, and the beauty mark beneath her eye curved with it.

"Did you see what you hoped to see?" Irene asked.

Lavinia's knees yielded. She sank to the floor, her shoulders shaking, her throat tightening around something she did not have vocabulary for. She bowed her head.

"You are," Lavinia choked out, "even more beautiful than I imagined."

The emergency council of the Habsburg lineage convened in the grand vaulted chamber of their main estate before Irene's conversion had fully run its course.

The Habsburgs were old money: ancient, historically dominant, and nailed to a specific humiliation that forty years of prosperity had refused to dislodge. In the Ovelia War of 1939, when the assault finally came, they had opened the gates without casting a single spell. The lineage had spent forty years performing a delicate, miserable dance around that choice.

They needed a victory. They had needed one for a very long time.

The plan they had drafted for the anomaly Lavinia brought through the gate was generous: the Habsburg surname, official registration as a legitimate daughter of the main branch, the full resource network opened for cultivation, an Elder seat as the ceiling. It was a staggering arrangement for a convert. It drew one precise, carefully calculated line. Placing a converted mortal into the Matriarch's succession was an inversion of the natural order they were not desperate enough to accept.

Irene sat at the far end of the mahogany table and listened to the entire proposal in silence. Her expression was unreadable. When the Matriarch finished speaking, the room waited for gratitude.

Irene turned her head and looked at Lavinia. "Do you think," she asked, her voice quiet in the cavernous room, "that you are stronger than me?"

Lavinia stared at the girl who had been a mortal four hours ago. The weight in the air was the same weight that had dropped her to her knees in the transformation chamber.

Lavinia broke eye contact, looking down at the polished wood of the table. The silence that followed lasted three seconds, and the Habsburg succession was dismantled without a single additional word.

The Matriarch leaned forward, the velvet gloves gone. "What makes you think," she said, her voice dropping into something cold and finally serious, "that we need you?"

"Because I am still sitting here," Irene said, "and none of you have raised an objection."

The Matriarch stared at her as the room held its breath. Then she threw her head back and laughed: a sharp, barking sound that rattled the crystal decanters. She stopped and turned toward Lavinia, her expression gone cold.

"You can roll out," the Matriarch said, pointing a ringed finger at Irene. "The seat belongs to her now."

Lavinia stood up, her face drained of color, and stepped away from the table.

Irene did not move to take the vacated chair. She remained exactly where she was, resting her chin against her hand.

"How," she asked, "does an Heir become the Matriarch?"

The Matriarch's eyes gleamed with a dangerous, predatory amusement. "Are you in such a rush to sit at the head of the table already?"

"I was born a mortal," Irene replied, entirely unfazed by the killing intent bleeding into the room. "I do not know how immortals handle the transition of power."

"Other families shackle themselves with tedious metrics," the Matriarch said, leaning forward. "They weigh age, honor, academic record, diplomatic ties. The Habsburgs observe only one law. Winner takes all. The victor is crowned."

Her eyes locked onto Irene, cold and absolute. "Kill me, and this seat is naturally yours. Unfortunately, you do not currently possess the qualifications to try."

She laid out the conditions, and as each one settled, the clarity in Irene's golden eyes faded. The heavy, suffocating ennui returned, settling over her posture like a shroud.

The Matriarch watched her, highly amused. "Are you disappointed? That you cannot raise a hand against me today?"

"Repeating the same exact sequence twice," Irene said softly, "is simply a bit dull."

All the predatory amusement drained from the Matriarch's face. She studied the impossibly beautiful anomaly sitting at the end of her table for a long, silent moment, looking past the porcelain skin and the heavy lashes and the impossible youth, until she finally recognized what was sitting quietly in her chair: a towering thing that had simply run out of prey on its first world and arrived here still hungry.

When the Matriarch finally spoke, her eyes blazed. "Berenice," she said.

Irene exhaled a faint, questioning breath. "Hm?"

The Matriarch's expression ignited with the fanatical, trembling fire of a gambler who has looked at her hand and found the card that bankrupts the casino, the one she stopped believing existed forty years ago.

"The Bearer of Victory," the Matriarch declared.

Irene closed her eyes and tasted the syllables, let them settle.

"Irene Berenice Habsburg."

The beauty mark shifted as the corners of her mouth curved upward.

"I allow it."
