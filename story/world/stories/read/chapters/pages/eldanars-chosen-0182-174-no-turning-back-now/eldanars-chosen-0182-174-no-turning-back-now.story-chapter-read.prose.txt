The rhythmic grinding of iron-rimmed wagon wheels against the cracked cobblestones eventually gave way to the soft, wet squelch of the muddy frontier road. As the sun dragged itself above the horizon, painting the dew-kissed plains in pale shades of gold and violet, the true, agonizingly slow pace of the refugee caravan became apparent.

For the first few hours, the relief of having left the smouldering ruins of the town behind sustained the spirits of the travellers. The tension that had kept everyone’s shoulders wound tight began to bleed away, replaced by the mundane realities of a long journey.

At the front of the column, riding atop Geth’s incredibly overburdened wagon, a surprising scene of domesticity was unfolding.

Bhel, the usually boisterous and bloodthirsty dwarven fighter, had somehow found himself conscripted into the role of entertainer. Geth’s three children—two young boys and a little girl no older than six—had initially been terrified of the gruff, heavily armoured dwarf occupying their father’s bench. But Bhel, sensing their anxiety, had rummaged through his numerous pouches and produced a smooth, beautifully polished river stone he’d kept from their journey down Ashenfall’s river. An adventure that felt so very, very long ago.

"Now, you see this rock?" Bhel rumbled, his deep voice softened to a theatrical whisper. He held the stone out on his thick, calloused palm. "This isn't just any rock. This is a mountain-troll's lucky skipping stone. Stole it right out of his pocket while he was busy picking his nose, I did."

The children’s eyes widened, their fear momentarily eclipsed by sheer wonder. The youngest girl, bundled so thickly in furs she looked like a small, spherical bear, leaned forward precariously. "Did he chase you?" she whispered.

"Chase me?" Bhel let out a hearty, booming laugh, tossing the stone in the air and catching it deftly. "He tried! But dwarven legs are built for sprinting, little one. Now, watch closely. Don't blink, or the magic escapes."

With a surprisingly nimble flick of his wrist and a subtle, practically invisible application of his high dexterity, Bhel made the stone vanish from his palm, only to miraculously 'pull' it from behind the little girl’s ear a second later. The children erupted into a chorus of delighted giggles, demanding he do it again. Brett, sitting beside the dwarf, merely rolled his eyes, adjusting his pristine robes and muttering something under his breath about the utter degradation of mystical arts, though he didn't move away.

A few paces behind the wagon, Carcan was walking alongside the tailgate, deeply engaged in conversation with Geth’s wife. The woman, who had introduced herself as Elara, looked utterly exhausted. The dark circles under her eyes spoke of countless sleepless nights listening to the Kobold siege, and her hands, clutching a worn woollen shawl around her shoulders, trembled slightly with lingering adrenaline.

"It all happened so fast," Elara was saying, her voice a fragile, reedy thing. She kept glancing back at the long line of wagons behind them, as if terrified the horde might suddenly appear over the horizon. "One day Geth is complaining about a shortage of good flux for the forge, and the next... the sky is tearing open. We left so much behind. The heirlooms, the winter preserves. Everything."

"You brought the only things that truly matter, Elara," Carcan replied gently. Her voice carried an innate, soothing resonance, projecting a subtle aura of calm that seemed to visibly relax the anxious mother. "Metal can be re-forged, and pantries can be restocked. You have your husband, and your children are safe. The Light preserved your family. Hold onto that."

Elara offered a weak, watery smile, reaching out to briefly touch the fine silk of Carcan’s sleeve. "You adventurers... you're a different breed. I saw what you did at the gates. I don't know how you can sleep after seeing such horrors."

"Faith, mostly," Carcan answered, a hint of ancient weariness touching her own eyes. "And the knowledge that if we don't stand in the dark, the dark takes everything."

Josh was walking near the front-left wheel of the wagon, his boots sinking an inch into the muddy road with every step. The physical exertion of walking in full, heavy plate armour was nothing new to him; his endurance was well beyond that of a normal man. But the frustrating, plodding pace was beginning to grate on his nerves. They were moving at the speed of the slowest, fattest draft beast, stopping every mile to let an over-strained axle cool or a tangled harness be righted.

Geth, noticing Josh’s restless scanning of the horizon, leaned down from the driver's bench, the leather reins held loosely in one massive hand.

"You look like a hound waiting for a rabbit to bolt, Josh," Geth noted, his gravelly voice cutting through the creak of the wagon.

"Just worried about our exposure, Geth," Josh replied, looking up at the blacksmith. "It’s a long road. I’m used to travelling light and moving fast. When my party made the journey from Ashenfall, we did it in a single, hard day of marching. Leaving at dawn, arriving by evening."

Geth let out a low, rumbling chuckle, though there was no real humour in it. "Aye, a single day for a group of unencumbered, magically enhanced warriors at the peak of their physical fitness. But you aren't leading an adventuring party anymore, Josh. You’re escorting a moving village."

Geth gestured with his whip towards the mile-long procession of groaning carts, limping oxen, and exhausted civilians stretching out behind them. "These beasts are pulling literal tons of steel, timber, and personal belongings. We have the elderly, the sick, and toddlers who need to rest. We can can't push the animals too hard, or they'll drop dead in the traces, and then we'll be truly stranded."

Josh frowned, adjusting the strap of his shield. "So, what's our realistic timeline to reach the town's outer gates?"

"If the weather holds, and the axles don't snap?" Geth narrowed his eyes, doing the mental arithmetic he had likely done a hundred times that morning. "We are hoping that we'll see the outer spires of Ashenfall by the late afternoon of the third day."

Josh physically winced. Three days.

Three full days crawling across the open plains and winding through the forested valleys. Three days of being a massive, noisy, incredibly vulnerable target. While the main Kobold horde had been broken at the portal, Josh knew from bitter experience that smaller splinter groups, opportunistic goblin raiding parties, and desperate bandit crews always prowled the wake of a major disaster, looking for easy pickings. And nothing was easier picking than a slow-moving refugee caravan.

"Three days," Josh muttered, rubbing his gauntlet over his chin. "That changes the logistics significantly. We don't have the manpower to maintain a solid defensive perimeter for that long without fatigue setting in."

"I know it's not ideal," Geth said softly, his jovial demeanour fading into grim realism. "But it's the hand the gods dealt us. We are relying on you, Josh. All of us."

"I'll get you there," Josh promised, his voice resolute. He clapped a hand against the thick timber of the wagon bed. "Keep the pace steady, Geth."

Josh broke away from the wagon, slowing his pace slightly to let the heavy vehicle pull ahead. He navigated through a cluster of straggling pedestrians and made his way towards the right flank of the caravan, where the sprawling, dew-covered plains met the encroaching edge of a dense, ancient pine forest.

Walking parallel to the tree line, about thirty yards out from the caravan, was Perberos. His fluid, haunting grace made a mockery of the muddy terrain. While everyone else squelched and stumbled, Perberos seemed to glide over the ground, his eyes locked entirely on the dark spaces between the towering pine trunks.

As Josh approached, the elf didn't turn his head, but his long, sharply pointed ears twitched backwards, acknowledging the Vanguard's heavy, metallic approach.

"See anything of concern?" Josh said quietly, falling into step beside his scout.

"The woods are holding their breath," Perberos murmured, his voice as smooth as polished glass. He kept his bow in his left hand, an arrow resting lightly against the wood, though not yet drawn. "I haven't seen any tracks. I haven't heard the snap of a twig or the cry of a startled bird. But the mana in the air feels uneasy. Something is watching the road, Josh. I can feel it on the back of my neck."

Josh trusted Perberos's instincts above anyone else's. If the elf felt uneasy, there was a reason for it. The forest to their east was a vast, untamed expanse that stretched for hundreds of miles, a perfect staging ground for an army, let alone an ambush.

"We're moving too slowly. If there's an ambush waiting for us further down the road, this caravan won't survive the initial volley," Josh said, his mind racing through tactical scenarios. "I need you to range ahead. Get off the road, slip into the treeline, and scout our path. I need to know what's waiting for us in the next valley before the caravan crests the ridge."

A rare, genuinely eager smile touched the corner of Perberos's lips. The slow, noisy pace of the caravan had clearly been torturing his wild sensibilities.

"With great joy," the elf replied, his eyes gleaming.

"Don't get too far ahead," Josh cautioned, raising a stern finger. "Stay within a mile of the lead cart. If you engage, fall back immediately. You are our eyes, not our executioner. Understood?"

"Like a ghost in the morning mist, my friend," Perberos promised. Without another word, the elf pivoted sharply. He took three running strides towards the forest and leapt effortlessly over a tangled briar patch, soon vanishing into the dense, dark green undergrowth so completely it was as if the forest had simply swallowed him whole.

Josh watched the spot where his scout had disappeared for a long moment, feeling a slight knot of tension loosen in his chest. With Perberos ranging ahead, they wouldn't be caught entirely blind. He turned back to the tedious, muddy march of the caravan, preparing himself for a long day.

Miraculously, the hours passed without a single incident, and before they knew it they’d made it through the first day. The sun eventually sank below the horizon, bleeding a brilliant, bruised purple across the sky before plunging the plains into a freezing, star-speckled darkness.

When the call to halt was finally sounded, the true logistical nightmare of managing the caravan began. It took over an hour of shouting, swearing, and frantic manoeuvring to pull the dozens of wagons into a massive, interlocking circle—a defensive laager. Draft beasts were unhitched, watered, and corralled into the centre of the protective ring of timber and iron, whilst the refugees began the arduous process of setting up a temporary camp.

Despite the bitter cold and the exhaustion that hung in the air like a physical weight, the atmosphere within the wagon circle was sombre but undeniably relieved. They had survived day one. Several large, roaring campfires were quickly established in the centre of the camp, casting warm, dancing orange light against the faces of the travellers. The comforting, universal smell of woodsmoke soon mixed with the scent of boiling root-vegetable stew and cheap, salted travel rations. People huddled together beneath shared blankets, speaking in hushed, exhausted whispers.

Josh didn't have time to rest. Once his own party had secured their bedrolls near Geth’s wagon, he grabbed his helmet, tucked it under his arm, and marched towards the largest of the central fires.

He had sent word through the camp, calling for a mandatory meeting of the adventurers. As he arrived at the fire, he found them gathered. The same broken, haunted veterans who had stood near the town gates that morning. In the flickering firelight, they looked even worse. Their eyes were hollow, their postures slumped, and the silence between them was thick with shared, unspoken trauma.

"Right, gather round," Josh said, pitching his voice to be authoritative but calm—he hoped. He stepped up to the edge of the firelight. "We survived the first day. The terrain was flat, the visibility was good. But tomorrow we’ll enter the wooded valleys, and the risk of ambush gets a lot higher. We need to organise a solid, reliable watch rota for the night. I want three-man shifts, two hours a piece, patrolling the outer perimeter of the wagons."

A heavy, oppressive silence followed his words. The young spellcaster just stared blankly into the flames.

Suddenly, a man near the back of the group pushed his way to the front. He was a tall, lean fighter, his leather armour slashed to ribbons and a long sword resting lazily on his shoulder. He was missing his scabbard, and his left cheek bore a fresh, ugly burn scar. His eyes, however, were not vacant; they were bright, angry, and distinctly defensive.

"And who exactly made you Guildmaster?" the man sneered, his voice dripping with a bitter, exhausted snark. He spat a wad of phlegm into the dirt near Josh's boots. "You march over here in your shiny, un-dented plate armour and start barking orders like we're your personal militia. We just survived a bloodbath, mate. We're tired. Who the hell put you in charge?"

Josh felt an immediate, white-hot spike of anger boil up in his chest. He wanted to close the distance, grab the man by his torn lapels, and remind him exactly why his plate armour was intact. He wanted to scream at them for being so foolish as to think a fire and a ring of carts meant they were safe.

He took a slow, deep breath, forcing the anger down into a cold, tight box in the back of his mind. He relaxed his shoulders and met the man's angry stare with complete, unwavering calm.

"Nobody made me Guildmaster," Josh replied, his voice deadly quiet and perfectly level. "And I'm not trying to be your commanding officer. What's your name?"

"Kaelen," the man spat, gripping the hilt of his sword a fraction tighter.

"Right, Kaelen," Josh said, spreading his hands in a gesture of open diplomacy. "You're tired. I get it. We are all exhausted. So, I'm open to suggestions. You don't like my plan? Fine. What would you like to do? How do you suggest we secure the perimeter of a camp containing more than a hundred sleeping civilians in unknown territory?"

Kaelen opened his mouth to snap a retort, but the words died in his throat. He looked around at the other adventurers, looking for support, but they just stared at the ground. He looked back at Josh, his bravado suddenly evaporating, leaving only the terrified, broken man underneath.

"I... I don't know," Kaelen finally mumbled, lowering his sword so the tip rested in the mud. "I just... I can't stay awake. But I can’t sleep. Every time I close my eyes, I see those little scaled bastards crawling over the barricades. I see them dragging Marcus down. I can't go out into the dark again tonight. I just can't."

The fight completely left Josh. He looked at Kaelen, and then at the trembling spellcaster, and the rogue pair holding each other by the fire. He realised, with a sinking feeling, that Brett had been absolutely right. These people were entirely combat ineffective. Forcing them onto the perimeter would just result in them falling asleep at their posts, or worse, panicking and fleeing at the first sign of a shadow.

"Okay," Josh said, his voice much softer now. "Okay, Kaelen. Sit down. Get some food in you. Rest."

Josh spent the next twenty minutes painstakingly coaxing a fragile, incredibly bare-bones rota out of the group. He managed to find four individuals who were lucid enough to stay awake, though he had to pair them up just to ensure they didn't wander off. The rest he dismissed, telling them to sleep near the centre of the camp.

Leaving the fire, Josh walked back through the maze of wagons, the cold night air biting at his exposed face. He found his party huddled around a much smaller, discreet fire near Geth’s wagon. Brett was nursing a cup of tea, Bhel was sharpening an axe with a whetstone, Carcan was humming softly, and Perberos had returned from his scouting, sitting silently on the edge of the light.

"Well?" Brett asked, raising an elegant eyebrow as Josh approached. "How went the grand council of the broken?"

Josh sat down heavily on a wooden crate, resting his elbows on his knees and running his hands through his hair. "It's worse than we thought. Half of them are broken. The other half are too exhausted to stand. I managed to scrape together a few pairs to watch the southern and western arcs, but I wouldn't trust them to spot a troll if it was dancing naked in the firelight."

"So, the burden falls to us," Perberos stated, his voice devoid of judgment, merely stating a fact.

"I'm afraid so," Josh said, looking around at his party. "I don't trust the perimeter. Not tonight. I need us to cover the gaps. It means double duty. We'll be operating on half the sleep we need."

"Ha!" Bhel barked, slamming his whetstone down. "Sleep is for the weak and the dead! I'll take the first four hours. Let those snivelling cowards rest. A dwarf's eyes are sharper in the dark anyway."

"I will provide enchantments and traps," Brett sighed, looking profoundly put out, though there was no hesitation in his agreement. "Though I expect my heroic sacrifice to be noted by a means of letting me sleep through the night?"

"Nice try, Brett. I will take the first watch with you, Bhel," Carcan offered gently. "I have stimulant potions if we begin to flag."

Josh offered them a tired, deeply grateful smile. "Thank you. Perberos, you and I will take the second to dawn shift. That's when the camp is deadest, and when an ambush is most likely. Brett, you can join Carcan and Bhel."

"I will take the treeline," Perberos nodded, his eyes already drifting towards the impenetrable darkness beyond the wagon circle. "You hold the road."

"Get some sleep whilst you can," Josh commanded quietly, unbuckling his heavy breastplate. "It's going to be a very, very long night."

As Josh lay his head down on his rough woollen bedroll, he listened to the wind howling across the plains, rattling the tarpaulins of the wagons. He closed his eyes, but his mind remained alert, listening for the snap of a twig, the growl of a beast, or the scream of a dying sentry.

The road to Ashenfall was a long one, and the shadows were deep.
