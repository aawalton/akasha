Vir was elated to hear that Maiya’s grand speech had been a success, though he hadn't once doubted the outcome. She had always been great at addressing the public. More comfortable in the limelight than he’d ever be. Ever since they were little, she’d dreamed of fame, and Vir could think of no human more famous in any realm than her.

For his part, while he was no stranger to speeches and public appearances, he had always detested it, always doing so with great reluctance, and despite his best efforts, he often did more harm than good.

Vir had learned that lesson the hard way, and that had been plenty.

When Maiya spoke, people were reassured. They saw her as a leader. Untouchable, yes, but still someone like them. Someone they could relate to.

When Vir spoke, people treated every word like divine scripture, and the last time he’d spoken in public, it had taken two years to quell the Aindri faction that had misinterpreted his words, inciting a secret rebellion he’d only just barely managed to stop.

He was the Akh Nara, and no amount of joviality on his part changed how demons received his words. In fact, humor only seemed to set them on edge.

Gods didn’t laugh, after all. Well, not unless they were Badrak, anyway.

Every casual remark affected one policy or another and every plea of his seemed to spawn a new subsect of worshippers. At this point, he would honestly be surprised to find a demon who didn’t worship him, even if they wouldn’t openly admit it.

And while Saunak’s broadcast system existed in the demon lands, Vir had simply never been comfortable using it, and today was not the day to start.

So while Maiya stood in front of her crystal array, addressing millions, Vir sat at the head of the long table in Samar Patag’s grand hall and let the Rajas carry the news to their own people in their own words.

Cirayus would have told him this reclusion only amplified his deification, and Vir agreed, but he didn’t have it in him to save the world and also be the perfect leader.

Everyone had their limits. Even him. Especially him.

Siya sat beside him, her back as ramrod straight as always, contentedly watching the assembled faces with eyes far too aware for her tender age.

Tara had come in her half-naga form, which Siya found endlessly fascinating. She’d even gotten the hapless Rajni to promise to let her examine her tail in great detail later.

“Is it true?” Tara asked, voicing what was undoubtedly on the tip of every Raja’s tongue. “Are we truly free of it?”

“The Ash’s encroachment has been halted and the Tears will no longer bother us, yes,” Vir said, confirming her hopes.

She held his gaze for a long moment before slumping into her chair in a most un-Rajni-like fashion, letting out an enormous sigh.

“Thank the gods,” she said, glancing at Vir. “Or, you, but I suppose there’s hardly a difference at this point.”

“And before you deny it,” she held up her hand, forestalling Vir’s response. “I’d say any differences are moot at this point. You collapsed three realms into one and lived to tell the tale. Good luck convincing anyone, even me, that you’re not what everyone claims you are.”

Vir looked to the other Rajas for support, but despite their silence, had their thoughts written all over their faces.

It seemed nobody was on his side this time.

Thankfully, every Raja was in attendance for this meeting.

Thaman of clan Baira was in a chair that looked comically undersized, despite being fully double the width and depth of any regular chair.

Matiman, crowned Raja of the Chitran several years prior, sat to his left. Sagun’Ra of the Iksana was there too, perched atop a bookshelf in the corner of all places. Still, the reclusive Raja’s presence spoke volumes of the gravity of the situation.

At the far end sat Disanna of the Pagan Order, sent in place of Lord Reth and the ghael Zora, which Vir felt was a wise decision on their part. As a human, Reth would have put the demon Rajas on edge, whereas Ghaels like the Iksana never hesitated to demonstrate at every opportunity that they were terrible social creatures.

Disanna, however, was a red demon and a pragmatist, which made her a good choice for a congregation such as this.

“Before we begin,” Vir announced, “I’d like to introduce someone.” He glanced at Siya. “This is Janak’s daughter Siya. And before you ask, yes, she is that Siya, after which the Altani city is named.”

He had an odd sense of déjà vu, as though he’d said these exact words to the same people in the past, but quickly dismissed the sensation. That would have been impossible.

The table went predictably quiet at his declaration. Worse, every Raja and Rajni who hadn’t seen Siya before froze up as though they were schoolchildren who’d just been informed of a surprise exam.

Thaman nodded so vigorously, his head nearly touched the table, while Matiman’s tail stood on end.

Only Sagun’Ra had no visible reaction, instead staring at the girl with his unblinking eyes as he loved to do.

To her credit, Siya met their gazes without flinching.

“With that out of the way,” Vir said, “let’s get down to why I called you all here.”

The conversation that followed was remarkably constructive by any standard of Raja gatherings.

They talked through the implications of the new world order and generally agreed on how to navigate the times ahead, even agreeing to meet regularly to appraise one another of developments in their territories.

“There is a great deal of unclaimed territory now,” Thaman pointed out. “Between the eastern demon lands and the western human territories, who decides how it’s divided?”

“There will be a negotiation process with the humans,” Vir said. “A fair process with equal representation from both peoples and from all countries and clans. The land will be divided equitably with oversight from myself and Maiya.” He waited for any comments, but the Rajas remained quiet. “My preference is that it not be divided at all,” he continued. “That humans and demons settle it together rather than drawing new lines between themselves.”

The reaction around the table was mixed, which he’d fully expected.

“You speak words of wisdom, Akh Nara, yet after millennia of separation and rumor-mongering,” Matiman said carefully, “I suspect it will be a difficult task. Even with the progress of recent years.”

“It will,” Vir agreed. He looked down the table at Disanna. “But when has anything worth taking ever been easy? It is why I’m asking the Pagan Order to take a lead role in facilitating cooperation between our peoples.”

Disanna said nothing for a long moment. “The Order’s mandate has always been to carve out a place for demonkind.”

“Then this falls squarely within it, yes?” Vir said.

He knew well that the Pagan Order had no love for humans. That they’d planned to stage an uprising that would have slaughtered thousands—and most likely gotten the Order annihilated in the process.

The logic was simple, as Vir’s favorite plans tended to be.

By handing them the responsibility of ensuring cooperation between human and demonkind, he both gave them prestige and made it nigh upon impossible for them to lash out at humans. They now had a vested interest in greasing the negotiations between the two species, after all.

Disanna, of course, understood that as well as he did.

“The Order accepts,” she said with a wry smile, knowing full well it was hardly a choice. Despite everything, the opportunity was far too great for the Order to pass up, and they both knew it.

The conversation then shifted to more practical matters, such as when each Raja would address their people, what venue to use, when and where the follow-up gathering would be held, and so on.

Details that in any prior meeting would have generated hours of argument resolved themselves in minutes without Vir even having to intervene.

He simply sat and listened, contributing sparingly. Mostly to remind the Rajas to communicate to their people what exactly to expect with the impending ‘nighttime’. Daylight, despite its differences, was similar enough to the perpetual sunset that had plagued the realm since the Fall.

Night, however, was something else entirely. Explaining to the Rajas that demons would be unable to see, that people would need to rely on fires and magical lighting to navigate, just as the Iksana did in their deep caverns, took some doing.

To say nothing of outfitting homes with lights. While most buildings had some, none were designed for total darkness. That was a project that would take years on its own.

Vir ensured the point was properly conveyed. The last thing they needed were people hurting themselves in panic come nightfall.

Even still, the joy in the room was thinly veiled and easily noticed, and Vir hoped for more such occasions in the future. Disagreements dissolved into thin air, and Rajas set aside petty grievances to act in the benefit of the realm, as Vir had always hoped they would. Gone was the usual jockeying for favors and benefits to their individual clans.

As things were winding down, Vir felt a tug at his sleeve.

He looked down to find that Siya, despite displaying more patience than any child he’d even known, had reached her limits.

Vir smiled, an inexplicable  warmth blossoming within his chest at her strained expression. “Yes, Siya. I apologize.”

Not one to waste an opportunity, Vir pushed his chair back and announced his departure. “It would seem our resident goddess wishes to leave.”

“Now,” he said, turning to Siya. “How about I showed you around our little city?”

The look of joy on her face made Vir’s heart melt.
