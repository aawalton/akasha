[Time]: Autumn Semester, Day 63 — 6:40 AM[Location]: Yggdrasil Academy · Dormitory [Golden Bough] · Room 302

The last node went down at twenty to seven, and Hathaway sat back in a chair that had held her since the previous afternoon and looked at what she had built.

Every frantic Ludwig workaround had come off the sheet line by line: the pressure-relief lattices, the staged venting arrays, the beautiful redundant scaffolding that three generations had bolted onto a matrix that had never once asked for it. Under all of it, unchanged since the day Ovelia handed the thing down to a house that had knelt to her, was the original. The architecture that came out from under them was so clean it felt like a rebuke.

She copied the file, attached it, and sent it to the main house.

[Ctrl+S]: Model complete. I need a Master Alchemist for the draught and a range rated for apocalyptic yield.

She had crossed the room and was reaching for the kettle when the terminal went from chiming to ringing.

Hathaway opened the channel. Behind Wilhelmina's voice, somewhere in the Palace of the Eternal True Sun, a crucible was running hot enough to be audible.

"Niece." Not the usual sun-drenched boom. Flat, clipped, the voice this house kept for a mistake it had already lost somebody to. "Your containment envelope is the finest piece of work anyone in this family has produced in forty years, and I am going to have to ask you about the third page. You have inverted a node in the ignition sequence. If you push mana through that orientation, the backdraft will come out through your spine."

"It isn't inverted," Hathaway said.

"Hathaway."

"The book is wrong, Aunt Wilhelmina."

The crucible roared into a silence that had gone somewhere very cold.

"That volume," Wilhelmina said, in a tone Hathaway had previously only heard aimed at Wellingtons, "was transcribed from Ovelia's own hand."

"I know. She wrote it down wrong." Hathaway looked at the ceiling. "She built the matrix, she slipped a pen on one node, and then she never bothered to correct it, because she assumed anyone competent enough to cast it would catch it on the way past."

Something heavy hit a stone floor at the far end of the connection and broke into pieces.

When Wilhelmina spoke again the aristocracy had come off her voice entirely, and what was underneath it was raw.

"How could you possibly know that."

"The Fifth Seat told me," Hathaway said. "Personally. She poured the tea herself."

The line did not make a sound for four seconds.

"Your meaning," Wilhelmina said, and the words came out unsteady, "is that you..."

"Yes." Hathaway kept her voice flat and professional, because it was the only setting she trusted. "I know how to cast the original."

"Do not move." The command came down like a portcullis. "Do not leave that room."

The connection died.

One hundred and eighty seconds later the morning went dark, and Hathaway looked up from her boots to find a six-griffin armored carriage in beast-bone black, the golden lion of her house roaring across the door, descending into the Golden Bough courtyard at an angle no traffic authority would have signed off on, ignoring the designated pads entirely, the downdraft folding two third-years into a hedge.

Hathaway opened the window and climbed out into it.

The carriage banked hard enough to put her into the opposite cushions, and she lay there among the velvet doing the one piece of administrative work standing between her and the rest of her life.

It was Sunday. Lab 606 opened at nine.

[Ctrl+S]: Professor. A Tier-8 family matrix has become available for live activation this morning under supervised conditions. I am requesting leave from today's shift. I understand this is the second occurrence and I accept whatever schedule adjustment you consider appropriate.

The reply came back before she had finished feeling noble about it.

[Response Window: 22:00–22:15]: Approved.[Response Window: 22:00–22:15]: Eleven hours. Distributed.

The sky came apart ahead of the griffins, and Hathaway found that she was, unhelpfully, very fond of that woman.

[Time]: Autumn Semester, Day 63 — 7:20 AM[Location]: The Palace of the Eternal True Sun · Ancestral Trial Chamber

Wilhelmina crossed forty meters of polished marble in a cloth-of-sun mantle out of a case that had not been opened since 1963, two attendants still jogging to keep the train off the floor, and hit her like weather, and Hathaway distinctly heard two of her own vertebrae relocate.

"The draught is in final reduction," the Matriarch said into her hair, not letting go. "Frederica has been on the burner since your model came through and has not blinked once. Niece, before you drink it we condition the vessel. We do the rite."

"The rite," Hathaway said, into a mouthful of cloth-of-sun.

"Holding the Hearth." Wilhelmina put her at arm's length and steered her by both shoulders down a corridor hung with oil portraits of women who had all held it. "The yield comes back through every bone you own. We calibrate the skeleton to accept it, or the skeleton decides for you."

The chamber at the end of the corridor was basalt, circular, and stripped of everything. In the center, on a plain pedestal, sat a sphere of star-iron and brass about the size of a melon.

"Lift it," Wilhelmina said. "Against the sternum. Both arms crossed over."

The thing came off the pedestal like it resented being moved. Hathaway locked it against her chest and found the metal was already faintly warm.

"Inside is a kinetic core. I will trigger it, and the yield escalates by stage." The Matriarch's voice dropped into something clinical. "Two rules, and they are the only two. You do not let go. And you do not vent."

"Define vent."

"Anything that moves the force off you. A buffer, a brace, a knee bent to put it into the floor." A pause. "Your grandmother took four stages standing on one leg. Do not copy it. It has been done, and a thing done twice is not a statement."

"...Is there a list."

"There is a wall." The Matriarch's chin came up half an inch. "Bella held six through a monologue of her own composition and did not drop a syllable. Rhode took a call from a lover partway through hers and flirted the rest of the way. The woman on the other end never heard a thing. What has not been done is yours to find."

I am going to stand here, Hathaway decided, with both arms around it, like a person.

Hathaway looked down at the seamless iron pressed over her heart and let her threat arrays finish their sweep.

"Aunt Wilhelmina. I have a question."

"Put your heart entirely at ease." Wilhelmina waved a hand, radiant with reassurance. "We hold extensive empirical data. Worst case, absolute worst case, you are looking at catastrophic internal hemorrhage and full-body comminuted fracture. The agony is genuinely indescribable, but it is purely mechanical damage. You will be bouncing around the courtyard before lunch."

Before lunch.

Hathaway set her boots against the basalt, adjusted her grip on the family's favorite instrument of instruction, and accepted her sentence with the solemn dignity of a woman climbing the scaffold in front of relatives who had all done it before her and enjoyed it.

"Begin."

The first stage was a dull thud that arrived through the iron with no sound attached to it at all, and drove every cubic centimeter of air out of her in one push.

The second put her boots back half an inch across polished stone and filled her mouth with hot copper.

On the third, something in the left side of her ribcage gave with a wet, structural crack, and her entire nervous system stood up at once and screamed at her, in a single unanimous voice, to open her arms and throw the thing across the room.

She did not open her arms.

Take it in, she told herself, arms locked, jaw locked, forcing her mana down into the marrow instead of out through the skin. Nothing leaves. Nothing leaves.

And somewhere in the white static behind her eyes, her developer brain, which had never in two lives known when to stop working, finished a calculation she had not authorized.

It teaches you to hold. It says nothing about the heat, nothing about the door, nothing about who you are holding it open for.

The fourth stage came through the iron and took her hearing with it. It's the reason nobody in this family has ever solved it. They passed this, and then they walked out onto a range and vented.

The fifth stage arrived, and Hathaway went down onto the basalt with her arms still locked around it.

She was flat on the stone and entirely awake when Frederica knelt over her, pried her jaw open with a gauntlet, and poured something thick and green into her at a rate that suggested the Elder considered choking a design feature.

"Swallow."

Hathaway swallowed, gagged, and felt the knitting that had already started in her sternum go from a crawl to a hot wire-drawn rush that was somehow worse than the breaking.

"She held five." Frederica said it to the ceiling, and the phoenix on her shoulder threw off a spray of white sparks. "Wilhelmina. She held five and she never bent a knee."

Wilhelmina crouched, both palms glowing, and pushed high-tier restoration straight into the wreckage of her niece's chest.

"Of course she did," the Matriarch said.

Then a crystal flask was in Hathaway's hand, and the thing inside it moved like sunlight that had been persuaded to sit down.

She tipped her head back and took the whole of it in one unbroken swallow.

It tasted of caramel: warm, sweet, and generous, spreading through her chest like something being forgiven. Underneath it, arriving only after the sweetness had gone, was a faint thread of bitterness that did not announce itself and did not leave.

She was still turning that over when the draught reached her stomach and came apart.

Tens of millions of neurons in one small quadrant of her cerebral cortex, mapped and reserved and waiting since the day she was born, lit up in sequence. The matrix she had spent three months arguing with slotted into place as an organ, fully compiled, load-bearing, and hers.

[Time]: Autumn Semester, Day 63 — 9:30 AM[Location]: Blazing Day Plains · The Sun Volcano

Hathaway walked to the casting mark with the wind pulling at her coat.

The crown asset of this house, her inner producer observed, demoted to a firing range. By me. Personally.

She turned around and stopped breathing.

They had all come.

Wilhelmina stood at the center of the observation ridge with her scepter planted and her eyes shut. Frederica beside her in militarized formal plate, phoenix on her shoulder, thousand-yard stare aimed straight down the caldera. Rhode leaned on a shattered pillar. Sunglasses on, one boot crossed over the other. Bella stood at the very lip of the rock, cloak snapping, her single uncovered eye fixed and enormous.

Behind them the ridge kept going. The three Elders whose names nobody had ever offered her, halos locked and burning. Rows of women she had never met, in the scarlet and the gold, who had cleared a Sunday morning on ninety minutes' notice to come and stand on a hot rock and watch.

And at the end of the line, Margaret with her fingers laced through Anna's, and Anna with Rory riding her hip, one small hand fisted in her collar.

Hathaway measured the distance and went cold.

One hundred and fifty meters.

Nineteen forty-seven had managed two thousand two hundred. Nineteen sixty-three, nineteen hundred. Bella, the best of the young generation, fifteen hundred. Her grandmother, the ceiling of everything this house had ever recorded, nine hundred, and dead on the attempt that was supposed to break eight.

Her family had walked six times closer than her grandmother ever had, put the baby on a hip, and not one of them had thought to ask whether she could hold the door.

Margaret mouthed something that Hathaway could read perfectly at that distance. Anna gave her the single nod she used to approve a procurement report. Rhode raised two fingers off the pillar in a salute so lazy it was almost an insult.

She turned her back on all of them, because if she looked any longer she was going to lose the thread.

She emptied her lungs. She brought the matrix up whole. She traced every somatic component in full, feeding the mana through a sternum that had been broken and rebuilt ninety minutes ago, laying the ignition sequence down in the order Ovelia had built it and never bothered to write down correctly.

The last node seated. The matrix closed.

No light. No heat. No pressure. The wind went on moving across the crystal, and the sky stayed the ordinary grey of an autumn morning in the mountains.

On the ridge, the Ludwigs moved.

Every woman standing up there had cast this spell or watched it cast, and every one of them knew that the interval between closure and payload was not measured in seconds, because there had never been an interval.

Frederica stepped forward with her mouth already opening.

Wilhelmina's hand came off her scepter and clamped over the Elder's mouth hard enough to rock her head back, and the Matriarch of the Eternal True Sun held her sister-in-arms in place with one arm and did not open her eyes.

At the lip of the rock, Bella took a step, and the theatrics went out of her all at once, the way they occasionally did, leaving behind something quiet with nothing holding it up.

"She's inviting," Bella said.

Down on the crystal, Hathaway stood absolutely still.

The pressure sat behind her sternum exactly where the iron sphere had sat, heavy and demanding and completely available, and one unit of mana would have moved it. Every cell she owned was filing the same request at the same moment.

Hold the door, she thought. Do not reach for her.

One second.

Two.

For a Tier-8 working, two seconds of hang was a geological era.

And then, without any sound at all, the invitation was accepted.

The air inside the boundary acquired an occupant, and Hathaway's skin knew it the way skin knows it is being looked at from behind, except from every direction the boundary held and all at once, particle-fine, seeded through every cubic centimeter of it. It had been asked politely, and it had decided, on balance, to come.

Then it began to divide.

Sixty-five million times a second, and the divisions went gold.

On the ridge, Wilhelmina opened her eyes.

Rory stared straight into it. The light was past anything a body was built to take, and the tears ran down the little Siren's face in unbroken lines, and she held it, unblinking, both fists locked in Anna's collar, looking directly at the sun.

Inside one hundred and fifteen meters, everything was simply subtracted: the crystal, the stone under it, the dust, the air, removed from the record without argument. The heat stayed in. The light stayed in. A hundred and fifteen meters out, the wind kept moving over volcanic rock that had not gained a single degree.

And Hathaway stood in the middle of it and paid for it by the second. The armor on her skin took the heat and took the radiation and did nothing whatsoever about the mass. Her shins went first. Then her forearms. Something in her spine filed a formal complaint and stopped transmitting, and she held the routing open with whatever was still answering, because the door does not stay shut by itself.

Then the gold went out, and the occupant was gone, and the morning came back.

Hathaway came down flat on the smooth new floor of a perfectly hemispherical bowl, one hundred and fifteen meters from her to any edge of it, and looked up at an ordinary grey sky.

For one full second the ridge made no sound at all.

Then the house of Ludwig lost its collective mind.

Hats went into the air. Rhode's whistle came off the caldera walls twice. Frederica's phoenix launched straight up and opened a column of gold and crimson fire wide enough to be seen from the far side of the plains, and Frederica was roaring underneath it with both gauntlets over her head.

Wilhelmina did not move at all.

The Matriarch stood at the edge of the ridge with her eyes open, and the two Elders nearest her had quietly relocated, and she looked down into the crater and did not stop looking. The tears came out and cut two clean tracks through the powder on her face, and she did not raise a hand to either of them.

Boots hit the crystal. Margaret and Anna came down the incline with no regard whatsoever for the residual heat, Anna's wand already moving, flooding Hathaway's ruined legs with bone-knitting hexes at a rate that made her teeth buzz.

Margaret got both hands around her daughter's soot-streaked face.

"Baby." She was crying and laughing in the same breath, kissing her forehead, her cheekbone, her hair. "Oh, my brave girl. You did it."

Hathaway got a smile up from somewhere.

"Tell Aunt Wilhelmina," she managed, "I want lunch."

Above her, Wilhelmina had arrived at the lip of the crater, and her voice came down over all of it, wrecked and certain.

"One hundred and fifteen meters. Log it. Log the hour." A breath. "The hope of this house."

Hathaway lay in the bottom of her crater and let her family be proud of her, which cost considerably more than the rite had.

Then something small came over the rim.

"HATTIE!"

Rory hit her square in the chest at the terminal velocity of a toddler who had been carried for three consecutive minutes and had developed opinions about it, and two of Hathaway's freshly knitted ribs gave out with a sound that carried to the ridge.

"Guh—"

Hathaway's eyes went wide. Her arms came up on reflex, achieved roughly four centimeters, and were overruled by a small cold person with both fists in her collar who was crying furiously into her coat and had no intention of being relocated.

She stopped struggling.

The whole ridge will be casting the original inside a year, she thought, running on almost nothing, and I still have no idea what I paid for it.

She got one arm around her sister, turned her head, and looked up out of her crater at a family that had walked to a hundred and fifty meters and stood there for her.
