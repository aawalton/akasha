I watch the ship sail away from the balcony of the watchtower that stands on the top of the hill above the cavern. My enhanced eyesight needs no binoculars to see it as it pulls out of the harbour and then, moving faster than our ships did, rounds the headland to the right and disappears past it.

“It’s gone,” I remark to my companion. Arguably, we could have assigned this task to any of the scouts under my command, but I wanted to see the merchants leave with my own eyes. Gulnis and his partners were perfectly pleasant, but I can’t say I’m sad to see them go.

From the expression of satisfaction on Elarion’s face, I can tell that he feels the same way – probably stronger. “Well done for getting the food ready for them in less than two days. You must have worked through the night.”

Elarion stretches languidly, his smile of satisfaction deepening.

“We did,” he agrees. “My mages are exhausted – and we’ve run through almost all of our stock of potions. It was worth it, however, to be rid of those leeches as soon as possible.”

He casts a dark glance out to where we last saw the merchant vessel.

“What do you have against merchants anyway?” I ask curiously. There hasn’t been a good time to ask until now. Though we need to go down and discuss the information I gained from Gulnis with the others – not all of it exactly willingly – we have a rare lack of urgency. I can only hope it lasts more than a moment this time.

Elarion’s satisfied smile fades and a faint crease forms between his brows as he stares out to sea. I feel tempted to tell him that he doesn’t need to answer the question if it makes him so uncomfortable, but hold myself back. The fact is that, while his sharpness served us in the recent negotiations, it clearly wasn’t only a tactic, but a frustrated expression of genuine antagonism.

“What do you know of my family?” he asks abruptly.

“That you rule Icemeet. That your main exports are specialised wood types, minerals, and farming potions. That your family Class is Ice Druid, an odd combination that nonetheless has some very powerful Skills, as we saw in the recent battle.” I note with some trepidation that the reminder still fails to draw a smile from him. I hesitate before my next words. “That since your grandfather’s time, your line has been Bound to that of mine.”

I suddenly realise that though I know a good deal about Elarion, I don’t know much about his family.

His mouth tightens into two bloodless lines. And then, he forcibly relaxes them, his expression blanking.

“Then you know little of our family history. My grandfather’s act against your family was wrong, I do not deny it. Yet we have suffered far more than is reasonable.” His eyes flash at me, challenging me to refute him.

“Explain?” I invite gently, holding his gaze without reacting one way or the other.

Elarion’s expression twists and he turns to gaze out to sea again, his chest inflating in a deep breath. A moment later, his face smooths and he regains his calm.

“As a result of grandfather’s crime and sentence, my family lost a great deal of standing,” he relates, his tone uncaring. I might be deceived if I hadn’t seen just how much this matters to him a moment ago. “Houses that preferred the favour of Titanbend distanced themselves; merchants put up their prices. I was not yet born at that point, but my father still holds a good deal of resentment towards those who proved themselves such insecure friends.”

“That’s the root of your dislike of merchants? That they put up prices when they saw you were vulnerable?” I ask tentatively.

Elarion chuckles humourlessly.

“I am not finished, Markus.”

“Apologies,” I quickly tell him, then wait expectantly, leaning against the crenellated wall.

“When I was fifteen, disaster struck our territory. A poisonous miasma that spread from the deepest wood, sucking the life from anything it enveloped for a length of time. Whole sections of the forest withered into dust. Lumber operations ceased, and our herbalists failed to meet their quotas for the herbs that make up the potions we export. Worse, as it spread into our farmlands, our crops withered under the helpless eyes of our farmers. Our healers could do little. Our warriors less. And with every length it engulfed, the miasma spread more quickly.

“Soon whole villages were taken. Those who decided to move with as much as they could carry by foot survived. Those who chose to stubbornly remain in their ancestral homes vanished without trace.”

I listen with increasing sympathy. My thoughts flash back to the recent events with the manastorm and the parasite. I find I can identify deeply with the Foresthearts and the frustrated helplessness they must have felt.

“Of course, we tried to help where we could, but our income, already reduced as a consequence of the tithe we are required to pay to Titanbend, and the cooling off of relationships with other Houses and merchants, struggled with this new disaster. And what did the merchants do?” He spat the question, showing more anger than I’ve ever seen. “They saw our desperation and spotted an opportunity for profit.

“Prices for even the most basic foodstuffs tripled overnight. The merchants tried to excuse it as a safety expense – that the increased danger required them to pay for more security. Lying leeches. They sought only to profit from our misfortune.”

He visibly pulls himself together. I wait in silence, sensing that any words from me right now will draw an adverse reaction. “We approached everyone we could. The Lesser Houses in our territory were struggling themselves – the harvests of the previous two years had been poor too. A couple of our closest allies who had not abandoned us after grandfather’s punishment helped where they could, but banditry was worse that year than ever before and half the supplies they sent never made it through to where the greatest need was.

“We were forced to watch our people, displaced from their villages, robbed of their livelihood, begin to starve. And Lord Nicholas Titanbend,” he spat again, “who had only recently succeeded his father, refused to help – not even to temporarily lessen the tithe we owed him.”

I swallow and look out to sea myself, uncomfortable with this revelation. I can understand it to an extent from Nicholas’ point of view – if this happened shortly after he took the lordship, he would have been hurting from losing his father far too soon to the blood curse, and be disinclined to help those who had applied it. Still, it sits badly with me that those who suffered most were those who were innocent in the conflict between the two Great Houses.

The silence elongates, but I don’t break it. I now understand far more deeply the deep-seated rancour that motivated Elarion’s original attitude towards me. In some ways, I’m grateful that this pus-filled wound has been revealed – perhaps now I can prove that I’m not like Nicholas in the ways that matter most.

Finally, Elarion speaks again.

“Eventually, we found the cause. Unbeknownst to us, a powerful Tier four beast had arisen in the deep wood. A beast aligned with death mana, that grew more powerful the more it consumed. It took us a long time to find it – its mana was the antithesis to so many of our mages and the miasma around its den was thick enough to kill even a Classer in seconds. We had to develop a new type of full-body protection – including a mask – that filtered out the death mana before our scouts could get close.

“My grandfather and father both went to confront it. They could take only a few elite soldiers and mages due to the resources required for the armour.” I sneak a glance sideways – his lips are pressed together tightly again. “By the time the force emerged, more than half of them were dead. Including my grandfather.”

I breathe out a silent breath, practically feeling his grief. Elarion’s grandfather might have been a poor lord, but he was clearly still family.

Elarion turns to me, anger and grief visible only in his eyes.

“You asked why I hold so much antagonism towards merchants? Because that time revealed just what they are at heart. Vultures that will pick the eyes out of a dying man. Had they even kept the prices the same as normal, we would have been able to feed far more of our people, and make more of the protective suits of armour. Far fewer would have died – perhaps my grandfather would have lived.”

I nod slowly.

I don’t answer immediately, giving the emotional echoes of the story time to dull again. It’s only when I see Elarion’s expression smooth again that I speak.

“You have good reason to be angry at them – and at a good many Houses, my own included.” I don’t shy away from that, and I see surprise in Elarion’s expression at the acknowledgement. “But just as you and I have been able to put aside a good deal of family history for the benefit of the expedition, perhaps you can do the same for the merchants. I assume that Gulniss and his company aren’t the same ones who took such advantage of you at that time.”

Elarion’s face hardens.

“They are not, but Markus, don’t be fooled – all merchants are the same.”

“Then the solution is simple,” I tell him. He quirks a brow fractionally upwards.

“Do tell.”

I lean forwards.

“We must never become dependent on them. Use them to enrich our settlement, yes, but ensure that we are always able to get what we need without their support.”

Elarion looks at me sceptically.

“You do not think that we tried to do that? We were the ones exporting food – until the beast appeared.”

“Then we must make sure that we don’t depend on a single area of fertility, a single harvest. We must establish fields in different areas, stockpile food, and ensure that what we sell is valuable enough that merchants cannot afford to antagonise us with unreasonable price hikes. Trade should strengthen us – but not be something we depend on. But refusing to engage with them entirely isn’t the answer – nor is making enemies of them by being too antagonistic in initial negotiations.”

Elarion is silent for a long moment. Then he nods slowly.

“It sounds good, Markus,” he agrees, “yet I fear that you will find such idealism is difficult in practice.”

I shrug.

“Probably. But I’m still going to try. And I’d much prefer it for you to work with me on this.”

Elarion regards me for a moment, then shrugs elegantly.

“I suppose the most recent negotiations were promising.” His lips part in a savage grin. “Their hunger was only partially assuaged even as they begged for us to take their pitiful wares in payment for the secrets only we know. Exactly where you want such leeches to be.”

“Then you’re not upset we traded the knowledge of how to make Moriaxar crops viable for more information about the other settlers on the coast?”

Elarion shrugs again.

“I revealed only the most basic method. Just with the practice we’ve had in the last day and night, we’ve already refined it significantly more. Without the knowledge we have of the plant network – which I did not include in the method – others will be limited to gardening in the dark.”

I hesitate for a moment, reviewing the letter of the trade we made, then relax. We agreed to trade ‘a method that enabled Moriaxar crops to grow on the new land and remain edible’. We didn’t agree to trade the most productive or efficient method that we know. As long as it works at all, we have fulfilled our obligations.

Elarion seems to be in a slightly better mood, now he’s been reminded of how he’s had a little bit of petty revenge against merchants in general.

“Shall we go down? The others are waiting to discuss the new knowledge we have.”

“Yes,” Elarion agrees, a faint frown creasing his brow. “I have to admit that the flag furthest to the south concerns me.”

I nod.

“That none of us – including Gulnis – can identify it is not a heartening sign,” I agree. “But let’s put our heads together and decide our approach – and not only to that, but to all the settlers.”

Gulnis revealed more than he intended, especially since Lathani was in the room and has been able to tell me of things that he most likely wanted to keep to himself. I make a mental note to pass on the information about the guard with the loose lips to Sarran – he’ll need to make an example so that others don’t repeat the behaviour the next time visitors come. I have a feeling a certain man who let his libido get the better of his judgement will be on scut work for the foreseeable future – or worse.

But, while useful to know, that wasn’t the most valuable piece of information Lathani lifted from Gulnis’ thoughts. Nor was it the most concerning. The unknown banner worries me more than all the others combined. If we're to be forced into contact with the continent's native inhabitants before I’d hoped, then we need to establish our position – and quickly.
