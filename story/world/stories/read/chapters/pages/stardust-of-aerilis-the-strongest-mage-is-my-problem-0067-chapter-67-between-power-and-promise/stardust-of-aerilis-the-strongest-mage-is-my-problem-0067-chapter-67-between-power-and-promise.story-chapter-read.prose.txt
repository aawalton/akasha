Aiven sat on the chair of his suite in Vulpine’s headquarters, his gaze fixed on the matte obsidian surface of his left arm. The cyan etchings pulsed with a soft, rhythmic light—a silent reminder of the power he had borrowed, and the much larger ocean of energy still locked behind the seal in his soul.

His thoughts were a tangled ledger of risks and rewards. The path forward should have been clear. If he truly wished for the freedom he had craved since leaving his desk in Lowhaven, then rejecting Lucien’s offer was the only sensible choice. Joining him meant becoming an instrument in a global reset—destroying dungeons, perhaps committing atrocities in the name of the greater good, and living under the constant threat of being erased if he ever chose to say no.

Choosing autonomy meant a fight would come faster, but at least his life would belong to him.

He knew that access to the Loom-Breaker was guaranteed either way. Whether he stayed with the Fox or ran to the God, both sides viewed him as a necessary battery.

Lucien might be the one with the ancient knowledge to unlock his potential more efficiently, but Aiven wasn't ready to sacrifice his soul for a shortcut to power.

Then, his mind drifted to the one variable that outweighed all the others.

Virelle.

Cyria’s revelation that Virelle was originally a sentient Dungeon Core had been a shock that nearly derailed his composure. The Starweave—a parasitic entity meant to eat the world, reshaped into a girl by a legendary artifact.

But as Aiven stared at the tropical tapestry on the wall, he realized that he didn't really mind. Whether she was a dungeon core, an elf, or a creation of pure mana, it didn't change the facts of their journey. It didn't change the way she had shielded him from kobolds, the way she had looked at him in the forest spirit’s hut, or the way she had poured out her sincere, honest feelings on the deck of the Cinder-Fox.

“I want to be the reason your heart races as much as mine does.”

Her voice echoed in his mind, persistent and warm. He still hadn't figured out the exact label for what he felt for her—the shadow of Lyra was still a heavy, respectful weight in his heart—but he was resolute in one thing: he would not let Virelle be used as a weapon or a bargaining chip ever again.

It was time for him to be the one protecting her.

But a familiar, pessimistic voice whispered in the back of his mind. What if you fail? What if the Loom-Breaker can’t open the seal? What if you’re just a clerk playing with a star you can’t control? If he failed, he would have to rely on Virelle again.

He would have to watch her bleed, watch her exhaust her core until she was in pain just to keep him upright.

Aiven leaned forward, resting his elbows on the desk and burying his face in his hands. He rubbed his temples, trying to massage away the crushing weight of the responsibility.

"Master?"

The voice was soft, lacking its usual theatrical sharp edge. Aiven looked up to see Virelle floating just a few feet away. She was watching him with a look of profound, uncharacteristic worry, her eyes shimmering in the amber mana-light.

"Are you okay, Master?" she asked, drifting closer. "You look as though you are attempting to calculate the end of the world again."

Aiven let out a long, weary sigh and leaned back. "I'm fine, Virelle. It’s just... there are a lot of things to think about."

Virelle’s gaze dropped to the floor, her silver-lavender hair obscuring her face. "Is it…the revelation?" she whispered. "Does my Master think of me as a freak now?"

Aiven blinked, his heart giving a painful thud. "No! Virelle, no. That’s not the case at all. I wouldn't think of you like that."

Virelle looked up, her expression fragile. "When the Fox spoke of the Starweave... I felt a tremor in my own core. It means I am not normal, Master. I am not a summon, nor a true elf. I was a parasite that was meant to consume this world, weaponized by a man who views souls as fuel. Everything I am is... artificial."

"Maybe the start was," Aiven countered, his voice gaining a rare, steady strength. "But everything since then is yours. You aren't a cold, heartless weapon. I’ve felt your warmth, Virelle. I’ve seen your joy when we find a good bun, and I’ve seen your fury when I’m in danger. Do you think those things are fake? I mean, you were the one who told me that your feelings were real."

Virelle bit her lip, her fingers twisting in the translucent fabric of her sleeve. "But deep down... I am terrified that you will eventually realize what I am and leave me. Or worse... that I will lose myself someday. That something will wake up and I will become that mindless tool again."

Aiven stood up, closing the distance between them. "Virelle, I told you back in Aelira’s place. I’m not leaving you. And I will do everything in my power to ensure no one—not Lucien, not Cyria, and not the government—ever turns you into a tool again. We’re almost there, Virelle. The Loom-Breaker is the key. Soon, I’ll have the strength to stand in front of you."

He realized then that he couldn't afford to fail. Thinking of the worst-case scenario was a luxury he didn't have. He would use the artifact. He would have Cyria and the Vulpine elites teach him how to control the tide. He would become the ocean he was meant to be.

Virelle looked at him, her eyes wide. "I... I apologize, Master. I am adding more burden to your mind when you are already so stressed."

Aiven let out a small, thoughtful smile. He reached out and, for the first time, initiated a gesture of comfort without fumbling. He placed his hand on top of her head, gently rubbing her hair.

Virelle froze, her breath hitching in a startled, cute little gasp. She didn't pull away; instead, she slowly closed her eyes, leaning into his palm to savor the grounding contact.

"There’s no need to apologize," Aiven said softly. "Actually, you just made the dark clouds in my head disappear. Talking to you... it reminds me why I’m doing this."

Virelle let out a soft, shaky breath. "It is a bit ironic, is it not? A few days ago, I was the one doing the comforting. Deep down, I suppose I am quite weak."

"At least with this, I can feel like I'm finally doing something to help you," Aiven replied, his thumb grazing the soft silk of her hair.

"And very soon, I’ll be able to do much more."

The Loom-Breaker sat on the desk behind them, pulsing with a crimson light that no longer looked like a threat, but like a promise.

Knock-knock.

A quick, perfunctory knock sounded on the door, but before Aiven could even draw a breath to answer, the latch clicked and the door swung wide.

Marnie Anvilrun burst into the room, her brass-rimmed goggles perched on her forehead. She looked energized, her usual blunt confidence radiating from her. Trailing just behind her was Vane, whose massive, stoic frame filled the doorway like a mountain.

"Oh, Aiven!" Marnie shouted, her voice booming in the quiet suite. "I heard you actually pushed the Fox into a corner! I couldn't be more grateful than—"

She stopped dead in her tracks.

Marnie’s eyes darted from Aiven’s hand—still resting tenderly on top of Virelle’s head—to the way Virelle was looking up at him with wide, misty eyes and a faint, uncharacteristic blush. Even Vane, usually a statue of professionalism, allowed his ears to twitch a fraction of an inch as his golden gaze swept over the intimate scene.

"Ah," Marnie muttered, her loud voice suddenly dropping into an uncharacteristically awkward whisper. Her eyes drifted between the two of them, her jaw working as she processed the lack of bickering and the presence of... whatever this was.

"I... uh..." Marnie cleared her throat, a look of profound realization crossing her face. "I think I might have accidentally interrupted a very specific kind of session."

Aiven’s face reached a shade of crimson that likely rivaled the Loom-Breaker’s glow. He jerked his hand away from Virelle’s head as if the hair had turned to molten lava. "Marnie! It's not—we were just—"

"Right, right. 'Just,'" Marnie said, already taking a hasty step backward into the hallway. She gestured vaguely at Vane, who was already turning around with a stoic, silent nod. "Look, I’ll just... come back when the air isn't so thick you could cut it with a smithing chisel. Have a good... whatever this is!"

Before Aiven could stammer out another word of defense, Marnie pulled the heavy door shut with a sharp thud.

The room fell back into a silence that was ten times heavier than before, leaving a stunned Aiven and a somewhat amused Virelle looking at her Master.
