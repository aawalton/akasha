“Did you hit your head during one of those train jumps? Or perhaps it’s the lack of sleep talking? From staying up all night...” Aerion said, giving me a look that clearly said she thought I’d lost my mind.

She knew about that? I gave her a sidelong glance. Maybe she wasn’t out as hard as I thought she was.

“I’ll be the first to admit it is surprising to find a Rocky here,” she continued, “but can this not simply be Order finally helping us properly? Guiding us to resources we need? Loathe as I am to admit it, if this ends up being another Galia, it will only strengthen our plight against the Cataclysms.”

“I want to believe that. Truly do,” I said, and I meant it. “But there’s just too much evidence otherwise.”

“Such as?” Aerion asked, raising an eyebrow.

I hesitated, trying to figure out how to explain without sounding completely paranoid. “Cosmo once brought me to this place. Specifically this exact store.”

“What do you mean?” Aerion asked with a frown. “He brought you to the Cataclysm?”

“It was in one of our meetings,” I said, waving my hand to dismiss the idea. “He normally pulls me into his bar, but sometimes he takes me other places. One time, he brought me here, to a recreation of Akihabara.” I felt my face heating up. “And he uh, had me… pick out merchandise. A figure of you, specifically. He was weirdly insistent.”

“Figurines,” Aerion deadpanned, her eyebrow twitching. “Of me, you say?”

Oh, shit Greg. Don’t lose it now.

I didn’t dare dare tell her half of those were either naked or scantily clad in poses that would make Aerion pass out. Honesty was good in a relationship, but that level of honesty was just stupid.

Not that I didn’t want to see her in those outfits and poses, of course—I wanted it more than damn near anything, which was why Cosmo had shown them to me—but it had felt like an overstep, even for him.

“Yeah, er, just like these,” I said, gesturing to the shelves filled with the badass looking ones, being very careful not to point anywhere even close to the scantily clad ones. “Action figures, you know? In various poses. Except all of them were of you.”

Aerion’s eyes narrowed and her voice was like ice. “I see. And?”

“He, uh, had me choose one I liked.” I tried to meet her eyes, and failed, coughing awkwardly instead. “At the time, I thought he was just fucking with me. Being Cosmo, you know?”

“I see,” she said. Cold sweat started beading on my back, and I could’ve sworn the temperature just dropped ten degrees. “And you somehow never thought to mention this.”

“Well, you can understand why,” I said weakly. “It’s not exactly a normal thing to bring up in conversation. ‘Hey, by the way, my patron god made me pick out action figures of you.’ The last thing I wanted was you hating Cosmo even more than you already do.”

“Then perhaps your god should act in a manner befitting his station,” Aerion said, crossing her arms. “And I deserved to know.”

“You did,” I admitted, folding under her gaze, wondering whether now would be a good time to tell her about the Aerion masseuse Cosmo summoned that one time, but her death glare told me that would be a supremely bad idea.

Coughing, I shifted the conversation as deftly as I could. Which was to say, not at all. “Anyway, Cosmo brought me here and made sure I made choices here that were uniquely mine. And now there’s not only an egg sitting exactly where he led me, its surface looks identical to the ruby red of the armor outfit I chose of you.”

“Oh?” Aerion asked, her scowl giving way to a curious frown. Which was still a frown, but was at least an improvement. “What armor is this?”

“You were in a flying action pose in red armor that was on fire. That was the one I picked out. You looked mesmerizing in that, if I’m honest. Like a war goddess or something. The color was unmistakable, and this egg has the exact same plating. I’m guessing Cosmo wants me to turn this into an armor set for you once whatever’s inside hatches. Which means he put this here—this specific Rocky—for us to find. And I’d bet anything he did the same for the other one.”

“That’s… not possible,” Aerion said, but her eyes dropped just a bit and her voice lacked the certainty of her earlier words.

“Is it? Phoenix eggs are supposed to be incredibly rare, right? To the point of being myth. We just happened to find one in a Leviathan. Which might have been an incredible coincidence, sure. But now we get another Champion in the exact location Cosmo showed me months ago? With that coloring? Sorry, Aer, but that’s not luck.”

“Perhaps he tampered with the Cataclysm,” Aerion suggested. “Snuck the egg in after the dungeon formed?”

“Then why aren’t the other gods doing that?” I asked, looking Aerion in the eyes. “They’ve never once tampered with the Cataclysms, right? The world would’ve heard about it if it had.”

“That’s…” Aerion was scowling again, but not at me.

“Besides, there’s more evidence. You remember when you crashed my Ascension Trial and the System glitched out?”

“You’re referring to the meeting we witnessed between the gods,” Aerion said, more a statement than a question.

“‘The Cataclysms are a costly expense,’” I quoted. “At the time, I thought that meant it was an expense for the world. The devastation and loss of life they caused, forcing them to expend their energy to summon Champions to fight.”

I paced back and forth in front of the narrow corridor between the cases. “But that doesn’t make much sense if you think about it. The gods are nearly all-powerful, as we know from the visits Dominion makes and the other myths from before the Cataclysms, when they were more active. So why summon Champions at all? Champions that are initially extremely weak, and who can fail in their quest? Why not just take out the Cataclysm themselves?”

Aerion’s expression was slowly changing from skeptical to deeply troubled as I spoke. “And you believe it is because they create the Cataclysms, too?”

“It’d make sense as to why it’s never truly vanquished. And sure, maybe they do summon Champions to deal with them, expending their energy to give us power. But Cosmo’s message here is clear.” I pointed to the egg. “They can clearly influence them, and when you consider their words in that meeting and why they keep coming back, it’s not a stretch to guess it’s their doing as well.”

She was quiet for a long moment, but when she spoke, her voice was hoarse. “But why, Greg? Why summon the Cataclysms and the Champions? And if what you’re saying is true, would that not mean Order has intentionally allowed this state of affairs to continue? That makes him complicit in countless deaths across the cycles, does it not?”

It was a damn good question, because it kinda did look that way. If Cosmo was against this plan as I so desperately wanted to believe, and if he had the power to influence the Cataclysms, couldn’t he have sabotaged them? Or found some way to shut down the cycle for good?

“He’s restricted,” I said, reasoning through things as I spoke. “In his words and actions. I’d always wondered why he danced around topics so much with me. At first, I thought he just got a kick out of making me work for information.”

He probably did, knowing the guy.

“But the more I got to know him,” I continued, “the more it seems like he wants to help but just can’t. Like he’s bound by some set of rules he can’t talk about. Or, restrictions.”

“Restrictions?” Aerion asked, but she was way smarter than I was, and she picked up on my meaning immediately. “You believe he tried something like this before?” Aerion asked. “Tried to stop the Cataclysms and was punished for it?”

“Maybe,” I said. “I admit, that’s the one part we don’t really have any evidence of.”

Neither of us had heard of any prior Champion of Order, which sounded like something we ought to have heard of. But maybe he hadn’t summoned a Champion back then. Maybe he’d messed with the Cataclysms directly. If that were the case, Axius might never have found out.

“It’s one possibility, anyway,” I said. “He went against the other gods, tried to change things, and they slapped restrictions on him because of it. But now he’s found a loophole. Or rather, made it over the course of…” I trailed off, trying to wrap my head around the timescale. “Hundreds of millennia?”

It was almost impossible to comprehend how anyone or anything could operate on such an absurd timescale. If Cosmo was doing what I thought he was doing—if he was laying the groundwork for a rebellion across fifty thousand years—he was playing a game so far beyond me that I wasn’t even the lowliest pawn in his grand plan. I was just a single move. A single event in a strategy that had been planned before my entire species even existed.

The thought was both terrifying and awe inspiring.

Of course, I could have just been giving Cosmo way more credit than he deserved, but I somehow doubted it.

“I don’t know, Greg. This feels very much like conjecture. And if it is even true, what does he hope to achieve?” Aerion asked, gesturing to Rocky. “What’s he building toward with all this?”

I glanced at the egg, then back at her. “He wants us to build an army? One that’s strong enough to kill the Cataclysms once and for all?”

I’d come up with that idea on the spot, but now that I’d voiced it, it didn’t sound so crazy.

“With two phoenixes?” She raised an eyebrow. “Assuming this one is even a phoenix. It could be something completely different.”

“Not just phoenixes. Champions of Order.” I gestured at the egg. “And if we keep finding them…” My eyes narrowed. “We already have more active Champions than any cycle before us. It won’t be that long before we have something that qualifies as a squad of elites, if not an army.”

I frowned, trying to piece together Cosmo’s intent from the snippets I’d gleaned from him. I now had to wonder if he’d let all those slip intentionally, making me think like I’d weaseled something out of him when it was his plan all along. “I can’t say for certain, but if Cosmo’s signaling what I think he’s signaling, they’re how we end the cycles. Permanently.”

“Like the myths,” Aerion whispered.

“Like your myths, yeah,” I agreed. “I’d always wondered why Cosmo summoning his Champion would trigger prophecies about ending the cycle entirely.”

Nevermind the fact that he’d been summoning Champions all this time, but I suppose the spirit of that prophecy still stood.

“How, exactly?” Aerion asked.

“No clue,” I admitted. “Somehow, I don’t think the Archon is gonna get any deader just because I showed up. Still, it feels like we’re onto something here. I don’t know, what you think?”

“I think this is ridiculous,” Aerion said, though her voice was uncertain. “What you’re speaking of… if the other gods even hear of you speaking this way…”

“Yeah,” I said, my mouth dry. “I somehow doubt they’re aligned with Cosmo’s plan, if this even is his plan. Especially if we’re right about him operating under restrictions. If they find out what he’s doing, or what we’re doing, they’ll shut us down.”

And despite the gods’ policy of noninterference on Axius, I got the inkling they’d make an exception for us. Or make an example out of us.

We stood in silence, the weight of the revelation pressing down on both of us.

“As much as your ‘hunches’ have served us in the past, Greg, you’ve yet to explain why the gods would create the Cataclysms in the first place,” Aerion finally asked. “It makes no sense. What could they possibly gain from killing millions?”

I shook my head. “That, I don’t know. But I aim to find out.”

The bigger question was how. How did I confront Cosmo about this? And assuming it was all true, how did I go about convincing the other Champions without their patron gods finding out?

For the first time since arriving on Axius, I felt like I understood my purpose. It was a hunch, as Aerion pointed out, nothing more. But as she’d also pointed out, they hadn’t led us astray yet, and my spidey sense was tingling so hard, I felt my hair stand on end.

For the first time, I had the gut feeling that Cosmo hadn’t summoned me here on a whim. That he hadn’t made me his Champion just to fuck with me or because he was bored.

Quite the opposite. I was the culmination of a plan spanning tens of millennia. A plan to end the Cataclysms permanently.

And I still had no fucking idea what I was supposed to do about it.

“We should go,” Aerion said quietly. “The others will be wondering where we are.”

“Yeah,” I said, sliding the egg into my spatial inventory. “Let’s get back.”

I took one last look back at the store before we headed back toward the subway, unable to shake the feeling that I’d just stepped into something far bigger than myself. A divine conspiracy that had been brewing since before human civilization existed on Earth.

And I got the sense I was supposed to be the one to bring it all crashing down.

I’d never wanted to be more wrong in my life.
