Alric had not intended to turn the common room into a cartographer’s office, but then he had not intended to turn it into a barrel store either and he’d long given up on keeping it an inn or headquarters.

The long table had been cleared only in the loosest sense. Cleared, in this case, meant that enough space had been created for paper to lie flat if one ignored the corner resting on a tally ledger and the other corner curling against a tankard. The barrels that were on the table were currently in Alric’s item box. A map occupied most of the surface, weighed down by a small knife, a cup that had once held tea, and one of Stromni’s spare metal punches, which had been chosen because it was heavy and because no one had yet dared move it.

Moreen sat opposite him with the sort of patience merchants developed when watching other men discover things they had already known. It was not quite smugness.

Alric leaned over the map, one finger resting on the river line where it passed the capital. The charcoal marks around it had multiplied. Some were routes, some were numbers, and some were the remnants of earlier assumptions that had been crossed out hard enough to bruise the paper.

“But this doesn’t make sense?” he said.

Moreen lifted his tankard and shrugged. Moreen had shown a very quick fondness for the new port.

“No, this specifically doesn’t.” Alric tapped the capital. “The imports, The timing uh... The way stock appears so fast. Grapes, barrels, copper, salt, grain. Everything arrives like the city is pulling it in directly, but it isn’t?”

Moreen drank, watching him over the rim.

Alric moved his finger down the river. “If the capital were the main hub, everything should bottleneck here, but it doesn’t? It gets crowded, expensive, stupid and political, but it doesn’t choke the way it should.”

Moreen’s mouth moved slightly, though not enough to become a smile. “Stupid and political when talking trade? You sure registered with the right guild.”

Alric ignored him and dragged the charcoal line farther downstream. “Goods come here by barge first. A lot of them. But not all of them are meant for the capital.”

“No,” Moreen said.

Alric stared at him for a moment, then looked back down at the map. It was there once he stopped trying to make the city more important than the road and water allowed. The river curved away, widening enough in places to matter, narrowing in others where the banks did what banks always seemed to do when men tried to build plans around them. A cluster of marks sat downstream, not large enough on the map to be impressive. Trade Town. A dull name.

“It goes there,” Alric said.

Moreen’s expression did not change.

“The river trade goes to Trade Town,” Alric continued, slower now. “That’s where the barges unload properly. From there, wagons take it to Aven Port? Then ships?”

Moreen nodded once. Alric sat back.

For several seconds he said nothing. The construction work was being done around them, though most of it did so from behind barrels.

“The capital isn’t the hub,” Alric said.

“No,” Moreen replied.

“It’s just a point, a node.”

Moreen’s eyes warmed slightly. “There it is.”

Alric looked down again. The capital remained large, walled, guarded, taxed, officious and pleased with itself, but the lines around it had changed. It was not the mouth of the system. It was one thick knot tied into a longer rope. Goods did not arrive because the capital demanded them and the world obeyed. Goods passed through a chain that already existed, had existed long before him, and would continue existing as long as boats floated, roads held, and men found ways to complain about tolls while paying them.

He moved his finger back along the river. “That’s why the imports don’t cause immediate shortages?”

Moreen leaned back slightly.

“If something is bought here, it isn’t always pulled from the city’s supply,” Alric said, more to the map than to Moreen. “It can be pulled from the chain before it settles. Stock can be diverted at Trade Town. Or before Trade Town. Or from wagon contracts already heading to Aven Port.”

“Sometimes,” Moreen said.

“Sometimes is enough.”

“Often enough,” Moreen corrected.

Alric nodded, the correction landing in the useful part of his mind. “And the nobles?”

Moreen did smile now, though only faintly. “What of them?”

Alric’s gaze moved to the road lines. They were darker, straighter, and in some places marked with noble holdings, estates, old toll points, and the small miserable symbols that meant someone had inherited the right to make travel worse.

“They control roads better than water,” Moreen explained gesturing to and fro with his wrist. “Land routes. Wagons. Estates. Toll points. Teamsters. Local guards. Warehouses in places where their cousins have keys.”

Alric’s eyes widened by a fraction.

“But barges…” Alric traced the river again. “Barges are different. Boatmen, dockmasters, city charters, river rights, old toll exemptions, trade contracts. Too many pieces… And too many people who are not technically under one hand.”

“Water is irritating,” Moreen said. “It moves.”

Alric gave him a flat look.

Moreen shrugged. “That is not a small problem for certain kinds of men.”

Nobles could squeeze roads. They could lean on tenants. They could make a warehouse inconvenient or a wagon expensive. But a barge already on the river was a harder thing to intimidate, especially once the men aboard had been paid and the current had joined the conversation.

“Nobles are strongest where they can make men wait,” Moreen explained. “Roads make men wait. Gates make men wait. Documents make men wait. Rivers are not immune, but the king manages the city, town, river and port.”

Alric let out a slow breath through his nose.

“So if someone pressures a road route,” he said, “You use the river?”

“It’s possible.”

“If a capital warehouse becomes awkward, You can arrange with Trade Town.”

“Maybe.”

“If the merchants here close ranks, you build relationships downstream.”

“Perhaps.”

Alric looked up. “You say perhaps like a man who knows exactly how often that works.”

Moreen folded his hands over his stomach. “I say perhaps because every route has a person standing beside it with a price. The trick is knowing whether that price is coin, favour, fear, habit, pride or the simple pleasure of making another man ask twice.”

Alric stared at the map.

The world had not become easier but it had become less blurry, which was a different kind of problem.

He did not smile. Instead, he reached for another sheet and began marking possible routes with small, careful strokes.

Moreen watched him for a while.

“You do realise,” the merchant said, “that most men spend years learning enough of this to become dangerous.”

Alric did not look up. “And?”

“You appear to be attempting it over this wine, which is very good.”

Alric made another mark, then stopped. A thought that had been waiting behind the map chose that moment to step forward.

He set the charcoal down.

“Why didn’t you tell me?”

Moreen’s gaze sharpened slightly, though the rest of him remained comfortably arranged.

“About Trade Town?”

“No.” Alric leaned back. “The grapes.”

The room seemed to quiet, though it probably did not. It merely felt that way because Alric had stopped listening to the parts of it that were not Moreen.

Moreen looked at him for a moment. “Ah.”

“Yes. Ah.” Alric tapped one finger against the table. “Why didn’t you tell me how much?”

“I did say it was a great deal.”

“You said that like a merchant. I heard it like a brewer.”

“That is unfortunate.”

“That is shady.”

Moreen sighed. It was the sigh of a man asked to account for the weather after having mentioned clouds in advance. Alric didn’t sense any remorse in it.

“You have,” he said, choosing each word with mild care, “a quantity ego.”

Alric did a double take despite hearing it perfectly. “A what?”

“A quantity ego.”

“That is not a real thing.”

“It is in you.”

Alric opened his mouth, then closed it again because several recent memories had lined themselves up in a manner he did not appreciate.

Moreen gestured toward the room around them. “You enjoy scale. Someone says too much, and you begin looking for tools. Someone says impossible, and you ask whether the walls can be removed. I have met men who fear excess. You court it, then complain when it accepts.”

Alric’s expression flattened.

Moreen continued with the calm of someone who had already decided accuracy mattered more than comfort. “If you had needed a small loan to manage the overflow, I would have supplied one.”

“At interest.”

“Naturally.”

“Friendly interest?”

“Friendly interest is still interest. It merely smiles.”

Alric stared at him.

Moreen spread one hand. “Business. Nothing personal.”

“That phrase has never once made anything sound less personal.”

“It is not meant to. It is meant to make the speaker feel tidy.”

Alric looked down at the map again, though he was no longer seeing it properly. Moreen had helped him. That remained true. Moreen had opened doors, given information, arranged contact, handled matters Alric would not have known how to approach. But he had also allowed Alric to walk into a harvest large enough to occupy buildings, staff, ledgers, sleep, and possibly the structural patience of several floors.

Both things could be true. Which was irritating.

“I’m going to talk to Vale about this,” Alric said.

“I assumed you would.”

“He’ll be less relaxed about it.”

“Vale is rarely relaxed about anything. That is part of his charm.”

“He’s going to call you something.”

“I look forward to hearing which word he selects.”

Alric rubbed at his face, then dropped his hand. “You know it was shady. Of all people I expected you to understand that trust is its own form of currency.”

Moreen did not deny it. He merely shrugged.

The merchant looked toward the nearest barrel, then back at Alric. “On my best year as a merchant, I made close to four hundred gold.”

Alric went still.

Moreen’s voice stayed even. “My best year. Not an average. Not a comfortable expectation. A year where several risks paid off, two men failed to cheat me successfully, and one cousin died at a helpful time.”

Alric said nothing.

“When your boilers are all running,” Moreen continued, “you will approach that consistently. Perhaps pass it. Not as a triumph, just routine.”

Moreen lifted his cup, remembered it was empty, and set it down again. “Jealousy is not admirable, but it is common, and I have never found pretending otherwise improves trade.”

Alric looked at him for a long moment. “So you let me drown in grapes?”

“I warned you wine was a poor idea.”

“You did.”

“I still think it is a poor idea,” Moreen said. “Nobles are not chasing it. Not this wine. Not yet. Commoners will pay for beer because beer belongs to them. Wine though? It thinks it should be served with silver and poor conversation.”

Despite himself, Alric almost smiled.

Moreen caught it and continued. “You wanted grapes. You wanted enough grapes to matter. I gave you that. I could have secured another vineyard.”

Alric’s almost-smile disappeared.

Moreen held his gaze. “I did not.”

The words had no flourish. No apology either. Alric leaned back again.

Outside, a wagon rolled past, its wheels dragging sound through the street. Somewhere in the building a barrel was shifted, wood scraping against wood, followed by a short instruction from someone who sounded very tired of giving short instructions.

“You know it wouldn’t have sunk me? Just would have been an expensive loss,” Alric said.

“That is not as comforting as you seem to think.”

Alric looked at him, really looked this time. Moreen remained Moreen. Fine clothes, careful hands, agreeable posture, the face of a man who could make generosity and predation share a cup if the table was properly set. Alric had known he was a merchant. He had somehow allowed that to become a softer word because Moreen had been useful.

That had been foolish. Not disastrous, perhaps. But foolish.

Moreen folded his hands again. “Take it as a compliment.”

“I don’t want to.” Alric said a half shrug.

“Nevertheless.” Moreen said with a dismissive gesture.

Alric snorted once. “Merchants don’t test people they think will fail?”

“No,” Moreen said. “We avoid standing near them.”

Alric picked up the charcoal again, though he did not write. Vale would understand the shape of this better than he did, or at least he would understand the part where helpful men charged for being helpful without putting it on the first invoice.

A blare of trumpets cut through the thought.

Alric straightened so quickly the charcoal rolled from his fingers.

The sound came again, bright and sharp, pressing itself through the walls and into the common room.

Alric turned toward the door. “Warning? Fire?”

Moreen closed his eyes briefly.

“What?” Alric asked.

“That,” Moreen said, opening them again, “is the king’s voice.”

“The king’s voice uses trumpets?” Alric asked, his eyes already wide glancing around.

“Only when it wishes to be heard by people who might otherwise enjoy their day.”

The trumpets sounded again.

Alric stood. “Why would the king’s voice be outside?”

Moreen was already rising, smoothing the front of his coat with a small motion. “I suspect it has come for you.”

They moved toward the front of the inn, Moreen first, Alric half a step behind and still trying to decide whether he should have brought anything with him. A weapon seemed wrong. A ledger maybe? He settled for empty hands.

The street outside had arranged itself around the interruption. People stood at a respectful distance, though not so respectful that they could not see. A royal carriage waited before the inn, dark-lacquered and polished enough to reflect the buildings in distorted strips. Two guards stood beside it. Four trumpeters occupied the space ahead of them with the solemnity of men whose profession involved making everyone else stop thinking.

Between them stood a man in an outfit so aggressively formal that Alric needed a moment to understand it as clothing.

It was not merely fine. Fine clothing suggested wealth. This suggested institutional confidence. The sleeves were wide enough to imply jurisdiction. The hat had been shaped by someone with either great skill or no enemies willing to speak honestly. The whole arrangement seemed to involve layers placed over layers, each one declaring that the previous layer had not yet expressed the dignity of the crown with sufficient fabric.

Moreen bowed. Alric did the same a fraction late.

The royal messenger unrolled a scroll.

“Mister Alric,” he said, projecting the name with practiced clarity.

Alric looked up slightly. “Yes?”

Moreen’s head remained bowed. “Do not answer proclamations conversationally,” he murmured.

Alric lowered his gaze again.

The messenger continued, untroubled by the interruption or perhaps trained not to notice the lower forms of life between sentences. “By notice of the crown, the merchant Alric, brewer of record, registered practitioner of the spatial school, and person of unusual commercial and magical irregularity, is hereby informed that His Majesty would take pleasure in receiving him for private inquiry in the spring season, at a date to be provided under royal seal.”

Alric kept very still. The messenger stepped forward and presented the scroll.

Alric accepted it awkwardly still bowed. The trumpets blasted causing Alric to flinch.

The messenger bowed with the precise amount of respect due to a man who had been summoned but not ennobled, then turned and climbed back into the carriage. The trumpets sounded again as the door closed. The carriage began to move. The trumpeters, having completed whatever part of government required breath control, formed up and marched after it.

The street remained still until the last note had faded. Alric looked at the sealed scroll in his hand.

“That,” he said at last, “was the strangest street theatre I have ever seen.”

Moreen straightened beside him. “That was fairly restrained.”

Alric turned his head slowly.

Moreen looked down the street after the carriage. “No heraldic animals, No public reading of lineage, No symbolic sword.”

“Why would there be a symbolic sword?”

“You would know if there had been one.”

Alric looked back at the scroll.

Spring. That gave him time. Time was good. Time allowed preparation, ledgers, advice, possibly several conversations with Seren that would begin calmly and then stop being calm as more details were introduced.

Moreen glanced at him. “You should not ignore that.”

“I wasn’t planning to ignore the royal trumpet man.” Alric said looking at the seal and ribbon.

“Good.”

Alric tucked the scroll carefully under one arm and looked back into the inn, where barrels filled the common room and maps covered the table. A few minutes ago the city had become clearer. Now the kingdom had noticed him.

The timing felt personal.
