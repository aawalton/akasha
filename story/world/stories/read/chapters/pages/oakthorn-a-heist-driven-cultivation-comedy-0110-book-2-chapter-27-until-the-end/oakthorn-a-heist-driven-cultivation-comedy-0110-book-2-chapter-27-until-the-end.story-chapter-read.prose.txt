Death had many smiles. Orro had witnessed most of them.

The exiled assassin’s hands trembled—not from fear, but from the absolute certainty that surrounded them. Hundreds of undead Arbiters filled every corner of the vast chamber, their empty eye sockets blazing with sickly green light as they turned as one toward Chubbs. The toddler stood behind the altar, golden eyes wide but unafraid as the horde converged with singular purpose.

With the altar activated by her touch, she had become their primary target. And now they moved with coordinated intent, desiccated bodies navigating the chamber with unnatural grace, closing in from all directions.

“Stay behind me,” Orro commanded, positioning himself between the advancing undead and the child. His broken blade pulsed with orange light, drinking in the unnatural energy that animated these long-dead guardians. The weapon felt unnaturally heavy in his exhausted grip, his muscles trembling from the sustained assault they’d already weathered.

Jer moved to his side, the acrobat’s usual smile strained but present. “So,” he remarked with forced casualness, “I count three hundred at minimum. Those are much better odds than I expected. I thought we’d be facing at least a thousand.”

“Your optimism remains terrifying,” Orro replied, eyes never leaving the advancing horde.

Behind them, Rayka clutched Chubbs protectively, her daggers ready despite the tremors of exhaustion that ran through her arms. The situation had deteriorated from desperate to hopeless in the span of heartbeats. There was no path of escape, no strategy that could overcome such numbers, no miracle waiting to save them.

For the second time in his life, Orro faced the absolute certainty of defeat.

The first undead reached them, skeletal fingers grasping for purchase. Orro’s blade flashed, orange energy igniting as it connected with desiccated flesh. The creature shrieked as its form disintegrated, green light spilling from widening cracks as the magic that animated it was severed.

But for each one he destroyed, three more pressed forward, an endless tide of decay and ancient purpose.

“Get Chubbs to the far corner,” he ordered, cleaving through another attacker. “We’ll hold them here as long as we can.”

“No,” Rayka countered, her voice steady despite the fear that tightened her features. “We stay together. If we die, we die as one.”

The words struck Orro with unexpected force, slipping beneath his practiced detachment to touch something he’d thought long buried. Not his professional pride or tactical mind, but something dangerously close to his heart.

Bunny chirped anxiously at their feet, the dragonling’s scales reflecting the ghostly light of the chamber as he positioned himself protectively in front of Chubbs. Despite his diminutive size, his stance was pure predator—alert, ready, fearless.

“They’re not supposed to be here,” Chubbs observed with the casual certainty of a child commenting on misplaced toys. “The bones are angry.”

Orro exchanged a quick glance with Rayka, the toddler’s words carrying implications they had no time to unpack. The undead surged forward, their coordinated assault intensifying as if responding to some unheard command.

“Jer,” Orro called as he dispatched another attacker with brutal efficiency, “we need space. Any ideas?”

The acrobat launched himself upward in a perfect somersault, evading grasping hands before landing on an undead’s shoulders. He used the momentum to drive the creature into three others, creating a momentary gap in their ranks.

“Working on it,” Jer replied, flipping backward to land beside Orro. His face was flushed with exertion, but his eyes remained sharp, calculating. “They’re funneling toward Chubbs specifically. If we create a corridor away from her—”

“Using ourselves as bait,” Orro finished, understanding immediately. “Draw them off, create an opening.”

“Exactly,” Jer confirmed, spinning to dispatch another undead that had broken through their line. “Though I typically prefer my bait roles to involve more applause and fewer walking corpses.”

Despite everything, Orro found himself appreciating the acrobat’s gallows humor. It was easier to face certain death alongside someone who could still find something to smile about—who could transform this nightmare into something almost manageable through sheer force of will.

“On my mark,” he decided, gathering his remaining strength. “Three seconds.”

Jer nodded, readying himself for their desperate gambit.

“Rayka,” Orro called, unable to look back as he continued holding the front line, “when we move, take Chubbs and head for the passage on the right. Don’t wait for us.”

The unspoken implication hung between them—that this was goodbye, that he didn’t expect to survive this maneuver. That this final sacrifice would have to be enough.

“Orr—” she began, his nickname carrying a universe of unspoken emotion.

“Now!” he roared, cutting off whatever she might have said.

He and Jer exploded into motion, darting away from the altar in opposite directions. The effect was immediate—the undead horde split, a significant portion breaking away to pursue these new targets while others continued toward Chubbs.

Orro carved a bloody path through the ranks, his broken blade glowing brighter with each strike as it consumed the unnatural energy animating the undead. He didn’t look back, couldn’t afford to—his entire focus narrowed to the next strike, the next opponent, the next breath.

Across the chamber, Jer demonstrated why his acrobatic skills had earned him a reputation even among master thieves. He moved like liquid lightning, each leap and twist perfectly calculated to maximize impact while minimizing exposure. The cooking knife in his hand—a ridiculous weapon by any professional standard—nonetheless found vulnerable points with surgical precision, sending undead crumbling to dust with each strike.

Their gambit worked—for precious moments, the pressure on Rayka and Chubbs eased, the undead ranks thinning as many redirected to pursue these new threats.

But it couldn’t last.

Orro felt his strength waning with each passing second. The crystal shard embedded in his shoulder ground against bone with every movement, sending fresh waves of agony through his system. His vision blurred at the edges, focusing narrowing to an ever-shrinking circle of immediate threat.

A glance across the chamber showed Jer similarly flagging, the acrobat’s movements losing their fluid grace as exhaustion took its toll. They had created a temporary diversion, nothing more. Soon they would be overwhelmed, and the horde would return its full attention to its primary target.

“Fall back to Rayka!” Orro called, already fighting his way toward where she defended Chubbs near the promised passage. “Regroup!”

Jer acknowledged with a quick nod, executing a series of flips that carried him over the heads of his pursuers. They converged at the passage entrance, forming a defensive triangle around Chubbs as the undead closed in once more.

“Nice effort,” Jer panted, blood trickling from a cut above his eye. “For a moment there I thought we might actually survive this.”

“Don’t count us out yet,” Rayka replied, though her voice carried little conviction. “There’s still—”

The rest of her sentence died as the floor beneath their feet suddenly collapsed, plunging them into darkness. Orro’s stomach lurched as they fell, his hands instinctively reaching for purchase that wasn’t there. He caught a glimpse of Rayka clutching Chubbs to her chest, of Jer twisting mid-air in an attempt to control his descent, of Bunny spreading damaged wings in a futile effort to slow his fall.

Then impact—less severe than he’d feared, but still enough to drive what little air remained from his lungs. They’d landed in some kind of tunnel, its dimensions claustrophobically tight after the vast chamber above. The walls pressed close on either side, the ceiling low enough that even Jer couldn’t stand fully upright.

“Is everyone alright?” Rayka’s voice came from the darkness, strained but steady.

“Define ‘alright,’“ Jer groaned, the rustle of movement suggesting he was checking himself for new injuries. “If you mean ‘not converted to a fine paste upon landing,’ then yes, surprisingly.”

“Chubbs?” Orro called, forcing himself into a sitting position despite the protest of his damaged ribs.

“I’m okay,” the toddler’s voice replied, unnervingly calm despite their circumstances. “But the tunnel doesn’t like us. It’s getting smaller.”

Orro’s eyes had begun to adjust to the gloom, allowing him to make out basic shapes in the darkness. What he saw confirmed Chubbs’s assessment—the tunnel was indeed constricting, its walls drawing inward with glacial but undeniable purpose.

“Move,” he ordered, forcing himself to his feet despite the agony that lanced through his injured shoulder. “We need to find an exit before we’re crushed.”

They stumbled forward through the narrowing passage, guided by the faint bioluminescence that clung to the tunnel walls like a reluctant afterthought. Rayka carried Chubbs, while Bunny scampered ahead, occasionally chirping in what might have been encouragement or warning.

The tunnel continued to constrict, forcing them into a hunched posture, then a crawl as the ceiling dropped lower with each passing minute. The air grew stale, carrying the scent of ancient decay and something else—something metallic and sharp that reminded Orro of blood.

“I think we’re heading deeper,” Jer observed, his voice echoing strangely in the confined space. “Every turn seems to slope downward.”

Orro had noticed the same pattern. Each fork they encountered presented options that all eventually descended, as if the crypt itself were guiding them toward some predetermined destination in its deepest recesses.

“No choice but to keep going,” he replied, wincing as his injured shoulder scraped against the rough stone wall. “Unless you’ve developed a sudden talent for tunneling through solid rock.”

“Working on it,” Jer quipped, though the jest lacked his usual commitment. “Give me another crisis or two, and who knows what hidden talents might emerge?”

They continued their descent, the tunnel growing increasingly hazardous with each passing yard. Sections had partially collapsed, forcing them to squeeze through gaps barely wide enough for a child, much less fully grown adults. Sharp protrusions from the walls tore at clothing and skin alike, leaving them bleeding from dozens of minor lacerations.

And still the passage constricted, its dimensions shrinking with inexorable patience.

“We can’t keep going much further,” Rayka said finally, her voice tight with controlled panic as she struggled to shield Chubbs from the worst of the tunnel’s architectural hostility. “Another hundred yards at this rate and we won’t fit through at all.”

She was right. The passage had narrowed to the point where they were forced to proceed in single file, shoulders scraping against stone on both sides. Ahead, it constricted further still, becoming a bottleneck that would soon prove impassable.

“There has to be another option,” Jer insisted, though his usual optimism sounded strained even to his own ears. “A side passage we missed, or—”

A familiar sound interrupted him—the dry, rattling wheeze of desiccated lungs drawing breath they didn’t need. From the darkness ahead came the clatter of bone against stone as something dragged itself toward them.

“You have got to be kidding me,” Jer muttered, disbelief evident in every syllable.

The first undead Arbiter rounded the bend in the tunnel, its skeletal form somehow compressing to navigate the narrow passage. The green light in its eye sockets illuminated the confined space with sickly radiance, revealing dozens more behind it.

“Back,” Orro ordered, already reversing direction. “Back the way we came.”

But retreat proved equally impossible. The passage behind them, which had seemed marginally more traversable moments before, had continued its inexorable constriction during their brief pause. What had been barely passable was now an impenetrable bottleneck, the walls drawn together like pursed lips refusing entry.

“We’re trapped,” Rayka realized, clutching Chubbs tighter as the undead advanced from the only direction still open to them. “They herded us down here deliberately.”

The tactical brilliance of it—if such a term could be applied to a structure that might not even possess conventional intelligence—was undeniable. The crypt had funneled them into a killing zone tailored specifically to negate their advantages. The confined space rendered Jer’s acrobatics nearly useless, while the narrow dimensions prevented Orro from executing the sweeping strikes that had proven so effective above.

They were rats in a collapsing pipe, with predators closing from the only exit.

“Get behind me,” Orro commanded, drawing his broken blade despite the tunnel’s constraints. The orange glow illuminated their desperate situation with merciless clarity. “Jer, protect Rayka and Chubbs if they break through.”

“When they break through,” Jer corrected grimly, the first time Orro had heard him acknowledge the inevitability of their defeat. Yet even now, facing certain death, the acrobat managed a smile—strained but genuine. “It’s been an honor fighting alongside you, grumpy.”

“Likewise,” Orro replied, surprised by how deeply he meant it.

The undead reached them.

In the confined space, combat transformed from art to brutal necessity. Orro’s blade found undead flesh with mechanical precision, carving through bone and desiccated muscle with each carefully controlled strike. But the tunnel’s dimensions prevented proper follow-through, robbing his attacks of their usual devastating power.

Still, he created a chokepoint, a bottleneck within a bottleneck, forcing the undead to come at him one at a time. Each fell to his blade, creating a growing barrier of unmoving bodies that further restricted the passage.

Behind him, Jer had positioned himself as the second line of defense, dispatching any undead that managed to slip past Orro’s guard. The acrobat had adapted to the confined space with remarkable ingenuity, using the tunnel walls themselves as leverage for kicks and strikes that would have been impossible in open terrain.

For several minutes, they held their position, creating a buffer zone between the undead and where Rayka protected Chubbs in the minuscule space behind them. But their situation remained fundamentally unsustainable—a delaying action against an enemy that didn’t tire, didn’t bleed, didn’t fear.

Orro felt his strength ebbing with each passing minute. The crystal shard in his shoulder ground against bone with every movement, sending fresh waves of agony through his nervous system. His vision blurred at the edges, focus narrowing to the next strike, the next undead, the next breath.

Beside him, Jer fared little better. The acrobat’s movements had lost their fluid grace, becoming increasingly mechanical as exhaustion took its toll. A jagged cut across his forehead leaked blood into his eyes, forcing him to blink constantly to maintain visibility.

And still the undead came.

Their temporary advantage—the chokepoint created by fallen bodies—began to work against them as the passage filled with immobile corpses. Orro found himself fighting not just the advancing horde but the increasingly restricted space as the tunnel continued its gradual constriction around them.

“I can’t—” he began, then broke off as his blade caught on a protruding section of wall, the momentary hesitation nearly costing him his life as skeletal fingers clawed for his throat. He recovered just in time, severing the offending limb before it could find purchase. “Can’t maintain this position much longer.”

“Open to alternatives,” Jer gasped, dispatching another undead that had slipped past Orro’s guard. “Preferably ones that don’t end with us joining these fashion disasters in undeath.”

“Could use that famous optimism right about now,” Orro replied through gritted teeth, cleaving through another undead’s skull.

“Never left,” Jer assured him, his smile returning despite the blood that streaked his face. “Just weighing my options. Die heroically defending friends, or flee screaming into the night? Tough call.”

“There’s no flight option here,” Orro reminded him grimly.

“Details, details.” Jer dispatched another undead with a precision strike to its skull, his movements economical despite his exhaustion. “At least we’re going out in style. That has to count for something.”

And strangely, it did. Orro found unexpected comfort in the acrobat’s refusal to surrender his essential nature, even in the face of certain death. Where Orro met the world with caution and calculation, Jer faced it with open arms and laughter—different approaches to the same ultimate destination.

For the first time, Orro didn’t consider the younger man’s attitude a liability, but a strength—perhaps even one he himself could learn from.

But those reflections would have to wait for another life. In this one, death approached with the inexorable patience of the truly inevitable.

Orro’s final dagger shattered—not the entire weapon, but a crucial section of the already-chipped edge. The failure sent him stumbling backward, directly into Jer, disrupting their carefully maintained formation. The undead Arbiters seized the advantage, surging forward through the momentary gap in their defense.

“Back!” Orro shouted, drawing a conventional dagger to replace his broken weapon. The lesser blade would be far less effective against these supernatural opponents, but it was better than bare hands. “Fall back to Rayka!”

They retreated the few steps available to them, consolidating into a final defensive position around Rayka and Chubbs. The tunnel had constricted further during their battle, the walls now close enough that Orro could touch both simultaneously with outstretched arms. The ceiling had lowered to the point where even Rayka couldn’t stand upright, forcing them into an awkward crouching posture that sapped what little strength remained.

“Hell of a place to end it,” Jer observed, his smile faded but not entirely gone as he assessed their final stand with remarkable composure. “Always thought my dramatic exit would involve more audience appreciation and possibly pyrotechnics.”

“Sorry to disappoint,” Orro replied, a strange calm settling over him as he accepted the inevitability of what came next. Death had been his companion for most of his life—first as a concept during his training, then as a profession during his years with the Grassblade, and finally as a constant shadow during his exile. That it had finally caught up to him seemed almost... fitting.

His only regret was that he couldn’t save the others. That Rayka would fall alongside him. That he would never again see her smile in the morning light, or hear her laugh at Cade’s ridiculous antics, or watch her face soften when Chubbs did something unexpectedly endearing.

As if sensing his thoughts, Rayka met his gaze over Jer’s shoulder. No words passed between them, but none were needed. Years of unspoken understanding, of actions that spoke louder than any declaration, of moments stolen between missions and dangers—all compressed into a single look that contained multitudes.

I’m sorry, his eyes said.

I know, hers replied. And in that knowing was not just acceptance, but forgiveness. Understanding. All he’d ever sought and never believed he deserved.

The undead reached them.

Orro braced himself for the final assault, dagger held in a reverse grip that would allow maximum mobility in the confined space. Beside him, Jer shifted into a defensive stance, his cooking knife looking woefully inadequate against the encroaching horror.

Rayka positioned herself in front of Chubbs, her own daggers ready despite the exhaustion that trembled through her arms. Bunny chirped defiantly at their feet, scales bristling as he prepared to sell his life dearly alongside his chosen family.

The first undead lunged forward, desiccated fingers grasping for Orro’s throat. He slashed upward with his dagger, the blade catching at the joint where jaw met skull. The creature’s head separated from its body, but its hands continued their relentless advance, finding purchase on his arm.

Three more surged through the gap, overwhelming Jer’s defense with sheer numbers. The acrobat cried out as skeletal fingers tore into his flesh, drawing first blood in what promised to be a swift and brutal end.

“Get down!” Orro shouted, using his remaining strength to shove both Jer and Rayka toward the floor. If they were to die, he would ensure he fell first, his body a final shield for those he had failed to protect.

The undead swarmed over him, their weight driving him to his knees as bony fingers tore at his armor, seeking vulnerable flesh beneath. Pain lanced through him as something sharp—a blade or talon—found the gap between his pauldron and chest piece, slicing through muscle.

This was it. The end. His final stand.

Then a blinding flash of silver-gold light erupted from the passage behind the undead, so intense that Orro was forced to shield his eyes despite the imminent threat to his life. Heat washed over him in a concentrated wave, carrying with it the unmistakable scent of destruction magic.

The undead nearest the source disintegrated instantly, their forms unable to withstand the devastating energy that poured toward them. Those further back fared little better, green light spilling from eye sockets and joints as whatever magic animated them was forcibly dispersed.

And through the carnage strode a familiar figure, silver-gold energy swirling around his hands like living flame, eyes blazing with the same intensity. Behind him came others—Elena with her whip-blade already carving through undead ranks, Nora with her massive sword cleaving through bone and armor alike, Gav transformed into a lupine nightmare that tore through the remaining attackers with savage efficiency.

“Sorry we’re late,” Cade called, his voice carrying over the chaos as he dispatched three more undead with a casual flick of his wrist. “Traffic was murder. You wouldn’t believe the detours we had to take.”

Relief crashed through Orro with such force that he momentarily forgot the pain of his injuries. They weren’t dying here. Not today.

“About time,” he managed, though the words lacked his usual edge. “We were running out of witty one-liners.”

“Impossible,” Cade replied with a grin that contained equal parts exhaustion and triumph. “Jer alone has an infinite supply.”

The tide of battle shifted with stunning rapidity. What had been an inevitable slaughter transformed into a coordinated assault as the reunited crew fell into familiar patterns, each covering the others’ weaknesses, amplifying their strengths. Even Bunny found renewed purpose, darting between combatants to hamstring undead ankles or distract attackers at crucial moments.

Within minutes, the last of the undead crumbled to dust, leaving only exhausted defenders standing in the confined tunnel.

“That,” Jer announced to no one in particular, “was entirely too close for comfort. I vote we never do it again.”

“Motion seconded,” Elena agreed, flicking black ichor from her whip-blade with practiced disgust. “Though I have to say, your haircut was already atrocious, and those dead bastards didn’t improve it one bit.”

Jer patted his blood-matted hair with exaggerated concern. “What are you talking about? This is the latest style in near-death experiences. Very exclusive salon, you wouldn’t understand.”

Despite everything—the pain, the exhaustion, the lingering certainty of how close they’d come to annihilation—Orro felt something unexpected bubble up from deep within. Not quite a laugh, but certainly the closest he’d come in longer than he could remember.

“Are you... smiling?” Cade demanded, mock horror painting his features as he surveyed the assassin. “Who are you, and what have you done with our Orro?”

“Temporary insanity brought on by blood loss,” Orro replied, though he made no effort to suppress the expression. “Don’t get used to it.”

While the others bantered, Cade made his way to Chubbs, kneeling to bring himself to her eye level. The toddler goddess regarded him with solemn golden eyes that contained knowledge far beyond her apparent years.

“You okay, squirt?” he asked softly, genuine concern replacing his earlier levity.

“The bones wanted to hurt me,” she replied, her small voice steady despite the horror she’d narrowly escaped. “They were very angry.”

“Well, the bones can go straight to the deepest hell,” Cade replied, gathering her into a protective embrace. “No one gets to hurt my sister on my watch.”

The simple declaration—my sister—struck Orro with unexpected force. There was no hesitation in Cade’s claim, no qualification or uncertainty. Just absolute conviction that transcended blood ties or conventional kinship.

Family, not by birth but by choice. By shared danger and sacrifice. By moments like this one, where death brushed close enough to leave its mark, yet somehow they emerged intact—changed, perhaps, but unbroken.

“We need to keep moving,” Nora announced, ever practical as she studied their surroundings. “This tunnel is still constricting, and we’re not safe yet.”

“The center,” Gav agreed, his partial transformation receding to reveal his human features once more. “All paths lead to the center, where the Crown waits.”

“And Bernard,” Elena added grimly. “Let’s not forget our friendly neighborhood rival with delusions of competence.”

They gathered their strength, supporting each other where injuries threatened stability, sharing what meager supplies remained to bind wounds and ease pain. Orro found himself between Jer and Rayka, an arm around each as they navigated the continuing descent.

“You know,” Jer said quietly, the words meant only for Orro as they made their way forward, “for someone who prides himself on deadly silence, you’re not half bad as a battle partner.”

“High praise from the man whose idea of stealth is announcing his arrival to every guard within earshot,” Orro replied, though without his usual acid.

“Hey, that’s just my marketing strategy. They can’t appreciate my skills if they don’t know I’m there.” Jer’s smile faded into something more sincere. “But seriously. Thanks for not dying back there. Would’ve made things awkward if I had to explain to Cade how I let his grumpy shadow get himself killed.”

Orro considered a deflection—his usual response to anything approaching genuine emotion. But something about their shared brush with death made such evasion feel... dishonest. Inadequate.

“Thank you,” he said instead, the words unfamiliar on his tongue. “For the optimism. For refusing to surrender it, even when logic dictated otherwise.”

“Well, logic’s overrated,” Jer replied with a careless shrug that Orro suspected concealed deeper consideration. “Makes for boring stories. And I’ve always preferred the interesting ones, even if they’re less likely to end with everyone alive.”

“In this case,” Orro acknowledged, “I find myself grateful for your preference.”

They continued their descent, the reunited crew moving with renewed purpose despite their exhaustion and injuries. Whatever awaited them at the heart of the crypt—Bernard, the Crown, or some horror they couldn’t yet imagine—they would face it together.

And for the first time in longer than he could remember, Orro found himself thinking not just of survival, but of what might come after. Of Rayka’s smile in the morning light, of Jer’s ridiculous jokes, of Chubbs’s innocent wonder at the world.

Of a future worth fighting for, not just a past worth escaping.
