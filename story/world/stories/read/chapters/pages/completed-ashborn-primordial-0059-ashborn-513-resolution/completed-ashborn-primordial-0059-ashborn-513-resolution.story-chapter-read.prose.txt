The days that followed blurred together, and not in a good way.

Vir was trapped in a living nightmare, and when he did attempt to escape to the land of sleep, he was met with visions of black machines and the pain of living through his soul being ripped by forces unknown, his body split as he tried to channel impossible levels of prana through him.

He’d wake up screaming, as he always did, though now he awoke to a reality that proved nearly as bad.

Classes continued as they always had, but Vir found himself unable to focus, even in those he attended purely to observe what changes the Fateweavers had wrought.

Professors droned on about prana circulation and affinity theory while Vir sat in the back, Prana Vision active, watching the hollow-eyed students around him burn fragments of their souls with every spell cast.

The mejai of this universe had considerably more damaged souls compared to the one he’d just come from, first-year students included.

It spoke to the far direr alternate history that plagued this place.

Maiya fared no better. She went through the motions, scouting the city and cycling prana, even sparring occasionally with other students, but the light had gone out of her eyes. They were going through a pantomime of normalcy in an alien world, desperately grasping at straws.

To their immense relief, the wolves had returned on the second day, slinking through their door with their tails between their legs.

Vir had never seen them so frightened. They refused to leave the room, huddling in corners and whimpering at the shadows, aware of the wrongness that tainted this place.

By the end of the week, Vir and Maiya had both fully recovered their power. The recycler tattoos had done their work, reclaiming the vast oceans of prana that had been rendered inert by this universe's twisted rules. Black flames danced at Vir's fingertips whenever he wished and Maiya could cycle through her affinities with ease. All of them, for she was now an omni-elemental. The first in history.

It was an accomplishment worth celebrating with feasts and dancing, but here, it may as well have been a drop in an ocean.

What good was individual power in the face of such a foe?

Vir had held out hope that no one had truly been erased. A foolish, desperate hope that a resistance still existed somewhere, hidden from sight.

That hope died when he made inquiries.

No one in Alt Ashani had even heard of a Saint Amara. The name meant nothing to students, professors, or the general populace. When Vir asked about humanity's savior or some grand plan to turn back the Ash, he received only confused looks and hollow laughs.

“There is no plan,” one old mejai had told him. “There is only survival. One day at a time, until the Ash claims us all.”

If his words didn’t say it all, his gaunt face and sunken, hollow eyes certainly had.

Without Amara and the hope she’d brought, the city had changed, and not for the better.

The grim determination Vir had seen before had devolved into something darker. The mejai pushed themselves harder than ever, and Vir could only watch helplessly on with his Third Eye Chakra as they burned away significant portions of their souls to accomplish it.

He couldn’t even imagine the pain they must have endured. The agony of feeling one's very essence consumed, day after day...

He knew it all too well.

Maiya grew more withdrawn with each passing day. She stopped going to the Bard and the Brew, stopped seeking out Ira or Tiyana. She would sit by their window for hours, staring out at nothing, her fingers absently stroking one of the wolves' heads.

“It's like watching a dream fade,” she said one evening. “I’m beginning to question if that Ira and Tia were even real. If I really beat Mina, or if it was all fake. It feels more distant every day. Like holding onto the last drops of water in my hands.”

Vir understood. The weight of the old reality was slipping away, replaced by this new, bleaker version of events. Soon, he feared, even their memories would feel like nothing more than wishful thinking.

At this rate, they’d inevitably lose… and Vir still didn’t have a plan.

— — —

Some days later, Vir found himself on a high rooftop alone, staring out at the city that sprawled beneath him, its spires and streets covered in soot, with the Ash Boundary looming like the death sentence it was in the distance. The border between civilization and oblivion. Beyond that was an endless gray expanse that had grown to consume everything.

Vir's mind wandered, thinking of the problem from every angle. The Fateweavers could rewrite reality itself. They had erased Amara, erased the Vimana, erased any hope this universe might have had. And they'd done it so completely that only he and Maiya remembered the truth.

Why? What had triggered such a drastic response?

His visit to the Vimana, obviously. But why was that such a threat? What about his presence there had necessitated unmaking an entire plan, erasing a savior, rewriting—!?

Vir froze.

What if we've been thinking about this wrong?

All this time, they'd been treating the Fateweavers like humans. Like beings with agendas and plans and political machinations. Entities that thought like them.

But what if that wasn't the case at all? Yuma in the Imperium universe had said it herself, hadn't she? That they more resembed forces of nature than anything mortal?

Vir's heart began to race as the pieces fell into place. He knew precious little of them—was only one of a handful in all of history who’d ever even been aware of their existence—so what if they had it all wrong?

He turned and ran, leaping from rooftop to rooftop until he reached the building that housed their modest apartment, taking the stairs three at a time.

He burst through the door to find Maiya sitting with the wolves, looking up in surprise.

“Vir? What—”

“We’ve been going about this wrong,” Vir said, pacing the small room. “Our enemy, what they are, and how they operate. What if we came at it from a different angle?”

“Slow down,” Maiya said, rising to her feet. “What are you talking about?”

“We've been treating them like an enemy with an agenda,” Vir said. “Like humans, or demons, or even gods. Beings that make choices, that have goals and desires and can be understood on some level.”

“Well, they certainly seem to behave that way,” Maiya said, frowning. “Some of the time, anyway.”

“But what if it only resembles that because of how monstrously powerful they are? What if they're more akin to an all-powerful Ash Beast?” Vir said. “What if they're not thinking at all, or at least not in any way we'd recognize? What if they're just... driven by base instincts?”

Maiya frowned, considering. “What instincts?”

“The oldest instinct of all,” Vir said. “The desire to survive.”

Understanding dawned in Maiya's eyes. “The changes… You’re saying it sees us as a threat to its continued existence... and so they’re trying to eradicate us by creating a reality that’s more favorable to their survival?”

“They, it, who knows? And it's just a guess,” Vir said. “But if it’s a living organism, it stands to reason it possesses the desire to continue existing like all creatures do. And I think we can safely say it does, based on what we’ve seen.

“It’s not just this. We saw it with the Children of Ash. With how they manipulate events to make the Ash take over. Why? Because they’re evil?”

“No. Because they view intelligent life as a threat…” Maiya replied. “Gods. You think they’re eradicating all intelligence as a self-defense mechanism? That seems rather excessive, don’t you think?”

“They have the power,” Vir said bitterly. “And you have to admit, not much could threaten them if all that’s left are Ash Beasts.”

“So how do we use this?” Maiya asked.

“Well, think of animals. They won’t jump off cliffs or do anything to hurt themselves, yeah?”

“Which means...” Maiya trailed off, her eyes widening.

“Which means we just need to show them that this new reality is actually worse for their survival than the previous one.”

Maiya stared at him for a long moment, then rolled her eyes. “Well, when you put it like that, it sounds so simple. And how exactly do you intend to prove that to them? Seems like this reality is much better for them than Amara’s.”

Vir grinned viciously. “Why, by destroying this entire universe, of course.”

“But that'll kill us,” Maiya pointed out.

“Let me ask you this. If we had no hope left,” Vir said, “don't you think we'd go down fighting? To take them down with us rather than let them live?”

“Absolutely.” Maiya met his eyes, looking deep into his. “But we do have a choice. Right?”

“I intend to use that,” Vir said, pointing at Maiya’s chest. “My guess is we won't actually have to destroy the universe. We just need to prove to them that we're capable and willing to do so. That we're dangerous when cornered.”

Maiya's eyes bulged as the full implications hit her. “You intend to use their ability to read the future against them!”

Vir's grin widened. “It's handy when your enemy can see the effects of actions you haven’t even taken, isn't it? As long as what they see is terrifying enough.”

“Okay,” Maiya said slowly. “But how? What kind of threat could possibly make them restore the old reality? What weapon could we possibly...” She stopped, her face going pale. “That look. I’m not going to like this, am I?”

“You know,” Vir said, his grin becoming even more vicious, “Saunak had a reputation for a reason. While working with him on the airship project, I came across... plans. For when demons banished him into the Ash and all he had in his mind was revenge. Something so terrible I incinerated them the moment I understood what they were.”

Not that the mad Thaumaturge would need anything as mundane as plans to craft it if he wanted to.

“What plans?” Maiya asked, now triply certain she wouldn’t like what he was about to say.

“A prana bomb,” Vir said quietly. “Or more accurately, a method to teach beings of pure prana how to catalyze all pranic affinities, allowing the reaction to expand infinitely, consuming reality itself in a chain reaction of magical annihilation.”

Maiya frowned, working through the implications. “A being of pure prana...” Her eyes widened in shock and horror. “You can't mean...”

Vir’s eyes narrowed. “Yep. Prana Swarms. Teach one how to absorb other affinities, and it’s suddenly no longer just a horror that lurks in the deep Ash. It’ll become an ever-expanding sphere of destruction that nothing can stop.”

“That's insane,” Maiya breathed. “That's... Vir, that's actually insane. Saunak was working on that?”

“He called it a theoretical exercise,” Vir said. “A proof of concept to show that pranic chain reactions were possible. But I caught the bitterness in his voice. I was pretty sure the thought of ending all the realms crossed his mind back when he’d first been banished. Regardless, the principles were sound. I checked them myself before I destroyed the documents.”

“And you never told me about this?” Maiya asked.

“How would you feel knowing there was a weapon capable of ending everything you ever knew?”

Maiya grimaced. “Right… Thanks, I guess.”

“Believe me, I wish I could unsee what I saw that day. Believe me, I tried. Unfortunately, the process is terrifyingly simple.”

“So we find a Prana Swarm,” Maiya said slowly, “teach it to become a weapon of universal destruction, and then... what? Hope the Fateweavers see it coming and panic?”

“Essentially,” Vir said. “Though we'll need to be genuine about it. If they can read intent as well as probability, they'll know if we're bluffing. We have to actually be willing to do it.”

“Are you?” Maiya asked quietly. “Willing to destroy an entire universe? Kill everyone here, including Ira and Tiyana and everyone else we've met?”

Vir was silent for a long moment. “As you yourself said, if the alternative is to die and let them have what they want, then yes. I am.”

Maiya closed her eyes, then nodded. “Gods help me, but you’re right.”

“Then we have a plan,” Vir said. “Or at least the beginning of one.”

“There's just one problem,” Maiya said, opening her eyes. “Where exactly do you intend to find a Prana Swarm? They're not exactly something you see hanging around every corner...”

Vir's grin returned, though there was no humor in it now. “Oh, I know exactly where to find one. Or have you forgotten? There happens to be one lurking deep beneath Daha. Or what’s left of it, anyway.”

Maiya stared at him, then barked a laugh that might have had just a tinge of hysteria in it. “Of course. Of course we’re going into the Ash. Why would it be anything else?”

She walked to the window, looking out at the distant Boundary. “When do we leave?”

“As soon as we’re ready,” Vir said. “But let’s make sure we’re prepared. Without Ashani, we won’t have access to Ash Gates to return. This is a one-way trip.”

“And what about Ashani?” Maiya asked, looking at the goddess’ sleeping form in the other room. “I’m guessing we’re taking her with us?”

Vir shook his head. “No, she’ll just be a liability. If this works, it won’t matter where she is. Where any of us are.”

“Right,” Maiya said. “Everything goes back to the way it was before. To think we’re trying to restore that pathetic state of the world… Life is not without its irony, huh?”

“Better than here, at least,” Vir said with a bitter smile. “Sometimes, that has to be enough.”

Vir moved to stand beside her, both of them gazing out at the gray wasteland beyond the city. Somewhere out there, in that nightmare realm of soot and monsters, they would find their weapon. Their one chance to force the Fateweavers' hand.

“You know,” Maiya said quietly, “a few months ago, if someone told me I'd be planning to destroy a universe just to threaten cosmic entities into undoing reality, I'd have called them insane.”

“And now?” Vir asked.

“Now I'm just hoping it works,” Maiya said. She turned to look at him. “Because if it doesn't, we'll quite literally have killed ourselves for nothing.”

Vir nodded grimly. “Then I suppose we'd better make sure it works.”

The wolves whimpered from their corner, as if sensing the dark path their masters had chosen.

Outside, the denizens of Alt Ashani continued their slow descent into despair, unaware that two outsiders were planning something that would either save them all… or doom them utterly.
