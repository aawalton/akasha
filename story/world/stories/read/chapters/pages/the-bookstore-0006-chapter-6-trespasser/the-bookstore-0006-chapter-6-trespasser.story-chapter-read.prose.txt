“Moruh, keep an eye on the entrance and call me if any customers arrive. And don’t you dare fall asleep on the job.”

Anne gave the familiar a narrow look before heading for the backyard.

The blue feline answered with his usual, “Yes, mistress,” though inwardly he doubted anyone would come. In his opinion, buying this place had been one of the rare mistakes made by his otherwise brilliant mistress. Still, a familiar’s opinion did not outrank duty, and if he had to serve in a half-ruined roadside bookstore, then serve he would.

Besides, being greeted by a talking blue cat in a magus hat would certainly leave an impression on any visitor.

Fortunately, most people were used to familiars. The sight alone would not send them fleeing.

Anne, meanwhile, wanted nothing more than to offer future customers a proper welcome, but the store was still full of problems demanding her attention. The most urgent that morning was the miserable sun cover over her crops. A sandstorm during the night had knocked part of it loose, and now the frame leaned crookedly over the patch of growing plants like a drunk trying to remain standing.

Even blessed seeds had their limits. Morning light did them good, but the midday sun in this region could be brutal enough to scorch young growth in a matter of hours. To protect them, Anne had rigged a simple cloth shade over the field, one meant to let in the gentler early light while softening the noonday blaze.

It would have worked better if her carpentry had been less embarrassing.

“Damn it, can these stakes not be sturdier?” she muttered, yanking one upright again.

She knew one or two spells that might have helped, but she refused to waste grimoire pages on something so minor. That was the burden of being a bookmancer. Learning spells and rituals was easy enough compared to other paths, but choosing what to record was a constant struggle. They could only cast what had been written into the grimoire, their page limit depended on spiritual strength, and once a spell was recorded, it stayed. Permanently.

Her grandfather’s voice rose from memory with perfect clarity. For a bookmancer, strategy is as important as the quality of their recordings.

Anne clicked her tongue and forced the stake deeper into the ground. Books could teach structure, technique, methods, and a hundred solutions, but practical work always reached the point where thought had to surrender to hands. That, too, was something she had learned repeatedly.

While she struggled with the shade frame, someone else stood some distance away, staring at the building with suspicion.

The boy was poorly dressed, sunburned, and tired enough that the road seemed to cling to him. Mid-height, brown-haired, lean in the way of someone used to long effort and inconsistent meals, he stared at the simple wooden sign hanging on the side of a building that, according to his memory, should have remained abandoned.

He could not read the words.

But the painted arrow pointing toward the side entrance was simple enough to understand.

Rick frowned.

He had passed this inn not even a month ago and it had still been sealed, dead, and empty. Now there were fresh cart tracks, hoof marks, animal prints, and signs of recent use all around the place. Under normal circumstances, he would have kept walking. He was tired, and whatever trouble the building held was not his business.

But if some gang had decided to use the old inn as a base, that could become everyone’s business soon enough. So, against his better judgment, he went around instead of approaching the marked entrance.

If there was danger, he was not stupid enough to walk straight into it.

Rick moved through the sparse trees in a careful arc, using what little cover the dry land allowed. He saw two open windows but nothing suspicious through them. No laughter, smoke, or shine of drawn steel. Still, he kept going until he reached the wooden fence surrounding the backyard.

That was when he heard a woman cursing under her breath.

He moved closer, peering through the narrow gaps between the planks.

The sight confused him more than it reassured him.

A tall young woman with dark hair was struggling to straighten some sort of crude shade frame over a patch of plants. Her bearing did not match the place. Neither did her coloring. Even from behind, there was something in the set of her shoulders that suggested she did not belong to the borderlands. Northeastern, maybe. He had heard stories of that kind of look.

Rick spent a few moments trying to make sense of the scene.

Then he made the mistake.

He touched the fence.

The reaction was immediate.

The woman turned so fast that it felt less like a person noticing him and more like a trap springing shut. A dark, ancient-looking book appeared at her side in a bloom of blue light, pages flaring open on their own, and before Rick could even step back, she cast.

“Restrictum!”

Blue force burst from the ground around him.

It was not a flashy spell. No roaring flames, radiant explosion, or grand storm of color. It was much worse. Clean and efficient. The energy twisted up from the earth in narrow bands and locked his legs in place before his body fully understood what had happened. He tried to move and found nothing obeyed him below the waist.

The sensation hit him like cold iron. Every hunting instinct in his body screamed trap.

Anne was already moving toward him.

“Sir, you are trespassing,” she said, voice clear and hard.

Then, after one fast assessing glance, her tone shifted just slightly.

“You are welcome to visit my bookstore, but this area is restricted. Please state your business.”

The warning from the bookstore circle had jolted Anne so badly that it took real effort to keep her legs from trembling. She had imagined threats often enough while preparing the place, but imagining danger and having the circle suddenly announce a living intruder were not the same thing. Still, once she saw him more clearly, her fear eased.

Young. Poorly dressed. Dirty from travel. Small hunt slung over one shoulder. Alarmed, yes, but not dangerous..

Her security worked. That thought arrived with almost embarrassing force. The relief of it steadied her more than pride should have.

Rick, for his part, was trying very hard not to look as frightened as he felt.

“Eh? Miss, I’m sorry. I—no, I didn’t...” He swallowed and tried again. “I didn’t trespass.”

Anne raised a brow.

“Oh? Then shall we fetch the deed and measure exactly where my property begins?”

She meant to sound stern. Instead, the edge of dry mockery slipped in. Not enough to ruin the effect, but enough to show him she was no longer considering whether to kill him.

That was probably for the best.

She had already decided to let him go unless he gave her a reason not to. Still, the lesson was important. A store owner had to be polite, patient, even forgiving at times. Customers would arrive ignorant, intrusive, or rude. That was inevitable. But politeness without backbone invited worse problems. Her master had warned her of that more than once.

Some things had to be discouraged firmly.

Trespassing was one of them.

Rick realized quickly enough that the strange woman did not intend immediate harm. She wanted an explanation, not blood. So he forced himself to breathe and answered more coherently.

“I’m sorry, miss. I was coming back from the forbidden forest and noticed the place had changed. The old inn has been empty a long time. I thought maybe a gang was using it or something illegal was happening here, so...” He grimaced. “I came to look.”

The explanation was not entirely unreasonable.

The state of his clothes, the tiredness in his face, the dirt worked into his skin, and the small game tied over his shoulder all supported the story. Anne examined him for another few moments, then let the spell go.

The blue bindings sank back into the ground so smoothly they might never have existed.

Rick nearly sagged in relief.

In his defense, he told himself, that spell had looked strong enough to stop something much larger than a half-starved hunter. A taurus, perhaps. Certainly a man.

Still, the humiliation bit. He ducked his head, muttered another apology, and vowed privately never to panic like that again.

Anne gestured toward the proper entrance.

“If you truly meant no harm, then come through the front like a civilized customer.”

The word customer caught Rick off guard, but he followed. The moment he entered the bookstore, most of his embarrassment vanished beneath surprise.

He had expected, at best, some magitech shop or improvised trader’s den. Perhaps a potion seller with delusions of grandeur. Instead, he stepped into something he could not immediately categorize.

The six tables scattered through the main room were simple enough, but the walls were lined with shelves—real shelves—and though some were clearly new and rough, they were crowded with books in every size, thickness, material, and color. More books lay stacked in piles waiting to be sorted. The room smelled of wood shavings, paper, dust, old leather, fresh repairs, and something else he did not recognize at first.

Tea, perhaps.

Rick stood still for longer than he intended. So this was what she had meant.

A bookstore.

Not scroll bundles behind glass. Not magic trinkets and bottled nonsense. A place full of real books.

He forgot to offer excuses. Forgot even the woman beside him for a moment. He walked slowly between the shelves, staring openly. Titles meant little to him because he could not read most of them, but their mere presence was enough to unsettle his expectations. He had never seen this many books in one place outside of stories told by people who liked to brag.

After several minutes, his attention shifted from the books to the shelves themselves.

They were rough in places. Not amateurish exactly, but clearly made by someone without long professional experience. The joints were firm, though. The supports were decent. The proportions were sensible. He glanced back toward the entrance, remembering the tall woman wrestling with the crop shade outside.

So she built these too. That realization strangely made the place feel even more unusual.

He knew enough woodcraft to recognize effort when he saw it. His father had insisted on teaching him before the old man decided Rick’s future ought to be spent on more practical work. Technically Rick was not a professional either, but if not for those lessons, he would likely have done worse than this.

Anne let him wander.

She knew better than to interrupt a potential customer too early, especially when curiosity had already done part of the work for her. So she waited, watching the shift in his attention with patient calculation. Wonder first. Then interest. Then the practical inspection of shelves and craft. Good.

Very good.

Once he reached that point, she decided it was time.

“Would you like some tea?”
