I envy them. The little worms. Mayflies. Such small lives, but such clear purposes. Every day, one must eat. Every day, one must sleep. What is to be eaten is well known, how long to sleep is well known, how to bathe, what materials can be worn. Those with power must carve their own paths, uniquely, and moreso as progress is earned. Imagine being so small. Knowing that nothing you do matters. Flitting from one essential, pre-designed, pre-planned task after another, surrounded by others just like you. It’s no wonder they cannot see the possibilities we gift them, the heights beyond their reach. How can they, when they exist in such a state? Where everything is always within a hand’s reach, and must be grabbed now, now, now?

I wouldn’t trade with them, of course. But there is something to envy, in being so utterly small.

–Imperial Marshall Jun Li, Mistress Of Steel And Tungsten, Glory Of Broken Bone, She Of A Million Bloody Fields, ruminating to one of her commanders during a siege.

The initial excavation took her most of a day. Refining it? Building it? That takes a bit longer.

The better part of a week goes by as the work goes on.

Raika gets into a rhythm with it. There’s something profoundly calm, profoundly comforting, about the way that things fall into a pattern, becoming something that everyone acknowledges and enjoys in a semi-equal fashion.

Raika spends her nights rearranging her plants, preparing her supplies, reorganizing the interior of her cabin or compressing dirt into tiles and structure. In the pre-dawn light, she works in her kitchen. She pulls yeast out of collections of mushrooms and fungi she has hidden in the back of her closets, mixing it with flour to make rising dough. She butchers trapped rabbits and fish she’s kept stored, salting and spicing them, leaving them ready for later. She takes some herbs and leaves, roots and tubers, chopping them into dense, chunky salads, storing them for later on.

And then, she spends an hour or two making tea.

She makes it in whole batches, in individual cups, with ritual and without. She practices making it good, and in enough quantities that she can cool it and serve it to the volunteers that keep coming by to help.

She doesn’t ask them to. She doesn’t even particularly encourage it, but they come anyway. They deserve that much, at least.

With the tea steeping and cooling, with bread baking and meat marinating to be cooked, she takes a cup of the worst result of her experiments and walks down the back of the hill, facing the fields.

By then, there’s already movement out there. The villagers of West Laketown aren’t the type to be idle, and in the midst or harvest season, there’s little enough reason to be. She knows that, behind her, she could turn and see boats trawling the cool blue waves of the titular lake, fishing and crabbing, farming bits of algae and seaweed near the banks. In front of her, rice paddies, fields of millet, and rows of leafy vegetables and rooted plants go out for a few miles, out and out until the mounds of earth at the edge of the farms separate them from the wilds beyond.

The hustle and bustle of human life, or at least this version of it, echoes up to her, out and out from miles and miles away.

It’s one aspect of her biology that she never lost, and that she doesn’t regret. It was expensive, holding onto her enhanced senses while her body deteriorated after the Knife, but worthwhile. Now that she has access to her near-limitless resources again, she’s only refined it.

The ability to smell the earth. To taste the clouds in the sky. To hear the breaths and the heartbeats and the idle chatter and songs of hundreds of people, all working in harmony.

When she first transformed, after her first Tribulation in the wilds of the third ring, it overwhelmed. It exhausted and damaged and crippled her. Now, she can’t imagine living without it.

She can shut it off, if she wants. Modify it anytime, sure, but she’s built in mechanisms to limit her perceptions, shifting wavelengths, frequencies, intensities.

But to feel the whole world, so vastly, so intimately, is…

It’s beautiful. It’s intimate, and powerful, and so beautiful it hurts.

Once she’s done feeling it all, she drinks some of the burnt, watery, shit-ass tea.

Then the work continues.

She digs deeper into the hillside. She takes branches and logs that have been put there for this very reason and builds supports into the hillside, and then brings in clay and tiles to build stronger ones around them. She makes tunnels and pipes, creates placements for flooring and walls, designs the space inch by inch.

When the sun is an hour past its highest, and sweat has soaked her robes, she walks back up the hill to her cabin. With the application of a few coals and a metal grate, the smell of spiced meat and fish starts to waft down the hill, mixing with the scent of bread that’s done baking and starting to cool.

The workers come by not long after.

“Any chance you’ve cooked us something tasty this time, you big brute?”

“If I cooked them any better, your wife would get worried,” she replies, tossing him a piece of thinly-cut rabbit meat. La Gao, ever ready for a bite, catches it with his mouth, of course. La Ru beside him chuckles, then flinches as he fails to catch a cut of meat that goes his way instead, splattering a bit of meat-juices on his mustache.

“Oh, shit, that is good,” La Ru says as he scrapes the meat back into his mouth. “What did you do to it?”

“I promised the secret to my spice rubs after we’re done, not before.”

“You promised me the rub that you used last festival, not all of them! Give the boy some insight, will you?”

She snorts, pulling open one of the small buns she’s made and filling it with some cuts of meat and some of the heavy salad she made earlier, wrapping its softness back shut afterwards. “It’s no secret. Sugar, spice, salt. Make it spicy enough to hurt, let it rest in sugar, then salt it on the grill. Everything else is details. Pass this out, will you?”

The buns do, in fact, get passed around, steamed and full of a well-spiced combination of meat and veggies. The tea, left to cool until it’s refreshing, follows not long after, cups of it being passed from one to the next, as men and women fresh from morning and early afternoon chores enjoy the gifts of hospitality in preparation for the work.

Though it’s not without some hiccups.

“Woman, do you not eat?” La Gao asks her, munching happily. “Unless you’ve been out fishing every day where I’m not looking, I don’t know how you have so much fish here. And I could swear I had La Ru over there bring you some rabbits not last week?”

“I’m good at preparing things.”

“And I don’t remember you having so many cups when we finished building you that cabin! You got a clay oven you haven’t told us about?”

“Nope.”

She takes a sip of her own tea, re-warmed to be scalding hot and only further ruined by it.

“Fine then. Keep your secrets,” he grumbles good naturedly.

She admires that about him, and all the other people enjoying the pre-work snack. No one around them flinches from the questions. If he weren’t the one asking, it’s likely someone else would, even if they’d do it just a little bit less bluntly.

Most of the time, if someone has a bunch of weird, unexplainable coincidences going on, it’s only good sense not to poke around. The business of cultivators is not one to be trifled with, and not particularly safe for mortals. But he just… asks. And apparently, they trust her enough to answer or not.

She prefers not to, and they seem to respect that.

She’s a weird lady who can make teacups as easily as she can make blends of herbs and medicine and spices. And who, in spite of missing an arm and needing a cane to walk, excavated a few tons of earth and clay in less than a day. And who they’re actively helping to build a house for people she admits are cultivators.

It’s nice.

She could build this place herself. It wouldn’t cost… well, almost anything, really, to reach out through the altered space within herself to other parts of the world. She could craft a structure of flesh and bone that could tower to the heavens, smelt alloys that can resist every natural phenomenon and drink the winds of Qi of the world to grow further. She could probably design an array complicated and silly enough that it could just tell the world how to be, shaping a manse out of symbols and Qi.

She could build a place that is as much physically a part of her as her own hand and feet, and within it, a palace, going on for miles and miles and miles.

It’s strange, feeling what she can do. Even after years, it’s still interesting, examining the memories that became a part of her after the fact, and even more interesting feeling what she can do herself, now.

When she came into being, Raika was still all… one person. One single, unified whole, with only the slightest distinctions between carefully built, partitioned-off parts of herself. As a centipede of Blacksteel and gunpowder, an abomination of war and wanton devastation, she waged violence along the breach in the Wall, and saw an opportunity. Through a crevice in the Imperial defenses, the barest millisecond of lapse, she launched a piece of herself over that barrier, and left it to grow on its own.

Not much later, she died.

It was luck that saved that part of her. Luck, and Imperial magic.

The Wall is more than just a high barrier. More than just an architectural marvel of violence. Something of the Empire is tied to it, acting as a foundational barrier against the outside.

Her connection to herself had already been strained to near the breaking point. With the entirety of her being save a fragment still on the outside of the Wall, she could barely move, barely exist, barely access her powers, just enough to make a single body to support herself from. She could do enough to anchor herself and spread veins back out and deeper into the fourth ring, though agonizingly slowly.

A backup plan, at most. Something to provide a little extra security to the rest of her, to run in the background while she focused on the more foundational things back out in the wider world.

And then she died.

She died, and that death hunted her across existence. Hunted her across reality. It tore the concept of Raika into as close to nonexistence as it could.

It tried to do the same to that little anchor, that bit of vein and flesh out beyond the barrier, and hit the Wall.

It didn’t stop it. But it slowed it. It slowed it enough that she managed to sever herself from everything, that the death of her other parts was delayed by just an instant. She went from being all one, to being shattered, to being nothing, and in that shattering, she cast every part of her powers away, until there was only a single body left, bereft of her Heart, her Qi generators, her Dao, everything but what fleshcrafting she could fuel with her own, human-scaled body.

It was a long few months, then. Hunting in the wilds. Hiding. Refusing to rebuild any part of herself, because she could still feel it there, in the distance.

The Knife. Her END. Waiting to take her away the moment she became too much like herself.

It wasn’t sustainable. Not long term.

She’d… she’d lost.

Things changed. She wasn’t idle. There was some progress, on a few different fronts. But at the end of it, she had to do what she feared most.

Meeting people.

Choosing a village. Traveling there, slowly, as mortals must. Offering her services, and asking for help.

Agony of agonies.

And entirely worth it.

She’s better now. Reconnected to herself, through no fault or action of her own, really, and introduced to a whole host of new selves.

She can call on herself and break the world, never mind build a little house.

But it’s better this way. Months of being human, or close to it, weren’t wiped away by becoming a demigod again. If anything, it only made her hold herself closer, to keep herself more intimately grounded.

Let the rest of her be Divine. There is Divinity enough in simply being alive.

Now, years later, she still feels the weight of the earth in her hands, and the laugh of company, and the smell of baked buns, and knows it to be good.

She’s not ashamed of what she is, or of who she’s become. She’s ashamed of what she did when she wasn’t herself, what parts of her did and are even now working to make up for, but they weren’t her responsibility then. It’s not shame. It’s just…

She got so powerful so fast. Her relationship to the world changed so dramatically in such a short period of time.

It’s important to remember that there’s more to the world than the sun above or the depths below.

“Right then!” says a voice, snapping her out of her reverie. La Gao is standing, one hand on his hips, the other licking a last bit of juice from his fingers. “Plenty of time for rest later! If you’re done eating, then get to work! This place won’t build itself!”

La Ru, La Gao, her neighbor Lai Shu, and plenty more all grumble or laugh good-naturedly, bringing their tools or just their hands with them.

There’s a lot to do. But time goes fast when there’s good people and good work to be done.

The home she’s building for her son is developing well. It should be done in no less than a week.

She’s almost as anxious about that as she is happy.

She sees him every day. Sees her other disciples every day. Every moment, there’s fresh memory, other parts of herself sharing with her as freely as she does with them.

But soon, they’ll be here.

A bit nerve wracking.

She finishes her tea, and walks down the hillside to get back to work. The second floor is shaping up well, but she’s considering adding a basement. Lots of work still to do.
