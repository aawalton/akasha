Chapter 51

Home

She hadn’t been in Japan for a long time. When she finally returned, the feeling of being home in the first weeks was a bit bittersweet at first. She didn’t have to worry about getting up early or constantly looking over her shoulder.

Although the latter was somewhat relative.

When they had said goodbye to Jinat, Jinat had called Mai aside for a moment to make sure she tried not to draw too much attention to herself. At least for a while.

Getting used to a new routine was something she’d grown accustomed to, but now that things had settled down, she had a lot to think about regarding what had happened over the past few years.

And, of course, the question was what she would do now with her new life away from Wishper. She had plans, but she wondered if she’d be able to carry them out.

Saitama welcomed her with the same fresh air as always, a breath of tranquility amidst the chaos of the world she had left behind.

She immediately moved into the house Miho had left her. That house had been her refuge many times over the past years, the place she had returned to again and again during the brief intervals between missions.

Now, being back there, she felt calm, something she had forgotten in recent years. The house was a secluded corner that seemed frozen in time. The landscape, just as she remembered it, hadn’t changed a bit since the last time she was there. The same distant mountains, the lake with its crystal-clear water, the whisper of the wind through the trees… everything remained the same, unchanged. And that brought her comfort.

It was as if the place offered her a truce, a rest for her mind that, after so many years of fighting and mission after mission, she desperately needed. The fact that nothing in the scenery had changed was a consolation. It gave her the peace she longed for.

She had truly grown old, she thought.

Tokyo, on the other hand, continued on as always. It had changed, of course, but only in small ways. Some of the old locations she had known for over three decades now appeared more modern, with taller skyscrapers, new train stations, and new buildings. The new floating islands generating tidal energy, drones flying back and forth. Amid the global chaos, Japan and parts of East Asia had remained relatively orderly. But inside, Tokyo remained the same bustling, hectic city, always in motion. The noise never ceased, the pressure of daily life, the constant comings and goings of people… everything remained the same.

What had changed, however, was the emergence of the Aeon. The new species, as they were called, had left an irreversible mark on the world. In many Asian countries, especially those at the forefront of artificial intelligence and consciousness studies, Aeon had sparked a new wave of advances.

Thousands of investors were betting on the possibilities this new species brought, leading to expansions in technology and understanding of human consciousness. Research extended beyond AI into the boundaries of biotechnology, synthetic consciousness, and the merging of organic and digital life, although research in the latter would take decades. The world was changing, for better or worse.

Mai couldn’t help but feel somewhat disconnected from all of it. Though missions, battles, and adventures had been central to her life, the reality of these massive technological changes seemed distant from her experiences in the field. Although Wishper used cutting-edge technology, the fact that she was moving through alleys or remote corners of the world made her feel a little out of place walking through the city as if she were just another person. She had used many false identities during her time with Wishper on missions, but now she was just Mai again. It made her feel a little strange walking among people who went to work every day or stopped for coffee while taking in the sights of the city. It wasn’t her world, yet it was impossible to ignore. The technology, the AI, the Aeon, and everything they represented, were heralding a new era, one she began to see more clearly as she walked through the familiar streets of Saitama and Tokyo.

It was a return home, without a doubt. But it wasn’t the same home she had left behind. The world, no matter how much she wanted to ignore it, had changed—and with it, she had changed too. Something inside her no longer felt entirely at peace with all that was happening, though the Saitama landscape offered her a momentary calm. The future, which she had so rarely paid attention to, now seemed to be approaching quickly, unbidden.

After visit the cities, once in her home, Mai liked to sat on the backyard porch of the house, observing the landscape that stretched before her. Just with the shikigami wolves of the house running around, while drinking a beer or a tea, depending on her mood. The soft murmur of the wind through the trees and the occasional bird song gave her a strange peace. As the years passed, she realized that although her life had been marked by violence, missions, sacrifices, and difficult decisions, she had never had the time to truly reflect on what she had accomplished or why she had made certain choices.

Letting things flow that way was difficult.

At forty-two, those words from Miho still echoed in her mind like a distant reverberation. Miho was gone, but her words remained alive in Mai. Time, elusive as it might be, had taught her that doing the right thing was not always clear or easy. She had made decisions she never imagined she would have to make, and she had lost people she never thought would disappear from her life. Yet, had she done the right thing? Had she done enough?

Reflecting alone, she came to only one conclusion: she had done what she could. For a long time. She wasn’t perfect, and she had made mistakes—mistakes from which she had learned.

It hadn’t been much to change the world. Still, it had been enough.

She might be little one, Mai, mothy or a garden gnome. But she had done something with her life.

Mai thought that perhaps feys were a little slower to grasp certain things. After all, they lived much longer than humans. For them, time stretched differently, like a sand that extended endlessly on a desert. But that didn’t mean they were immune to doubts and worries. Even if they lived longer, in the end, just like humans, it depended on each individual what to do with that time. And Mai knew she had spent more time than she would have liked dedicated to the cause, without pausing to consider if her purpose was truly fulfilling her.

In her mind, images of past missions intertwined with the faces of those who were no longer there.

The answer was not clear, but something inside told her that there was still something left to do. Even if the future was uncertain, a spark still burned within her, refusing to go out. And though fatigue and uncertainty were starting to take their toll on her body and mind, Mai could not simply remain idle. After all, time kept moving forward, and she still had a life to decide how to live.

Finally, in December 2046, fatigue caught up with her irrevocably.

The first and most dangerous symptoms came on suddenly; she might fall asleep unexpectedly or even feel dizzy at times. Like when she slipped in the shower and hit her knees so hard that the dizziness vanished as mysteriously as it had come.

The drowsy spells seemed to linger, and she even noticed that her period hadn’t come. Unlike human women, feys could have variable cycles. In Mai’s case, it was once every two months, so when November arrived, she knew what was happening. She wasn’t pregnant. The dizziness, the drowsiness, the fatigue—even though she wasn’t doing any strenuous physical activity.

She knew exactly what it was.

She had been postponing the idea for months, perhaps even years, but she knew her body no longer responded as it once had. The physical and mental wear accumulated over decades of missions, sacrifices, and difficult decisions could no longer be ignored.

She went to the clinic of the Inoue section. And the medical exams confirmed what she had feared: her metabolism had begun to slow considerably, preparing for the inevitable hibernation period that, sooner or later, her kind was destined for.

But Mai would not go to one of the fey centers. Though she had nothing against those places, she had made enough enemies over the years to feel uncomfortable leaving her body vulnerable in the hands of others. She remembered the times she had rescued fey bodies that had been mistreated by humans, displayed in glass cases as if they were mere museum pieces—living statues in the hands of those who exploited their longevity. That image had never stopped haunting her, and though she trusted many of the centers, the idea of being so vulnerable without the ability to protect herself caused unbearable discomfort.

Everyone might be trustworthy, but the thought of falling asleep in a place that could be attacked by some force from the dark world made her feel terrible. After all, on several occasions, she herself had rescued feys in Whisper from places where they were being held captive and kept in a state of hibernation. The moment of awakening was terrifying.

“I’ll stay here,” she thought, when she went back home after knowing the results.

The wolves came running out from passing through the walls to greet her and frolicked around her.

Even though they were nothing more than magic contained within paper dolls created by Aika, Mai felt as though they were a little family. The three of them were Kara, Kuro, and Kiri, and they were the ones Miho had originally created to protect the house in Tokyo—previously, the house in Saitama had only had a barrier.

Mai knelt before Kara, the she-wolf, and pressed her nose against hers.

“You’ll protect me, won’t you?”

A lick from Kara was her only answer, and Mai smiled.

Yes, staying in the house was the best thing to do.

It was the only place where she felt truly safe. Not only was it her physical refuge, but also her emotional one. In the middle of nature, was the best place for her rest. There, where time seemed to stop, she could surrender to the long, restorative sleep her body needed.

She was worried about how long her break would take, but she had no way out.

However, she could not leave everything behind without caution. Even though she had chosen this secluded place to avoid interference, she needed to ensure that, in case of any emergency, someone would watch over her.

She decided to notify Aika.

“Please, come at least once a month,” she asked.

She didn’t want anyone coming too close, but she felt it was necessary to have a pair of external eyes and ears to make sure everything was fine during her hibernation. Aika not only agreed but put a special extra barrier around the house, which would be guarded by some of her familiar wolves. Mai also left some arrangements and money with Aika to handle taxes while she was asleep.

With the decision made, after some days, she began preparing everything necessary for her long rest. Aika would help with the process by performing a deep relaxation ritual, in which a normal person would sleep for two days, but since Mai already exhibited signs of fatigue, this would help her body enter the drowsiness of hibernation while the effects of the ritual lasted.

She closed the windows of the house, turned off the lights, and ensured everything was in order. Her body, so tired, seemed to recognize the need for this pause. The previous days had felt like a wordless farewell—a farewell to a life full of action and decisions, of battles won and inevitable losses. And although uncertainty about what the future might bring lingered in her mind, at that moment she could only focus on resting.

Finally, in a silent stillness, Mai lay down on her futon, surrounded by the peace of the place she had learned to call home. She closed her eyes, letting sleep take her as Aika began the ritual. Thus began her hibernation—a long, deep rest that would disconnect her from the world and immerse her in the stillness of a timeless sleep, where the passing of years had no place.

Somewhere, in some corner of her mind, she knew this would not be an eternal sleep.

But for now, all she could do was let go and rest.
