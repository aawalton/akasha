The queen had not expected the garment to look so innocent and vaguely feminine. That was already suspicious.

The thing came folded in a box with silk lining, carried by nervous assistants, and described with words like support, comfort, and discretion.

The bra, as she had heard it called, lay on a low table inside the curtained fitting area.

It had soft lining, firmer shaped pieces, straps, and fastenings. Stitching that had been done by someone who resented neat stitching but had pride in their work. It did not look like a political object, which meant it was probably about to become one. She had seen simpler saddles.

The curtained area had been arranged with great care. Only women were present inside it. Three of the queen’s attendants stood nearby with the trained stillness of women who knew that reacting too early was a good way to be noticed. Her senior dresser stood closer, expression neutral, eyes sharp. Gwyn, Terven’s assistant, waited beside the table with a measuring cord around her neck.

Terven himself stood outside the curtain. That was all correct and proper.

A male tailor could attend a queen’s fitting but be kept at a respectful distance, provided he stayed on his side of the moat, or screen in this instance. He could answer questions, offer explanations, or even take notes from the far side of the screen, provided no one described the matter too vividly.

“Your Majesty,” Gwyn said, then stopped.

The queen waited, watching Gwyn.

Gwyn swallowed. She spoke slowly. “If it pleases Your Majesty, I will explain each adjustment before I make it.”

It was a practised sentence. Frightened people rehearsed such things that might save them from death, embarrassment, or dismissal. The queen had heard many such sentences, never seeing an improvement.

“Proceed,” she said, turning her gaze to the curtain.

Gwyn lifted the garment. Her hands trembled at first. Then her fingers touched the cloth properly, and the trembling eased.

The queen saw this out of the corner of her eye.

Fear remained in her shoulders, in the line of her mouth, in the way she remembered not to look too directly at the queen for too long.

A cook entering a kitchen after a lord had shouted. A clerk moving to fix a mistake. Some people would displace their fear simply by keeping busy.

“Terven,” the queen said, still observing Gwyn carefully while not facing her directly.

There was a faint rustle beyond the curtain, followed by the sound of a man straightening too quickly.

“Your Majesty?” came the nervous voice of Terven.

“Explain the garment, if you would be so kind,” the queen said slowly, keeping her tone and accent controlled.

“It is to provide support without the restriction of wraps, Your Majesty,” Terven said. His voice was formal and polished. “It improves comfort during extended wear while preserving modesty and the proper line beneath a gown.”

The queen nodded at the answer. It was a practised answer, the typical merchant’s answer.

Gwyn was checking one of the straps for the second time. The queen suspected her hands wanted something useful to do while Terven used words.

“Dignity need not require discomfort, Your Majesty,” Terven added nervously from the other side of the curtain, having received no response.

Gwyn did not smile. Her fingers, however, moved briefly to the lower band as though the word discomfort had reminded her of the real matter.

The queen nodded at her attendants to begin.

There were many privileges to being queen, but this process was not among them. There was no dignity in being dressed and undressed by a roomful of attendants trained not to notice the obvious. Royalty did not make a body less inconvenient. A royal body simply became a new way to annoy professionals.

The outer gown came away, followed by the usual layers. The wraps were loosened last.

The queen did not dislike wraps. They were not important enough for dislike. They were cloth doing a poor job under the long weight of tradition. Like so many things at court.

Gwyn waited until the queen stood ready, then stepped forward. “May I, Your Majesty?” Gwyn said, studying the queen’s shoulders.

“You may,” she said, watching Gwyn’s eyes.

The first moments were awkward because they had to be. No amount of etiquette could make intimate clothing less intimate; it could only make the awkwardness move slowly with better posture.

Gwyn handled it well, the queen thought.

She did not flutter or apologise constantly. She asked permission when needed, touched only where needed, and adjusted the garment with increasing concentration.

The queen watched her in the polished mirror. She had seen squires do something similar with armour before, but Gwyn appeared to be putting their lessons into practice.

Gwyn looked at her body without impropriety. A fool would have stared where they should not. A timid person would not have looked at all and would have become useless. Gwyn looked only where she worked.

She was calculating, the queen noted. The garment settled into place. Gwyn stepped back half a pace. Then her eyes narrowed.

“No,” Gwyn murmured, and the queen’s attendants went stiff.

Gwyn realised what she had done a breath too late and froze with the expression of someone who had just insulted a tax collector’s mother.

The queen looked at her reflection. “No?” she asked with a raised eyebrow and a slight smirk.

Gwyn’s lips parted. For a moment, hesitation and skill battled for control. Skill won.

“The shoulder will do too much work like this, Your Majesty,” Gwyn said, seeing how the straps fell.

Outside the curtain, Terven made a small sound. The queen’s eyebrow lifted at it.

Gwyn continued, stammering slightly. “It will feel fine at first, better than wraps certainly, but by afternoon this pull here will become unpleasant. The lower band should take more; this should guide, not carry.” Gwyn gestured to her own shoulder to explain.

There was a long silence. One of the attendants looked horrified on Gwyn’s behalf. The queen’s dresser looked much more interested.

“Adjust it,” the queen said, her eyes narrowing slightly.

Gwyn breathed again. Then she made the adjustment.

A tug, followed by a settling motion. A slight shift in the angle of one strap and the position of the lower band.

The result was immediate. The queen’s shoulders put down a weight they had carried for too long.

It was not a dramatic moment in the normal sense; no choir entered at a convenient moment. Her body simply stopped doing work it had apparently been doing for so long. The dramatic aspect was displayed in how the queen’s eyes closed in a satisfaction she had not expected.

The queen lifted her arms slightly. She turned. She took a careful breath, then a deeper one. The garment allowed all of it.

The queen stared at herself in the mirror and watched her expression shift to reluctant admiration.

She had expected improvement. Improvement could be dismissed, managed, or purchased in quantity. She had not expected relief.

Women would talk about relief. More importantly, however, they would remember it. A woman could be convinced to endure discomfort for fashion, rank, marriage, ceremony, family, or simple habit, but only until she learned discomfort was optional.

The queen shifted again.

Gwyn was watching the garment, not the queen. Her eyes moved to the side seam, the lower band, the shoulder, then the fall of the queen’s posture.

The queen had known craftsmen like that. They were useful but usually exhausting.

“Riding?” the queen asked, still looking at herself in the mirror.

“For gentle riding, Your Majesty, better than wraps. For proper riding, not this one. The lower part would need to be firmer, with less upward shift and stronger side support, I think,” Gwyn answered, stammering over her words. “There is talk of a riding version, but I do not know the final pattern.”

The queen nodded, then stood upright and tapped her chin. Suspicion flared in her expression once again.

A bad idea died where it stood. A good idea wandered off, married into other problems, and produced children before anyone had found a way to tax it.

The attendants helped her back into the gown when the second revelation followed. The garment was not merely comfortable beneath the dress. It improved the dress.

The front line sat cleaner where the weight fell differently. The shoulders rested. If she pushed her shoulders back, it made her chest seem larger. The queen turned once, and the gown followed instead of fighting her.

Her dresser noticed, and so did Gwyn. The attendants noticed too, though they had the sense not to form expressions about it.

The queen stepped out from behind the curtain fully dressed.

Terven bowed immediately, the queen noted. Too quickly, perhaps, but the man was in a palace chamber with a queen and a dangerous piece of cloth. Some allowance could be made.

“The gown sits beautifully, Your Majesty,” he said, his face pointed at the floor as he held his bow.

The queen gave him a moment. The tailor should have at least looked at her.

Gwyn came from behind the curtain with the measuring cord still in one hand, uncertain where to stand. For one heartbeat, she drifted toward the attendants. Then toward Terven. Then stopped, clearly uncertain.

The queen turned her head toward Gwyn, then shifted it toward Terven. Gwyn followed the indication and moved to stand near him.

The queen walked slowly across the chamber. The garment continued to work. She paused in front of Terven and Gwyn as they continued to bow. The queen looked at them thoughtfully.

And let the silence grow.

The queen’s brow was still furrowed in suspicion, however.

Terven had the name, the shop, the manner, the correct clothes, all the typical merchant parts. He could enter rooms where a man of trade was expected and leave with orders.

Gwyn had the hands, the eye, the irritation at the shoulders, and the knowledge of where the garment failed before it had failed.

Gwyn seemed to think in weight, movement, failure, daily use, and the private resentment of bodies forced to behave for clothing.

The queen stopped walking. “Terven,” the queen said. She smiled, but the smile did not reach her eyes.

He had just straightened, but he bowed again. “Your Majesty.”

“I must ask, are you brilliant, stupid, or merely indifferent?” the queen asked, standing more upright and still carrying the same false smile.

A silence spread through the room. Terven’s mouth opened. No answer emerged.

The queen gestured lightly, not at him, but at the garment hidden beneath her gown. “You have apparently created a garment women want badly enough to abandon dignity in tailors’ streets. It improves comfort and the line of a gown. It will change how women stand, sit, travel, and endure. If my sources are correct, it is also spreading through the city faster than anyone sensible would allow.”

Terven had begun to pale.

The queen continued. Her hand came up, and she rested a finger on her chin. “And yet, you did nothing to protect it.”

Gwyn looked down. She swallowed hard.

“No royal protection, I note,” the queen said, still holding the same pose. “No quiet approach to a patron with enough influence to keep every seamstress in the lower city from copying it badly.”

Terven glanced around the room. His eyes settled on her shoes.

“So, either you are brilliant enough to invent it and foolish enough not to understand it, or there is something else. Such a conundrum,” the queen said, still holding the same smile, speaking slowly and keeping her accent controlled.

Terven’s eyes flicked once toward Gwyn. The queen saw that; Gwyn saw it too and became even more still.

That was not the look of a man checking whether his assistant would support a lie. It was the look of a man remembering where the useful knowledge was.

The queen turned her gaze to Gwyn.

“You understand ze garment,” the queen said. An icy edge entered her voice as her accent crept in.

Gwyn did not answer. The queen had not asked a question, so that was appropriate.

“You know where it sits and where it ’urts. You know when the shoulder is doing too much. You know zat a riding version needs to be different, so you know when to stop speaking as your knowledge ends.” The queen cursed inwardly, trying to regain control of her voice.

Gwyn looked as though praise had become a threat. The queen turned back to Terven.

“So, I am left to wonder whether you are taking credit for your assistant’s work,” the queen said with a deeply icy edge.

The wrong accusation, if placed close enough to the truth, often did better work than the right one. The right accusation gave people a wall to defend. The wrong one made them turn toward the real danger by accident.

“Then the other matter, Terven,” she began before he could answer, “you designed the brewer’s outfit? However, you do not support its style yourself.”

She looked at Terven’s own clothing.

Typically, fine cloth, rich albeit gaudy colour. Merchant prosperity, arranged for witnesses.

Terven began speaking. “Your Majesty, t-t-tailoring is a process of... A commission may begin with one need—”

The sentence was not yet a lie. It was walking toward one with a candle and a prayer.

Then the queen looked upward and sighed.

“Terven,” she said, using a sweeter tone of voice, “you do understand that lying to the Crown is a capital offence.”

One attendant’s breath caught as Gwyn’s face went pale. Terven closed his mouth so quickly his teeth clicked.

For a moment, the only sound was the faint movement of cloth as someone beyond the chamber shifted in the corridor.

Then Terven bent his head. “It was the brewer’s idea, Your Majesty.”

The queen closed her eyes.

Terven continued, now that confession had proven survivable for the first breath. “The first drawing was his, as was the purpose. He described the garment, the straps, the lower band, the need for comfort. He wanted no name attached. He was quite firm on that.”

The queen opened her eyes. That sounded like him too.

Cowardice in the face of gratitude dressed as wisdom.

“I am not even ze least beet surprised,” the queen said with a sigh, a comfortable sigh she noted with disdain.
