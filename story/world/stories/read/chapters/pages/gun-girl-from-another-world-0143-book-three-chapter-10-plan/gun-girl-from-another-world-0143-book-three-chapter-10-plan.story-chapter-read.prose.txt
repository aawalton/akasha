Chapter 10

Plan

“So Sacred Yorin was right, it is your fault.”

I frown under the weight of my best friend’s judgmental gaze. “I said it was possible!”

“When you said it, you were joking.”

I hold out my hands to either side as I turn my head away dismissively. “How was I supposed to know it would go this far?”

“It's you,” he points out unhelpfully.

“Otsui!” I shout back like it’s the greatest comeback imaginable.

Of course, he goes red. “If you knew what you were saying, you’d stop saying it!”

But I grin victoriously. “Oh? Then maybe you should tell me!”

Even. Redder. “Absolutely not!”

Angel pokes her head in, and I’m reminded that she can only understand half of the conversation as she keeps glancing from me over to Ayre before trying to focus on me again. And, going back over what she just heard, my half was certainly the less useful one.

“Remmi? What’s wrong? What’s the problem? Why are you fighting? Is it something I said?”

I turn back to her, and the poor girl is practically distraught. Her eyes are wet, and she’s quivering just enough that her feathers are ruffling. I rush back to her side to stroke her shoulders and soothe her down.

“No, no,” I say quickly. “No, no, no. First off, we’re not fighting, not really. We just tease each other for fun! It doesn’t work for everyone, but it does for me and Ayre. It helps us keep each other in line!” I ignore the thigh elf doing the eye roll again behind me. “And it’s nothing you said. Or, rather, it’s nothing you’re at fault for.” I break my gaze from her and look to the side and toward the ground. “We just … know where you got that idea from.”

She seems almost owl-like when I look to her again, her great, big, green eyes staring up at me like I might be about to share the secrets of the universe. “You do?”

“Ack, don’t look at me like that, kiddo,” I reply bashfully, “it’s not that impressive … It’s … me. It’s me. I’m the one that did it.”

The girl’s facial expression is like she just realized she’s having lunch next to the Governor. Or, well, Regent, I guess. “Heroes can give orders to dungeon cores?!”

Now I’m rubbing the back of my neck in discomfort. “Not really, no … Or, at least, I get the strong impression it’s not part of the normal kit. I wasn’t aware it was possible at all when I did it, and I’m honestly not sure why it listened.”

Angel retracts a bit, frowning as she considers this information. “But then why is it a problem? Isn’t it a good thing that we’re doing what you asked?”

Ooh, and there’s the difficult question. Just hearing it spoken aloud makes me grimace. “It’s not that it’s not a good thing, Angel. It’s just that, well, do you know why adventurers come to dungeons?”

Her face lights up, and she nods fervently. “Yeah, yeah! They come so they can get things they can’t get anywhere else! Like special herbs and treasure! And to pay tribute to the core!”

I nod slowly, careful how I answer. “Yes, those are all excellent bonuses for coming to a dungeon. But there’s a reason we have to do it, even if none of those good things were around. You see, if you leave a dungeon alone long enough, it will eventually expand to claim more territory. You have to wear it out regularly, or else it could expand into roadways, infrastructure, even villages.”

She takes that in for a moment. “What happens to the village?”

“It gets absorbed into the dungeon.”

I’m pleased to see that she’s putting in the time to carefully consider the implications of these things, but I realize there’s more work to be done when her face ignites again with joy.

“Oh, isn’t that a good thing?!” she beams. “The people in the village can come stay with us, and we’ll take care of them and keep them fed and safe!”

“Angel, no!” My immediate rebuke shocks her, but I don’t leave it hanging. “Do you really think the Forest Cavern could maintain an entire village worth of intelligent monsters? What happens to those it can’t support? And even if it could, those people would never be able to leave again! They’d never get to go to their favorite bakery, or visit friends and family a village over! Their lives would end! And the villagers that didn’t get caught in the expansion would lose everything they ever had for generations! Their homes, their land, their places of precious memories, their ability to provide for themselves!”

“Oh …” She slumps quite heavily when she realizes what she’s been suggesting. “Yeah, when you say it like that, it doesn’t sound good at all …” The birb looks up at me when I rub her head. “What do you usually do to stop it, then?”

I give a sigh even though I knew it was coming. “Well, like I said, you have to get the dungeon to tire itself out. It spends a lot of its energy making challenges and monsters to fend off adventurers with, and when we overcome those, much of that built-up energy is lost or absorbed, so that it’s no longer climbing toward an overflow.”

I lean back a bit and cross my arms. “Unfortunately, right now, the Forest Cavern only has a single monster, and no one can bring themselves to attack an innocent child who just wants to greet people and make sure they’re taken care of. So, as it stands, unless we were total psychopaths, it’s currently impossible to cull the dungeon.”

The poor girl looks crestfallen as she follows what I just said to its logical conclusion. “So if I didn’t exist, then people wouldn’t be in danger …”

“No!” I whip back around to her and grab her by her shoulders again, firmly this time, maybe even a little too tightly. “No. Angel, I am banning that line of thought here and now. You are forbidden from thinking along those channels, do you understand me? A good person is never a suitable sacrifice for a maybe.”

This isn’t just because it’s an incredibly self-destructive way to view something from a psychological perspective, either. Angel isn’t just a person that exists independently in the world; she’s a dungeon monster. Depending on the connection between her and the core, if a thought like that is allowed to take root, the core might delete her on the spot in its desire to “behave itself.”

Once I get confirmation from her that she understands, I release her and turn away again, thinking through the problem out loud. “You are not the obstacle here, Angel. The problem is that you and the core don’t know how to fulfill your objectives in a way that doesn’t contradict them through unforeseen consequences. You don’t have the knowledge necessary to make informed design decisions. You’re also running off of a relatively small battery, so you don’t have the wiggle room to make sweeping changes.”

An idea catches in my head, and I wheel back toward her. “Angel, what do dungeon cores get from people?”

“... What?” She blinks back at me in confusion at the sudden topic shift.

“You’ve implied that dungeons know they’re doing things to draw people in,” I explain. “You immediately understood that you offer unique materials and desirable items. But why? The dungeons obviously also want to prevent people from actually reaching their cores. Dungeon lords exist with a very explicit, instinctive desire to prevent access to that space, even when they’re sapient beings that understand it’s necessary for treatment.”

I motion with my hands to try to indicate the shape of my thought process. “But if the core was truly sustained solely by its arcane well, there would be no need to let the outside world in at all. The most effective defense would be to prohibit access entirely. Don’t provide an entrance. Better yet, make the entire dungeon space solid bedrock. If nothing can get to the dungeon core, then nothing can threaten it.”

My arms go out to either side of me. “But that’s not what happens! Every dungeon builds a complex internal space. Every dungeon makes an entrance. Every dungeon includes valuables that people are going to want. Why? Why do dungeons want people close when they put so much effort into keeping them at arm’s length?”

Angel, bless her heart, really struggles with that one. Even the others can tell how hard she’s thinking, and Ayre gives the girl a sympathetic nod.

“Remmi really does ask people all of the hard questions …”

“Well,” the harpy eventually offers up, “I know that having guests provides us with … mmm, stuff? I don’t really know what to call it. There’s not really a solid idea of it, but the dungeon likes it. It makes the dungeon stronger and teaches it how to do new things.”

I nod, as that checks with what I already know. The pirates of Desert Cove told us that the dungeon uses data from the people coming through to update its portrayal, but Angel’s answer helps explain another major mystery.

“I think what you’re getting from people is Essence,” I offer her. “As pure Arcane constructs, dungeon cores probably can’t absorb Essence directly like living beings can. However, when beings who have a high concentration of Essence in their bodies, like those with Classes, come into a dungeon, or even its domain, that Essence probably bleeds out, providing the core with additional power.”

Ayre inhales sharply, eyes snapping wide. “That’s what Desert Cove was doing!”

I point to the elf with a snap of my fingers and nod. Then, for the benefit of Angel, Korrigan, and Kyuuga, I elaborate. “There’s a dungeon Ayre and I cleared called Desert Cove. It’s only a Bronze-Rank dungeon, the same rank as this one, and yet it hosts an entire crew of sapient pirates. Almost nothing else, mind you, but that’s still a huge power load. Ogre’s Grotto had only four sapients, and it’s a Silver dungeon.”

I cross my arms for a moment as a sort of punctuation. “Of course, just because Forest Cavern and Desert Cove are the same rank, that doesn’t mean they’ve got the same output. At the very least, though, they should be in the same neighborhood, even if they’re at opposite ends of it. So what does Desert Cove have that allows it to power an entire crew while we struggle to fuel one adorable, little birb girl?”

“People?” Korrigan offers.

Again, I nod. “A thriving tourism and fishing industry. Desert Cove hit the jackpot of prime real estate, a gorgeous coastline, and fish that are cheap enough for it to spew out en masse, so people flock to its domain. Because tons of people are voluntarily spending all day fishing its waters and playing in its surf, it has a continuous influx of actionable energy, not only allowing it to keep an entire crew alive, but preventing it from running dry even with near-constant adventuring.”

But the little oni looks confused. She’s taken the opportunity of all of this talking to sit down next to her red, rigid backpack, and has her staff laid across her knees. “How does that help us, though? Isn’t too much energy the problem right now?”

“It’s not a solution for right now,” I explain. “It’s a component of a solution to keep it from happening again. I think I have an idea, but it’s going to require giving people a reason to come here. If this works, Forest Cavern Dungeon, and Dabun Village with it, is going on the map in a way no other dungeon in all of the world has ever been.”

Korrigan is beginning to look very uncomfortable. “This is going to be dangerous, isn’t it?”

“And probably heretical and five different kinds of illegal,” Ayre agrees with an expression of resignation, “but even I have to admit that that seems to be where Remmi manages to turn moronic insanity into miracles, and we aren’t exactly flush with time or alternatives.”

The archer drops his arms and takes a resolute step forward. “What’s the plan?”

“I’m going to draw up a detailed operational plan and try to convince the dungeon core to follow it,” I declare as if it’s foolproof. “Meanwhile, you and Korrigan are going to make Angel indispensable.”

“How are we going to do that?” the mage asks, tilting her head to one side.

“By teaching her to speak Imperial Common before the expansion timer runs out!”
