Chapter 27: Betrayal's First Steps

I found a backpack in the hotel room, plain black canvas that would have to do for hauling my materials. I began stuffing it with everything I could fit. Void crystals that drank light, vital herbs that pulsed with green energy, Flux-infused mercury that moved like liquid starlight. Anything I could carry.

The backpack wasn't something I wanted to lug around through the dungeon's twisted corridors. The weight would slow me down, make me a target for anyone looking to take advantage of an overburdened opponent. But I saw no other alternative. Lady Vash could produce objects from seemingly nowhere, materials appearing in her hands whenever she needed them. Some kind of spatial storage ability that I didn't yet possess, whatever it was. So a backpack had to do.

I looked around the hotel room one last time, taking in the impossible luxury that had been my sanctuary for these past days. The soft carpet under my feet, the warm lighting that didn't hurt my eyes, the absence of anything trying to kill me. And strangely, for the space of a heartbeat, I didn't want to leave. The feeling surprised me with its intensity.

Another form of weakness. We enjoy our safe places, don't we? The illusion of security, of walls between us and the world. Not having to go out and face the pain, the death, the grinding reality of what life actually was when you stripped away all the comfortable lies. In here, I could pretend I was still someone who worried about normal things, who had a normal life to return to.

In the world outside, anything could happen. Some control left your hands. Chaos ruled, and survival depended on your willingness to do whatever it took. In here, nothing was trying to kill me anymore, at least, and I could rest. I could breathe without wondering if each breath might be my last.

Well, I could try to breathe. The damaged tissue in my lungs had other ideas about that.

I wouldn't stay. Couldn't stay, even if I wanted to. The trial was over, the hotel's purpose fulfilled. I could sense its power fading, the Flux energies that maintained this pocket of reality slowly dissipating. Soon it would collapse back into whatever void had spawned it, taking all its impossible comforts with it.

No, power lay out in the world, and I intended on collecting it. More than that, I needed things like food, water, shelter from the elements and the creatures that prowled the dungeon's depths. While the hotel had provided such necessities during the trial, there was no longer any mystical force regulating it. No guarantee it would keep sustaining me. In fact, I could sense through my connection to the Flux that it wouldn't. The Flux was already leaving this place, abandoning it like water draining from a broken sink.

I also needed to find a way to breathe. I couldn't continue as I was, my lungs damaged beyond what even my awakened regeneration could repair. Every inhalation was a struggle, every word a rasp of pain through scarred airways. The poison was gone, but it had left its mark, crystallized scar tissue that would never fully heal.

Maybe I should have been ashamed for the thought I considered next. I like to pretend that I was in that moment, when I look back on it now. That some part of me was still a good man, still the husband and father who believed in doing the right thing. That the old Varus Thorne, the one who had died the night monsters tore through my front door, still had some say in what came after.

As with all lies, they are sweet in the telling. But lies all the same.

Vathwin Zar had a respirator. The cyborg-like warrior had needed it himself, due to some tragedy in his youth that had left his own respiratory system compromised. I'd watched him during our time together, noted the way the black and silver device covered the lower half of his face, the mechanical rhythm of his breathing that never varied, never faltered. It was keeping him alive, allowing him to function in a world that would otherwise suffocate him.

I could take it.

The thought formed with crystalline clarity, cold and sharp as winter morning. Not ask for it, not try to bargain or find some alternative solution. Just take it. By force. By violence. By whatever means necessary.

He wouldn't give it up willingly, why would he? He wanted to live as much as anyone else, had fought just as hard to survive the trials and challenges that had brought us all to this place. The respirator was his lifeline, his connection to continued existence. Asking him to surrender it would be asking him to die, and no sane person would agree to that.

But I didn't need permission to take things from a corpse. My mouth turned downward, lips pulling into a grim frown that felt more natural than any smile I'd worn in weeks. The expression felt right on my face, like coming home to a place I'd been avoiding.

I tried to take a deep breath then, to steady myself for what I was contemplating. But the clawing, restricting pain like a boa constrictor made of hot coals coiled around my lungs, squeezing tight. Horrific wheezing sounds emanated from my chest, wet and rattling, the sound of damaged tissue struggling to do its job. Each attempted inhalation sent spikes of agony through my chest, reminding me of my weakness, my need.

I doubled over, hands clutching my knees as a coughing fit seized me. Blood flecked my lips when it passed, dark drops that tasted of copper and failure. This was what the poison had left me with. This was the price of survival, the cost of choosing life over death when the universe had marked me for ending.

Vathwin Zar. The warrior's respirator gleamed in my mind's eye, like a prize I desperately wanted. Black metal housing with silver accents, tubes and filters that would let me breathe without pain, without struggle. Technology that would make me whole again, or as close to whole as I was ever going to get.

Not only would killing him give me initiate points toward my advancement, but it would allow me to breathe again. To speak without pain, to move without gasping, to function like something more than a broken man slowly suffocating on his own damaged lungs.

I needed to breathe in order to protect Elisa. The justification formed automatically, as natural as breathing used to be. My daughter needed her father strong, capable, able to face whatever dangers this world would throw at us. How could I protect her if I could barely draw breath? How could I be the father she deserved if I was constantly weakened by my damaged body?

Oh, sweet little lies. My thoughts dripped with mocking venom, scouring through my mind like acid. The internal voice that had gotten stronger since my awakening, the part of me that refused to let comfortable fictions stand unchallenged. It wouldn't be for her. It would be for me. But doing it for family? For love? Oh, you could justify anything that way, couldn't you?

The hero's journey. The noble sacrifice. The father who would do anything, become anything, sacrifice anything for his child. It was a story I could tell myself, a narrative that made the coming violence seem necessary rather than selfish. Elisa needed me strong, so I would take what I needed to be strong. Simple. Clean. Righteous, even.

Bullshit.

Evil, good, these were concepts I had often considered in those days. Labels people used to make sense of actions that defied easy categorization. The universe didn't care about such distinctions. Monsters killed children and called it survival. Heroes slaughtered enemies and called it justice. In the end, the dead were just as dead regardless of the motivation behind their killing.

At some points I would say that power was the only thing that mattered, but that too was a lie. Or at least, not the fullness of the truth. Power was a means, not an end. A tool to be used in service of something deeper, something more primal.

As I left the hotel behind me, climbing back up the chasm on legs that shook with exhaustion and effort, the fullness of the truth came to me. It settled into my consciousness like a stone dropping into still water, creating ripples that spread through everything I thought I knew about myself.

Power was survival, and I would seize it because I wanted it. I wanted revenge against the forces that had torn my old life apart, against the casual cruelty of a universe that allowed children to be devoured by monsters. I wanted strength enough to never feel helpless again, never be the man who could only watch as everything he loved was destroyed.

Not because Lynn or Jackson needed it. They were dead, gone beyond any help or healing I could provide. Their bodies lay in graves I would never visit, on a world that might not even exist anymore. The dead have no need for revenge, no hunger for justice or retribution.

Not even for Elisa, if I was being honest. All she wanted in these moments was her Daddy, the man who used to read her bedtime stories and check under her bed for monsters. She had no use for the thing I was becoming, the creature that wore her father's face while harboring thoughts he would never have entertained.

No, it was for me. For the hollow ache in my chest that had nothing to do with damaged lungs and everything to do with damaged soul. For the rage that burned in my veins like molten metal, never cooling, never satisfied. For the hunger that grew stronger with each life I took, each choice that led me further from the light.

Vathwin Zar. The respirator.

I walked on, each step carrying me closer to a decision I had already made.

The journey back through the dungeon's twisted passages felt different now. The corridors that had seemed threatening before now felt merely inconvenient, obstacles to be navigated rather than dangers to be feared. My awakened bloodline hummed with power, and even in my damaged state, I could sense the Flux flowing through everything around me. The walls, the air, the very stones beneath my feet, all of it connected in a web of energy that I was beginning to understand.

Flux beasts still prowled the shadows, but they gave me a wide berth. Something in my aura, some change in my presence since the trial, warned them away. I could feel their primitive minds recoiling from whatever I had become, recognizing a predator more dangerous than themselves.

Good. Let them fear me. Fear was honest, at least. More honest than the lies I told myself about noble motivations and necessary sacrifices.

I tried to sit under a tree and meditate at some point, needing to address the notifications from my advancement that I had dismissed in the hotel. Four levels gained from completing the trial, attribute points waiting to be allocated, new abilities to explore. But I couldn't manage it. Breathing was too hard, required too much concentration for me to focus on anything else.

The irony wasn't lost on me. I needed to meditate to grow stronger, but I needed to be stronger to meditate properly. Another problem that could be solved with Vathwin's respirator, another justification for what I was about to do.

It required focus I didn't have. Vash would have scorned my weakness here, would have found some way to turn my inability into another lesson about the necessity of strength. But meditation wasn't something you could do mid-battle, and that's what my existence had become. A constant fight for each breath, each moment of consciousness.

Some realities even the Nyxen had to bow to. Some limitations couldn't be overcome through willpower alone, no matter how much they preached about strength conquering all obstacles.

So I walked on, tracing my steps back to the camp I had left from. My feet found the familiar paths without conscious thought, muscle memory guiding me through terrain that had become as familiar as anywhere I'd ever known. The weight of the backpack dug into my shoulders, but I welcomed the discomfort. It reminded me that I was alive, that I had survived what should have killed me.

Surprisingly, Kari and the others were still there when I returned to our little camp between the crack of the wide, dark mountain. I had expected them to be gone, moved on to safer hunting grounds or fallen victim to the dungeon's many dangers. But there they were, exactly where I had left them in our hidden area with its narrow but defensible space. Kari was sharpening one of her resonance blade hilts with a whetstone that sparked against the crystal housing. Zoey sat motionless in what little filtered light reached us, her bark-like skin shifting between different shades of green as she absorbed whatever illumination she could.

And Vathwin Zar stood watch at the camp's entrance, his armored form unmistakable even in the shadows between the mountain walls. The respirator caught what little light existed in our refuge, black metal gleaming like a beacon in my vision.

"Hey! Look who's back, yeah?" Kari's voice carried easily in our enclosed space, bright with genuine relief. She set down her equipment and started toward me, concern creeping into her expression as she got a better look at my condition. "Oh, you don't look so good. Are you okay?"

Her voice, touched with concern and actual care for my wellbeing, might have put to bed any treacherous thoughts of violence I harbored. Might have reminded me that these people were my allies, had watched my back and shared their food and treated me like family when I had nowhere else to go.

But my eyes landed on Vathwin before I could respond to her question. The respirator dominated my vision, black and silver metal that covered the lower half of his face like a technological mask. His breathing was almost mechanical, a steady rhythm that never varied, never hesitated. Each inhalation and exhalation was perfectly measured, perfectly controlled.

It sounded like a clarion call to me. Like salvation made audible.

That greyscale world I had experienced with Zorin so long ago returned, color bleeding out of everything except my target. Vathwin Zar came into sharp focus while everything else faded to indistinct shadows. The same tunnel vision that had allowed me to kill Vash's former apprentice, the same cold clarity that had let me bisect him without hesitation.

The world narrowed to two things: me, and what I needed to take from him.

"Varus?" Kari said, her voice reaching me as if from a great distance. Her stance subtly shifted, weight moving to the balls of her feet, hands drifting toward her weapon hilts. Combat instincts honed by weeks of survival in this place.

Something must have been on my face, or in my eyes, because her voice carried a warning note now. The concern was still there, but it was joined by wariness, by the recognition that something fundamental had changed in the time I'd been away.

She could see it. Whatever I had become during the trial, whatever the awakening of my bloodline had done to me, it was written across my features for anyone who knew how to read the signs. The predator's stillness that preceded an attack. The cold calculation that turned allies into obstacles.

My resonance blade ignited with its familiar hum, white light touched by crimson casting dancing shadows across our hidden camp. In that moment, suspended between decision and action, I felt the last chains of my old life fall away.

I wanted that respirator.

I would get it.
