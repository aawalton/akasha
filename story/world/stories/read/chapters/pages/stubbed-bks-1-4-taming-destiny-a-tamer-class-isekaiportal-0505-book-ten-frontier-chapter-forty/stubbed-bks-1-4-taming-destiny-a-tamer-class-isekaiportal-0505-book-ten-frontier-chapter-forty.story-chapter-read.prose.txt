“I’ve been hearing some rather interesting rumours recently, Markus,” Valence starts, his impatience clear as I’ve only just sat down with my glass of fermented milk.

It’s taken a bit of time to get used to the taste, but since we finished the last of the alcohol we brought with us from Moriax, and we haven’t yet started production with the plants we’ve been growing here, fermented milk is our only alcoholic option. And something alcoholic has become essential to separate official meetings with my allies from these relaxed gatherings with my peers.

“Oh? What might those be?” I ask idly, though inside I’m grinning.

“Something about games.”

“Are you talking about the numerous new games that have been popping up in the marketplaces over the last week, usually with strategically-placed chairs or benches? Games no one has ever heard of, with written instructions for how to play them?” Layton asks. Then he glances at me knowingly. “You wouldn’t know anything about that, would you, Markus?” he accuses me, though with a playful tone to his voice.

Josiah snorts.

“Not enough of the commoners know how to read to make it worth writing instructions,” he says with some derision.

“Perhaps it will help motivate them to want to learn if there are more and more things around the city that require it,” I reply with a hint of sharpness in my own tone.

Unfortunately, he has a point. Although we’ve made classes for adults available at a very reasonable price – and during both day and night to suit everyone – there haven’t been as many people taking us up on the offer as I would like.

Alyna has indicated that it’s due to a combination of skepticism – people questioning why they need to learn to read when they’ve been doing fine up until now – and priority order – when someone returns home tired after a long day of work, the last thing they want to do is attend a class. So, the only ones who are going to the classes are those who see sufficient benefit to them to put in the effort.

Putting various signs around the city – the instructions for these games, for example, or news about events that are to come – is one way I’m trying to motivate people. And, in a way, it’s helped the local economy – several scribes have popped up in the marketplaces and will read anything if they’re paid for it. I haven’t seen any of the enchanted quills I once saw in Azaarde, though – the ones that would take a dictation. I guess probably because there isn’t as much need for writing.

Unfortunately, having options that allows them to avoid the classes means my scheme has somewhat backfired. I think I’ll need to go with what Sarran was suggesting – offering promotions that require reading and writing as skills. A higher salary is an excellent motivation, especially as more crafts have popped up in the city, offering places to spend it.

“Those aren’t what I was talking about,” Valence refutes. It takes me a moment to backtrack enough to join the conversation. His eyes are fixed on me questioningly. “Some sort of games tournament, I hear.”

“Oh, that,” I comment, surprised that the rumours have come to Valence’s ears already. “Who did you hear it from?”

“My hunters,” Valence answers impatiently. “But what’s it about?”

“Huh,” I opine. “And what were they saying about it?”

“Markus!” Valence objects, going red-faced. Clearly his curiosity is getting the better of him.

“Calm down,” Elarion chides him coldly. “Whatever it is is surely not worth wearing your emotions on your face so obviously.”

Valence subsides, clearly doing his best to control his emotions.

“The hunters just seemed rather excited about the prospect, yet they avoided the subject when I asked,” he explains with more dignity.

“It sounds intriguing,” Layton agrees, turning to me and resting his chin on one hand. Somehow, he makes the pose look poised and graceful. “Do tell, Markus. Like Valence, I’m sure you’re at the root of this.”

“Well, I noticed that there aren’t that many fun things to do in Steadfast at the moment,” I say. “So I decided that a tournament would be a good idea.”

And yes, that’s what the people decided to call the settlement. There were lots of suggestions, including an odd one that I suspect someone suggested either while drunk or by just shoving as many letters on the small entry form as they could, however Steadfast was the one that gained the most votes over the three rounds of voting we had. Oddly enough, the drunk suggestion, Hygsbafallenugh, made it to the top twenty in the first round of voting, but was eliminated when we took the top five for the second round of voting. In the final round, Steadfast and New Moriax battled it out, Steadfast finally winning with seventy-two percent of the votes.

“A tournament – of these odd games that you’ve been sharing? This…tic tac toe? And chess? And Home You Go?” Josiah asks uncertainly.

I can’t help but smile at how carefully he pronounces the names.

“Yes, those, but also Moriaxan classics like Supremacy,” a fancy name for the first game I saw played, “or Beast Races, or Lords and Servants.” Those are two new games I now know how to play – Beast Races is kind of similar to snakes and ladders, if it was snakes and ladders and potholes and water and webs and a map that changes every time. The pieces are each different beasts that require specific minimum numbers on the die to get through particular obstacles. It’s a fun game that leaves plenty of people cursing as they get eaten by a snake or fall into a pothole, while others cheer as their chosen piece swims through water or breaks through the webs without difficulty.

“Adding Lords and Servants to a tournament like this makes something of a statement,” Elarion points out delicately.

I shrug.

“It’s a strategy game like the others,” I reply. The objective of Lords and Servants is to become the Lord, by either backing opponents into a corner where they are unable to operate without trespassing in someone else’s territory and getting caught, forcing them into slavery because their debt has become too big at the moment they roll the debt collector, or buying them out of the game entirely. It’s kind of a cross between Monopoly and a turn-by-turn land grab game. “And is that not what we are all doing now? Someone who wins the Lords and Servants tournament would be a good person to bring into the team, would they not?”

That makes them think.

“Then that is why you are choosing to run a tournament for these games rather than traditional martial sports such as archery or sword-fighting?” Layton asks thoughtfully. “You wish to identify those who think strategically?”

“Well, that, and it’s fun,” I shrug. “We need a bit of that. But, honestly, I haven’t set it up yet – I wanted to find out how people would react first.”

“That’s why you haven’t spoken to us about it yet?” Josiah asks with a raised eyebrow.

“That, and I know you’ve been busy with scouting out areas for your own settlements,” I agree.

Layton and Josiah exchange glances, their expressions slightly sheepish. Elarion, on the other hand, just looks at me steadily.

“I know that things are still a little precarious, but we feel it’s a good idea to be prepared,” Layton says, a faint note of apology in his voice.

“I’m not aggrieved by it at all,” I assure him. “Who do you think sent the first round of scouts out a week ago?” Layton blinks, clearly taken-aback. I turn to Valence. “I’m more surprised that you haven’t asked them for any information.”

Valence gives me a half-smile.

“I swore myself to your service for the competition,” he reminds me. “I can hardly do that from a different settlement.”

“Fair point,” I admit. “Though it might be more beneficial later if you do set up a settlement somewhere.”

“Returning to the question of the tournament,” Josiah breaks in. “I understand that you wish to…scout for strategic thinking, but why can there not also be some normal martial battles?”

“It would be a good experience for many of our soldiers – either to see how unexpectedly well they do after the recent battles, or to deflate their egos a little as they are set against each other,” Layton agrees. “And if there are some prizes for both Classers and non-Classers, it would likely motivate them to train harder in the in-between time.”

“That’s certainly an idea–” I respond, but am cut off by Elarion.

“And what of the mages? They have also been working hard ever since we’ve arrived. There should be a magical division of the competition,” he argues with more fervency than normal.

“This seems to be much more serious than I was originally intending,” I comment. “What about if we did two sets of tournament? Or even three? Spread out the pleasures.”

“Or,” suggests Valence thoughtfully, “what if we do them all in the same tenday – and invite representatives from other settlements?”

We all go silent and turn to gaze at Valence. He blinks rapidly at the focused attention, but doesn’t back down.

“Invite other representatives here,” I muse. “Why?”

“Well, we wish to build trade agreements with them, do we not? Ideally without going via the merchants.” He nods at Elarion who responds with his own nod, thoughtful respect in his eyes. “How better to do that than to invite them to be here in person?”

“Where they can see how well we’re doing, and the strength of our forces,” muses Layton. “It’s an interesting idea. Risky, though.”

“Only if we compare poorly against the others,” I point out, warming up to the idea. “And if the merchants’ reactions were anything to go by, we’re not doing that badly.”

“It will take significant investment – money and time,” Josiah warns. “Not only for preparing for the tournament itself, but for housing and feeding the guests, as well as significantly increasing security to prevent fights breaking out or them gaining more knowledge about us than we really want them to have.”

“Risk and reward go hand in hand,” Valence points out. “Any merchant knows that.”

“And not just merchants,” Elarion points out, a sharp note in his voice. “Bringing others to our territory requires a lot more investment on our part, yes, but it also gives us the advantage. Being the first to suggest something like this will also establish us as a regional power – if it goes well.”

“We’ll make sure it does,” Layton says with determination. “How long ahead should we organise it?”

“Not before a month,” Josiah answers immediately. “There is too much to do.”

“No more than two months,” Elarion counters. “We must strike while the others are still on the back-foot, or risk losing some of the advantages we could have gained.”

I’m slightly amused that the whole thing seems to have been pulled out of my hands entirely. I’m not overly upset about that – since putting into practice the delegation style of management that Sarran suggested over a week ago, I’ve found that I have far more time for my own development. I haven’t yet made any breakthroughs on either Plant-Shaping or Lightning-Shaping, but I feel like I’m much closer to figuring them out.

After a short squabble, Josiah and Elarion agree on a month and a half. They then start a heated discussion over how soon they should send out the invitations. They don’t want to give those coming too much time to prepare, but, at the same time, they acknowledge that there is a good bit of distance between us.

I wonder whether maybe I can use this as an excuse to see more of the land – if Sirocco, Noir, Ivor and I work together, we can use Bond Teleport to deliver invitations relatively easily. Then again, I’m not sure that’s the best use of my time.

I’m distracted from my thoughts as a knock falls on the door.

Standing up, I pull open the door to find a messenger on the other side. The young girl bows low.

“My lord, I come from the wall.”

“What is it?” I ask, hoping that she’s not coming to tell me of another beast attack. The conversation behind me falls silent, the atmosphere suddenly charged.

“A ship has been spotted. Captain Sor’az is back.”
