Chapter 39

Flow

The following months passed like seasons flipping across a calendar, almost without Mai realizing it.

The autumn leaves vanished, winter settled in, and with it came a new rhythm— a bit different, measured by Miho’s work, their friendship and also the small responsibilities that now rested on Mai’s shoulders.

Ever since that day she met Aya, Mai had felt the world expand around her—vaster, and yet somehow darker.

Miho, however, refused to let her drown in her thoughts. She didn’t know the secrets Mai carried, but in her own quiet way Miho tried to anchor Mai to something else, to keep her mind occupied.

Whether it was going out, now that she knew more people, spending fun nights at a jazz and swing bar—which Miho liked as much as enka or punk—meeting up with other feys, or simply working.

With the calm of someone who had lived through too many years and far too many problems, Miho began to train her in what she called fieldwork. She didn’t throw Mai into dangerous encounters, instead, she taught her the supporting tasks: holding artifacts, placing wards, securing seals around a perimeter before a ritual.

Small things, apparently. But they demanded precision. And calm.

At first, Mai’s hands trembled through every motion, though she was eager to be useful, and to learn anything new. The mere word “ghost” still sent a shiver crawling up her spine. She had witnessed so much at Miho’s side those past months, but yet fear clung to her like gum stuck under a shoe—persistent, impossible to scrape off.

Sometimes she caught herself holding her breath when lighting incense or pressing a seal onto a wooden frame. Miho watched her in silence, rarely scolding her. She corrected her with stubborn patience instead.

According to Miho, Mai needed to shed that fear—after all, ghosts seemed oddly drawn to her.Though perhaps that was simply because she was a fey. Fey creatures, across almost every culture, held some tie, loose or ancient, to the spectral world.

Yet there was something deeply fascinating—and really unsettling too—in Miho’s rituals. As an itako, she could lend her voice to the dead or to tutelary deities tied to a place. Becoming a kuchiyose, a living bridge where echoes from the dead whispered through her. She could see through the eyes of the deceased, glimpsing memories as they had lived them, and share those visions aloud. Her throat seemed to split in those moments: it was not only Miho who spoke, but a chorus of tangled voices—could be pleading, demanding, or confessing secrets. And that was only a fragment of a much broader gift.

When she worked with Shinto artifacts—polished mirrors, bells, carefully woven shimenawa, hand-drawn ofuda—the very air changed. These weren’t tools, they were extensions of herself, conduits through which the unseen became almost tangible.

Miho used her seals the way others used breath: effortlessly, precisely, as though the secret language of the kami had been etched into her bones.

Each ritual blended mediumship and magic. First invoke—open the path, allow the invisible world to manifest—then seal, contain, guide the entity toward quiet. In those choreographies of silence or chants, incense, and cadence, Mai always felt she was witnessing something ancient—a heritage not passed down through books, but just through practice, patience, blood and breath.

Despite her apparent carelessness, Mai admired that side of Miho. She seemed like an entirely different person when she worked.

And still, Mai couldn’t shake the feeling that she was falling behind.

Every night she returned home and dove into online searches or sifted through Nakano’s local records, searching for anything—any scrap—that might tie back to what Aya had hinted at in the cemetery. She avoided raising suspicion. She worked quietly.

Nothing.

Hours slipped through her like sand, and after weeks, then months, her search in the web and libraries yielded only frustration. She woke tired from thinking about it, fell asleep even more exhausted, and every day of training felt heavier because she carried that invisible weight.

Winter left and Spring of 2005 arrived—dressed in cherry blossoms—but it brought no comfort.

She had been living with Miho for a year now, and the two seemed to have shared much more than it appeared. The fact that she had gotten along with Miho made her happy, even though she was still a little sad.

Mai accompanied Miho on more and more assignments, now reasonably competent at the basics. But her spirit was low, and that crack in her concentration inevitably led to mistakes.

The first happened during a rest ritual.

A woman had requested help so her husband’s spirit could move on. Mai’s role was simple—reinforce the barrier while Miho served as medium to understand the spirit’s lingering purpose. All Mai needed to do was align the barriers across the floor and walls, a basic, almost mechanical task.

But one slip, one instant of hesitation, and the seal remained incomplete.

The consequence was immediate: the spirit found a gap and rushed—not toward Miho—but straight into the widow’s body. The woman’s eyes turned glassy, and a voice that wasn’t hers vibrated out of her throat. Mai froze, barely able to react. She had tried to make the nine seals, but she had done it a little late.

It was Miho who stepped forward, performing a purification ritual that lasted long, grueling minutes and left everyone drained. She had to be careful, it was not the same for her to give her body to a spirit as it was for a normal person to do so.

Mai spent days unable to sleep properly after that. She couldn’t forget the look on the woman’s face—the mixture of love and desperation the spirit projected through her. She replayed it endlessly, blaming herself, a single small mistake had come dangerously close to costing someone their life.

The second incident happened not long after.

A lake west of Tokyo carried the bitter memory of dozens of drownings, and over the years an elemental of water had formed—an amalgam of those souls, nearly becoming a local deity in its own right, one that now required purification.

The two of them went there along with two priests from nearby shrines and a fey from the Tokyo Office of Anomaly Control. The task was to contain it at first, reinforce the perimeter, and allow the energy of the tormented spirits to dissipate without harming anyone.

Mai, responsible for one section of the barrier, miscalculated by just a few centimeters.Just enough to open a breach.

What surged through was a warped, murky shape—a whirl of hands and voices that nearly dragged the fey from the Anomaly Office into the depths. Miho and one of the older priests intervened again, sealing it swiftly, but the tension on their faces revealed how narrowly they had avoided disaster.

After that, Mai felt she had crossed a line. She no longer saw herself as a helpful assistant, but as a burden. Several times she thought about confining herself to household chores, avoiding these dangerous outings entirely. The fear and shame were simply too heavy.

But Miho didn’t push her away.

On the contrary, she continued taking Mai along, continued giving her small tasks. Mai began noticing how Miho watched her silences, her subtle gestures of discouragement, as if she understood that the real danger wasn’t the ghosts—it was her own surrender.

***

Spring slipped into summer once again and into the loud cry of cicadas.

The city shimmered with color and a nasty humid heat that seemed to cling to everything. By then, Mai had accumulated months of missteps, lessons, and frustration. She was no longer the same girl who had met Aya, but she still hadn’t reached the clarity she longed for. Her personal investigation remained stuck, and all it left her with was the gnawing sense of not being enough.

It was in the middle of that fragile state that the conversation began.

A evening, the sky painted in few oranges and more purples as the sun slowly melted into the horizon and the first stars began to appear.

The streets of Shinjuku were cooling, though the breeze still carried the day’s heat. They were crossing an overpass after finishing a job that had taken barely a couple of hours. Mai walked in silence. Beside her, Miho said nothing, but Mai could feel something had shifted in her behavior.

It was Miho who broke the silence.

“You're very quiet.”

“It's too hot to even talk.”

“Don’t be afraid, girl.”

Mai looked at her with mild surprise, as if Miho’s words had risen from somewhere far deeper than the noisy traffic below them.

“Afraid?” Mai echoed, a note of disbelief in her voice. But deep down, she knew Miho was right. Something inside her had been building ever since she met Aya, even if she didn't told Miho about it. Something that made her want to understand everything and yet terrified her at the same time. And it had only grown over the months.

Miho nodded slightly, her gaze fixed on the skyscrapers and then on the flow of cars passing beneath them, as if watching things only she could see.

She stopped, leaned against on the beam of the overpass, and lit a Black Ocean cigarette.

“Hey, you’re not supposed to smoke here,” Mai muttered.

“Fear is a shadow that’s followed you since last year,” Miho said calmly. “But if you let it rule you, it will become the darkness that won’t leave your side—one that consumes you little by little. Like a dead soul that can’t rest.” She exhaled, the smoke drifting into the evening air. “No matter what happens, you have to keep moving forward. There’s no going back.”

Mai listened, leaning against the opposite side. But something twisted in her chest. She couldn’t stop thinking about a past slipping through her fingers, about memories lost—memories she couldn’t even claim as her own.

How can I move forward when everything I am lives in shadows? she thought, though she didn’t say it aloud.

Miho was right, but Mai couldn’t shake the feeling that if she had known those memories—and that meeting with Aya—would bring her such frustration, a part of her might’ve wished she had never regained them at all. She hated thinking that… but sometimes the thought slipped in, intrusive and sharp, on her more defeated days.

“I keep wondering about things,” she finally said.

“Like what?”

“M… my past,” Mai whispered.

“I can’t hear you,” Miho said, exhaling smoke.

“My past!” Mai shouted louder, making herself heard over the roar of traffic. Suddenly a breeze on Miho’s side swept across her face. It felt surprisingly good. Miho’s hair fluttered in the sudden wind.

“That’s better.”

Miho continued, her tone dropping into something softer.

“Feys… you’re like new lives. In a way, not remembering is a form of protection for you. It’s almost… evolutionary. If the theory those eggheads have is right, when you arrive here, in this world, memories fade. You start over. The past dissolves and what remains is simply… moving forward. No ties. No chains.”

Mai frowned, watching a cluster of crows fly overhead.

“So… we don’t have to remember? Is that what you’re saying? That it’s fine to forget who we were?”

“No, I still think that sooner or later those memories will come back. But, in the meantime, all we can do is move forward. Even with mistakes and problems. I think you're trying to be too perfect, even though no one has told you to be. Making mistakes is part of learning. But sometimes holding on to ideas over and over again and ending up frustrated can be worse.” Miho glanced at her, her eyes behind the glasses dark and deep. “For some people, the past is too heavy to carry. Forgetting can be a kind of freedom. A new beginning. For feys, it might be the only way to survive in this world… for now till the day everything could became clear.”

“It sounds like running away…”

“Who said anything about running?” Miho replied. “It seems involuntary, that erasing. Part of the process.”

A heavy silence settled between them. The air felt dense yet somehow cooler, as though Miho’s words had woven an invisible layer around them, gently pressing against Mai’s chest.

“It sounds like we did something bad in another life. Karma?” Mai asked.

The crows’ cawing was loud in that part of the overpass.

Miho let out a short laugh—dry, but genuine.

“I don’t go for karma. Never have. Karma is not about something good or bad that carries weight in the next life, it is simply action and continuity. Life isn’t some cycle of rewards and punishment. It’s messier, more chaotic if you want to see it that way. Karma is just an excuse to make sense of things we don’t understand. We try to interpret everything through our own symbols, but not everything needs to be symbolic. We carve our own paths. And maybe being a fey isn’t special at all—maybe it’s just… random. Another trial along the way. Overcoming it depends on the choices you make from here on, not on some curse from past lives.”

Mai fell silent, letting Miho’s words settle. Part of her wanted to believe them. But another part resisted, as if something deep inside was shouting that not everything was fine, that forgetting was not the answer. But she said nothing. Maybe, at that moment, she simply didn’t know what to think.

Miho looked up at the sky, as if the early stars might offer guidance.

“Sometimes, all we can do is move forward. Not everything has an explanation. And that’s fine. We don’t always need to know everything. We are just small little things.”

Mai looked at her, feeling as though she finally grasped something more—something Miho had been trying to make her see. Maybe what frightened her most wasn’t what she had lost…but what she could gain by continuing forward. Had she been a good or a bad person? What had happened in that forgotten period?

Yet the question kept circling inside her:

What happens when you can’t let go of a past you barely remember?

Finally, without saying more, Mai nodded—as if accepting that, for now, she had no choice but to follow Miho’s advice. She took one more step forward, feeling the weight of her fractured memories pressing on her shoulders, but also sensing she couldn’t allow fear to take control. Miho was right about that. It wasn’t just about the work—she could fall into a much deeper depression if she didn’t regain her footing.

“I was like you once, girl,” Miho said quietly. “Try to enjoy this life. Just flow with whatever you find along the way. Don’t fill your head with thoughts of what you might have been. Think of what you can be. And when everything ends, you’ll be able to look back and say: damn—pretty amazing. I’d do it again. Even with the mistakes.”

“Those mistakes can be serious…”

“Mistakes exist to teach us. How else are we supposed to learn if we don’t fail at all? Or do you think I never messed up? It happens. Even to the best. Sure, maybe you can ask yourself about your mistake once, maybe twice, reflect on it. No one died, so you shouldn’t keep spinning hypothetical what ifs.”

Miho looked at her directly then—as if sharing a truth she’d carried for years.

Despite living with her for a year, Miho remained in many ways just as mysterious as the day they met.

She was her boss, yes. But also the one who had found her, given her a roof, clothes, a job. A friend—almost an older sister.

Mai, still processing her friend’s words, waited in silence, though the unease kept stirring inside her.

“So there’s no such thing as karma.”

Miho crushed the cigarette under her shoe and tossed it into a trash bin she found nearby—though it wasn’t the right one.

That doesn’t go there, Mai thought.

“Nothing truly dies, Mai. At least that’s how I see it,” Miho began, her tone soft yet firm. “When a soul dies, it can remain tied to a place, but that’s just the personality. The mask, the ego, if you want to call it that. That’s what we know as a ghost, a spirit… someone who can’t let go of what no longer exists, what has already passed, clinging to a place and unable to move on. It’s an endless cycle, but eventually that personality fades away too. ”

Mai stared at her in silence, absorbing every word. She didn’t know whether she should try to understand what Miho was saying, or simply try to accept it. The fear of forgetting, of disappearing—somehow it made more sense now, yet overwhelmed her all the same.

“So then…?” Mai began, but Miho cut her off before she could finish.

“The soul doesn’t die, Mai,” Miho continued, her voice softer now. “The soul keeps traveling. It can be reborn at another time, in another place, even in a different universe. That’s how I see it. The same happens with feys. A blank canvas for the soul to write new experiences. But even feys, like anything that has a beginning, will one day die. Maybe thousands, maybe hundreds of thousands of years from now, but even feys aren’t eternal.”

Mai remained quiet.

“The only thing that survives,” Miho said, sounding more like she was speaking to herself than to Mai, “is the soul, reborn over and over, seeking new experiences. And if I think about it, maybe that’s the true nature of destiny—not karma. Not a cycle of punishments and rewards, but a continuous rebirth, a desire to live more, to explore, to learn. Personality, identity, consciousness… all of that changes throughout a life. And with each life, the soul changes too. It becomes something new, something different. Just like the feys.”

Mai looked at Miho, unsure whether her words soothed her or tormented her even more. The idea that there was no definitive death, that everything was a constant cycle of rebirth and transformation, unsettled her. But at the same time, something inside her felt there might be truth in it.

The fear of disappearing, of being forgotten, began to feel more distant—more ghostlike.

“And that’s what you think awaits us?” Mai asked, her voice low, almost a whispering reflection. “That we keep reincarnating, endlessly?”

Miho looked at her through her glasses, as if searching for the answer somewhere deep within herself. “Yeah. That’s what I believe. I don’t know if that cycle will ever end, or if the experience of living becomes so addictive it just keeps going forever. But that’s the nature of the soul. And whatever lies within it is always searching for something more.”

Darkness was spreading across the city streets, and the lights were coming on. Mai felt a shiver run down her spine. She couldn’t deny that Miho’s words gave her a strange sense of comfort. But they also made her wonder… if everything was a cycle, if everything kept turning, then what happened to what had been lost? Was she supposed to simply let it go and flow?

“Like I told you when we first met, maybe everything comes in its own time. Though it’s just my theory. What do I know?”

Without saying more, Mai slowly nodded and smiled, as if accepting—though not fully understanding—what Miho had told her. There was something in that philosophy, in that eternal rebirth, that seemed to resonate deep within her. Perhaps her existence wasn’t as fixed, as definitive, as she had thought. Good or bad she was there, that was the only truth.

“Thank you,” she said at last. “For telling me that.”

Miho gave her a small smile, as if she knew exactly what Mai needed. “It’s nothing. Sometimes you just need to hear something to realize things aren’t as dark as they seem. Other times you just need a beer… or a good roll in the hay.”

“That was unnecessary.”

“It’s the truth. You’ve been here a year and you never even try to ask anyone out.”

"I'm not interested," Mai said, lifting her chin with dignified defiance.

"There are plenty of people interested in you."

"For now, I'm better off like this."

"You're going to erase your fingerprints at this rate, girl," Miho said, putting on a ridiculous voice.

Mai felt her face heat up instantly.

"I’ve seen your search history, you little pervert. That angel face doesn't fool me anymore. I didn’t think you were into that," Miho continued.

Mai was about to say something, but then froze.

I’ve seen your search history.

Miho had looked through her computer.

She stared at her, but Miho was only smiling—far too amused, and not because of the joke she’d just made.

Then it clicked.

Mai sighed, letting the weight of those words settle softly inside her chest.

Miho grinned and smacked her on the butt before ruffling her hair again.

As they walked toward home, part of Mai felt oddly lighter, but another part remained braced—still chasing her past, still yearning to understand what it truly meant to be a fey.

Mai walked beside her, still tangled in the thoughts Miho had planted.

The city swirled around them in its usual vortex of lights and noise. Tonight, it felt strangely distant, like it couldn’t quite reach the calm that was slowly washing over her.

"I don’t know if there’s some point of perfection after millions of reincarnations. But I do believe the experience is worth it. There are bitter moments, tears… but that’s life, Mai. It’s not always easy, but it always goes on. All you can do is keep moving forward and do the best you can with the time you have."

Mai looked at her, a knot in her chest loosening with each word.

Was that it?

Was all she needed simply to keep going, the way Miho suggested?

The idea that life was made of imperfections—and that the true meaning came from the experience itself—comforted her in a strange, intimate way.

Maybe, in the end, all she could do was live.

Let herself flow with life, like a river unafraid of where it ends, even when it doesn’t know the path.

Miho smiled softly and, without warning, reached out to stroke Mai’s head again, messing up her hair. The gesture was brief, but warm—warm enough to feel like shelter from everything that clawed at her mind.

"I don’t know what kind of experiences you had on the other side, if the ‘Other Side’ even exists. But I do know that almost every living being, at some point in their adulthood, ends up facing this same dilemma. It’s not about finding a perfect answer—it’s about accepting that we’ll never know everything."

Mai stayed silent, watching people and vehicles drift past, letting Miho’s words dissolve into the dusk breeze.

She didn’t know if answers would ever come, or if she’d one day comprehend everything happening around her.

But Miho’s words carried a weight that felt familiar, like something she had always known deep inside.

“You’ll understand someday,” Miho added, giving her a friendly smack on the back.

Maybe she didn’t need all the answers now.

Maybe she didn’t need to force herself to understand.

Maybe it was enough to live—with all her fears, losses, and fragile hopes.

Night had settled fully over them, marking the end of the day, and Mai knew that somewhere, someday, she would understand too.

She didn’t need to force anything.

Her past might have been erased, most of it swallowed in silence, but she still had the memories from her dream. Maybe she should treasure what little she had—because perhaps other feys didn’t even have that.

She had parents.

They had cared for her.

Even if she had lost them—even if their world had vanished—the fact that she had existed was proof enough that her beginning mattered.

There was no need to force anything.

She was becoming too much like ghosts—those trapped souls that clung to the past and could no longer move into the future.

She didn’t want to become a bound spirit.

No.

She wouldn’t let that happen.

She breathed out softly and looked at Miho.

“I’ll do the best I can. Can I keep helping you?”

Miho smiled and ruffled her hair again.

“Of course!”

“Seriously… what do you have with my head?”

“I just can’t resist,” Miho said, laughing.

Mai smacked her on the butt this time, and Miho let out a playful, exaggerated moan.

“No, Mai-san! You mustn’t do that in public. Onee-san is ashamed.”

“Stop talking weird!”

“Tag: outdoor threesomes,” Miho said in a fake timid voice, pretending to be all flustered. “Don’t go introducing me to anyone with that idea. I’m not ready for that.”

“I’m going to kill you!” Mai said, trying to kick her, but only managing to kick air.

Miho bolted off, and Mai sprinted after her—both of them running like two idiots through the night, dodging pedestrians along the sidewalk.

But she couldn’t deny it.

For the first time in months… she actually felt better.

“What am I doing?” Mai wondered, smiling as she chased Miho down the street.

Miho glanced back over her shoulder, still grinning, and whispered just loud enough for her to hear:

“Let it all flow.”
