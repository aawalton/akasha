Nonhuman cultivation is almost universal, in spite of what certain purists will try to tell you, but what some people classify as “artificial” cultivation is rare. Plants and beasts and rivers and mountains and all sorts of things accumulate Qi and gain what can be recognized as patterns to it, and orthodox cultivation is only one style that can occur, in spite of how we hold it over all other forms.

I am perhaps an exception to the traditional ideals, as I consider even formations a way of creating an artificial cultivation to a place, rather than viewing it as a simple extension of its creator. But there are some who take things a step further. Flesh-crafters that create unique biologies, golemancers that generate thinking constructs, illusionists that shape existences that don’t need to adhere to traditional physiks. Can these beings also cultivate?

Almost universally, the answer is yes.

Do they practice orthodox cultivation? Bestial cultivation? The cultivation of plants and animals and air and water?

Almost never.

Unique entities tend to cultivate their uniqueness, whether or not they intend to or are designed to. Human beings and our derivations often see so much difference between each minute aspect of ourselves that we sometimes forget just how broad the realms beneath Heaven can be, and all the things that can fit in them. In the big picture, we are all so very alike, and only as we grow beyond our origins can this idea even be challenged.

“Artificial” constructs remain just as real as we are. Many of them put evidence in the idea that anything can cultivate, however it might appear to an ignorant observer.

However, there is something to note: beings designed to be thoughtless have equivalent difficulty in cultivating or becoming more than they are.

Interesting, no?

It calls into question ideas of sentience. Is a mountain thinking? Does it ruminate in the shifting of geological plates? Is a formation a thought, expressed in symbol, a one-dimensional sentience that shapes the world around it? What is “thought”, and is our understanding of it as limited as the understanding of some about cultivation itself?

A question for another chapter, perhaps.
