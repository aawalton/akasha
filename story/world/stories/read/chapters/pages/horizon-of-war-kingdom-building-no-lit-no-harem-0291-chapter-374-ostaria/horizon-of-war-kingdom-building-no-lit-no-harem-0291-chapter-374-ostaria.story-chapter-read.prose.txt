Chapter 374

Ostaria

Corna Castle

Gemstones set within polished silver reflectors cast a harsh white glare across the vast silver airship, their light gleaming off its reflective skin. Beneath the vessel, ground crews shouted to one another as they hauled the mooring ropes taut and hurried around the enclosed gondola. Farther out, armed patrols moved along the edge of the field, their lanterns swaying as they walked.

Amid all that frantic movement, a solemn column emerged from the rear of the gondola. At its head strode three half-kins, followed by a mixed company of lords, guardsmen, SAR members, squires, and several unfamiliar guests.

Murmurs spread among the assembled officers and men of the garrison as they watched the arrivals.

Beyond the landing field, hundreds of nomads, now free of their duty escorting the supply carts, rode out across the darkened fields and formed a wide ring around the town and castle. Their torches and lanterns burned in long trails of moving light, announcing throughout Corna that a great force had arrived.

As the column drew closer, the waiting officers saw two guards walking behind the half-kins, swinging brass censers filled with burning incense. Sweet, heavy smoke curled around the procession, warding off miasma as it mingled with the cool night air.

Within that milky smoke walked two mysterious figures, tightly flanked by guards.

The column came to a halt several paces before the assembled officers and garrison commanders.

“My Lady,” Lord Avery, as their host, greeted the lady as she pushed back the hood of her cloak.

A stir ran through the assembled officers and men as they recognized the Lady of the House.

“Lord of Dawn,” the Lady of the House replied courteously.

At the same moment, Sir Michael and the Lord of Galdia stepped past the guards.

Upon seeing them, Lord Avery called out, “Lord of Galdia. Sir Michael.”

“My Lord,” the one-eyed knight responded formally, his back straight.

Meanwhile, the Lord of Galdia came forward and clasped Lord Avery’s hand. “Lord of Dawn. Do you have any news for me?”

“Not yet,” Lord Avery answered, keeping his tone level. “Which may be a good thing. It could mean your son escaped safely or has fallen into good hands. The hour is bleak, but fate may yet be kind. The Ancients will show us the way.” He turned toward the cloaked man still holding a sleeping baby in his arms. “Isn’t that right, My Lord Count of Midlandia?”

The assembled officers and men of the garrison fell silent. The air suddenly grew tense with expectation as thick streams of incense continued to waft from the censers and across the gathering. Many showed doubt, afraid to place their faith in a hope that might yet be crushed. Others stared at the cloaked figure with growing recognition, scarcely daring to believe what they were seeing.

At Lord Avery’s side, Angelo swallowed hard.

The Lady’s sharp gaze softened as she turned toward the man beside her, and a gentle smile touched her lips. Without a word, the hooded figure raised his free hand and pushed back his hood, revealing the pale face of a man with black hair and a thin beard.

For one heartbeat, the silence held. Then recognition struck. Cries erupted among the officers and the garrison. Above them, the men lining the battlements raised a great roar that pierced the night. Some below still stared in disbelief, while others stood stricken by the sight of him.

“My Lord!” one man called out, and the rest swiftly took up the greeting. Their voices were filled with pain, longing, and relief.

The baby, bundled in the Lord’s arms, stirred at the sudden noise. He and the Lady both chuckled. He gently rocked his daughter, and within moments, she settled back to sleep.

Amid the continuing uproar, the Lord turned toward his host. “Lord of Dawn,” he said in return.

Their eyes met, and each gave the other a single nod. No words passed between them, nor were any needed.

Then his gaze shifted to the old lord’s side. “Angelo.”

“My Lord,” Angelo replied, still scarcely able to believe what he was seeing.

Before addressing the eager officers and men, the Lord passed the baby into the Lady’s arms, knowing that the next few moments would certainly be loud.

Gasps and murmurs rose from the crowd as they saw the child and realized who she was. Many broke into grins and traded delighted looks. Then they caught sight of her long black hair and could no longer contain themselves. Even the older officers, who had remained composed at the Lord’s arrival, joined the crowd as it erupted once more in joyous cheers.

Amid the renewed cries, the Lady gathered the baby securely against her chest.

The Lord turned toward the assembled officers and men, their restraint already swept away by all they had witnessed. He nodded to those he recognized, then said, “I’ve been gone for far too long. It's good to be back, though I wish our reunion had come in better times. Now, there is an army to be saved. Gentlemen, in this dark hour, can I count on your strength once again?”

The answer came as one great, jubilant roar. With that, the garrison was as good as rallied. Every man was eager to march and fight.

On the far side of the field, the smaller airship was now touching down, and another ground crew rushed forward to receive it.

The Lord Shogun turned his gaze toward the Lord of Dawn once more. This time, Lord Avery gave a faint, satisfied smile and gestured toward the castle.

“Come. Make yourselves at home. There is much to discuss.” The old lord was pleased. With Korelia at his side, he would make the Empire and the Margrave bleed for what they had done.

Ostaria City

Morning, Second Day of the Retreat

For the tens of thousands of souls inside the city, dawn came far too soon. Many were still dazed, exhausted, horrified, and heartbroken after the previous day’s fighting, which had continued well into the night. Brave men-at-arms and horsemen had sortied time and again to rescue groups stranded amid the burning, smoke-choked fields, despite sporadic attacks from the Empire’s mounted irregulars.

Now, with soot still blackening their faces, limbs, and clothing, many awoke and began preparing to depart once again.

The cooks and kitchen staff in Ostaria had been baking bread throughout the night, knowing this moment would come.

Across the city, thousands of men formed quiet lines for bread and hot soup, then tried to enjoy the meal, knowing it might be their last. Nearly everyone knew that Osteria’s walls would not survive a prolonged siege. White Lake was still a good seven or eight days’ march away, and that was without accounting for the camp followers and the multitude of refugees who might slow them down.

It was estimated that more than 200,000 people had fled Sigisha and its surrounding towns, yet fewer than 30,000 had reached Ostaria. This left more than 170,000 souls unaccounted for. It was a devastating loss on an almost unimaginable scale.

Many still harbored hope that large numbers had escaped north or south under cover of darkness. Yet even the most optimistic could not dismiss the grim possibility that at least half of the missing had been killed or captured by the Empire’s mounted irregulars.

To save as many innocent lives as he could, Sir Harold had met the refugees the previous night and urged them as firmly as possible to head north or south, away from the fighting. They were still the Margrave’s subjects, and so long as they kept away from the Shogunate forces, he had no reason to kill them.

With the Empire’s advance still blocked by Ostaria and the river, forcing its troops to detour south, many of the refugees took Sir Harold’s advice.

The situation was different now. As long as Ostaria held, they could flee without the Empire’s horsemen at their backs.

As the overcrowded city stirred awake, the western gate was opened, and thousands of refugees who had already prepared to leave streamed out. They crossed the stone bridge and passed through the fortified gatehouse on the far bank. Beyond it, they divided, some turning north while others headed south.

Many thought it better to flee early, before the battle trapped them inside.

Against the throng of departing refugees, the Shogunate scouts made their way back into Ostaria. Their reports brought welcome news to the command staff. There had been no sign of the Margrave’s army to the west.

“They’re not waiting for us...?” Sir Stefan, the vice commander, muttered in disbelief. He knew their absence was no stroke of luck.

The fourteen officers glanced at one another.

Karl exhaled sharply. “They’re likely hoping the Empire will finish us off.”

Sir Stefan looked toward the Armiger and gave a grim nod. It was the most likely explanation.

The hall filled with low murmurs as the officers began to speculate.

Sir Harold saw no use in dwelling on it. “At least the people can march unhindered for half a day. So can we.”

“Then, your orders?” Sir Stefan asked.

Sir Harold deliberated for a moment, then asked, “What of our SAR?”

“No word of any fighting,” Sir Stefan answered.

South of Sigisha stood another bridge. Most Imperial maps, drawn centuries ago, still showed it as a sturdy stone crossing, but the bridge itself was ancient and had long fallen into disrepair. Traffic had increasingly favored the newer crossing at Osteria, leaving the old bridge neglected. In its place now stood a wooden bridge built over the ruined stonework.

A separate company of SAR members, accompanied by five hundred riders, had ridden out the previous night to burn it.

Sir Harold nodded once, then turned to his officers. “Tell the men to fill their waterskins. We march.”

The officers struck their breastplates with their right fists in acknowledgment. Before dismissing them, the vice commander entrusted them with more detailed instructions. They needed to get this right. The entire column had to travel light, or the burden would slow their march.

“Tell your men to carry provisions for eight days and no more,” Sir Stefan began. “Eight loaves will feed two men for the march, so each man should carry four, along with a ration of hard biscuit. Make sure they fill every spare waterskin they can find. The Margrave will likely try to deny us water. He may attack to drive us away from the water sources, poison the wells, or foul the streams.”

Murmurs rose among the officers. One asked, “What about the equipment? Right now, every pair of men shares a hand mill, a cooking pot, and a shovel.”

“Make it one set for every eight men,” the vice commander answered. “Have the stronger men in each group carry the heavier pieces. Every man must still be able to march and fight.”

Sir Harold added, “Keep the lanterns and torches. We may need to march through the night. Keep the shields as well. Every man-at-arms is to carry one.”

The officers nodded. They knew why. The Empire’s mounted irregulars were skilled archers, much like the nomads.

“How about the bolts?” an older knight asked.

More than a dozen carts had already been loaded with bolts from Sigisha’s stores. The stockpile had been meant for use against the Empire, but now its sheer weight had become a burden. Each sheaf of twenty-four bolts cost nearly eighteen copper coins, or a silver and a half. With roughly two hundred and forty sheaves to a cart, each load was worth around eighteen gold coins. Abandoning the carts would mean leaving behind well over two hundred gold coins in ammunition, along with enough bolts to supply thousands of crossbowmen through several battles.

To carry them all would slow the column, but to abandon them would be disastrous.

“We’ll have every unwounded man carry one sheaf of bolts,” the vice commander said. “The horsemen can carry three each, perhaps more, in their saddlebags. That should allow us to take most of them.”

While the vice commander continued sorting through the details, Sir Harold motioned for Karl to approach.

When Karl drew near, Sir Harold spoke quietly. “You and Alwyn did tremendous work yesterday. Are you two and your riders still in good shape?”

“We’re still fit to fight,” Karl said. The Mage Alwyn had been a tremendous help in the fighting.

Sir Harold nodded, then lowered his voice. “Can I ask you to speak with Sterling? Convince him to join the retreat, if you can.”

Karl inhaled deeply, nodded twice, and left the hall.

...

Karl and his squire, a stout man in his mid-twenties, climbed the western battlement looking for Sir Sterling. As they walked, they could see the stone bridge spanning the rushing water below. The river divided the mountainous lands of eastern Edessa from the broad plains to the west, and Ostaria stood at the crossing between them. Whoever held the city controlled the bridge, and with it, the passage between east and west. The bridge was fortified at both ends, while the city and castle occupied the eastern bank.

Unable to find the man he was looking for, they continued toward the eastern battlement.

As they neared the far side of the wall, the blackened fields outside immediately caught their attention. A strong summer wind from the south sent ash sweeping across the ruined ground. Dozens of overturned carts lay scattered across the scorched fields among the charred and mangled corpses of men and beasts. Hundreds had fallen within sight of the city gates. Many had likely died before the fire swept across the plains.

Along the battlement, groups of men-at-arms stood staring out at the same devastation.

“If only we had fought harder,” an older man-at-arms muttered to his comrade. Neither bore any wounds. They had likely been among the first to reach the city, before the Navalnian riders launched their vicious attack.

“Cast that thought aside,” Karl told the older man-at-arms. “Had we fought harder, many of us would be lying out there with them, dead.”

The men nearby reacted to his words. Many stood along the parapet, while others still sat with their backs against the stone beneath whatever blankets or scraps of cloth they had found the night before.

Karl glanced along the battlement. Weary eyes met his.

“Brothers-in-arms,” he addressed them. “Every one of us wanted to save the innocents. But our strength was not unlimited. In fact, our position had been compromised, and we could not hold out for long. The Margrave’s betrayal left us no choice but to retreat.”

More men turned toward him, waiting for him to continue.

“If the Shogunate loses us, its strength will be severely crippled. Today, it's Edessa, but tomorrow? Midlandia? Lowlandia? Anything is possible. I’m talking about your sons’ freedom, your parents’ lives, and your wives' honor. If all of us die here, they could lose everything. So be at peace. We did our best to save as many as we could without throwing away our own lives.”

Some men nodded. Others lowered their gazes and took his words to heart. For many, the burden upon them seemed to ease, if only a little.

“Remember this.” Karl swept one hand toward the blackened fields and the charred bodies lying beyond the walls. “This injustice. This barbarity. We have a duty to survive this shit. Let their deaths add weight to your swords. When you next face the Margrave’s army or the Empire, swing your swords harder. Make them pay for these people’s deaths.”

It was a strong speech, but there was no great outburst in answer. The men needed time to take it in.

At last, a lieutenant stepped forward and raised his fist. “For the Blue and Bronze!”

The men answered him. “For the Blue and Bronze!”

Their voices rang loudly from the battlement, but amid the drifting ash and the acrid stench of burned flesh, the cry had never sounded so hollow.

Even to the men of the Bone Breakers Brigade, the blackened fields outside the walls were a horrifying and sorrowful sight.

Having given enough speeches for one morning, Karl and his squire returned to their task.

...

Karl found the young knight eating his breakfast on the battlement, seated on a low wooden stool brought up from the guardroom. His injured leg was stretched stiffly before him, while a crutch rested against the stone nearby. Beside him sat an old camp follower he had known for years.

Sir Sterling was one of many wounded in the previous day’s fighting. He had joined a third charge to rescue a group of stranded refugees when several arrows struck his armor, jarring him long enough for an enemy rider to close. A heavy blade knocked him from the saddle, and several men rushed in to take him alive. Only the tenacity of his riders had spared him that fate.

The fall had worsened the same thigh already ruined in the battle for Korelia. Worse still, his squire was missing. The lad, barely a man, had been separated in the chaos and last seen fighting with another group near the carts. When the survivors staggered back, he was not among them. By now, he was most likely dead.

“Captain,” Sir Sterling greeted as Karl approached, seemingly unperturbed by his condition.

“Sir,” Karl returned the greeting. “How is the wound?” Before the young knight could answer, he added, “Do you mind if we speak alone?”

Sir Sterling glanced at his assistant. The old man hesitated before withdrawing out of earshot. Karl’s squire did the same.

Once they were alone, Karl did not mince words. “I’ll arrange a carriage for you, or you can ride behind one of the dragoons if you prefer.”

“There’s no need,” Sir Sterling replied calmly.

“I was ordered to bring you home,” Karl explained.

“Now I really am a cripple,” Sir Sterling said with a bitter scoff at himself.

Karl knew the story. The young knight’s thigh had been nearly ruined several years earlier, though the healers had managed to restore it. But after the lich’s magic struck him, the old pain returned worse than before, leaving him with a limp. His stamina also failed him easily, while his sword hand had lost much of its former finesse. Now, the fall had injured the bad leg once again, leaving him unable to walk or even sit in the saddle.

“You’re still young. You’ll get better,” Karl said, putting as much conviction into the words as he could.

The younger man refused to listen. “Ever since I faced the lich, I’ve brought shame upon myself as a swordsman. Captain, I’m not worthy of your concern. There must be other matters demanding your attention before the long march.”

“The House will not allow this, and neither will your wife,” Karl reminded him. “Don’t throw your life away—”

“I have no child,” Sir Sterling cut in. “Perhaps that is a sign from the Ancients. I’m sure Claire...” He faltered, but forced himself to continue. “I’m sure she can find another.”

“Don’t say things like that,” Karl scolded the younger man.

“My apologies.” Sir Sterling drew a breath, then spoke more firmly. “You still need someone to hold Osteria. Someone capable, someone the men respect, and above all, someone who cannot continue the retreat anyway. Of all the officers here, I believe I’m the most qualified.”

Karl could find no fault in his assessment, but refused to yield. “We’ll find another to take command.”

“Let me have this,” the younger man insisted.

His insistence frustrated Karl. “Why are you so determined to die?”

“What use am I now?” Sir Sterling replied. “At least my death would restore some honor to my House. Claire would gain honor instead of a cripple to care for. You know how these things are. You should understand.”

“You and your riders charged last night, saved the rear guard more than once, and saved many innocent lives. So don’t sit there sulking over your worth. Are you trying to make others pity you? I have none left to spare.” Karl drew a deep breath to calm himself. He felt as though he were facing a petulant boy, not the young knight he had once known.

The younger man did not argue.

“Two years have changed you,” Karl muttered as the wind picked up around them. “Does Claire know you came here not to fight, but to find yourself a place to die?”

“She’ll understand," Sterling managed.

“The Empire will try to take you alive, likely after forcing your men to surrender. Withstanding a siege isn’t about dying to the last man. You need to fight for terms. Without it, your men have no cause.”

“I don’t trust the Empire, or the Margrave,” Sterling retorted. “We’ll put a bolt through the head of anyone they send to negotiate.”

“That would be murder under a flag of truce.”

“Not if we warn them not to approach,” Sterling replied bluntly, then fixed his gaze on Karl. “Don’t doubt my resolve. We have ample food, medicine, and bolts. We’ll do everything we can to hold this city.”

Karl had nothing to say. For a moment, he looked out across the blackened plains while the wind carried the acrid stench of burned flesh over the battlement.

Below them, officers began mustering their men into columns. Men-at-arms packed their belongings while horses and mules were saddled and loaded for the march. Slowly, all of Ostaria stirred into motion.

Karl had one last card to play. “I don’t doubt your resolve, but you’ll most likely be captured. And when that happens, Claire will pay your ransom. She’ll fall into debt to get you back, broken and maimed, while all the hard-earned wealth of your House passes into the Empire’s hands. Think about that.”

He added, “I’m not saying you’re incapable, but of everyone here, you alone are one of the Lord Shogun’s own knights. The Empire will learn your value.”

Sir Sterling gave a soft snort and shook his head. “Go, Captain. You’re needed elsewhere. Give my farewells to everyone.”

Seeing that his resolve remained unshaken, Karl finally relented.

“I may or may not survive this,” he said, gazing toward the blackened plains beyond the walls, “but if you have any words for home, or a letter, I’ll do what I can to see them delivered.”

Sterling’s lips drew into a thin smile. All he left for Claire was a short personal message, reminding her that she was still young and asking her not to mourn him for too long.

Karl committed the words to memory and left the battlement with his squire.

Dozens of men watched him go, no doubt wondering what had passed between them.

Karl paid them no attention.

Indeed, as the young knight had said, the men defending Osteria would need a leader, someone who could inspire them. As one of the Lord Shogun’s own knights, Sir Sterling was the right choice.

With him sharing their fate, they would know they had not simply been abandoned to die. His presence would show that Osteria’s defense was vital, that their stand mattered. Their lives were not merely being thrown away, but that they still had a worthy role to play.

Aside from the wounded and crippled, many of Osteria’s elderly had also joined the defense. So had those who had lost someone dear the previous day. Others remained because their loved ones were still missing, and they refused to leave without them. Now they armed themselves, and many received hurried lessons in the use of crossbows.

Sir Sterling would command just over two hundred men, while nearly four hundred townspeople had volunteered to stay.

Six hundred in all would hold the city walls and castle, with the river at their backs. Even behind stone walls, they had little chance. The previous year, the Empire had marched tens of thousands into Edessa, and they would likely do the same again this year.

It was as good as suicide. But many were either stubborn enough to accept the odds or left with no other choice.

As for the wounded and crippled Shogunate men, they would gladly spend their lives delaying the Empire, buying the retreating army whatever time they could.

The Shogunate Force

Road to Derenza

Half a day had passed since their morning departure from Ostaria. Save for a brief midday rest, the troops had marched mostly in silence across the hard, dusty plains. Only the creak of carts and the snorts of mules and horses disturbed the stillness. There was no singing, no marching cadence, no coarse laughter passing down the column. Their guilt was too fresh, and their anger at the Margrave’s betrayal ran too deep.

Scouts came and went on fast horses.

For now, the defenders of Ostaria had bought them time.

The latest report said the Empire had begun its attempt to encircle the town, but its main army had yet to arrive. Enemy riders had also searched for a way across the river, only to find the water too deep and too swift. Knowing they still had time, Sir Harold deliberately kept the column at a measured pace. They were no longer being driven forward in the same desperate haste as the day before. He needed to conserve their strength.

Still, even with the Empire delayed behind them, their situation had improved only a little. The Margrave still meant to destroy them, and no one in the column doubted it.

Under such circumstances, Sir Harold continued the retreat with some 6,700 men and nearly 1,900 horsemen. Those numbers had already been bolstered by the garrison from Ostaria, meaning several hundred fighting men had been lost the previous day, most of them in the rear guard.

If not for Karl’s leadership, the new mage Alwyn shielding them from the worst of the fire and smoke, and the timely intervention of the airship, rear guard would have broken, and many would have been slaughtered.

Alongside the column of men-at-arms, over 12,000 refugees were retreating as well, a third of them from Ostaria.

Despite seeing thousands of others turn north or south in search of safety within the Margrave’s domain, this many had refused to leave and continued to entrust their lives to the Shogunate. Sir Harold and his officers had no real way to dissuade them.

Quietly, some among the command staff pointed out that the refugees were not entirely a burden. If the Empire’s mounted irregulars caught up with them, many would likely turn their attention to capturing the fleeing civilians before pressing the army itself.

It would give them time and serve as a distraction, perhaps even create an opening for a counterattack.

It was a cruel calculation, but a practical one.

Sir Stefan, his second-in-command, agreed.

The knight commander, Sir Harold, raised no objection. The grim reality had already settled in. His duty was to preserve the army entrusted to him, not the Margrave’s subjects.

Now the refugees mingled with the army’s thousands of camp followers. Dust clung to their clothes, faces, and hair. Many walked beside their carts and carriages to relieve their mules and horses, hoping to keep them from collapsing from the heat and exhaustion of the long march.

Around the column, mounted patrols and scouts came and went. They were the army’s eyes and lifeline.

As the sun leaned westward, the air finally began to cool.

But the coming evening brought little relief. Derenza was still a day away, which meant a night in the open. Somewhere on these same plains, the Margrave’s men had ambushed their relief force at the start of the war. If his forces were still roaming the area, they could easily stumble into another ambush before reaching Derenza.

That possibility weighed heavily on the command staff. Even the officers walked beside their horses like the camp followers and refugees, conserving the mounts’ strength for when they might truly need it.

Slowly, shadows stretched across the plains. The column passed several villages before Sir Harold finally called an early halt near one of them.

The early halt was not meant to give the men a full night’s rest, but to prepare them for what came next. After only two hours, just before sundown, word passed down through the lieutenants that the knight commander had ordered the column to march through the night, hoping to put as much ground as possible between them and their pursuers.

Crowds quickly formed around the village’s few wells. The physicians had inspected the water as best they could and found no obvious sign that it had been fouled. Even so, they ordered the drinking water boiled. No one wanted to take chances.

The men were told to finish the water they had carried from Ostaria first. Water drawn from the village wells was to be used only for washing and tending the animals until further order.

They were given two more hours to rest, and many chose sleep over everything else. Few had the strength to make a fire and cook. Most simply ate the cold bread they had brought from Ostaria, then lay down wherever they could find a place.

That night, with the wind cooler and the stars clear overhead, the column marched again in darkness, using as few lanterns as they dared. The camp followers and exhausted refugees had no choice but to keep pace. They were desperate to throw off whatever plans the Margrave had laid for them and gain as much ground as they could.

Somewhere ahead, the Margrave's army could be waiting.

By the next dawn, they would know whether they had escaped with their lives or become a feast for the crows.

Half a day’s ride away, beneath the darkening sky, a tribesman scout galloped hard across the plains. Arrows jutted from his back and from the flanks of his horse, yet he still clutched the reins and drove the wounded beast onward.

He carried news from the front.

Derenza had fallen.
