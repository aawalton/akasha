The death of any Calamity was a monumental thing.

Having climbed so high, each stood as a pillar within the Origin Plane of their affinity, their understanding and chosen path carved deep into its very fabric. Their existence shaped reality itself, and so when one died, it was never a gentle ending.

Not for the world.

And certainly not for those who wielded that affinity.

The death of the glass Calamity had been no different, and fortunately for Aodhán and Sophran, it could not have come at a better time.

After Leech had failed to work as intended—siphoning energy from Alaric without binding him in the slightest—Aodhán had taken the worst beating of his life.

His entire body was swollen and misshapen, several bones broken, muscles torn in places he could barely feel, but in that moment of hesitation—that fleeting instant when the culmination of a Calamity’s power had been brought to ruin—Aodhán had run, teleporting away as fast as he possibly could.

He had barely made it in time, collapsing onto the teleporter deep within storm ranks before his strength finally gave out beneath him.

The world twisted into light. And then—

Commotion erupted as he and Sophran appeared almost simultaneously within the viewing deck, their bodies tumbling through the portal to stain the polished marble floor in widening pools of blood.

Beside him, Sophran collapsed heavily, liquid vitality spurting from a hundred wounds as Living Convergence finally deactivated, granting his spirit its first rest in hours.

“Healers!” an elder shouted, and then a familiar voice. Darien.

“Leave Aodhán. He’ll regenerate on his own.”

Hands grasped him then—warm, steady, reassuring—and though Aodhán did not collapse, the sudden deactivation of Axis of Ruin left him violently disoriented.

His vision swam, his body burned with pain, but the absence of clarity was almost worse than the pain itself.

“He’s burning up,” another familiar voice noted. Crixis. And an instant later, Aodhán felt the cool refreshing feel of cold water sliding down his throat.

He hadn’t even realized how parched he was until that moment.

“Thanks,” he croaked, after drinking enough to wet his throat. The word came out rough and broken.

He forced his eyes open, blinking slowly as the world swam into focus and he saw the array of familiar faces gathered around him and Sophran.

“Are you alright?” Crixis asked anxiously, fear plain on his face.

Aodhán smiled thinly, agony shooting into his skull as he tried to widen the expression.

“I’ll be fine,” he murmured hoarsely. “How’s… how’s Sophran?”

“Focus on yourself for now,” Darien frowned down at him. “Sophran has healers to patch him up. You don’t. Stay still.”

Aodhán tried to nod and instantly regretted it as several spikes of pain lanced through his skull.

“Darien said stay still,” Crixis repeated firmly. “That means don’t move.”

Aodhán sighed, and closed his eyes, shuddering as he remembered how close he’d come to dying at Alaric’s hand. He’d tried to hold his own. He’d tried to bridge the impossible gap between them.

But he had been outmatched.

The Calamity’s death had saved him, giving him a chance to fight another day.

But Aodhán knew he would never get this chance again.

The next time he stepped onto that battlefield, it would either be his last or Alaric’s.

Feeling the pain slowly begin to dull beneath the steady work of his regeneration, he opened his eyes once again.

Before he could speak, however, a loud voice rang across the viewing deck.

“Elder Ghaltris, we have a problem.”

Everyone turned, and Aodhán did the same, wincing as he forced his gaze upward toward the massive screens where he found the result of his and Sophran’s bitter retreat.

Havoc.

Seraphine and Alaric were venting their fury upon the Mythics around them, and with no transmigrants left to oppose them, they tore through storm ranks like unleashed disasters.

Mythics fought desperately to defend themselves, rules and skills firing out in frantic attempts to bind and restrain them, but neither could be tamed. Neither could be slowed.

It reminded Aodhán too vividly of the first two weeks of the war—of how much havoc they themselves had wreaked upon the enemy.

Now it was their turn.

And it was no fun to watch.

“They’re cutting through our ranks like shears through dry stalks,” Seamus growled, fists clenched tight. “We can’t let them continue.” He turned sharply toward Elder Ghaltris. “We have to do something.”

“They wouldn’t have to if you sent us,” Seamus shot back immediately, his gaze hard with restrained anger. “Send us, Elder Ghaltris. We won’t disappoint.”

“No—” Aodhán began, shaking his head, authority flaring instinctively despite the pain still wracking his body.

But Seamus cut him off before he could continue. “Stop cuddling us, Aodhán. We are not Mirelle.”

The words fell like stone in still water, and Aodhán’s lips snapped shut, the ripples striking straight at the heart of his guilt.

“No one is saying you are,” Aodhán whispered.

“No one has to,” Seamus shot back. “It’s clear in how you treat us and how you try to keep us from the war.”

He turned back to Elder Ghaltris, resolve hardening across his face. “We are not Mirelle. We are stronger. I am stronger. And if you gave us a chance, we would prove it. I would prove it.” his expression softened slightly, and he swallowed. “Give us a chance, Elder Ghaltris. Stop wasting our potential and use us.”

Elder Ghaltris opened his mouth to speak then fell silent, a heavy quiet reigning within the deck as he contemplated Seamus’s words.

Seamus and Darien weren’t weak. Not by any measure.

But they were not transmigrants either. Their spirits were not as strong, nor were their paths as powerful.

But they were still far from helpless, and he could not keep holding them back simply out of fear that they might die as Mirelle had.

Expression darkening, he turned back toward the screen, his eyes narrowing as he watched Seraphine carve her way through a formation of Mythics who had already been engaged in battle.

He had to do something.

Unfortunately, all his transmigrants were either too injured to fight or unconscious. Only Nymera remained in any true shape for battle. And he could not use her.

Which left him only one true option.

Sighing quietly, he regarded them for a long moment, then nodded slowly. “You can’t die.”

This time it was Darien who responded. “We won’t.”

Elder Ghaltris studied them for a moment longer, searching their faces for hesitation or doubt and finding none.

Finally, he nodded. “Then go.”

Neither hesitated.

They turned and ran toward the teleporter immediately, urgency driving their steps, and a moment later, they vanished in a flash of light.

But Elder Ghaltris remained uneasy with the decision. He glanced at Aodhán, and a moment later, he sighed.

“Nymera.” He said quietly.

She turned toward him, frost-white hair flowing in a nonexistent breeze.

She looked sane. She was sane. One more battle couldn’t break her.

And so, against the instruction of the First Seat, Elder Ghaltris spoke. “Go with them.”

Back in the enemy camp, elder Clearfall lamented. “They had one job. One job. And they blew it.”

“Clearfall,” Elder Mournwind began carefully, but Clearfall cut him off.

“Malverin was right. They are sheep. We have to do something. We have to find a way to escalate things seeing as our transmigrants have proven themselves utterly worthless.”

“Elder Clearfall.” Elder Mournwind called out again, this time more firmly, and Clearfall finally paused his rant long enough to listen. Mournwind scowled at him. “We are already stretched thin as is. Escalation isn’t going to help us as much as you think it will.”

“And yet we cannot continue like this.” Elder Aspwick sighed, the death and consequent desecration of Kael Varros’s body still weighing heavily on him. “We are losing, Mournwind. In the duration of this war, we have lost two Calamities, and two transmigrants, while our enemy has only lost one of the former.”

Bloody mist pooled out of his body like smoke, but he controlled it before it could do any damage, eyes squeezed shut and heart heavy.

“We are losing, Mournwind. And if we don’t take serious action soon, we shall leave this war worse off than we began it.”

His words caused a heavy silence to descend on the room, and a few minutes later, Elder Sang-Hoon of the Earth Sect spoke, his voice gravely. “What are our options?”

When no response came, he continued. “Our people need hope to keep going. They need a decisive victory, and if we fail to give it to them, we will lose this war.”

Another beat of silence.

Then elder Mournwind sighed. “That is not an option. Losing this war doesn’t just mean death. It’s a complete eradication of our affinities and Origin Plane from the fabric of our reality. Failure is not an option.”

“What do we propose then?” Elder Aspwick asked. “Shall we deploy more transmigrants?”

“No.” Four elders spoke at the same time, shaking their heads. The blood and earth sect had just lost two transmigrants. They weren’t ready to lose another, and those who hadn’t weren’t ready to tempt fate.

“How about Icons?” Elder Clearfall suggested. “We could wipe off half their army with a few well-timed icon activations.”

“They will only do the same to us.” Elder Malverin shook her head. “Not only will we shifting the war into its final form, but we also stand to gain very little from that, save ripping the earth apart.”

“What then shall we do?” Elder Clearfall growled, turning to glare at the screen. “It doesn’t matter how many Mythics we kill. If we do not kill their transmigrants, we are simply wasting our time. How do we kill their transmigrants?”

A long silence followed.

Then Elder Malverin spoke quietly. “There is one way.”

Every gaze turned to her, and she swallowed. “We stop fighting the war… and start hunting.”

She took in a deep breath and squared her shoulders. “A few silence Mythics should be able to do it.”

The elders exchanged glances, and though they said nothing, a decision was made in that moment.

Far, far beyond the reach of the battlefield, a different kind of hunt was underway.

Hunting was not a skill expected of agents or sect emissaries—least of all someone like Ravesh Kivasmus. Yet for this mission, it had quickly become essential.

Seven days ago, when Kivasmus had first set out in search of his next target—the Space Sect—he had known it would be an arduous task. Unlike other sects, the Space Sect was located within chaotic space, barely tethered to reality by rippling constructs of spatial energy known as space gates.

For the past seven days, Kivasmus had searched tirelessly for a gate that never seemed to remain in one place for more than twenty-four hours. It was supposedly easier for spatial cultivators to find such entrances, but with none on hand, Kivasmus had been forced to locate the gates himself—practically hunting one down to a blight-ridden forest just beyond the Tidelands.

“By the storm,” he cursed under his breath as the rippling gate finally came into view.

It looked almost no different from a natural rift, save for its lack of colored rings. Spatial essence leaked steadily into reality from the edges of the distortion, and all around it only dead or dying plants remained, their twisted roots pointing toward the gate like fingers frozen mid-reach.

Kivasmus grimaced at the sight, muttering something under his breath as he drew closer, a faint tingle of wrongness racing down his spine.

There was a reason the Spatial Sect was known as the Vanishing Sect, and it wasn’t merely because they were impossible to track or lock down.

Hopefully, he wouldn’t have to experience that reputation firsthand.

Adjusting his emissary signet, Kivasmus drew in a steadying breath before stepping through the gate, feeling a sudden and violent displacement that sent the world spinning around him.

Light flashed.

Colors bled into each other.

Then—just as quickly as it had begun—the distortion stilled, and Kivasmus found himself floating within a vast expanse of space—endless and immeasurable.

It stretched as far as his eyes could see, and farther still than he could truly comprehend.

Distance felt alive here, pressing against his senses in a way it had never done before. It bent his perception at the edges, distant points of light seeming both impossibly far and dangerously close at the same time.

Kivasmus shuddered at the feeling of smallness and shook his head in amazement. This… this right here was the most abundant affinity in the world, in all the universe, and arguably the most fundamental

Nothing else could exist without it—not living or dead. The entire framework of existence hinged upon a single affinity.

Space.

He turned slowly, taking in the vast expanse in amazement—

Only to come face to face with a man dressed in rippling purple robes, his skin nearly as translucent as glass and his eyes like small suns.

Runic suns.

Kivasmus bowed immediately. “Good day, Calamity. I have been searching for the sect for days.”

“We are aware,” The Calamity stated casuslly, and Kivasmus finally raised his gaze to the Calamity.

“Then you must know why I’m here.”

The spatial Calamity regarded him for a moment before smiling thinly. “The Sect of stillness and Serenity have never been fans of ours. Space is expansion, after all. It is movement, just like the storm. But I fear we have no interest in fighting the storm’s battles.” He folded his arms behind his back. “We are perfectly content to watch how this plays out instead.”

Kivasmus nodded, having expected this. No sect who hadn’t joined the war in the beginning had any real reason to join now. To recruit them into the war, one had to push them.

Fortunately, Kivasmus had the perfect incentive.

He smiled. “What if we made the Spatial Sect an offer they couldn’t resist?”

The Calamity raised an eyebrow. “What could you possibly offer us?”

Kivasmus’s smile widened a touch. “Something that made the Sect of Silence ally themselves permanently with the storm sect.”

The Calamity stilled, eyebrows climbing, and Kivasmus’s smile widened fully into a grin, the irony of his sentence not lost on him. Silence allying with thunder and lightning?

It sounded impossible. Unthinkable.

But it was true, and when the Calamity realized it, his expression shifted—subtle, but unmistakable. “I’m listening.”

Kivasmus shook his head. “I’d rather the First Seat themselves were listening.”

The Calamity hesitated for a moment before a faint smile returned. “She is now. What is this supposed offer that we cannot resist?”

Kivasmus smiled widely, pulled out the box of space pendants elder Gumi had given him and opened it.

“Familiars,” he said quietly. “That is our offer.”
