Chapter 13

Tables

My guidance sheet that will form the fence outlining the boundary of my project complete, I can finally turn my attention to the project itself. Really, that my client is, in fact, an industrial 3D printer that likes constantly reprinting everything is the biggest boon I could ask for. I don’t have to ask for construction crews or building permits. I just have to provide it with what to print.

The central core of the project will be a simple square room. Everything will revolve around this one object to produce infinite possible iterations. To keep it interesting, I write out a list of environmental traits that the room may have, sort them by type - floor modifications separate from wall modifications, air modifications, and light modifications, for example - and lay them into tables, including the possibility of not using any of them and the given value remaining the default. Which is also precisely defined, of course. Each table is then numbered to allow the core to randomly select the value. These variations should be a basically nonexistent drain of energy relative to the room itself, while still providing a visually interesting experience that prevents a customer from getting lost in a maze of identical rooms.

Each square room, then, has between one and four exits that each lead to another square room, except the first room, which must have an exit leaving the wing. Before an exit to a new room can be generated, it must check for conflicts with already-generated rooms; if a room is already placed there, the exit must connect to that room, or the exit can’t be placed. Exits may be locked, but if they are, the key must be placed within the accessible area. This ensures that if a dead-end room is rolled with its one exit locked, the system requires the key to that exit be placed in that room. It prevents the possibility of someone ending up trapped by indestructible walls because of a bad roll. The exact nature of the key can also be randomly determined. It can be a physical object in a chest, in the possession of a monster, or hidden in the room, or it can be tied to the event of that room, wherein the completion of the event unlocks the door. It can also be an interactable object, such as a lever or switch.

So far, however, all I’ve done is design a randomly-generated maze with no content and no purpose. The next step, then, is to provide something to populate those rooms with. That’s where those aforementioned events come in. More options provide the possibility for a variety of puzzles, ranging from hidden object to switch combination to good ol’ block puzzles, or monster encounters, where the number and difficulty are based on the size of the participating group. However, events aren’t inherently required for moving on to the next room, and events without a purpose are just obstacles. So, for every event generated, the completion of the event must also generate a treasure chest to incentivize participation.

To make sure there are actually a reasonable number of encounters and you don’t just stumble right into the boss room, a further limitation on room generation must be placed: No matter the total number of rooms generated, any path that can be taken to reach the boss room must include at least four events across that path. This will make sure that the boss room is suitably far away and the dungeon experience itself is sufficiently long. I picked four as the number because that was the number of what would qualify as “events” the Forest Dungeon presented us with when I first experienced it, so I figure it’s a reasonable number that’s still small enough for it to handle. There is also the possibility to spawn empty rooms, with no event at all, and recovery rooms, which offer a place to rest and maybe some limited recovery items, or just snacks and a nice view; they’re a whole table to themselves. However, neither of these room types will count toward the minimum number of events.

To ensure that the dungeon core doesn’t get caught in an endless loop of infinite room generation, however, I set a hard cap on the total number of rooms. Since they’re individually fairly small, given the square footage it seems capable of handling, I place that upper limit at twenty-four, because I like bytes. Similarly, to make sure there actually is some exploration to do, I set eight as a minimum number of rooms. None of these numbers includes the entrance room or the boss room, neither of which counts as an event room either. This means that the size of a given dungeon run can be no more than twenty-six rooms, no less than ten rooms, and any route to completion must be at least six rooms long. A new room cannot be generated with more exits than there are possible rooms remaining, and if the complete generated map does not meet all of the requirements - such as that it meets the minimum size, but has generated a layout where it is impossible to fit in at least four events - the core must reject it and generate a new one before it starts printing.

The boss, too, deserves some randomization. My mind goes back to the golem that protected the core back then, and I decide there’s no need to reinvent the wheel. The core surely still has that thing’s blueprint in memory, and a golem is an extremely flexible base to work with. It can be a wide range of threats just by mixing up its combat systems. That’s another table, then, where the core will select between two and four abilities at random, without duplication. That should mean that, even if the boss is always the same, how it fights and what you have to do to overcome it will always be different. And, of course, in addition to allowing access to the core, defeating the boss should generate a “grand treasure chest,” with significantly better rewards than the event treasure chests.

But then, now I’ve included all of these treasure chests. What is the core supposed to put in them? The same money, ores, and plants would be horribly boring and uninteresting. Now, those can absolutely be some of the rewards, as they make great padding, but we want rewards that people are going to be invested in getting.

These are my most extensive tables yet, but it’s really more due to the breadth of items. There’s a weapons table, an armor table, a trinket table, and even a clothing table, and rather than a flat roll, the possibilities are percentage-based, with items with more effects being rarer than those with fewer or none. The process is roughly as follows: For a given treasure chest, depending on the size, the core first determines how much treasure is actually in it. According to that, it then generates a random collection of items, plus a random amount of the padding. To determine the item, it first randomly determines the type of item. It then rolls to determine how many effects it has. The grand chest has a much higher chance of more and better effects to increase the likelihood that something good comes from it, while it’s more forgivable if a mere event chest rolls hot garbage.

The chance of certain effects is also percentage-based, with better ones being less likely. However, the actual effects are fairly bog standard, at least by my estimation. Increased damage, added elemental effect, decreased weight, certain stat buffs, and so on. Nothing too crazy, we don’t want to exceed what the core can generate, but enough that some rookie is going to be very, very happy with the right rolls. The name of the object is then determined by the attachments. If it rolled fire and increased damage on a hammer, it’s a flaming hammer of power. Of course, every item category has a separate list of potential effects. As amusing as it would be to see a Flaming Skirt of Penetration, it would be more of an experiment in the bizarre potential of enchantments than it would be a practical item.

I also have an extra table that there’s a very narrow chance to roll into that should add a whole new depth to dungeon diving in Toleste, not to mention the economic benefits. I can hardly wait to see how people respond to the idea of very rare items that get stronger with each matching item from the same very rare list that they’re also wearing. In fact, I kind of want some of these “set” items, myself. That’s gotta be a sign that it’s a good idea, right?

As a final touch, there’s one last thing I have to include: The dungeon can’t kill the customers. If a party is defeated, they are not to be finished off or absorbed. Instead, they are to be knocked out, and agents of the dungeon, who themselves don’t need to be sapient like Angel but should probably follow the same design pattern, will come fetch them, remove all of the loot they had gathered from the dungeon up to that point, and return them to the lobby– er, the reception area. This provides the incentive not to lose - having to start all over without any of the nice items you might have found - while still giving a safety net that allows it to be an effective training environment. Death, outside of unavoidably extreme exceptions, is impossible. Customers simply “respawn” outside the run with an incentive to try again.

With that all sorted, I go through everything one last time to make sure I haven’t forgotten or miscalculated anything, then stack the sheets up, bind each of the two copies with twine, and put them into my storage. I’m two bottles of sludge into my time table and nearing time for the third, which means our time is about half over, so as I stand up, I set another three down on the table for Angel.

The sudden motion causes everyone else to pause what they’re doing and turn to look at me.

“You’re done?” Ayre asks, sounding both surprised and relieved.

“I’m done writing everything out.” My answer is both confirmation and correction. “Now it’s time for the sales pitch of my lifetime. Angel, how do I get to the core from here?”

She sputters a bit in surprise before pointing away from the square. “Oh, there’s a tunnel that way. Be careful, Mr. Golem is still protecting it. He’s just only awake when necessary to conserve power.”

I nod. “Don’t worry, Mr. Golem and I have met before. I’m off! Keep up the practice! Ayre, Korrigan, Kyuuga, be ready to pull out as soon as I return. We may not have much time to spare.”

I get nods and words of understanding from all of them, and I give Angel a reassuring smile and a hair ruffle as I pass by.

The programming’s written. Now, I just have to convince the computer to swallow it.
