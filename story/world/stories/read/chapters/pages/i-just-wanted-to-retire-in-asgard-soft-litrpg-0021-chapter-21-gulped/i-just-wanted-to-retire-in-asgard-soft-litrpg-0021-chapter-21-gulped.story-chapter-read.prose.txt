The enormous wolf did not move.

Loki stood on the broken ridge with the wind raking her face, every muscle tight, waiting for the hit that should have landed by now and hadn't. Fenrir was so huge the ridge seemed to shrink around him. His fur was the gray of old iron, and his eyes had no fire in them. He just stood out there in the dark and watched her, and the longer he did, the more she wanted to flinch first.

Violence was what she'd come braced for. It was how her children usually greeted her.

"Do you still remember?" His voice was level. "When I was little, you gave me a blade. It had a name."

Loki swallowed. She could picture the blade easily enough, the cord-wrapped grip, how it had looked far too big for him. The name wouldn't come. She kept reaching for it and closing her hand on nothing.

"Moonsli...?"

Fenrir's head tilted a fraction before she could finish.

"Wrong..." the wolf said.

He said it so quietly she almost missed it. His ears went flat against his skull, and he wasn't looking at her anymore, only at a point just past her shoulder.

"Quake..."

"Wrong!" He said it again, much louder now, his fangs showing. "I should've known. You never change. You never have."

Jade-green divinity rolled off him, and the air grew so heavy that each breath had to be pushed past her ribs.

"Fenrir, give me a chance. I need to talk to her. Your sister."

He answered with a growl, long and low. It was his growl, down to the rattle at the end, but it came half a breath late, as if he'd waited to see what she would do first.

"Fenrir..." She said his name softly, the way she used to. His jaw clamped shut on the next growl, and one ear swung toward her.

"...Last question," he said finally.

This one was about a night when he'd still been small enough to fit against her side. Snow had buried the mouth of the cave, and she had hummed a tune that existed nowhere else. No one else had ever heard it, and no one else could have known the words she whispered when the storm finally broke.

Loki's mouth opened. This one she had. The answer was just there, and she gave it to him straight, word for word. Only when the last of it was out did she hear how it sounded. No hedge, no joke, no lie to soften it. She would never have given him the truth that cleanly.

Fenrir's lip slid down over his fangs.

"That's not what she would say."

He moved once. His claws crossed the space between them, and the illusion tore like wet cloth. The wind cut out first. The ridge shuddered and went thin, and her own body was the last thing to go.

Fenrir stood there a few seconds longer, looking at the spot where she'd been. Then he turned and walked away without a sound.

Far away, the real Loki felt the blow land on her shoulder.

"Ohhh!" She doubled over, slapping a hand to the place where Fenrir's claws had come down. Nothing. She pressed harder, feeling for torn cloth, for blood. The skin was whole and dry, and it throbbed anyway, as if the claws were still in it.

She forced out a breath, then another, until her heart quit hammering. Then she looked toward the palace, far off in the distance.

If she'd gone herself and given him the blade's true name, he might have let her get close enough to talk. She remembered it, even if her illusion hadn't.

But she also knew her son had never trusted easily. She had taught him that herself, lie by lie, year after year. Now he'd turned all that suspicion on her. He knew her angles and her reflexes and which lie she'd reach for first, and she couldn't beat Fenrir by being Loki.

She needed someone he didn't know. The only one who fit was Baldr, who hated her more than anyone, and she would have to stand in front of him and ask.

Her jaw clenched hard enough to make her teeth ache.

"Haaa... I'm coming, baldy."

She vanished.

At the base of the column, the wind stopped.

Barry noticed the quiet first. Baldr went rigid a moment later, and their grips on the stone loosened, little by little.

"What's happening?" Barry asked, staring up at the enormous wings. "Did he notice us or something?"

Hraesvelgr's wings were still shifting in slow movements that stirred no air. A few feathers drifted down, each one longer than an extension ladder. The rest hung without a tremor.

Barry glanced at Baldr. The god was barely upright. What was left of his divinity sputtered around him in weak sparks that kept dying, and his golden wings hung limp at his back. Then his head jerked up, eyes searching the dark above and around them.

"She shouldn't be..." Baldr whispered, mostly to himself.

"Baldr, what's going on? Don't space out on me, buddy, not with this bird moving like that."

Baldr turned to him. "Why ask me? Ever since you showed up, it's been nothing but chaos here."

"Man, I'm just a mortal. You said so yourself. What do I have to do with the earthquake, or Hellheim, or this bird acting suspicious?"

Baldr sighed, still trying to coax some power back into himself. "I've been asking myself the same thing," he murmured, eyes on the dark above.

Barry looked up too. Maybe the thing hadn't noticed them at all. Maybe they were too small to bother with.

That hope didn't last. Baldr started whispering again, too low to catch, and the wings kept shifting without stirring any air. Barry didn't need his goggles to know who he was talking to.

"I hope you're talking our way out of this," he said.

Barry counted three more feathers drifting down before Baldr's whispering stopped and he opened his eyes. He looked at Barry.

"This is it for me. I can't do more than this."

Baldr tried to leave. His wings spread, and the last scraps of his power gathered in the joints and along the primary feathers. The wings gave him nothing, no lift, no light, and he dropped. Barry caught him against the stone and took the weight on his shoulder, then shoved him off, harder than he needed to. The guy had been ready to bolt, and Barry wasn't in the mood to hold him up.

"What the hell was that for?"

Baldr said nothing for a few seconds. Then, quietly: "Hraesvelgr spoke to me."

"What did he ask?"

Before Baldr could answer, Hraesvelgr moved at last. The long neck curved down until the bird was looking right at them. One enormous eye rolled in its socket, slow and deliberate, while the rest of the head stayed perfectly still. Neither of them spoke. Then the whole head began to turn.

The voice that followed shook dust loose from the stone.

"Baaaaallllldr. You tooooooook your time."

The words came out huge and muddy, and Barry couldn't make sense of any of it, so he watched Baldr's face. Baldr didn't flinch. He let out a slow breath, the kind you let out when someone you've been avoiding finally walks through the door. Barry looked from the god to the bird and back. They knew each other.

Barry leaned in so only Baldr could hear. "Come on, Baldr. What did it say? Do we get out of here?"

Baldr was silent for a moment. "Escape is impossible," he said at last. "We need terms."

Hraesvelgr had heard the mortal. The huge head swung around until one eye hung level with Barry's face, and it stayed there, unblinking, like it wanted to see how long a living man lasted among the dead.

Then Hraesvelgr made his offer to the god of light.

"Leave the mortal. I will erase your presence from Hela's knowledge. No records, no pursuit. You walk free."

Baldr's reply echoed through the vulture's mind. "Why let me go, giant? If your queen finds out, it's treason. It'll cost you your life."

The eye slid back to Barry, and Baldr, very carefully, looked anywhere but at him.

"Simple. The prophecy needs the gods alive. It says nothing about the mortal."

Barry caught almost none of it. He didn't need to. Baldr's face had closed up, and Barry's stomach clenched.

Baldr stopped talking.

Barry waited for him to say no, and gave him all the time he needed. The quiet just went on. Baldr's eyes stayed on the stone between his feet.

"Baldr."

Nothing.

"Look at me. I know we only just met, but surely..."

Baldr's jaw worked. The hand braced against the column was shaking. Barry watched it and knew Baldr had already decided. It had happened sometime in the last minute, while Barry was busy talking.

Barry tried anyway, keeping his voice low and steady. The bird wanted them split up, he said. Nobody hands out a free walk out of here for nothing. A deal that only worked if one of them got thrown away wasn't a deal, it was a trap with a door painted on it.

Baldr answered without lifting his head.

"I know."

Barry had been ready for a lie, or a plea. He hadn't been ready for that. His hands curled into fists. Baldr wasn't being fooled. He saw all of it, and he was doing it anyway.

"My heart won't accept this," Baldr said, "but I won't be the one who lets the light go out." His voice cracked on the last word. "If I stay, it all ends here. Do you understand? All of it. I can't spend that on you."

Barry did understand, and that was the part he couldn't forgive. It was a good argument. Baldr had been the first person here who asked for nothing. He'd sat down beside Barry, split his water with him, and never once looked at him like a tool. They'd barely met, and it hurt anyway.

"I'm sorry, Barry."

His hand closed around Barry's throat.

There was no anger in the grip. Barry could feel the tremor still running through the fingers.

Barry managed to get the name out once more, half crushed. "Baldr..."

Baldr threw him.

The ground hit him wrong. His leg folded under him, and the crack went up through his hip and rattled his teeth. Something tore along the thigh, hot and wet. Now he was only waiting for the pain. It arrived late.

"Aaaagh!"

He lay there, breathing, his eyes still on Baldr's back.

Baldr was walking away. He didn't turn around. His shoulders were stiff, his wings dragging in the dust, and Barry thought, through the ringing in his head, that it would've been easier if the guy had been cruel. He could have hated cruel. This was a good man doing the math, and Barry was the number he could afford to lose.

"So this is how it ends?"

He thought about the System that had dropped him here. About retiring: a porch, a thermos of coffee, a morning with nowhere he had to be. It had always seemed like such a small, reasonable thing to want. Lying in the dirt with his leg like that, he couldn't even picture the porch.

He kept watching, because some stupid animal part of him still wanted Baldr to stop.

Baldr did not stop.

A shadow fell over Barry. He looked up. The giant mouth opened.

There were no teeth, no tongue, nothing his eyes could hold on to. They kept sliding across it, searching for a back wall, an edge, and finding only black.

He couldn't have run on that leg, and his arms were shaking too hard to drag him anywhere and at some point, He stopped trying to.

"Haha... You win, you one eyed bastard."
