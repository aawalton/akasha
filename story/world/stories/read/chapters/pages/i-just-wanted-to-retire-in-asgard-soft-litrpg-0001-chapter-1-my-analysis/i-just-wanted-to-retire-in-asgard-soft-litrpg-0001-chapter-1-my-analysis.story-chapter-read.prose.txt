He had a title. One he'd given himself, of course.

Three years ago he'd scrawled SMARTEST PERSON IN THE UNIVERSE on a sticky note and slapped it on the bathroom mirror. The corners had curled and the ink had faded since then, but he still needed it every morning. He'd brush his teeth, look at the note, and read it out loud. It had never made him proud. It was a fact, and nobody gets proud of facts.

As usual, he was down in his lab cave, buried deep inside Everest, the highest mountain on Earth. Nine particle accelerators hummed and shuddered around him as he walked between them, putting out far more energy than the entire planet had ever produced.

He'd solved the heat problem years ago, back when the rest of the world was still arguing over whether real AI was five years away or fifty.

He stopped at the atomic scope and let out a slow breath.

"Today, I will make history… Multiversal portal travel, experiment three," Barry told the recorder. He bent over the scope, hands perfectly still. Steady as they come.

He'd promised himself this would be the last experiment. After today he'd retire and try to live like a "normal" person, whatever that involved. He was almost forty and still single. On paper, it was the perfect time to start over. But he'd always been consumed by his work, and his curiosity had never once agreed with his better judgment.

Maybe this time it would work. Maybe, after all these years, he could finally step away from the endless hunt for answers.

So he took the risk.

There was no way to test it first. The only way to find out whether it was safe was to already be past the point of no return, and he'd known that two weeks ago, when he first wrote the program. He hadn't rerun the numbers since. They were never going to be perfect, and that was exactly why he'd built his whole life around never leaving anything unfinished.

"…and done." He stepped back from the scope and crossed to his ancient IBM PC, which he'd rebuilt so many times that not one original part remained.

"Okay. Starting in five, four, three, two, one—"

Click.

Enter.

All nine accelerators fired at once, slamming a torrent of blue energy into a single atom at the very center. A tiny wormhole snapped into existence right in front of his eyes. He couldn't help wishing he'd put his goggles back on. It was exactly how he'd pictured it. For a second, that was enough.

The monitor flashed SUCCESS.

[Access reached][Number of parallel universes detected: …1..2..3..35..125..37163…99999999999–]

The numbers didn't stop. They kept climbing, past what the screen had room for, past anything a human mind could process. The old machine chewed through the count until it was buckling under the weight of what it had been asked to find.

He had predicted this. The math had told him so. But certainty is always hopeless. Hope only lives in the things we don't know yet.

He just hadn't known it would feel like this.

It wasn't wonder, though he still had room for that somewhere. It was the ground vanishing beneath thirty years of being the only genius in every room. He'd been so far ahead of everyone for so long that going it alone had never really been a choice.

He'd made his peace with that a long time ago. Even if the universe was too big for one man to understand, he'd told himself, the top rung of the greatest ladder ever built would still be within his reach. Up there, at the very peak of science, he'd finally find the fulfillment and purpose that would make all that isolation worthwhile.

But the numbers just kept going. There was no top. There had never been a top. He was alone. Forever. And now he had the proof.

His hands, steady all morning, had started to shake. As if from far away, he heard himself murmur into the recorder, "…retire, done—"

BOOM!

Everest itself erupted. The smoke rose so high that people in China and Nepal could see it, and the ground shook beneath their feet.

And with that, the smartest person in the universe was no longer on planet Earth.

Of course.

[Host found]

Huh.

The moment he opened his eyes, he recognized the blue screen hanging in front of him.

[Analyzing IQ…][Worthy!]

Of course there's a system.

[Assigning role…]

He'd read enough novels to know exactly where this was going.

He poked at the screen anyway, hunting for an edge to drag or a setting to change. Nothing. No menus, no way out, not a single sensor he could find. Who made this thing? His grandma could design a better UI, and she'd been dead for nine years.

[TERMS OF ROLE ASSIGNMENT ARE NON-NEGOTIABLE. NO EXIT CONDITION EXISTS.]

He stopped poking. The thing had spoken to him, and he knew right away it wasn't an error message. He'd take it apart later, the same way he took apart everything that made him angry.

[ROLE ASSIGNED: MULTIVERSAL ENGINEER]

Another title, and this one he hadn't even had to write himself. Then came the ride.

[Transit Bifrost acquired][Destination: Asgard, home of the Aesir]

"Hold on. Shouldn't this be some kind of fantasy—"

The rest of the sentence vanished. Color and sound rushed past him from no source at all, and a hard pull came from every direction at once. For a moment it felt like a live wire had been run straight through his body.

He knew that feeling. Leftover accelerator energy. What bothered him was that whatever was powering this trip, whatever was keeping him alive through it, should have been incapable of either. And yet it was doing both.

He landed somewhere, on something. He had no idea what.

All he knew was that the ground was rough under his feet. Fear should have been screaming at him by now. Instead, before he'd even opened his eyes, he was calculating weight, air pressure, and temperature.

Gravity was slightly off for his weight. The air pressure didn't match any atmosphere he knew. The temperature made no sense at all. Everything was wrong. Every last bit of it.

He opened his eyes and the whole hall blazed gold, so bright he could barely see. An old man rode toward him on an eight-legged steed of solid gold. Behind him strode a towering figure with a hammer on one shoulder, so big he seemed to fill the sky.

"Human… Welcome."

Barry wasn't looking at Odin. He was already on his knees, not bowing to a possible god but pressing his ear to the stone floor. The vibration came up through his palms, and it told him something nobody else had noticed yet.

The frequency was completely off. The structure he'd taken for solid stone was being held up by something that had already started to slip.

"Did you summon a deaf man, All-Father?" the giant rumbled.

Barry ran the numbers again while the Aesir stared at him.

"Human!" Odin snapped.

At last Barry stood and looked straight into Odin's single eye.

"Okay, I see the problem. All this ceremonial gold isn't just for show, is it? It's load-bearing. That's your first mistake. I can fix it in two hours, but the ornamentation has to come off."

Silence.

Odin looked at Thor. Thor looked back. Down the ranks, soldiers traded glances. Then the hall erupted in laughter.

The laughter rang off the gold ornamentation, which Barry now suspected was failing at a frequency nobody else could hear.

"Silence," Odin said.

Everything stopped. The laughter, the hum of the bridge, even the soft breathing of the horse.

Odin dismounted slowly and crossed to the stranger in a few long strides. He looked hard into the younger man's eyes.

"Four hundred years," he said. "My dwarves have walked this floor for four hundred years, and not one of them ever heard a thing. You have not looked up from it once since you arrived, and yet you heard it before I finished greeting you."

Barry didn't answer. This wasn't the time.

"Two things are possible," Odin said. "Either you are the mind the prophecy spoke of, one who can achieve what I cannot. Or you are the most fortunate of men, who pressed his ear to the floor of Asgard by pure chance and invented this wild tale to win Odin's attention."

"Check the eighth frequency," Barry said. "I don't know what you people use for instruments around here. Magic, no doubt. But check it and you'll find the error easily."

Something shifted in Odin's eye at the flat certainty in Barry's voice.

"You lecture me. Mortal."

"Why not? It's the job, apparently."

The god raised a glowing hand, and all around them the soldiers began to shut their eyes. They'd seen this many times before.

Barry closed his too, though not out of fear. It was an old lab habit, from years of dodging blinding light.

Thud.

A hand landed on his shoulder instead.

"Consider yourself blessed with the Odinforce," Odin said.

It wasn't the warm glow blessings were supposed to give. It felt like the god was measuring out something powerful and explosive and setting it inside him, very carefully.

"But I deal in results, Engineer. Bold claims are so often nothing but noise, so I hope you're ready to work." He looked Barry up and down, and his expression went flat. "I would say more, but I need you covered up first."

Barry glanced down. He was, in fact, completely naked.

Of course. Of course he was bloody naked. He had no idea how the transfer worked, and there was no reason to think it would treat his clothes any better than it had treated the rest of him.

"You could've mentioned that earlier."

"Where's the fun in that? Guards. Dress the Engineer. Then we speak."

"Speak about what?"

"Ragnarok. The realms. The war. All of it."

It sounded like a grand adventure, all glory and purpose, exactly what you'd expect from a god or a hero. Barry knew the shape of it from all those novels. It just wasn't what a man on the verge of retirement had in mind.

And this wasn't even a proper beginning. It wasn't even a damn reward. A god had locked something huge inside a body Barry didn't fully trust yet, even if it did look a lot younger than the one he'd left behind.

Barry had solved a universe's worth of problems in his time. He'd solved the last of them barely an hour ago, and he was ready to stop. The universe, it seemed, had other ideas.

He looked up at the starry sky. It was beautiful. That didn't stop him from shouting the next words at the top of his lungs.

"All I wanted was to just RETIRE!"
