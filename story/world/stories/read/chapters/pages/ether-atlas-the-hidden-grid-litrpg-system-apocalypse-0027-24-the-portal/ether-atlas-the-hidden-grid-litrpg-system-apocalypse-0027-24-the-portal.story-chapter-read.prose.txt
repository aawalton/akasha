Chapter 24

“Maintain the infrastructure. Embed the knowledge. Do not expect gratitude. Gratitude is a luxury the schedule does not accommodate.” - Codex of the Long Watch, Founding Tenet

Donna crossed to Arthur and pressed two fingers lightly against his left side. Arthur caught her wrist before she reached the worst area.

“Either bruised or cracked,” he said with a wince.

“Can you walk?”

“Yes, but not well. And your temple is leaking again.”

Donna looked towards the open shaft. “We get ourselves working before we go down there.”

Arthur nodded and lowered himself against the nearest column. He was asleep before his head settled against the stone.

When he woke, it was still light outside.

“How long was I out?”

“A couple of hours,” Donna said. “How do you feel?”

Arthur gently pressed his ribs. They were still tender, but the pressure no longer sent a jolt of pain through his side.

“Not great, but better. How are you two?”

“Not bleeding any more and the headache is gone,” Donna said, checking the cut at her temple with her fingertips.

“I’m mostly all right,” Steve said, handing Arthur some water and a protein bar. “You took the worst of it.”

“What do you think is down there?” Arthur asked as he chewed.

“I’ve been thinking about it. My best guess is either a secret passage or a hidden chamber,” Steve said. “It would be our luck to stumble across a Templar treasure hoard just after gold plummeted on the Exchange.”

“We could go down there and find out instead of brainstorming up here,” Donna said. “Once you’ve finished eating.”

As they descended the stairs the first thing Arthur noticed was the absence of joints.

A medieval masonry staircase was an assembly of worked stone. However precise the masons, the structure revealed itself in the bedding lines between treads, the joins around the central column and the points where each step entered the surrounding wall.

Here, there was none of it.

The treads, walls and central column flowed into one another without a single seam or a change in grain. The entire spiral appeared to have been cut from one continuous mass of a dense grey-blue stone that Arthur couldn’t recognise.

“Not medieval,” he said.

“Not anything,” Steve said from behind him, one hand on the wall. “Not Roman, not Saxon, not anything I’ve seen in fieldwork. This stone predates the church by a margin I’m not comfortable to estimate.”

The air warmed as they descended and the blue light on the walls grew stronger. About thirty metres down, the staircase opened into a chamber and Arthur had no doubt that this chamber was far older and yet far superior to anything on the surface.

The chamber was circular, roughly twenty-five metres across, and the walls curved upward into a domed ceiling. The entire chamber was made of the same grey-blue stone, and he couldn’t see a seam or a joint. Either the whole chamber was carved from solid bedrock or constructed from blocks so perfectly joined that tens of thousands of years had failed to reveal the seams.

“The dome geometry is a catenary curve,” Archie said. “Optimal for compression without reinforcement. The Curators were given the specifications. They executed adequately.”

Seeder symbols covered every surface. The geometric patterns flowing in continuous bands and glowing with ethereal blue light.

The air was denser than the surface but also felt cleaner. Arthur could feel it flood his Nadi-Matrix, a nourishing force.

At the centre of the chamber stood a stone circle.

It was ten metres across and raised six inches above the surrounding floor. The surface was unlike anything else in the chamber. It was not made of the grey-blue stone but something darker, almost the perfect black. It glowed with a faint translucence that caught the ambient blue light and bent it.

When Arthur looked at it directly the surface appeared solid. When he looked at it from the edge of his vision, it appeared to have depth, layers beneath layers, as though the stone were a window into something far below.

Concentric rings were etched into its face, each carrying a different symbol set, and all were oriented towards a central point that stood out in the chamber in sharp contrast. It was stark and white in a chamber of grey, blue and black.

Arthur took a step forward and held his hand over the edge of the stone ring. The air right over the central circle felt thicker, almost substantial. This was concentrated Ether at a density they had never encountered.

The Printworks had been heavy. The chamber above was heavy. This was something else entirely. This felt like the Grid’s circulatory system, and standing at a junction where the blood ran thickest.

Steve made a gasp and crouched at the edge of the circle, his eyes moving across the concentric rings, reading symbols he’d only ever seen as individual marks on scattered stones across four continents.

“This is the source,” he said. His voice was barely above a whisper. “Every symbol I’ve catalogued. Every mark on every ancient site. They’re all here. Together. In context. This is where they came from.”

“Transit platform,” Archie stated. His geometry had shifted into a configuration Arthur hadn’t seen before. It looked more complex, more active; the chamber seemed to be having an effect on him as well. “Grid conduit interface. Operational.”

“Operational,” Steve repeated. “As in functional. Right now?”

“The platform has been operational since the Ether density threshold was reached at System activation. The conduit network will increase in capacity as the planet Ether integration progresses."

Arthur looked at the white circle at the centre of the platform and the rings of symbols he couldn’t read. A piece of alien engineering built before human civilisation, maintained by people who didn’t understand what they were maintaining, and now, for the first time in thirty-five thousand years, switched on.

“Where does it go?” Donna asked. She’d completed her perimeter sweep and was standing with her back to the wall. Her face had an expression that Arthur had rarely seen, uncertainty.

“The platform interfaces with the next confirmed node in the candidate’s transit sequence,” Archie said.

“That’s not what I asked. I asked where.”

“I have provided the answer within my authorisation parameters.”

Steve stood up. “He can’t tell us. Or won’t. The destination is determined by the puzzle path. It goes where the path goes. It seems we’ve completed London’s node sequence. The next node is wherever the next node is.”

“So, this could dump us in Dallas or Dublin and we’ve no idea which.”

“We know it’s the next node in a sequence that the Curators designed. The path is logical. It follows geographic and historical connections. It won’t drop us somewhere random.”

“You’re theorising.”

“I’m pattern-matching based on every data point we have.”

“I’m not stepping on a glowing alien rock without knowing what’s on the other side,” Donna said, looking at the platform. “And I need to check up on Emma.”

“Agreed,” Arthur said.

“Archie,” Arthur said. “Has this platform been used before? By anyone?”

“Ether density on the planet was never sufficient to activate a transit node. This platform and the infrastructure around it have been sealed since construction.”

“I still can’t believe that the Curators founded an entire military-religious order to protect this site. Build a round church over it. Keep the underground chambers sealed and undisturbed,” Steve said, shaking his head, “All to preserve the infrastructure for a day centuries away. That’s a fair bit of forward planning.”

“They were guided adequately.” Archie said.

“Care to elaborate?” Steve said.

“No. Elaboration will not provide you with any useful information for your next steps.”

Arthur was making a circuit of the chamber while he was listening to Steve. It was not often that you got to stand in a chamber more ancient than anything he had ever seen. His hands trailed along the wall behind him. Smooth. Smooth. Smooth. Smooth. And then, on the far side opposite the staircase, his fingertips caught a seam.

A section of wall sat fractionally recessed from the rest. A millimetre, maybe two, it was invisible to the eye but not to a hand that had spent years reading stone. And a recessed joint in an otherwise flush surface meant one of two things: either a structural settlement or a concealed opening.

“There’s a door,” he said as he hooked his finger between the wall and the recess and pulled.

The panel swung inward on a central pivot, balanced so precisely that it moved with one hand despite weighing what Arthur estimated was three tonnes at least.

It opened into a passage where the only illumination were the glowing ether lines. The stone transitioned from Seeder grey-blue to London clay brick within the first few dozen metres.

“This is going southeast,” Donna said, reading the angle, “towards the river.”

“Must be Temple Stairs. The Templars had their own dock on the Thames. It’s still on old maps.” Steve said.

Donna looked at Arthur. “Makes sense, doesn’t it? One through the church for the puzzle, one through here for logistics. You don’t move cargo through a church nave.”

“The front door and the back door,” Arthur nodded.

It took them about five minutes to walk the passage. The exit was a concealed door in the old river wall. From inside, a hinged stone panel was balanced on a pivot. From outside, it would look like Victorian brickwork beneath the Embankment.

Arthur tested the mechanism from both sides. Opened it, then closed it, then opened it again. “Stone on stone. There is no metal to corrode. Someone has been keeping this passage working for a very long time.”

They emerged onto the Thames foreshore at low tide. The Embankment walkway above was empty. London, quiet in the early morning.

They had a way out of London. A platform that could take them somewhere they couldn’t name, through a mechanism they didn’t understand, to a destination the system wouldn’t reveal. It would be a leap of faith through an alien stone.

But not yet. Not without checking up on Emma. Arthur looked north; it was three streets to Emma’s restaurant.

“Let’s go and find your sister,” Arthur said, turning to Donna.

As they took the side street, a pair of Ether-mutated rats, chitin running along their spines, bolted from behind an overturned skip and came at them.

Donna killed the first before Arthur’s shield was fully raised, the Conduit-Edge Axe bisecting the creature mid-leap. The second veered towards Steve. He planted the Reacher and let it impale itself on the blade. Three seconds. Both dead.

THE ETHER ATLAS: FIELD NOTES OF STEVEN PARR

London

THE STATIONS CAME FIRST

The platform beneath Temple Church is approximately thirty-five thousand years old. London is not.

Archaeology normally explains a city from the surface down. A useful river crossing attracts settlement. The settlement acquires roads, walls, markets and temples. Each generation builds over the last until the original reason for the place has been buried.

Except the platform was already here.

Before the Belgae. Before the Romans. Before the name Londinium. Before anyone decided that this bend in the Thames should become anything more than mud, water and a place to cross between them, someone had already built a station beneath it.

Which brings me to the three possibilities.

The first is that the Curators knew where London would eventually stand. Not merely that humans would build beside the Thames, but that this bowl of chalk would become important enough to justify placing planetary infrastructure beneath it tens of thousands of years in advance.

The second is that London stands here because the platform does. Perhaps people can feel a node without understanding what they are feeling. For millennia cultures have documented place seems sacred, fortunate or Powerful. A shrine appears, then a road, then a market. Centuries later, historians explain the city through trade and geography because those are the causes that leave evidence.

The third is deliberate guidance. The Curators place the infrastructure, then spend millennia steering human hands towards it. A Roman temple here. A Templar church there. Different religions, different empires, the same patch of ground protected and rebuilt whenever history knocks it down.

Historians have a term for this: continuity of sacred use. Archaeologists find a church over a Roman temple over an older ritual site and describe the persistence of holy geography. We congratulate ourselves for recognising the pattern, then stop just before asking why the same ground keeps being sacred for different cultures.
