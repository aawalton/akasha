Thunder cracked, echoing through the streets. Cinder paid no mind to the rain soaking her, sliding from her hair and getting into her eyes. She was too intent on hopping from island to island, the ships of her feet landing on uncharted territories, avoiding the seas where nothing but monsters of the deeps lurked. Her boots kicked up mud with another successful arrival. She scrunched her nose, wondering if perhaps the explorers before her failed, their ships sinking, leaving treasures in the infinite depths of those mysterious waters.

Another rumble boomed, rattling the unsteady beams of the wooden shacks lining the dark alley. Cinder set her eyes on the next island, this one large enough to be a continent. Cinderland, she would name this one. The rain had uncovered two little peaks of jagged rocks around the middle, shining and wet. Maybe she would build his capital there, high in the clouds. Unless there was a dragon's lair around those parts. She grinned with anticipation, piles of gold and emerald adorned swords before her. No, she needed to defeat the dragon first. Then she'd get the swords. Magic swords, actually. She lifted the stick in her hand, burning with brilliant fire that was the legacy of a rift phoenix slayer. Or was it another dragon? Her next sword will be a lightning sword, she decided. Just as soon as she defeated this new dragon. A lightning-dragon with storm clouds for wings and thunder to summon with his roar.

Cinder stepped back, ready to leap over the puddle-ocean, but there was another flash of light illuminating the alley. Just for a moment, she could make out a bundle of rags further down the road, tucked in the crook of a half-ruined stairway leading into a veiled backstreet. That wasn't an unusual sight in itself, and the light was only present for a second, but it almost appeared as if the rags were moving.

The world returned to Cinder, boring and uninteresting with no treasures to find and islands to uncover. Only the mud and the rain and the city streets. She was again aware of the cold. Night had fallen and the streets were empty, dark without the presence of moonlight still being held captive by the storm.

She looked back to the bundle of cloth. Could be rats under there. Rats, or maybe cats. Stray dogs, those too. Her hand clenched the stick tighter, but it didn't stop her from stepping closer to the bundle, even as water squeaked out and rushed back into her boots. She couldn't hear anything but the drumming water, and the occasional growl in the distance. She was always good at listening, relying on her ears more so than her eyes, but now her skills were useless. Hmph. Stupid storm.

Carefully, Cinder crouched at the bundle, leaning over it. She poked it with the stick. It was still dark, but she could tell it was definitely moving. Cinder sniffed. The other kids would have surely jumped away by now, thinking of monsters and alligators with sharp teeth on their tails. She'd never actually seen an alligator, but she'd heard they liked water, and it was raining. But she wasn't afraid of them. She hadn't come into her surge yet, but she had a stick-sword, after all.

Still, Cinder wasn't daft. She liked her fingers exactly the way they were, hers, so she didn't touch the rags. She set her eyes on them, and waited. When another flash of light came from the lightning overhead, she might've cried out in surprise, but nobody would ever know, swallowed by the storm.

The dark returned, but the pale face of a baby with his mouth open and crying was taking up her mind's eye that was normally reserved for monsters and magics. She let the stick fall from her fingers, and she hesitantly reached down. Her fingers touched skin, cold and wet from the rain and maybe from tears. She was so entranced, she jumped when another thunder rolled through the street.

She had found many things in the past, among the trash, left behind in the wake of rolling carts, but a baby was a first. She looked down the road, then into the narrow backstreet, but she was alone. Strange.

She sniffed again, and after some thinking, she bent down and lifted the bundle and the baby nestled inside. From this close, there was no doubt it was a little human, shifting, eyes blinking open from time to time.

Cinder stood with the baby in her arms, her quest completely forgotten. Was this where babies came from? A storm sweeping over a busy city, and then pop, there it is, an unexpected treasure to find. She never wondered before, until now. Was it possible to tell the difference between a girl storm and a boy storm? Mom would know the answer. When she gets home, she would ask her. When she'd brought kittens home in the past, dad never let her keep them, but maybe they'd let her keep the baby? She had many friends already, but a friend at home would be even better.

Cinder walked faster, and by the time the next boom of thunder arrived, she was already making plans for the next quest.

But in this one, she wasn't skipping alone.

Warmlight found the city of Parlest in the usual state of rousing, like a well-worn wheel spurring into action.

Mera hurried along with Kalsin holding her hand as if the tide of humanity threatened to sweep him away. Soon the cobblestone road beneath their feet fell away, replaced by dirt as they entered the heart of the underworld. Even without guards, without walls or any sort of blockade separating living quarters of Clenn and Scarrd, the difference was obvious. Chiseled brick houses gave way to wooden structures, some well maintained, while others stood worn by time, crooked, breaking into the wide streets like jagged teeth of a bestial angler fish.

Mera instinctively pulled Kalsin closer, but she didn't slow her stride. She ignored the calls from beggars sprawled along the road, as did most people, knowing that even the smallest amount of contribution wouldn't amount to anything. Or they just didn't care. It was hard to tell nowadays, the divide between those with spoilt, and those with whole surges having grown in the past years, as well as the number of Scarrd in the population. Mera could feel Kalsin drag occasionally, slowing down, pulling whenever they neared one of their kind, their tattered clothes required to display the black lines marring their skin.

That's why she'd brought him along today. At the age of five, the boy was much different than their Cinder. While Cinder had jumped headfirst into the swirling city life since she could walk, Kalsin was short on words, and even shorter on taking initiative. She could tell he was curious, trying to stare at everything and everyone at once, but still he squeezed her hand almost painfully. Cinder often teased him about it, and Tűzmother only knew if Kalsin noticed the twinkle in his sister's eyes, the smiles tugging at her lips. Curiously enough, she managed to draw him out of his shell more than often than not, her efforts ending with laughter in the end.

They turned a corner leading on a less populated path, and not a minute after they walked up to a homely looking house with a fenced off garden stretching next to it. Mera entered without knocking. With the opening door, the familiar chime of bells welcomed them inside.

A colorful assortment of anointments rested on the table nearby, some of them bottled up, with a hastily left pestle and mortar in the room further back. Mera watched as Anise took a piece of wet cloth and held it to the man's face, covering the black scar running across his forehead. "Hold it, just like that."

"It always is, after Blindlight had come and gone." She was already back to work, scuttling with age-defying speed between the maze of tables farther inside. "You can never have enough remedies prepared for burnt eyes, temporary blindness, and the like."

So naive, so wrong.

She'd been slacking with his education, but that would change. They were Clenn. It was time he understood its meaning.

A loud crack snapped the silence in half from behind her. Terrin turned to see his daughter frozen mid-step, her feet right on top of a broken branch, a sheepish expression on her face.

"Sorry," Cinder said, then winced. "Sorry," she repeated, whispering this time.

Terrin set his sights back on their prey, just in time to watch the disappearing backside of the deer as it fled deeper into the forest. He held back a sigh, slowly releasing the bowstring, then sliding back the arrow in the quiver at his waist. There went another day's work, hours of creeping and trailing with no result.

"It wasn't on purpose, promise," Cinder said. She was clutching her bow awkwardly in her hands, gazing wistfully ahead. Barely thirteen, she already had a strong resemblance to her parents, with piercing eyes that gave her an air of seriousness, but a mouth made for laughter to compromise.

"Matters little, doesn't it?" Terrin said. "That right there was the first deer I've seen in a month, a good week's worth of meat. Gone, just like that." But he wasn't angry, not in truth. When Cinder accompanied him on his hunts, his main priority was to teach his daughter the way of the forest, hunting, putting food on the table. Scoring dinner would've been a blessing, but he'd expected things to go wrong from the start. Still, it was important to scold her. Let her know she had made a mistake so she'd learn from it.

"Doe," Cinder said, pushing next to him, not even pretending to be quiet anymore.

"What?"

"It was a doe." She held one of her hands to her head, index finger extended. "No antlers."

Terrin turned away, hiding the stirrings of a smile. "And just where'd you learn that, huh?" he asked. "And don't you say school, because we both know you spend more time interrogating travelers in whichever tavern you happen to fancy."

Cinder shrugged. "Could've been any of them," she gave him a sidelong glance, mouth quirking up. "It was, doe."

Gods above and below, he prayed for another grain of patience to keep himself from cuffing the smartass in the head.

Shadows stretched in the late afternoon, falling on the lines of white birch trees standing at attention. It was quiet, save for the sounds of nature hugging them like a warm blanket, so different from the intrusive atmosphere of the city nearby. They drank it all in as they walked, letting their minds wander from the straining concentration of the past hours. Now with their hunt over, Terrin wished for the coming of rain, the light smell of petrichor a guest he would always savor.

"Dad?" Cinder asked, deep in thought.

Terrin kept walking in silence, only raising his brow in question.

"Next time you bring me along, could Kalsin come as well?"

And just like that, worries flooded back inside Terrin, the peace of the forest gone in an instant. Kalsin, Cinder's brother-by-raising. The boy that despite all his trying, all his time spent with him, he couldn't truly love. He still remembered that dark night years ago, Cinder coming home with a babe clutched tightly in her arms. A single look from his wife, Mera, taking the sleeping child in her hands, smiling and giggling not unlike when she'd held Cinder, and Terrin knew the future was set in stone.

They had argued. Him, saying they should give him to an orphanage, and her, that inextinguishable heat of hers. Terrin smiled despite himself. Of course, she knew he loved that about her. She'd always wanted another child, a brother or sister for Cinder, and that night it quite literally fell into her hands without Terrin having a say in the matter.

It wasn't Kalsin's fault. Not Cinder's, not Mera's. If there was anyone to blame, it was the heartless bastard who left a child in the middle of a storm, abandoned. Yet the lack of blame did nothing to change the harsh reality; Terrin didn't care for his children equally. And he hated the fact that he didn't. He hated that when he took in Kalsin’s odd features, his otherworldly silver eyes, they were so alien from his own.

"Dad?"
