Chapter 372

March of the Betrayed

Korelia, Lansius

Within their private wing, their squire and servants hurried about, preparing for their departure. Amid the commotion, Lansius held his daughter in his arms for the first time. They had yet to choose a name for the little one. Many had taken to calling her the raven-haired baby, for her lustrous black hair was as dark as her mother’s finest black dress. Audrey, however, preferred to liken it to the mane of her destrier.

“Raven-haired,” Lansius murmured softly as he studied his daughter’s delicate face and rocked her gently in his arms.

Unlike Gilly, this little one was remarkably calm despite being unfamiliar with her father’s scent.

As he considered possible names, Lansius remembered a lovely town in Italy called Ravenna. Then there was Arvena, the first land to which he and Audrey had pledged their loyalty. Somehow, the two names seemed connected.

“Raven, Ravenna, Arvena,” he repeated quietly. Then another possibility came to him. “Arven... Arwen?”

Lansius looked across the chamber at Audrey. “Drey.”

Audrey had been checking and packing her bows, but she immediately turned and came to him. “Yes, dear?”

When she reached him, the two merely stared at each other. Lansius had yearned for her so deeply that the question slipped entirely from his mind, and for a moment, he could do nothing but drink in the sight of her. They had been separated for far too long. Audrey gently placed her hand upon his arm and rested her head against his shoulder. The soft caress of her fingers was her wordless way of saying that she had missed him too.

“Was the birth difficult?” he whispered.

“Not as difficult as with Gilly,” she answered.

“Apologies for not being there for you again.”

“You’re here now,” Audrey said. “Don’t leave me again.”

Lansius gave a solemn nod.

“What made you wake up?” she asked.

“I wish I knew,” he answered. “But I feel so much better now. My vision in the left eye is clear again. The red has disappeared completely.”

Audrey said nothing, but continued to caress his arm gently. Her gaze was warm, her lips alluring, and even her familiar scent was enough to drive him mad with longing.

“How did you arrive here so quickly? I thought you were in Korimor,” he asked, forcing his thoughts elsewhere as Margo and an assistant passed behind them carrying a heavy chest filled with armor, cloaks, and spare clothes.

Instead of answering, Audrey drew a fine chain from beneath her collar. Hanging from it in place of a pendant was an earring.

“Ah,” Lansius murmured, recognizing it.

“Do you remember Francisca saying that she possessed an heirloom like this? I finally secured it."

“I see.” Lansius glanced around before lowering his voice. “And you keep an agent here?”

“Yes, someone of my own. I needed another pair of eyes here to watch over you.”

“Clever. You did the right thing."

“Francisca refused any payment for it. She believed that she had failed to protect you at the Monastery. Nevertheless, I granted her a title. She is Armiger Francisca now.”

Lansius drew a sharp breath. “After all she has done for our House, I’ll knight her myself. Remind me when we reach Canardia.”

Audrey’s lips curved into a pleased smile. Then, returning to his earlier question, she explained, “When I heard that you were awake, I ordered our departure at once. We fed the airship a rich mixture of volatile oil and drove her as hard as we dared. We set out around midnight and arrived a few hours after dawn.”

Lansius nodded. Troops marching from Korelia to Korimor usually needed fifteen days to complete the journey.

At an average of twenty miles per day, the roads between the two cities covered roughly three hundred miles.

An airship could travel directly, without following roads, or stopping at known sources of water. Lansius estimated that the direct distance was closer to 250 miles.

Judging by how easily the airship could outrun a horse at full gallop across the plains, he estimated that it could reach 40 miles per hour when driven hard.

At that speed, 250 miles would take little more than six hours. Even allowing for the prevailing southerly winds of summer, the journey could be completed in roughly seven hours.

Not even ten hours.

The silver vessel was phenomenal. Now they would rely upon that same speed to reach Edessa.

“How is Gilly?” Lansius asked, already knowing that the boy was staying at the Eastern Mansion, which had a spacious hall and garden for him to play in.

“He’s grown so much, you know.” Audrey smiled at the question. “He runs, he climbs, and he tries to do everything. But he’s still a fussy eater. Every time I leave, the staff pamper that boy. Ingrid says they see you in him.”

Lansius grinned, amused by the answer. Even now, he could scarcely comprehend that he had a son, let alone one who was already two years old. “Can he speak yet?”

“Yes, though not very clearly yet. He still prefers to point at things and go, Nuh! Nuh! until someone gives him what he wants.” Audrey imitated the boy’s demanding voice as she spoke.

At the sudden noise, their daughter stirred uneasily in Lansius’ arms and turned her face against his chest.

Lansius and Audrey could only look down at her with fond smiles.

Audrey then asked, “Has Ingrid told you about the arrangements for Gilly?”

Lansius nodded. Ingrid had already explained that it was not yet advisable for him to meet Gilly. The physician had warned that some illnesses could pass readily from parent to child, particularly between those of the same blood. Since no one knew what had caused Lansius’ unnatural sleep, they feared that Gilly might fall victim to the same affliction. As the boy was also his heir, everyone believed that such caution was necessary.

“There’s no need to worry. I understand,” he assured her. “I think it’s best for now. I don’t want to risk his safety.”

“Before the physician warned us, I brought Gilly to visit you often. He would sit on your chest and even kiss your cheek, though he stopped doing that after your beard grew so thick.”

Lansius grinned. “Do I look better with it?”

“Somewhat,” she admitted. “But you need to trim it a little.”

Lansius snorted softly.

“You should at least see Gilly from the carriage before we leave. There should still be enough time. He'll be so surprised to find you awake.”

Lansius hesitated before asking, “Wouldn’t it upset him to see me only from afar, especially if we must leave him again so soon?”

“At least he’ll remember that he saw you awake." She then added, "We’re going to war, remember...”

Lansius nodded, accepting that Audrey was right. “Who’ll look after him?”

“Whenever I’m away, Lady Astrid looks after him. Our son and her daughter are inseparable.”

Lansius nodded again. He was glad that Gilly had a companion his own age.

As they continued talking, Lady Ella entered with Claire amid the bustle of servants gathering the baby’s belongings for the journey.

“My Lord, My Lady.” The red haired girl curtsied, keeping her voice low so as not to wake the baby, who had fallen asleep in Lansius’ arms.

“Lady Ella,” Lansius greeted her, then carefully passed the baby into Audrey’s arms.

He continued, “My gratitude for your help, and my apologies for all the trouble.”

“My Lord.” Lady Ella inclined her head, then handed Claire a small wooden box, the sort used to protect small, delicate objects.

“Please accept these mana flowers, gathered by my grandfather,” Lady Ella explained as Claire opened the box and loosened the silken pouch within.

“Mana flowers,” Lansius murmured as he studied the dried blossoms with growing interest. Even after being dried, their petals retained a purple radiance. Something about them felt familiar.

“My Lord may not remember, but while you were asleep, a concoction made from these flowers was the only thing that drew any response from you. That is why—”

“No,” Lansius interrupted, his expression brightening. “I remember.”

His words drew everyone’s attention.

“I remember hearing Lord Avery’s voice, and I remember him giving me something.” His face softened as he recalled the help he had received when he needed it most. He nodded and continued, “Yes, I believe it had a good effect on me. It gave me strength. It may even have been what finally helped me recover. Then I shall accept it. Gratitude.”

Lady Ella was pleased to hear this and dipped her head. “We have only enough left for a single dose, but I hope it will serve you well.”

“I shall treasure it.” He then offered, “Lady, if you have a letter for your grandfather, entrust it to me. I shall carry it to him myself.”

“You intend to meet him, My Lord?” she asked.

“Indeed. I believe your grandfather may hold the key to resolving this crisis in Edessa. I intend to depart within a few hours.”

Lady Ella considered this briefly before asking, “Whom will you take with you, My Lord?”

“My guards and the Lady of Korimor,” he said, turning toward Audrey, who gave a nod. “We shall also take the squires, several people whose skills may prove useful, Sir Michael, and the two lords who are here. And we shall bring many, many gifts for the Margrave’s army.”

“Don’t forget our own army,” Audrey reminded him as she rocked the sleeping baby in her arms.

“Certainly. As it happens, I found several interesting things in our stores,” Lansius remarked. The time he had spent examining the House’s records had paid off.

Among the supplies Lansius intended to bring was an unusual assortment of valuable provisions acquired during their campaigns in Midlandia, Five Peaks, and Edessa, many of which were regarded as costly medicines.

He had also ordered his men to search the market for other unlikely ingredients, including verjuice pressed from unripe grapes, wine vinegar of the kind used to make posca, and whatever dried citrus could be found, whether citron, lemon, lime, or sour orange.

None of them understood what he intended, but with only a few hours before departure and every hand occupied, explanations could wait. They were all too pleased to see the Lord had a plan.

Skodra, Marc

Inside the market, amid a dizzying array of shops, wares, peddlers, and buyers, Marc relied on the coachman, who had traded before. He followed the older man in search of a reputable apothecary. Dried medicines, scented oils, and prepared salves seemed promising, as they would occupy little room in their carts, keep well on the road, and might command a fine price once they reached Korelia.

If they found a trustworthy vintner, the coachman also hoped to purchase several small casks of strong medicinal wine for the journey east.

The deeper they went, the louder the market became.

“The finest wares in Skodra, brought from Three Hills and Umberland!” an attendant proudly proclaimed from the entrance of a large shop. Several attendants were already busy serving the many shoppers inside.

The shop across the street answered at once.

"Dried pasta, fresh pasta! Every kind you can think of!”

“Copperware, brassware! Whatever you need, we have it.”

“Wine from Three Hills! Its fragrance speaks for itself!”

Marc and the coachman stopped briefly, but soon walked on, as the vintner’s shop was too crowded and its wares looked too expensive.

“Olive oil! Fresh-pressed oil!”

“Knives! Midlandian knives, straight from the Shogun’s workshops!” a young assistant outside a cutler’s shop cried. “Sharp enough to kill the undead and keep fell beasts away!”

“Ceramics, ceramics...” an older merchant called with far less force, though several customers were already browsing his wares.

“Hey, be a man! If you come back again, I won’t offer you the same price," an old woman merchant called after a departing buyer, drawing laughter from everyone around them.

“Mirrors and combs for the ladies!”

“Furs from the lands of the half-kins! Come and take a look."

Marc saw grey and white pelts hanging outside the shop, their thick coats rippling whenever the wind stirred them. A crowd had gathered to inspect the unusual wares, their faces showing either awe or doubt.

“Salt! Salt! Spices of every kind!”

“Come, don’t be shy. My costard don’t bite, though I might if you keep gawking. Buy while they’re fresh,” a costermonger called with a laugh, beckoning several hesitant customers closer.

“Dormice! Dormice, fat and plump!”

The two slowly made their way through the narrow streets, pressing ever deeper into the market. Carts and donkeys no longer passed through this part of the market. These lanes were not meant for bulky wares, but for smaller and more delicate goods.

After several turns, they passed two cheerful minstrels in bright doublets, one playing a lively tune on a gittern while the other accompanied him on a flute. Beyond them, Marc and the coachman found not merely one apothecary, but an entire row of them.

The medicine shops lined a narrow street branching from the market square. Their shelves were crowded with jars, bundles of dried herbs, and small clay pots sealed with wax.

“Perfumes! One breath, and she’ll look at you as though you were a nobleman’s son!”

“Antidotes for venom! Snake bile and dried skins!”

“Come, give them a look! Medicines of every kind, cures for whatever ails you."

They entered one of the shops, where sharp medicinal scents mingled with the smells of spices, lamp oil, leather, and perfume. An attendant greeted them, and the coachman handed over the list he had been carrying. Before placing an order, he carefully asked how much the goods would cost.

The attendant swiftly reckoned the prices on a counting board and named the total.

Marc could only listen closely and try to remember everything the attendant said about the wares, including dried medicines said to soothe the liver after drinking, scented oils, and prepared salves for the skin.

The apothecary who owned the shop sat behind the counter, fanning himself with a fine folding fan. He had dealt with enough people to recognize men who were new to the trade.

“You are bound for Korelia, I assume?” he asked Marc, who stood nearest to him.

“Y-yes,” Marc confirmed, surprised that the apothecary had chosen to speak to him.

“Is there no room for anything more in your cart? That seems a modest order for such a long journey,” the older man asked, his tone friendly.

“We would gladly purchase more,” the coachman replied. “But that is all the silver we can spare.”

The apothecary nodded and spoke in an encouraging tone. “You’ll do well. Everyone must start somewhere.”

He then turned to his attendant. “Add two small jars of the new oil to their order.”

“Yes, Meister.”

Marc and the coachman exchanged surprised looks.

“They were freshly pressed,” the apothecary explained. “They won’t turn rancid on your journey to Korelia, even if you remain in Three Hills for some time.”

“But we cannot afford them,” the coachman replied.

“Think nothing of it. Just remember my shop when next you return.”

“Gratitude, Meister.” Marc and the coachman could only lower their heads.

As the attendant finished the tally and they prepared to pay nearly seventy in silver, almost all the savings they had pooled together, the apothecary spoke again. “It might be wise to open an account before you leave.”

Marc looked at him. “An account?”

“The Bank lends to merchants with sound prospects. If your cart still has room, there is little sense in sending it eastward half-empty.”

Marc had nearly forgotten his mission. “I’ve heard of the Bank, but I know little about how it conducts its business.”

“They’re like a moneylenders’ guild, only not so cutthroat,” the old man said with a chuckle. “Just don’t try anything clever with them. They have soldiers of their own and the Shogunate’s backing.”

The old man glanced at Marc and, seeing that the customer was listening closely, continued. “Just look at what happened to the mountain clans.” He gave a contemptuous snort. “Served them right. You did well to come here after the mountain smugglers lost their hold over the trade. Before that, these goods cost five times as much. Now, as you can see, this place is almost a merchant’s paradise. Everything is cheap, even the white gold.”

“White gold?” Marc repeated, his interest growing.

The apothecary turned to one of the shelves and took down a small glazed earthenware jar. He removed the lid, revealing the white crystals within, then dipped in a small horn spoon and motioned for Marc to extend his palm.

Marc did so. The old man gathered only a few crystals on the tip of the spoon and tipped them into his palm.

The grains were fine and white, almost like salt, but slightly translucent.

“Put them in your mouth and bite down. You’ll like them,” the old man said with an expectant smile.

Marc did as instructed and was immediately amazed. His eyes widened as the sweetness spread across his tongue, and he savored every grain.

“In the Eastern Empire, they call it succar. Here, we call it white gold, white spice, or white medicine.”

“That’s a medicine?” the coachman asked as Marc offered him the few crystals remaining in his palm.

“Yes. It restores vigor to weary men, soothes coughs, and makes bitter draughts easier to swallow,” the apothecary explained. “It is also served at marriage feasts. Nobles coat almonds with it to make small candies for honored guests. Anyone who claims to be wealthy is expected to offer it at his wedding.”

Marc could only nod.

“If you had more silver, I could recommend no better purchase. It will fetch a high price in Korelia, where so many nobles now gather. And that is where the Bank becomes useful. If you have something to offer as collateral, you can borrow money from them.”

Marc nodded once more.

“Also, on your return journey, you can deposit your profits in Korelia and withdraw the silver here in Skodra, or in any other town within the Shogunate. The Bank takes a small fee, but that is better than losing everything to thieves.”

“Truly? They’ll know who I am and how much silver I have deposited?” Marc asked.

“As long as you open an account, they’ll have ways to verify both. Just remember to keep your documents safe. If you lose them, you may have to wait a few weeks while they request replacements from the central office.”

With a little hesitation, Marc asked, “Do you really trust the Bank with your money?”

“A smart man should never place everything he owns in another’s hands. Still, the Bank has done nothing but help this city,” the older man replied. “Besides, the Lord himself pays his men through the Bank.”

Marc frowned. “You mean...?”

“I mean, the Lord Shogun pays his vassals through the Bank. Bannerets, knights, armigers, officers, they all collect their money from its branches. If they trust it, why shouldn’t we?”

“Really?” Even the coachman sounded surprised.

“I suppose that is how the Shogunate keeps order across such a vast realm and still manages its affairs so efficiently.” The old man chuckled. “In some ways, it seems better ordered than the old Imperium.”

They continued their conversation for a while, and from the apothecary, the two learned that much of the Shogunate’s official business passed through the Bank, including the payment of wages and even the collection of taxes. Marc struggled to comprehend it all. He did not understand why everything needed to be handled this way, or what purpose it ultimately served.

Yet he could see that it made travel far safer. Merchants with accounts no longer needed to carry all their silver on the road.

Only then did it occur to him that such a complex system had likely been introduced by Lansius himself.

Once their purchases had been packed, the two made their way to the doorway, each carrying a wicker basket. The sealed jars and small clay pots nestled in straw beside cloth-wrapped bundles of dried medicines. Before stepping outside, they turned and inclined their heads once more to the generous apothecary, who chuckled and raised his folding fan in farewell.

Still, Marc could not easily put the thought aside. As they stepped back into the noise and bustle of the market, his stomach tightened with unease.

Brother, why are you so capable...

His heart sank. If Lansius truly was the Lord of Korelia, then Marc was no more than a tallow candle beside the sun.

I should have known. He was always too clever. He just didn’t know our language. He must have been a knight’s son from the Mercantile Kingdom. It all makes sense now. He was probably kidnapped for ransom while travelling, only to escape and lose his memory.

His unease was not entirely without cause. There were countless tales of men who rose in station and forgot the relatives they had left behind, or refused to acknowledge them out of shame. People often said that rank, wealth, and power could change a man.

Even if they found Lansius and he acknowledged Marc as his brother, perhaps he would only offer him some lowly position out of pity, tending horses in his stables or serving as a common guard.

Marc released a long breath, so consumed by these fears that he had entirely forgotten he was a mage.

Then another thought broke through his gloom. If Lansius truly possessed such wealth and power, perhaps he had already saved Mother and Tanya. The knot in Marc’s stomach began to loosen.

For the chance to see them again, Marc would endure any embarrassment or humiliation that awaited him.

Edessa

Even before the council message arrived, Sir Harold had begun his preparations. Warnings were sent throughout Sigisha and to the surrounding cities, and the evacuation commenced. Since early morning, people had been streaming from the gates in carriages and carts, or on foot with whatever they could carry. As word of the evacuation spread across the frontier, the number of refugees soon swelled into the hundreds of thousands.

The towns and cities around Sigisha had been on the front line for two years, but the war had not yet driven away most of their inhabitants. Until recently, life had continued despite the threat from the Empire.

Now all hope of holding the region was gone. The roads were choked with frightened families, exhausted animals, and carts laden with everything their owners could not bear to leave behind. All were making for Ostaria and the river crossing it guarded.

Yet not everyone fled. Many were too poor to make the journey, with neither carts nor provisions nor relatives who could shelter them elsewhere. Others refused to abandon their land, livestock, workshops, and everything their families had built over generations.

Many of the prominent landowners hoped to make terms with the Empire. Farmers remained to protect the fields and the homes their families had occupied for generations. Laborers, bakers, cobblers, blacksmiths, and others whose livelihoods could not simply be packed onto a cart chose to take their chances behind the city walls.

They clung to the hope that any new master would still require their labor and skills. Perhaps their usefulness would spare their homes from pillage and protect their families from violence. It was a desperate hope, but for many, it was the only one they had.

The forces of Blue and Bronze offered no assurances. They had themselves been betrayed and were in no position to render aid. They could not even promise protection to those who fled alongside them.

With no other choice, the army left the cities and their inhabitants to their fate. As on the previous days, scouts reported sightings of thousands of horsemen gathering along the distant horizon. Beyond them, larger camps were taking shape as further reinforcements arrived with siege engines.

Sir Harold and his staff now realized that the advance of the Empire’s horsemen had been the bait. Two days earlier, when the Shogunate repositioned its forces from the other cities to withstand the expected attack, the Margrave betrayed them.

Two thousand men had marched to fight the Empire and save their fellow countrymen. They had brought food, weapons, medicine, and everything else needed to sustain the war in Edessa. While they were still on the march, the Margrave fell upon them in cold blood.

Now, two thousand Midlandians, well trained, well equipped, and led by talented officers and veterans, were dead.

Their supplies gone, scattered, put to the torch, or plundered.

Among the slain were comrades and kin, friends who had fought beside them, and even their own brothers and sons. They had been destroyed by the very allies they had intended to help.

Thus, the Shogunate columns withdrew with grief weighing upon their shoulders and hatred burning in their hearts.

With evidence of the Margrave’s betrayal and the accounts of witnesses as clear as day, there was no longer any hope of defending Sigisha. The entire region might as well have turned against them. No army could withstand a prolonged siege deep within hostile territory without secure routes for reinforcement and resupply.

Not even the airships could supply thousands of soldiers and an entire population indefinitely. Once their strength and provisions were exhausted, they would be trapped behind the walls, surrounded by enemies, with no hope of breaking through.

To Sir Harold and his men, it felt as though they had fallen into a trap.

Even after pulling troops from all the neighboring cities, Sir Harold commanded only seven thousand men alongside two thousand cavalrymen. With the Margrave free to strike at either flank, he was forced to place one thousand men in the vanguard and another thousand on each flank. That left him with only four thousand men-at-arms.

Of those, he committed three thousand to the rear guard, where the greatest danger lay, and kept the remaining thousand in reserve. His cavalry, the famed Dragoons, would decide whether they lived or died when the Empire’s horsemen descended upon them.

Behind them, smoke darkened the sky above Sigisha. The garrisons had put the storehouses, armories, and abandoned castles to the torch. They would leave nothing behind for either the Empire or the Margrave to use.

Ahead, dust hung heavily over the road to Ostaria.

The midday sun burned overhead, yet almost no one dared to rest. The soldiers continued marching despite their weariness, while the refugees around them struggled to keep pace.

The Empire’s irregulars, dreaded for their brutality, were soon spotted shadowing the retreat. Before long, they began seizing those who moved too slowly or strayed too far from the main body.

Panic spread through the refugees. The entire column surged forward again, carts crowding together as people pushed themselves and their animals beyond exhaustion. Yet this frantic pace would do them little good. It would only wear them down faster.

Sir Harold had still not fully recovered from the injuries he had suffered at the Monastery. They had been serious enough to keep him from joining the previous year’s war. As he rode through the dust hanging low over the road, he noticed a low rise nearby. He dismounted at its foot and climbed to the top.

From the crest, with his guards gathered around him, he observed the retreat. From that height, the countless columns resembled flocks of migrating birds, all streaming toward Ostaria.

A carriage and several carts rolled past below. He saw Emma and her Healers riding among them. They had no wounded to tend yet, so they had filled every spare place with women and children.

Seeing their leader observing the retreat, Sir Sterling rode up from the rear with his own riders and several of the Orange Skalds. Armiger Karl and his men approached from the opposite direction.

Sir Sterling arrived first, reined in his horse, and made his report from the saddle. “The irregulars are getting bold. They’ve already taken hundreds of stragglers.”

Sir Harold inhaled deeply. There was nothing he could say.

Karl arrived moments later, his expression somber. “Sir, you’ll need someone to command the rear guard. Our men will reach Ostaria before dark, but these people won’t.”

They had no obligation to protect the Edessans. Their Margrave had betrayed the Shogunate, and his people were not even its subjects. Moreover, the situation was truly bleak.

Even if they reached Ostaria that night, they would still face a two-day march to Derenza, followed by the long road to Corna. Many would likely fall along the way.

Between Derenza and Corna lay Sirmona, one of the Margrave’s strongholds. The Shogunate had deliberately left it in his hands so that the Margrave could not claim they had cut his province in two. Now it threatened their entire line of retreat.

Even so, there were still officers willing to come to the people’s aid.

“Please, let me do it,” Sir Sterling volunteered. “I was of no use during the last war. Let this be my penance.”

Sir Harold refused the young knight’s offer. “I already bear enough guilt over the loss of two thousand men. I dare not risk you as well, not when I could never meet the ransom the Empire would demand for you. Even if we paid it, they would probably return you crippled. Don’t ask that of me.”

Undeterred, Karl, who was much older than Sterling, spoke more calmly. “Please allow me. Knowing when to run has always been my greatest talent. Besides...” He looked over his shoulder and motioned to one of the riders accompanying him. “He's with me.”

A lean man in a weather-stained riding coat urged his horse forward and bowed his head to Sir Harold. He was a mage retained by Dame Daniella. As head of the Bank, she had earned the favor of many powerful guilds, and through those connections had secured the rare privilege of employing one.

Sir Harold recognized the mage, but his expression remained grave. “There will be no War Duck riders to rescue you this time.”

“Then I shall hunt forever beside the Ancients,” Karl answered firmly.

Sir Harold drew a sharp breath before making his decision. “The rear guard is yours. Take a hundred cranequiners and keep them under your direct command. You’re going to need them.”

Karl nodded and rode away to assume command. Soon, everyone on the road to Ostaria would face the greatest challenge of their lives.

Across the plains, the shadows were already lengthening.
