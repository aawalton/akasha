Chapter 94

Riley crossed the yard with the late afternoon sun at her back and her stomach already tight. She had put this off for long enough. The packed earth struck back hard under her boots, the way it always did this time of day, after the heat had baked the moisture out of it. Long shadows from the bulk houses and the silo lay across her path, and the light coming over her shoulder carried that warmer amber edge that softened the hard lines of the walls without making any of it feel soft. A cart creaked past her loaded with stone, the driver giving a quick nod of acknowledgment, which she returned without thinking. From the forge the rhythm of metal striking metal came in steady measured beats, and a thin column of smoke climbed against the sky, darker near the base where the fresh coal fed it. Somewhere near the stables a horse blew out a long breath and stamped once. The walls held firm around all of it. The watchtower rose above her and to the left, sharp against the brightening edge of a cloud overhead, guards pacing the wall walks in their steady rotation. None of it had stopped for her. It never did.

She had killed Valrik. She was almost positive he wasn’t going to stay dead, knew it the way she knew the recall timer and the resource cost and every other piece of system arithmetic she ran on this place. He was a soldier she had pulled out of a building.

But it was Valrik and without him she did not know where this place would be. She did not know where she would be. Valrik had been there for her more times than she could remember so she owed him this much, at least.

She stopped a few paces from the hospital door and made herself breathe. The yard was busy behind her, the sounds of it familiar and ordinary: hammers somewhere near the forge, a wagon being unloaded, two voices low and amused near the wall. None of it reached her properly. She had been a leader long enough to know the trick of going in cold when something inside you was hot. Just dive in. She pushed the door open and entered.

The hospital smelled the way it always smelled, clean linen and general sterility. The beds along the far wall were full. A soldier near the door slept with his arm wrapped to the shoulder, the linen pale and recently changed. Another lay flat with his eyes open, watching the ceiling beams as if there were something written on them.

Valrik was at the far end. He was propped on two folded blankets, his shoulders against the wooden headboard and his hands flat on the covers. The light through the small window caught the right side of his face and left the rest in shadow. He was thinner than she remembered him being only days ago. He turned his head toward her as she crossed the room.

“Warden,” he said.

“Don’t get up.”

“I wasn’t going to.”

She pulled a chair to the side of the bed and sat down. Valrik shifted himself a little more upright against the headboard. For a moment neither of them spoke. She had walked over here with a dozen versions of what she meant to say, and standing in the yard she had known which one it was going to be, and now sitting beside him she found that none of them fit. Valrik broke the silence first.

“I know you had to kill me; there was no other way.”

He looked down at his hands. His right thumb worked into the palm of his left, a slow press she didn’t think he knew he was doing.

“It was hard,” he said.

She looked around the room, down at her own hands and back up at him.

“I can’t even imagine what it was like. What do you remember? After?” she said.

He didn’t answer right away; he didn’t even seem like he was going to.

“What happens,” she said. “How does it…”

A long pause. He was somewhere else for a moment, and then he came back.

“Darkness,” he said. “But more like you’re floating. No floor. No walls. Nothing.” He paused. “Then you feel a glow. More than you see it. A hum, inside you, in the black of it. And then a colour to it. Faint. Blue.”

Riley leaned in slightly. Her eyes widened.

“You feel…”

He looked away and then looked back. He couldn’t hold her eyes.

“You start to see things. Everything you remember, before you died.” He stopped. “But not only the things that happened. But other things too.”

“Other things?”

His mouth tightened. His lips pressed thin until they were almost gone. He looked up and met her eyes.

“I saw…I saw you die.”

He looked down again and pressed his thumb harder into his palm.

“There was nothing I could do. I was there, and I wasn’t. At a distance. I can’t remember how or why. I didn’t see what came after. There was a flash and then I was here, except before I was here it was dark again, and cold. Colder than I’ve ever been. It started in my feet and my hands and it came up my arms and my legs, and as it came it hurt. The pain was like you put your hand in cold water and leave it there too long. It took my whole body.”

He stopped speaking and his breathing was a little louder than it had been. She could see the way his shoulders had drawn up while he talked, the way the cover had bunched under the press of his hands. He was not looking at her now. He was looking at the small square of light on the floor by her feet.

Riley put her hands over his and stopped the rubbing.

“It was a dream,” she said. “A bad one. You’d been through something terrible.” She bent forward to catch his eyes, and when he looked up she let half a smile through. “I’m all right. I’m so sorry I put you through this. You deserve better than this.”

Valrik looked back at her. He gave a small nod, and then he drew his hands out from under hers.

“I know,” he said. “It was what had to be done.”

They sat with it. Outside the small high window the yard was working through its day, faint and ordinary. A cart wheel turning somewhere. A voice calling for someone she couldn’t quite catch the name of. The healer at the back of the room moved between two beds and the sound of water being poured into a basin came and went. Neither of them looked at the other. They didn’t need to.

She thought, sitting there, about the beginning of this place. About Valrik standing at the gate when there had been almost nothing behind it worth standing for, no proper wall, only the people, the ones the system had given her.

She thought about how many times she had given an order that risked someone’s life, and how many times that someone would have to go through what Valrik had. She forced herself to think it through cleanly. She had learned to give those orders by not asking what carrying them cost. That had been a useful trick once. She was no longer sure it was useful.

After a while Riley pulled herself straight and pushed the hair back from her face. Valrik did not look up until she started speaking.

“We need you, Valrik. I need you.”

She stood and turned to the room.

“Whatever he needs. Whatever any of them need.”

Her eyes moved across the other beds as she said it, the wrapped arm by the door, the man staring at the ceiling, the rest of them, like she was speaking to every one of them at once. She held the look longer than she meant to. The man watching the beams turned his head, just a little, and she saw him register her, and she saw him understand that he had been counted.

She turned back to Valrik.

“I’ll check in on you later. I have to go.”

Valrik nodded and lay back down.

She gave a nod to the room, to all of them, and walked out.

The door closed behind her. She stood there a moment, looking out at the yard without seeing it. The sun had moved while she was inside. The shadows along the storehouse wall had lengthened and gone soft. Someone had started the next meal’s cookfire and the first thin line of smoke was climbing into the still air, joining the steadier column from the forge in the distance. A pair of soldiers crossed the open ground in a measured line, arms loaded with timber bound for the stacked wood piles. The watchtower’s silhouette had gone darker against the sky behind it. She watched all of it without moving.

She didn’t know what they were. But they felt pain. They built this place. They gave up whatever it was they had, over and over, for the Hoshin and for this settlement, because she asked them to.

She thought of Valrik’s hands moving against each other under hers. The press of his thumb into his palm. The cold he had described, traveling up his arms and legs, taking his whole body. She thought of the ease with which she had said there was no other way, and she did not take the words back, because they had been true. But true was not the same as paid for. She understood, standing there with the cookfire smoke rising thin and ordinary in front of her, that she had been treating true as if it settled the account, and that it did not. It never did.
