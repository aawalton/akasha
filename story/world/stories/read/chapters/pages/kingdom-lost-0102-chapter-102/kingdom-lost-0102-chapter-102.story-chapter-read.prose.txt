Chapter 102

They left the settlement at first light.

The column formed in the yard while the sky was still the colour of slate. Ten soldiers in marching order, mounted, weapons at their hips and a day's rations in their saddlebags. Thorne had everything ready by the time Riley came down. She had told him the night before, we ride for Hoshin at dawn.

They mounted and rode out through the inner gate together at the head of the column, two soldiers riding behind them and the other eight in pairs, and the gate closed at their backs with the slow heavy sound of the gate closing.

Riley did not look back as they entered the woods.

The light came up properly when they were an hour in. The forest canopy held it off the path for longer than open country would have, and the column moved through long lines of dappled grey and pale grey before the proper colour of morning reached the trail floor. The breath of the horses came out in white plumes that lasted longer in the air than the breath of men.

Thorne rode at her left, half a length back. He held the position the way he held most things, without making a point of holding it.

After a time Riley spoke.

"Nikola said you wanted half an hour with me."

"I did. About the Level Two soldiers."

"Walk me through it."

He gathered himself the way he gathered himself before a briefing, by drawing a slow breath in through his nose and letting it go.

"I have been thinking about the mix," he said. "When the Level Twos come, they will not come as a separate unit. They will come into the existing companies. We will have a Level Two next to a Level One in the same line, and the question is what the Level Two does in that line."

"Tell me."

"Three possibilities. The first is that the Level Two is simply a better Level One. He stands where a Level One stands and does what a Level One does, only better. This is the simplest doctrine and the easiest to train into the units. The disadvantage is that we are paying for a Level Two and getting only the work of an improved Level One."

"The second?"

"The second is that the Level Two becomes a small command. He takes three Level Ones under him and the four of them operate as a fire team. The Level Two makes the decisions at the small scale. The Level Ones execute. This gets us more out of the Level Two but it requires the Level Ones to be drilled to take orders from him rather than from the company lieutenant, and that is a doctrine change for the whole army."

"And the third?"

"The third is that the Level Two is a specialist. He has a role the Level Ones cannot fill. We position him for that role. The Level Ones in the line do their work, and the Level Two does the work only he can do. This depends on what the Level Two actually is, which we do not yet know."

Riley rode for a moment in the steady rhythm of the horse and thought about it.

"What is your preference?"

"The third. With the second as a fallback if the Level Two turns out to be no more than a stronger soldier."

"Why?"

"Because we are going to be outnumbered by higher-level soldiers. The Level Two soldiers will help close that gap, but if the Level Two is a specialist, that closes the gap more than if he is just a slightly better Level One soldier."

She nodded slowly.

"Train both," she said. "Drill the men to take orders from a Level Two in a fire team. That is the fallback. But plan the line on the assumption that the Level Two is a specialist, and be ready to position him that way the moment we know what he is."

"Yes, Warden."

"And start the drilling now. Pick four Level Ones who would take well to it and run them as a fire team under your own command for the practice. Stand in for the Level Two yourself. We will see what the doctrine wants before the Level Twos arrive."

He was quiet for a moment.

"That is a good way to test it, Warden."

"Do it when we get back."

"Yes."

They emerged through the trees, and could see the cluster of low buildings that was Hoshin.

She looked at it for a long time.

Tama came out of the cluster on foot.

She came alone. She walked down the path toward the column at a steady pace, neither slow nor fast, and she had no escort and she carried no staff and she was dressed in the same simple working clothes as her people. Riley had her column halt at thirty paces and dismounted and walked forward to meet her, and Thorne dismounted behind her but stayed at the head of the column, and the two women came together in the open ground between the column and the cluster, alone.

Tama stopped a pace short of her.

"Warden."

"Tama."

They looked at each other for a moment. Tama's face was thinner than it had been the last time Riley had seen her. The lines at the corners of her eyes were deeper. She had not slept properly in some time. Riley saw it the way she had seen the change in the soldiers when Thorne had spoken to them at the ford, and she let the seeing sit for a count of two before she spoke.

"You did not send word."

"No."

"It's been days."

"I know."

Tama looked past her shoulder at the column for a moment, at the ten soldiers in their order and the horses standing easy with their heads down after the ride, and her eyes came back to Riley's face.

"Not here, Warden. Inside."

"Inside where?"

"My hut. Bring whoever you wish."

Riley turned her head and met Thorne's eyes across the open ground and lifted her chin once toward the cluster, and Thorne understood without a word and began to move the column forward at the slow walk, the soldiers leading their horses now rather than riding them. The Hoshin people at the edge of the central ground did not move as the column came in. They watched it the way they had watched it from the ridge.

Tama turned and walked back toward the cluster, and Riley walked beside her, and behind them the column came on in the steady quiet way Thorne had taught it to move.

Tama's hut was at the northern edge of the central ground.

It was larger than the others around it but not by much. The walls were of stacked stone to waist height and timber above, the roof of turf laid over a frame of split poles, the door a heavy plank slab on leather hinges. Inside there was a single room with a hearth at the centre and a low table along one wall and sleeping pallets rolled against the other. The air smelled of woodsmoke and dried herbs and the long quiet of a place where one person had lived alone for some time.

Tama closed the door behind them. She did not light the lamp at once. She stood with her back to the door for a moment and her hand on the wood as if she were holding the door shut against something, and then she turned and crossed to the hearth and stirred the coals up and added a small piece of wood to them, and the light came up in the hut from the orange of the fire rather than from a lamp, and she sat down on the low stool beside the hearth and looked up at Riley.

"Sit, Warden."

Riley sat on the other stool.

Tama looked into the fire.

"Korr-va is coming," she said.

The name sat in the hut.

Riley did not speak.

"Soon," Tama said. "He doesn't announce his arrival but it has been too many days since his last visit. He will be coming."

Riley's eyes began to harden.

"He has been here twice since the move began. The first time he took the tribute and left. The second time the tribute was short. We could not make the new number. We tried. We worked through the nights and we emptied the storage hut and we still came up short."

She did not look up from the fire.

"He took a man for it. He picked one out of the square the way he did the first time he came, the way he did when the marks on his chest were still new to us, and he did it where the rest of us could watch, and then he loaded what we had into the wagon and he rode out and he left us the body in the dirt."

Riley's lips disappeared as her mouth tightened.

Tama looked at the fire. She did not look up.

"Your soldiers have saved us by helping us to meet quota, but nothing seems to be enough."

"Tama."

"I did not send word because I knew what you would do if I sent word, Warden. You would come. You would come with soldiers. You would set yourself against him. And the moment you set yourself against him you would be set against the Empress, and I have seen what the Empress does to those she is set against, and I would not be the reason she came for you."

"Tama."

"I made a choice, Warden. I made it for you. I made it knowing what it cost. I made it eleven days ago and I have lived with it since, and I would have made it again four days ago when your last runner asked me whether the move would proceed, and I told the runner the move would proceed, and I did not say the rest."

She sat in the hut with the orange of the fire on the wall behind Tama's shoulder and she thought about Korr-va, who had been only a name to her until five minutes ago and who was now a person who had brought horror to these people even when they paid the tribute he was not entitled to.

She thought about the quotas. About the village that had been thinning for months before the move began. About how the remaining villagers suffered while the others were free under her protection.

She thought about how keeping up appearances was putting these people at risk. And she thought about how pulling all the Hoshin out now could change the timeline of a probable battle with the Clawborn.

Riley let it sit. The fire moved in the hearth. A coal cracked and settled.

"Tama. How many Hoshin are still here?"

"Forty-one. Counting me."

We may have to speed this plan up.

"Warden… Riley, that could put your community in danger. It could put all Hoshin in danger."

Riley looked into Tama's eyes and said nothing for a long moment.

"This cycle of terror cannot continue. My plan was to reduce risk with the Clawborn and the tribute they demanded from you. Even when you meet the demand, there is still too much risk. Things have changed and we will change with them."

Tama shook her head and looked into the fire.

"Tama, I need to speak with my troops. I will return, and I want to know more about this Korr-va."

Tama nodded as Riley turned and walked out of her hut.

Riley closed the door of the hut behind her and crossed the central ground toward Thorne. The cool of the late afternoon was full on her face now, and she walked with slow steadiness. She walked with purpose.
