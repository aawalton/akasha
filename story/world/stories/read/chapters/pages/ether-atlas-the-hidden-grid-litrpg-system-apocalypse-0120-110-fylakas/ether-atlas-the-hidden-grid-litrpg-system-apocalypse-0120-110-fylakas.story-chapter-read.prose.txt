Chapter 110

We spent a long time building walls to stand against the sea, and lost most of them. The ones that lasted were the ones that gave the water somewhere to go, and let it spend itself. — Codex of the Long Watch, Watcher's Commentary

Stavros lifted the shield off the bench with both arms, his chest straining against his leather apron, and set the massive weight into Arthur’s hands.

It was heavier than the Ironhold had been, and significantly broader: a wide, imposing round of dark iron. The rim was turned inward along its entire circuit, rolled tightly back on itself so the outer edge presented a smooth lip rather than a flat face.

Arthur turned the iron round in the orange wash of the forge, reading the curve with an engineer’s eye. A thrust coming in flat would hit that rolled lip and slide harmlessly off rather than catching and biting into the metal. The centre was thick where the impact load would be highest, tapering gradually towards the rim where unnecessary weight would only drag his reaction time down.

“Argive grip,” Stavros said, tapping the two heavy fittings riveted inside. A broad, reinforced leather strap sat exactly at the centre for the forearm to run through, and a solid hand-grip stood near the rim. “One to carry, one to steer. The strap takes the weight so your arm does not die in the first hour. The hand moves it.”

He mimed the motion, bracing his own thick forearm tight across his chest and throwing an imaginary rim across an invisible line. “Othismos. You put your shoulder in it and you push, and the man in front of you goes back whether he wishes to or not.”

Arthur ran his forearm through the heavy leather sleeve and locked his hand around the rim grip. The massive shield settled against his frame, the weight transferring smoothly along the bone of his forearm instead of hanging off the weak leverage of his wrist. He drove the iron face forward into the empty air, testing the brace and checking his stance, feeling exactly where his boots would need to anchor to ground the weight.

“It sits right,” he said.

Stavros gave him a pleased look. “A proper aspis. I make you a Greek legend and you carry it into whatever hole you climb into next.”

Arthur lowered the massive rim of the shield, letting the heavy iron edge rest against the toe of his boot. He looked across the smoky shop to where Steve stood. The silent question in the look was plain enough.

“Here?” Steve asked.

“Here,” Arthur said. “Stavros already has one bound to the forge. He isn’t going to faint looking at ours.”

Stavros didn’t move. He simply folded his thick arms over his chest and leaned his back against a wall to watch. It was answer enough.

Steve reached into his leather pouch, pulled out the stone, and passed it over. In the amber glare of the hearth, the blue core shimmered like a sapphire. Behind him, Steve heard Stavros’s gasp when he saw the stone.

Arthur laid the shield flat across the face of the anvil. He glanced at Stavros, who gave him a nod of permission. Placing the guardian core directly onto the centre of the black iron, Arthur covered the stone with his palm, pressing his hand flat against it.

He closed his eyes, cutting off the forge to find his intent for the shield. The noises fell away as he poured his intent down into the iron. The shield is the wall. The shield would take whatever came at it and withstand. It would turn blows away so nothing could bite or catch on the edge. Defend and deflect.

The core sank.

It was absorbed slowly over the course of two minutes, and where the stone dissolved, the metal woke up. Arthur watched the grain of the iron rewrite itself, the crystalline structure realigning along entirely new structural vectors, the iron remembering that it had once been something else. The dark iron took on a deeper, iridescent blue at the central boss, throwing out slow, branching paths towards the perimeter. When the transformation reached the turned lip, the rolled edge thickened, drawing itself tighter and denser.

ARTIFACT CREATION DETECTED

BINDING LAW ENGAGED

Vessel: Argive Aspis (Transmuted Spring-Stock)

Core: Node Guardian, Sounion (Tempered-Grade)

Intent: Denial

BREAKWATER — TEMPERED

Structural Integrity: Enhanced Edge Geometry: Turning

Signature: Deflection Emphasis

“Breakwater,” Arthur read out for the others. “Structural Integrity enhanced. Edge Geometry, turning. Signature, Deflection Emphasis.”

“Tempered,” Steve said, his eyes tracing the new crystalline grain in the metal. “That is a full tier above the wolf gear.”

“Test it before you fall in love with it,” Donna said. She had crossed the floor with her usual quiet efficiency, appearing at Arthur’s shoulder with her prybar swinging loose in her hand. “Line it up.”

Arthur brought the shield up, angling the rolled rim to meet her. Donna lunged, driving the iron bar straight at the lip in a hard, flat thrust. The point skated. It struck the turned rim and rode cleanly off the edge without ever finding a surface to catch, and the kinetic shock that should have jammed Arthur’s wrist spread evenly across his entire braced frame instead.

Donna adjusted her stance and came again, aiming higher, driving the point straight into the upper face of the round. It glanced harmlessly off the blue curve. She changed her grip, hooking the crook of the bar over the rim to haul it sideways, mimicking a claw trying to strip a guard away. The thick, rolled edge gave the tool nothing to lever against. It slid free, and the shield did not shift an inch against Arthur’s arm.

“I cannot catch it and I cannot dislodge it,” Donna said, stepping back and giving a brief nod. “There is no way in behind it.”

“That was the intent,” Arthur said, testing the tension in his forearm strap. “Give a blow nothing to catch and nothing to lever, and the line stays shut.”

Until you meet a blow big enough that it does not care about leverage, Archie stated.

There was a short silence in the forge, the dying fire ticking quietly in the nearby quench tub.

“Cheerful,” Sarah said.

Accurate, Archie replied.

Stavros had watched the test without a word, his thick arms folded tight over his chest and his face completely still. When the ringing subsided, he pushed off the masonry, crossed the soot-stained floor, and laid two calloused fingers against the blue-tinted boss.

“Theé mou,” he muttered, the words almost lost to the crackle of the hearth.

He stood there for a second, feeling the resonance of the metal, then shook his head, clearing the awe away as the practical smith took back over.

“Kalá. The shield is yours and it is a good one, and I have four more days of the god’s iron to beat into your knives and your plates, so go, all of you, out of my forge. You are standing in my light.”

Eleni packed them a picnic for a day she had not been told the truth about, though she clearly suspected it anyway, intending to feed it into submission.

“Bread, the good bread, from Stamatis the father,” she said, folding a cloth over the top of a basket. “Olives. The cheese from the tin, not the fresh, the fresh will die in this heat before you reach wherever it is you are not telling me you are going. Tomatoes. A knife. Water, and do not drink it all in the first hour like animals.”

“It is a walk to the coast, Mama,” Sarah said. “We will look at the sea, we will eat too much, we will come back. It is not an expedition.”

“Four people who look at the sea do not carry that,” Eleni said, tipping her chin at the sacking bundle on Arthur’s back without looking at it. “But I am an old woman and I know nothing, so. You take the food.”

Petros leaned in the kitchen doorway and enjoyed all of it enormously.

“A picnic,” he said. “With the big man carrying his church bells on his back.” He tipped his head at Arthur’s bundle. “Re, Sarah. Does the donkey fly?”

Arthur looked at him without comprehension, which Petros clearly found delightful.

“He means he doesn’t believe a word of it,” Sarah said. “Pull the other one.”

“The donkey does not fly,” Petros agreed, satisfied, and pinched an olive out of the basket before his mother could reach the doorway.

“Petro,” Eleni said, arriving in the doorway. He was already gone.

They said their goodbyes, promising to return well before dark, and set off down towards the shimmering line of the water, carrying a basket of Eleni’s food and every intention of eating it nowhere near the places she had imagined.

The picnic was a thin ruse, a necessary lie because Sarah refused to panic her mother with another dangerous trek so soon after Cape Sounion. But as their boots hit the baking gravel of the lower track, Sarah looked back at the villa, entirely unconvinced the old woman had bought the story for a single second.

The plan they had made at the cape did not survive contact with the map.

“We said Megara or Pachi,” Donna said once the coastal houses began to thin out. “Quiet water on the far side. Five hundred metres of it.”

“My fault, that. It was a good plan for avoiding eyes,” Sarah said. “But it is the wrong plan for where we actually need to land.”

She stopped by a low, crumbling stone wall and looked out over the flat, grey stretch of the strait.

“The Skiras site is on the east side,” she said, squinting across the water. “Facing Athens. That is the whole reason the ancient battle happened where it did. Everything that mattered on that island faced the mainland.” She turned back to look at them. “If we cross at Pachi, we come ashore on the wrong coast entirely. We would have to walk the entire length of the island just to reach the site.”

“She is right. The Skiras cult, the town, Herodotus’s temple, all of it sits on the strait side,” Steve said, nodding as he held his pencil against the ledger. “Nobody built anything of importance facing the empty sea. Crossing at Pachi puts us at the back of the island. Then we are just hiking across a ridge line to get to the front.”

“So Perama it is, after all,” Arthur said.

“The shorter crossing is Pachi. The Perama crossing to Salamis is about a kilometre,” Donna said, her voice tight. “A kilometre of completely open channel with a working ferry quay on both ends. The Council has every reason to keep eyes on the one stretch of water connecting a fortress island to their front door. Quiet was the whole point of Pachi. Perama is the opposite of quiet.”

“Perama is an hour of walking and a kilometre of flat water,” Arthur said, his eyes measuring the distance across the slate-grey channel. “Pachi means a full day on foot just to reach the crossing, and then a blind hike across an island we know nothing about. We do not really have a choice here.”

“Doesn’t mean I have to like it,” Donna grumbled. “Fine. But the rest of the parameters stay the same. We find a boat that nobody is using, slip across, and hope the surveillance misses us.”

“We don’t have to cross near the main Perama boatyard,” Sarah offered. “The coast out here is littered with small boats people haven’t touched since Day Zero. They will be sitting under tarps with birds’ nests in the outboards. We scavenge one of those, use it, and drop it back before dark.”

“Then it is agreed,” Donna said, stepping back onto the gravel. The meeting was closed.

The Perama shore was a graveyard for the industry that had died on it. Concrete quays jutted into flat, grey water that lapped against rotting hulls destined never to float again. The air smelled thick with tar, old diesel, and the stagnant reek of a major shipping channel that had simply stopped dead.

Across a kilometre of strait, the near coast of Salamis sat clear in the afternoon light, flat calm water between the two land masses.

Donna found a vessel within ten minutes. It sat hauled up on a cracked concrete slipway beneath a tarpaulin that had gone stiff and pale from the salt air. It was a small wooden varka with a thick layer of grey dust coating the thwarts. When she peeled the heavy covering back, a dried bird’s nest sat undisturbed in the well behind the small inboard engine.

“Prophetic much,” Steve said, smiling at Sarah.

The painted name on the bow had flaked away entirely, leaving only a few isolated Greek letters that no longer formed a readable word.

“This one has been sitting here for at least a year,” she said, her hand trailing along the rough wood of the gunwale. “Nobody has touched it. The oars are still shipped, but the rowlocks are completely seized.” She glanced down at the rusted block of the engine, dismissing it instantly. “We do not wake that. We row.”

“I will row,” Arthur said.

“I thought that was implied,” Donna said. “Steve will bail. Sarah and I will watch the water.”

They rolled the varka down the slipway using rollers cut from a length of old pipe. Four people, a wooden hull and a basket of food, moving together until the boat took the water without complaint. Only a thin trickle of water crept through a seam that had dried out. Steve found the plastic bailer attached to a string in the bow and immediately began to earn his passage. Arthur set the heavy oars, put his back into the first long stroke, and the concrete lines of the Perama shore began to recede behind them.

“I came this way on a school trip once,” Sarah said, her voice drifting over the rhythmic thud of the oars. “On a proper ferry. Half the class was sick before we even cleared the quay. I was fine. I have always been steady on the water. It is about the only Vassilou trait I actually inherited.”

Arthur rowed across the dead-calm strait with long, even strokes, the dark iron of the shield a comfortable, solid weight resting against his knee. He watched the near coast of the island grow larger with every pull. Steve kept bailing. Donna kept her gaze locked on the open water and the receding quays behind them. The crossing that had seemed so daunting from the high vantage of the cape took about ten minutes of flat rowing, with no signs of surveillance along either shore.

They met Salamis at a low headland, where an old stone quay ran out into the shallows. Arthur shipped the oars, letting the varka glide through the last few metres of green water until the bow met the ancient masonry with a soft, hollow knock.

Donna was over the gunwale and onto the stones before the boat had even finished stopping fully. She dropped low, her axe loose in her hand as she automatically scanned the perimeter.
