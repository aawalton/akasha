The borderlands had turned cold with startling speed. Summer had ended only a month ago, yet the morning air already bit at the skin, and the late afternoon wind was sufficiently cold to justify fur-lined clothing. Fortunately, the bookstore’s walls held against most of it, allowing Anne to work in comfort while the wind worried at the boards outside.

Still, the change was a warning. Before long, she would need a proper heating solution. Otherwise winter would push through those walls one draft at a time and make the whole store miserable.

Anne made a note of it on a spare scroll, turned another page of the book in front of her, and then lifted her eyes for a quick survey of the room.

As expected, Asha had arrived early again. Since the day she first came to the bookstore, the elf had returned every morning with almost frightening consistency. That alone would have made Anne fond of her, but Asha also brought a livelier energy to the place, one that made the store feel less solitary.

More surprising was Rick. The hunter had started adding mornings to his schedule as well. Anne had her suspicions about the reason, and they were not entirely academic. Still, she had no objection to customers developing stronger study habits, whatever their initial inspiration.

To be fair, Rick could afford it. The boar hunt had earned him more than coin. It had bought him a degree of freedom from the work his father usually pressed on him, and Rick, being Rick, had responded to that freedom by throwing money back into the bookstore with almost painful responsibility. It was difficult to resent such behavior in a customer, even when it came wrapped in shy determination and a still-developing habit of loitering near a certain elf.

Not everyone had that luxury. The golden trio had been eager enough to continue studying, but their money had only covered a week, and that week had passed with the indecent speed of a grey wolf. Even Cyrus had spent his share sensibly, which Anne still found faintly miraculous. Most of it had gone toward keeping Iris and Hakon at their books a little longer. As for the warrior himself, he remained strangely proud of his slow conquest of literacy.

Anne respected that more than she let show. He could have complained and sulked. Instead, he treated each small gain as a real one, which was more than many supposedly educated men did with their own advantages. Even now, when he could no longer pay for extended study time, he still came almost daily to accompany the others, review notes, and make progress with the Syllable. Iris and Hakon did the same in their own way. Broke or not, they had begun orbiting the bookstore as if they already belonged to it.

They had also, Anne suspected, discovered that tea made studying less unpleasant.

That suited her perfectly well. An occasional rich customer could lift a month’s finances in a single visit, but her grandfather had always insisted that only regulars made a bookstore healthy. The store looked more proper with people in it—tables occupied, notes being taken, books opened with purpose instead of merely catalogued and admired.

Of course, success brought its own problems. The more the bookstore behaved like a real bookstore, the less quiet time Anne had for the heavier side of her own craft. Reading and light research still fit naturally around customers. She could finish half a book in a day even with interruptions, provided the material was not too delicate. But restoration work, grimoire planning, serious conceptual study, and certain spellcraft tasks demanded full attention. Those she had been pushing into the evenings.

The result was predictable. She was nearly a week behind on some of the more technical work.

Anne was beginning to suspect that she had grown too accustomed to the early days, when the store had contained more ambition than customers and she could spend long, uninterrupted hours on the parts of bookmancy she liked best. Now she would have to build a new balance, one that allowed her to be both proprietor and practitioner without failing at either.

Fortunately, the bookstore’s recent surprises had not all been inconvenient. Spring, for example, had become invaluable.

Moruh’s archiving abilities were still beyond replacement, of course. No one else in the building could sort, remember, retrieve, and cross-reference materials with his speed and offended dignity. But Spring had come astonishingly close to becoming equally helpful in a different direction. The supposed treefolk had a gift for domestic usefulness that kept deepening in unexpected ways.

Only a few days earlier, Anne had begun worrying that her stock of soul tea was down to a handful of cups.

Spring had solved the problem herself. The former homunculus had appeared with fresh leaves plucked from her own stalk-like arms, offering them with serene confidence. In hindsight, the result was not impossible. Spring’s original spirit had been bound to a young soul-tree vessel, and after the blessing, her transformed body clearly carried more of that nature than Anne had first realized.

Still, expected or not, it had been a revelation. From then on, Spring had only needed to pluck a few leaves from herself whenever the stock ran low, and the resulting tea was not merely usable. It was better. Better tasting, more soothing, and, if Anne’s judgment could be trusted, slightly stronger in effect.

That last detail pleased her and worried her in equal measure. She had no desire to discover that she had accidentally turned half her customer base into tea addicts.

Counseling, meanwhile, was becoming easier. Not easy, but easier. Anne had studied for years with the assumption that one day she would need to guide people through books rather than merely preserve them. Still, there was a difference between theoretical readiness and actual practice. Some lessons could only be learned by sitting across from a customer, listening properly, and watching how knowledge changed in a real mind.

She could not say much about Ceres or Dorgh at the moment. Ceres’s absence was expected. A traveling mercenary was not the sort of customer who drifted in every few days for tea and follow-up questions. Dorgh, on the other hand, had no such excuse. Anne suspected he had locked himself away with brewing experiments and a dangerously excited relationship to mystic alcohol. So she would have to wait patiently for his return and hope he came back with something drinkable.

Rick was far easier to assess. Anne could not have been prouder of him if he had been one of her own apprentices—which, she reminded herself, he was not. He had overcome the worst of his illiteracy obstacle, studied the beastiarium with genuine purpose, mapped a dangerous stretch of forest mostly by himself, then used that knowledge first for a careful solo success and later for a profitable group expedition. It would not be an exaggeration to say that he was the single greatest reason the bookstore now had regular occupation.

Knowledge had changed his posture as much as his prospects.

The golden trio were more complicated. Cyrus was, in some ways, the easiest to understand and the hardest to guide. His reading remained painfully slow. Anne had little hope of making him multilingual in this lifetime, and if he finished his current literacy work within a few months, she would count it a triumph. More efficient methods of knowledge transfer existed. She had already thought of them more than once. But offering them now would almost certainly rob him of the stubborn need to truly learn reading and writing.

So she refused herself that shortcut. He would advance slowly, and properly.

Iris was difficult for entirely different reasons. The girl was clever, driven, and fiercely attached to her own judgment. Anne could respect that. In fact, in another context she might have liked it very much. But in counseling, respect did not make disagreement vanish. Iris was still in the foundational step of the mage’s path—visualization, shaping, and the transition from cantrip habits to fuller spell logic.

Unfortunately, she remained determined to advance with pyromancy alone. Anne understood the temptation. A single-element mage could sometimes progress faster, and Iris had already invested precious years in surviving scarcity rather than building an ideal foundation. But the strategy had limits. Specializing early worked best when supported by strong natural affinity, and Iris’s was good rather than exceptional. Anne had suggested stepping back long enough to learn aeromancy as a complementary element before pressing for bronze. Iris had dismissed the idea immediately.

The girl’s reasoning was not foolish. It was simply narrower than Anne preferred. Iris wanted results quickly because quick results might change her circumstances. Anne wanted long-term strength because she thought in years. Neither perspective was entirely wrong, which made the disagreement harder to resolve.

Then there was Hakon. He had been a pleasant surprise from the beginning, and he continued to improve upon acquaintance. The foxkin thought carefully, answered honestly, and did not seem to treat ignorance as a personal insult. That made him easier to guide than either of his human companions. He had already advanced two chapters into his current text with understanding far beyond the superficial, and he had recently mentioned wanting to strengthen his herbology once funds allowed it, even though it was not strictly required for immediate advancement.

Anne approved of it strongly. It would not be long before he began using healing essence with real precision rather than blunt pressure.

Last came Asha. Anne’s satisfaction there was almost indecent. The elf studied with remarkable intensity. When she was not reading, she was clarifying definitions, testing ideas, or silently sorting her thoughts in ways Anne could almost see from across the room. On her first day with Magic of the Soul, Asha had already managed to sense her soul and identify its form. After only a week, she was experimenting with free casting inside her soul space.

Carefully, thankfully. But still, the speed of it was astonishing.

Iris deserved some credit for that customer. It had been her sharp eye, meddling nature, and inability to leave potentially useful things alone that brought Asha into the bookstore in the first place. Anne had no intention of saying so aloud. The girl would become unbearable.

Another gust pressed against the walls. Inside, pages turned, cups shifted softly against wood, and one customer made a quiet note while another pretended not to look in the same direction too often.

The days were passing quickly. But they were not slipping away. They were filling.

Anne let her eyes rest once more on the occupied room, on the books in use rather than in storage, on the customers who had become regulars, on the life that now moved through the building she had once bought as a gamble and a last chance.

She already felt comfortable calling this place home.
