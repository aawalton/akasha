It was full dark by the time Hua was able to get up under her own power, ruptured arm and all. Meditation had taken a full sichen and her near-deviation had eaten up an hour on top. Heat flushed her cheeks at the thought of being seen as weak. Maybe Weiji recognised that because he didn’t say anything as he picked up the tarp and the bucket with bile and sludge.

“I’ll find a place to deal with it,” he promised, and she found it easy to trust that he would. “It’s my job now to pick up your messes.”

She shoved him amicably with her good arm before making her way back alone. Despite failing to do anything about her meridian, and nearly experiencing a violent deviation, she felt lighter in a way. To be weak for a moment and speak truth to her fears. It made it easier because now she had a goal. Another thing to overcome.

“I’ll figure this out,” she promised Qing who skipped beside her. “I’ll be stronger for it. One step after another. Then I’ll get revenge for you.”

“You’re the reason I died. Why would I want you to do anything for me?”

Hua shook her head. Just a heart demon. Talking to it, giving it strength, those were the actions of a fool. She would not be a fool. Could not afford it. She did it anyway.

Camp came into view. Thankfully, it sounded like most people were at the camp kitchens. There would be rumours in the morn about her appearance, but no one would direct those words at her. The torches by her tent entrance cast the dozing servant in flickering shadow, and he startled off his stool at her deliberately loud approach. Instead of the extravagant carpet she expected, there were sensible mats waiting for her at the entrance to her tent.

“I won’t need attendants tonight,” she growled before the servant followed her in. “Have food brought for an hour from now.”

It would be after midnight but that wasn’t her problem. They could nap in the carts in the morning.

There was a cauldron of water at a low simmer over a coal stove. It was near the paper screen to the left side of the tent’s entrance that hid away the bathing area. There were all the cleaning supplies she could need and a change of clothes hanging on bamboo racks.

She scrubbed herself clean, taking special care of her bruised arm. It would take some time to heal.

Hua put on her lowest layer, an incredibly soft white silk, and towelled at her hair. She made her way to a low counter with a mirror and boxes with everything she might need to keep her appearance. Hua did not even know where the comb had been put.

“My Lord, this Liao Weiji is requesting entrance,” she heard from the tent’s entrance.

“You may enter.”

Weiji slowly peeked his head through her tent first, his silver hair held in a messy knot. When he saw her mostly decent, he entered, looking around cautiously. As if a savage might appear for no reason.

“I am told you dismissed your servants tonight. Might I offer my assistance?”

“You may,” she said over her shoulder.

Somehow knowing her tent better than her, Weiji found a horn comb in a beautiful box. The comb was painted and shaped to be a turtle, with large pieces of shell making up most of the handle and base. He combed out the few tangles in her hair. Weiji’s fingers were quick and nimble as he braided her hair in a sleeping knot.

She watched him in the mirror, amused at how quickly his eyes flickered away when she caught his gaze. They had grown up together. He still delighted her with how easy he could spook.

“For someone with no sister, you are rather good at this.”

His fingers stilled for a moment before resuming. “My mother had me do this for her on her bad days. There were a lot of bad days before the end. I learnt quick.”

“She was a good woman the few times I met her. Kind. Warm.”

“The best of us.”

“I would have liked to know her more,” she offered.

“We don’t have to talk about it. I would prefer we didn’t. It’s been five years. I’ve learnt to deal with it.”

“You’re also good at organising trips like this,” she said, changing the subject as he asked.

Weiji nodded, happy with the shift. “If an elder wants to go on vacation, they find a reason to saddle me with the work. Still easier than your brother. I had to organise every hunting trip he wanted to go on.”

“Here I thought you and my brother were just playing in the bushes.”

“Please never say those words in that order again.”

She smiled at him in the mirror, showing her teeth. But she left it at that and allowed him to check her arm again. He gave care instructions, which felt a bit beyond his remit. She nearly asked where he learnt these skills, but he read her thoughts and gave her a look of incredible discomfort. Hua very quickly decided she had experienced enough emotions for the day.

It was not long after that the servants arrived with food. They avoided gazing at either of them, gazes low. They were both household servants. They had never shown such fear in her presence.

“The rumour mill tomorrow is going to be so irritating,” Weiji grumbled, biting into his cut of meat. He chewed on it loudly, rudely. “I don’t know if trying to get ahead of things will make it worse.”

“Getting ahead of what, exactly?” she asked, shrugging on another layer of robes. A chill had swept in with the servants.

“If you can’t figure that out then I won’t tell you because you’ll just make things worse. It’s my job to handle things now, so let me handle this.”

“As you wish. Tell me, you’ve been taught some of the Clan techniques, yes?”

“Beyond the basics, not particularly. I know the standard reinforcements. But the most advanced things I was taught were the basics of the Radiant Lightning Body. My grandfather who learnt it from your father taught me.”

She sometimes forgot that Weiji was technically her grandnephew if one squinted at the family tree that occasionally looked more like a shrub. It meant very little in practice for Cultivator Clans—or the Liao Clan, Hua had no knowledge of what others did. You grew up with your agemates, played with an uncle that was younger than you and got spanked the same as your grandaunt. Anything else would be a bewildering mess.

“How good are you with the technique?”

“Acceptable for my Cultivation level. It’s a Foundation technique so I can only use some aspects of it. The physical enhancements, mostly. Maybe a flash of a lightning shield if I’m willing to burn through all my Qi for a month.”

“One day you should teach it to me. I’ll probably learn it faster than you.”

“That’s not in question—what do you mean you don’t know your own father’s signature technique?”

“Why would I? I’m not a Physical Cultivator like you.”

Weiji dropped his chopsticks. They clattered off the edge of the low table and rolled along the carpeted floor.

“Excuse me, you’re not a what? You. Hua who punches most things and slices the rest for variation. You’re not a Physical Cultivator? You! In what world do you live in where you of all people aren’t a Physical Cultivator. All our Spiritual Cultivators are priests. You’re not a daoshi!”

“No, I am not, and yes, they are. The priesthood is where they wanted to send me before Grandmother intervened. Haven’t you noticed how slow my physical reinforcement is?”

He stared at her. Slowly, he said, “I thought you were just going easy on me. All those years you were keeping up with me and you couldn’t even do a physical reinforcement properly. How much Qi are you shoving into your body just to keep up?”

“A lot.”

“I suppose that’s why you made the Thunder Palm. Punching through stone is good enough to punch through a Physical Cultivator.”

“Why does everyone keep saying it like it’s special? Thunder Palm this, Thunder Palm that. I just used it on a whim once and found it worked.”

“Fuck me, fuck your talent, fuck everything.”

“Don’t be hysterical.” He made a sound like a kettle boiling as he bit down a yell. “My main sparring partner is in the Foundation Establishment,” she offered before he could have a nervous fit.

Weiji’s left eye spasmed. It took him a moment to get that under control. “Let’s never talk about that again.”

He picked up his chopsticks from the floor and wiped them against them clean. He angrily chewed on his rice. Hua let him, taking a few bites of her own. The food would be decent for a while yet. Assuming they could make good time to Coaltown, and if it was still standing, they could replenish fresh stores.

“Alright, would you like to talk about Lee Shui?”

Weiji choked on his rice. “You did that on purpose,” he coughed, smacking his chest, loud thuds from his large hands. “Didn’t think you knew the name of my uncles.”

“Barely two years old and living in a brothel. For anyone to snatch. What exactly is your grandfather thinking?”

“Very little, I imagine. He’s too embarrassed to admit to having bastards and he has enough moral character not to smother a baby.”

“He could have joined a branch line, been adopted there. We could have sent him to a vassal Clan if need be. Just being part of our lineage gives him enough potential to increase the reputation of any vassal clan.”

“And let an Elder’s shame exist in the same compound? For all to know and mock him for? No, Grandfather would never do that. His face wouldn’t survive it.”

“When we get back, I might just force an adoption anyway.”

“Making enemies shouldn’t be your only goal in life.”

“But they make it so easy.”

“Do you think the other clans spend as much time with in-fighting as we do?” Weiji asked.

“What else are they going to do without a war to distract them? Can you imagine how convoluted the plots are in the Yu Clan with them being diviners?”

“Never knowing if you’re the mantis stalking the cicada or the oriole behind the mantis.”

“Or the boy shooting the oriole about to fall into the mud,” she continued, stealing a piece of his fried pork.

“Do you think the mud gets destroyed by the temple they build over it if we follow things long enough?”

“And then the temple gets washed away by a flood.”

“That’s just recent history, so I suppose the saying is accurate. You never know when a monster is going to appear to ruin your day the same way you don’t know Hua is behind you.”

She laughed and they talked a while longer. When it was deep night, she yawned. “I’ll see you tomorrow. Don’t trip in the dark.”

“Your tent has enough lights for me.”

“What’s wrong with your tent?”

“Besides being close to your savage? Do you think I’m leaving you after a Qi deviation?” He shook his head. “I’ll keep watch over you. Make sure the heavens don’t strike you down. Wouldn’t want my new lord dying on my watch.”

Hua found she slept easily that night.

***

Hua found Weiji dozing lightly on the thickest fur carpet when she woke, the tent walls lightened by the sun. He’d replaced the wood in the burner. It was pleasantly warm. She draped a blanket over him when he blinked his eyes open. Commanded him to sleep as she changed into a clean set of robes.

A frigid wind assaulted her when she opened her tent flap, waking her cousin, and dispelling any attempt to get him to sleep. A cloud of thick fog seeped into her tent. He joined her moments later, staring at the sight that greeted them.

There was frost on the ground.

It was spread across the camp. Poles had turned white as they froze. Some of the tents were stiff where a layer of ice had formed across their fabric surface. Soldiers and servants were all in thick layers, their breath white as they got the camp ready. A cold snap so quick, so deep, did not feel natural. Walking through the camp, she found the churned mud had frozen overnight.

“It’s too early in the year for a chill this deep,” Weiji said with a frown. He had a blanket around his shoulders over the sturdy cloak he’d slept in. “It’s autumn, not deep winter.”

“An auspicious start to your journey, my Lord,” said Liu Xin, approaching from her left, keeping Hua between him and Weiji.

“Savage,” Weiji greeted. “Your very presence here has cursed us.”

“This humble servant was unaware I was so influential. You have my thanks for the compliment.”

“Find me Captain Yao,” she ordered before they could bicker the day away.

In short order, the captain was brought before her. He was accompanied by his imperial counterpart and the scribe whose name Hua still hadn’t remembered.

“This won’t be a discussion,” Hua said before someone had the audacity to force her into a meeting before even breaking her fast. “I want simple and quick decisions. Will the sudden onset of winter delay us?”

“My patrols woke me up three hours ago over this matter. I sent them out before dawn. The road looks clear, but I would still recommend we wait an hour or two for the sun to warm the ground.”

“Explain your reasoning.”

“You sink through mud but over ice, you slip. One is more dangerous than the other. Frozen mud is harder to dig through if we need to as well.”

“Then I’ll leave it to you and my second to organise when we leave. Until then, I’ll be meditating.”

She wouldn’t, but it was a good reason to escape. The power to delegate was quickly becoming her favourite thing ever.

***

It was noon when they set out, the fog thinner. A very late start and they’d have a shorter riding day. But better that than dealing with injured mortals. One of the horses ridden by an Imperial soldier had slipped on ice whilst Hua was eating breakfast and proven Captain Yao correct. The horse was fine, thankfully. The rider… Hua didn’t think he was dead?

As with yesterday, Hua rode ahead with her cousin, taking in the landscape that quickly gave way from a light forest to an undulating expanse of wheat. It varied where the heads were golden and alive to those scorched black and some flattened by rain, ice, and mud. Two scouts followed with them at Captain Yao’s insistence, regular soldiers who had only barely entered Body Tempering. They only encountered one fallen tree and a natural dip in the road that had deepened to form a crevasse that was nearly a li across, either side of the walls frozen over with ice. They turned northward, riding parallel to the crevasse in search of a gentler route that wouldn’t involve building a bridge.

It had been filled up by enough wheat and plant life that there would be fertilizer enough for ten farms next growing season. Assuming the mortals to work the field would survive till the next growing season.

It became evident that someone had come through here earlier and made a path through the wheat crops that had survived, a path they followed.

The crevasse flattened the further they rode, becoming little more than a ditch filled with wheat and still water. Far to the west, hills grew taller until only the peak of a mountain could be seen past the clouds.

She slowed her horse to a trot patting its strong neck comfortingly. Hua narrowed her eyes, focusing hard. She suppressed her Qi signature.

Her cousin brought his horse closer to hers, his signature just as empty as hers.

“Is something wrong?” he asked, glancing back to the scouts a few horse-lengths behind them. Then he turned back to her. “Why are you smiling?”

“Because I think we’re going to kill people today.”

Hiding on the ridge were bandits.
