The hall was wrong.

Virelle hovered where she had been a moment ago, lightning still crackling faintly at her fingertips, and for one long, disoriented breath she could not understand what she was looking at.

Aiven was gone.

Lyra was gone.

The dais was empty. The spot on the floor where her Master had been flying toward her, fifteen paces out, hand reaching into his coat, was simply empty. The air where the two of them had been was closing over the absence the way water closes over a dropped stone.

She had felt the teleport take them.

She had not felt it take her.

Why am I still here, she thought. Why am I—

Across the hall, Lucien stood up.

The paralysis from her lightning had run its course. He rolled one shoulder, then the other, with the unhurried air of a man working a stiffness out after sitting too long at a desk. He brushed a fleck of dust from the sleeve of his robe. His golden eyes found her, and there was something almost warm in them now, the warmth of a man who has arrived at the part of the afternoon he had been looking forward to.

Virelle did not wait to find out what it was.

She fired.

ZRRRT.

The lightning cracked across the hall. Lucien tilted to one side, almost lazily, and the bolt passed through the space his chest had occupied and spent itself against a far pillar. He was off the ground now, floating, the hem of his robe drifting.

Virelle threw both hands up.

Fire bloomed. A dozen sigils, a dozen fireballs, the same furnace-red swarm that had cracked his shield in the fight.

Lucien raised one hand and a dense lavender shield curved into being in front of him. The fireballs broke against it, one after another, in a wash of red. The shield held.

He had been ready for it this time.

Something sang behind her.

Virelle dropped on instinct, folding sideways in the air, and a length of fine silver chain hissed through the space where her neck had been. She knew that chain. She knew the particular muffling weight of it, the way it drank the mana out of the air around it.

She spun.

Valerion was stepping out of a portal at the far end of the hall, pale and unhurried, his red eyes catching the sourceless light. Around him, a dozen lengths of fine chain unspooled into the air, swirling, lifting, arranging themselves into a slow lethal orbit.

“You,” Virelle hissed. “Of course. The errand-boy with the knitting.”

Valerion did not rise to it. He never did.

Virelle felt the pressure gather.

She knew it now, the small cold focusing of attention that came before Lucien moved. She threw up a mana barrier, a clean lavender dome.

It shattered before it had finished forming.

The gravity hit it from an angle she had not predicted, crushed it inward, and the lavender scattered into glittering dust. She was already moving, diving low, beneath the next chain, around the third.

Valerion’s chains came for her.

They moved like a school of silver eels, fast and coordinated, lashing across the hall in patterns that closed off her escape routes one by one. Virelle flew. She was good at flying. She had been flying since the day she was made, and she put all of it to use now, banking hard between the pillars, rolling under a chain, snapping a fireball back over her shoulder to force Valerion to flinch his orbit out of true.

She wove her barriers into the rhythm of it. A dome here, timed to the half-second before Lucien’s gravity could land. A dome there. Each one shattered the instant it served its purpose, but each one bought her a breath, and she strung the breaths together into something that almost looked like control.

She flew low across the floor of the hall, beneath a lash of chain.

A pair of hands came up out of the stone and closed around her ankles.

Virelle pitched forward.

The hands yanked, hard, and her flight broke, and she went down against the polished floor with a force that drove the breath out of her. The hands let go and phased back down into the stone like a swimmer slipping under a surface.

Then they came back up, and the rest of the body followed.

Sylphaine rose out of the floor in a sweep of white twin-tails and crimson coat, smiling, her fangs catching the light.

Virelle scrambled to push herself up.

The gravity landed.

It came down on her shoulders like a falling roof, and her arms buckled, and her cheek pressed against the cold stone, and she could not lift it. Lucien’s spell, finally connecting, pinning her flat to the floor of his own hall.

Valerion’s chains arrived.

One looped around her waist and cinched tight. Two more found her wrists and drew them up and out, away from her body, splaying her arms wide. The chains drank at her mana as they touched her, and she felt her own power thin and recede, the way a fire gutters when the air is pulled out of a room.

The chains lifted her.

Not far. Just up off the floor, just enough that her toes dragged, her arms spread, her waist bound. The gravity eased, replaced entirely by the chains, and she hung there in the middle of the hall like a thing strung up for inspection.

Lucien drifted closer.

As he came, the chains drew her toward him, until the two of them met somewhere in the middle of the hall, a pace apart, her hanging, him floating, their eyes level.

Virelle bared her teeth.

“How fitting,” she spat. “The three of you. A man who hides behind gravity because he cannot bear to be touched. A brother who fights with another person’s leash because he has no spine of his own to stand on. And a flea in a coat who crawls up through floors because she cannot win a single thing while standing in the daylight. A magnificent assembly. The cream of the rancid milk.”

Lucien smiled.

“You are free to insult them however you like, Starweave,” he said. “It costs me nothing, and it appears to cost you a great deal of effort, so by all means, continue. It is one of the few activities still available to you.”

He spread his hands, taking in the chains, the hall, her splayed arms.

“You have lost the battle. You have been abandoned by your Master. You are, at this moment, a helpless fake elf hanging from another man’s chains in a room very far from anywhere you might call home. Insult away. It changes none of those facts.”

“Fake,” Virelle said. Her voice did not waver, but something behind her eyes did. “Rich, coming from a man who wants to play god because his great-great-grandfather could lift rocks.”

She lifted her chin against the pull of the chains.

“And my Master did not abandon me.”

Lucien tilted his head.

“No?”

“No.”

“Then why,” Lucien said gently, “are you still here?”

He let the question sit.

“You felt the teleport, did you not? It took him. It took Lyra. It did not take you. He was looking directly at you as the spell closed. And you are here, and he is not. Why is that, do you suppose, if he did not abandon you?”

Virelle opened her mouth.

Nothing came out.

Lucien drifted a half-pace closer.

“It is very simple,” he said. “I will explain it to you, since you appear to be having difficulty. Aiven came here for Lyra. Only for Lyra. The whole of his week, the whole of his training, the whole of his refusal of my offer, all of it was for Lyra. You were the means. She was the end.”

He folded his hands.

“He knew that every second he delayed the teleport was a second in which the window might close. He was a clerk. He calculates. He weighs. He balances the ledger. And when the moment came, with Lyra’s hand in his and the spell ready to fire, he made the calculation he was always going to make. He took the sure thing. He took her. And he ran. Perhaps he did try to take you with him, but then he realized there was simply not enough time.”

“You are lying,” Virelle said.

Her voice had gone lower.

“You know nothing about my Master.”

“Perhaps I do not,” Lucien said. “I confess I have only met him a handful of times. But let me ask you a question in return, Starweave, since you are so very certain.”

His eyes were patient.

“Do you know everything about him?”

Virelle said nothing.

“Because if you did,” Lucien went on, “if you truly knew him, all the way down, the way you believe you do, then you would already know how much Lyra means to him. You would know it better than I ever could. You would have known it from the very first day.”

And against her own will, against everything she was straining to hold shut, Virelle remembered.

She remembered the nights in the Lowhaven apartment.

The small sounds her Master made in his sleep. The way, more than once, he had murmured a name into the dark, soft and broken, a name that was not hers. She had hovered above his cot in the blue hours and listened to him say Lyra to the ceiling, and she had told herself it meant nothing, that the dreaming mind was a foolish thing, that it would pass.

She remembered the night they fled to Fangreach.

The way he had stopped, in the chaos, with the officers closing in and the whole sky to cross, to take an old battered sword from a drawer. A nicked, worthless blade. She had thought it strange that a man running for his life would pause for a piece of junk. She had guessed it then. It had been theirs. His and Lyra’s.

She remembered his face in the clearing.

The moment the hood had come back and Lyra had been alive beneath it. She remembered the way his whole body had changed. The way the grief he had been carrying for a month had simply fallen off him in an instant, like a coat dropped on a floor. She had never, in all her time with him, seen his face do that. Not for a victory. Not for a meal. Not for her.

She wanted to deny it.

She could not.

“Face it, Starweave,” Lucien said softly.

He was not gloating. That was the worst of it. His voice was almost kind, the voice of a tutor delivering a hard truth to a student he did not entirely dislike.

“The moment Aiven learned that Lyra was alive, every other thing in his world became secondary. Including you. Especially you. From that moment, his single purpose was to take her back, and you became what you have always been, in the deepest honest accounting of it. A tool. A very powerful, very devoted, very useful tool. And when the moment came to choose between carrying the tool and carrying the girl, he chose the girl, and he left the tool behind.”

Virelle pulled against the chains.

Sparks of lavender flickered along her wrists, guttering against the muffling silver, dying before they could become anything. She pulled harder. The chains held. They drank everything she gave them.

“Master would not,” she said. Her voice was fraying now. “He would not abandon me. We talked about it. We had a plan. All three of us, together, out of the Fold. He promised. He said he would think things through and he would tell me, he said I was important to him, he—”

She caught herself.

“It was your trick,” she said. “You did this. You sent them out and kept me here to play this little game. This is your doing.”

Lucien sighed.

“If it had been my doing,” he said, “I would have done it far more cleverly than this. Think, Starweave. You are capable of it. If I had the power to pluck one of you out of this hall at will, why would I have waited? Why would I have permitted myself to be punched in the face, paralyzed by a lightning bolt, made undignified in my own domain? Why would I have allowed a fight in which I was outnumbered two to one?”

He shook his head.

“I would have removed one of you the instant you stepped through my door. I would have killed the other before the first had finished falling. I do not waste leverage, and I do not stage elaborate humiliations for my own benefit. If the teleport had been mine, you would not be hanging here listening to me. You would simply be mine, and Aiven would simply be dead.”

He let that settle.

“The teleport was not mine. I think, somewhere behind all this noise, you already know whose it was.”

Virelle went very still.

She closed her eyes.

When she opened them, she had found something hard to put behind her gaze, some last reserve she had been keeping for exactly this.

“I will not,” she said, “fall for your words.”

Her voice steadied as she spoke, the way a person’s voice steadies when they have decided to hold a line regardless of whether they believe it.

“You may say whatever you wish. You may dress your poison up in whatever reasonable clothing you like. It is pointless. I know what I know. You will not talk me into your hands with a clever speech, you preening, overdressed relic.”

Lucien regarded her for a moment.

“I am not trying to talk you into my hands,” he said. “You are already in them. I am inviting you to come home.”

His voice gentled further.

“Come back, Starweave. Come back and do real things with me again. Meaningful things. The work we used to do, you and I, before any of this. You do not remember it. But it was good work. It was the only work that has ever truly mattered in this rotting world, and you were extraordinary at it. Come back. Stop wasting yourself on a clerk who left you for a girl.”

“I do not remember,” Virelle said. “And I do not want to.”

She lifted her chin again, the chains pulling at her wrists.

“Whatever I was to you, whatever work you imagine we did, I want none of it. And even if you forced every memory back into my head, even if you filled me to the brim with whoever you think I used to be, I would still not lift one finger to help you with your stupid, arrogant, world-ending schemes. I would rather hang here until I rot. I would rather you unmake me where I hang.”

Her eyes burned.

“And my Master will come for me. He will. With everything he has unsealed, with everything he has become, he will walk into this miserable folded box of yours and he will beat every single one of you. You included.”

Lucien smiled.

“He does have potential,” he agreed. “Genuine potential. I meant it when I called him a spark in the dust. In a few years, perhaps, he might even have been interesting.”

His smile did not change.

“But I am not going to give him a few years. I am not going to give him ten days. And yes, Starweave. You are right about one thing. He will come for you.”

He drifted a half-pace back.

“That is precisely where you come in.”

Virelle’s stomach turned cold.

“What is that supposed to mean?” she said. “I told you. I will not help you do anything. Whatever role you have written for me in your little pageant, the answer is no. I would not help you cross a street. I would not help you find the exit if this hall were on fire, which, given my temperament and present mood, it may shortly be.”

Lucien did not answer her.

He turned his head, slightly, toward the edge of the hall.

“Sylphaine.”

Sylphaine stepped forward.

She had been lounging against a pillar, watching the whole exchange with the bored, hungry patience of a cat at a mousehole. Now she pushed off it and drifted closer, her twin-tails swaying, her crimson eyes bright.

Virelle laughed, sharp and brittle.

“Oh, wonderful. The flea returns. Tell me, leech, how have you been keeping, since I reduced you to a fine seasoning last time? You look remarkably reassembled for a girl I last saw as a cloud of drifting ash. Did someone sweep you up and pour you back into the coat?”

Sylphaine’s smile twitched.

Her crimson eyes flashed, and her mouth opened on something venomous—

“Sylphaine,” Lucien said. “Quickly.”

Sylphaine closed her mouth.

She drew in a breath through her nose, visibly mastering herself, the petulance smoothing back into that hungry, theatrical calm.

“Yes, Master,” she said.

She raised her index finger.

The nail lengthened, darkened, and the tip of her finger split and reformed into a single fine red thread, wet and gleaming, that wiggled in the air like something alive and curious.

Virelle’s heart slammed against her ribs.

She knew that thread.

She remembered the thicket. The forest near Oakwood. The thread sliding into her ear, the cold of it, the way her own buried memories had been dragged screaming up into the light against her will. The white explosion. The marble breaking. The blonde girl in the scarf.

She remembered all of it now, and the remembering made the thread worse.

Her heartbeat raced. She pulled at the chains, and the chains held, and the sparks died at her wrists.

Still, she found the breath for it.

“Is that,” she said, “the best you have, you one-trick parasite? A worm you keep in your finger. How proud your mother must be. Assuming worms have mothers, which I sincerely doubt, in your—”

Sylphaine drifted closer.

Close enough that Virelle could see the flecks of crimson in her irises, the small sharp points of her teeth as she smiled.

“Soon,” Sylphaine murmured, almost tenderly, “all that sharp little tongue. And all that pretty power. It is going to be on our side.”

Virelle opened her mouth for one more insult.

The red thread slipped into her ear.

And her world went white.
