The afternoon passed in a blur of baking. Flour and fire flew as loaves were built and baked. Felicity started with the simplest of the simple loaves, something almost identical to the same basic bread recipe that had formed the foundation of Jane's own education. Even in something so simple, there were differences to be found. The woman had refined her techniques over time, making small adjustments to her methods that added up to significant differences.

Jane pulled her notebook from her bag and began writing. She had expected to need to take notes, that Felicity was too brilliant to not have more to teach. She hadn’t expected to fill up page after page in just the first few hours, though she was absolutely delighted to do so.

Felicity noticed and smiled. "Good. Write it all down. I’ve worked hard for all this, but I’m too old to write another book. Just promise you’ll find someone else to teach, eventually. Someone who understands the complexities of all this"

She demonstrated what she meant by adjusting the sugar content of a cookie here or the soda content of a recipe there. Jane asked why each time, getting cogent explanations of the exact reasoning and expected effect of the changes.

"How do you know how much to adjust?" Jane asked, her pen moving across the page.

“Experience and experiments. The nice thing with our stage, Jane, is that everything we make is good enough. That leaves plenty of room to stumble around, looking for greatness. Now, taste.”

Jane flushed at being part of Felicity’s our stage, to be lumped in reflexively with her as an equal. The old woman gave her a bit of cookie dough to try, and Jane tasted. It was true. The cookies this recipe made would be better in a way both her and Felicity would see from a mile away. The average eater would just know they were very good, even if they didn’t know why.

"I see what you mean."

"Good. Now, here's something else I haven’t managed to write down."

Jane leaned forward, pen ready. A string of insights came through, all related to how the cookie dough was to be placed on the pan, in what shape and size, and why. It was a whole formula in and of itself, a relationship between time, heat, shape and ingredients that went beyond the cookbook once more. Again, it was a minor thing. Again, it would change her baking forever.

Jane wrote it down twice, just to make she she lost nothing.

They made three more batches of various things over the next few hours. Each one came with its own set of insights that Felicity had developed since she had first penned her text. By the time the sun began to sink toward the horizon, Jane had filled nearly ten pages of notes. Her hand ached from writing and kneading, but the cost was well worth it. Every word felt precious.

"Now," Felicity said. "I've shown you my baking. It's time for you to show me yours."

"I couldn’t."

"No, I want to see your baking. You said you learned from my book, but learning is only the beginning. What have you made of it since then? What's yours?"

Jane felt more nervous than she had cooking for the king. She could hardly say no, though. She walked Felicity through the making of some of her buns, using whatever meat the older woman had set aside for her own meals. Jane resolved to replace it the next morning, if she could. They put together a burnt-top loaf and a few cookies Jane had modified from the original recipes. They were all things her customers liked, which made her feel a little better about the probability that Felicity would like them, too.

Of course, keln would be part of that showing, too. It wasn’t hers, but in a way she had inherited it. That felt close enough to ownership that Jane felt comfortable showing it off, and the older baker agreed.

Once all the batches were in the oven, Jane and Felicity finally took a break, drinking down an entire pot of tea before the first of her products began to emerge. Felicity examined each item, first sampling the keln and declaring she very much approved of it. She spent minutes turning the burnt-top bread over in her hands and studying the seared crust from multiple angles, asking questions about the properties of the flash of fire magic that had burned it.

She broke off a piece and tasted it, chewing slowly and thoughtfully.

"This is good," she said finally. "Very good. The sear is impossibly even. That’s your magic at work?"

“Yes. I can convert magic to heat at a fairly exact rate. It made it possible to dial in the burn in a way I couldn’t using just a lit charcoal. It’s the same way with keeping the temperature in an oven consistent. It’s easier once you get the trick of it."

Felicity's eyebrows rose. "You use magic for temperature control?"

"Mostly for sensing. The heat itself is from normal sources. I can make some fine adjustments during the process, but it’s usually better to just do it the old fashioned way."

"Interesting." Felicity took another bite, still thinking. " These herbs and seeds are good, but they could be better. Have you tried toasting them before you apply them? You could probably squeeze a little more crisp out of them that way, without risking the bread."

Jane hadn't. She wrote it down.

Felicity broke down each of her dishes in turn, approving of everything but tweaking and improving where she saw the opportunities. After a while, Jane began to sense a certain pattern to it all, a vague sort of formula that unified all the suggestions together. She would have never understood it if she hadn’t already spent so much time immersed in the woman’s way of thinking, but as a student of her work, she understood the continuation in an almost natural way.

What suggestions they could put in play quickly, they did. Another batch went into the oven, and Jane and woman took a break, throwing the doors of the house open to let in some of the cool mountain air.

"That's better," Felicity said. "I always thought old women were supposed to be cold, and here I am covered in sweat."

By the time the last batch was done, the clock on the wall showed nearly midnight and Jane had filled her entire notebook. Jane was exhausted, and she couldn’t imagine how tired Felicity must be.

“Bedtime, I think.” Jane said. “I’m beat.”

"Agreed. These old bones can only take so much." Felicity slumped a bit, looking even older once her perfect posture broke. "We should rest."

Jane nodded and began to move to the door, but there was something she still wanted to ask before the night ended. It had occurred to her that Felicity had in some ways followed a similar path to Jane, if not quite as voluntary. More importantly, she had followed it longer, in ways that had nothing to do with baking, exactly.

"Can I ask you something?"

"Of course."

"Are you happy? Here, I mean. In this place, living this life."

"Yes," she said instantly. There was no hesitation on that subject, it seemed. "I am."

"But you had to leave your career. That must have hurt."

"It did, at first. For almost a year after I left the foundry, I wandered, visiting places I had heard of and finding them, well, nice enough. Good places filled with fine people, but not for me. Then I found this place. Something about it felt right after the first night, and I just never really managed to leave.”

"It just fit."

"Like a key into a lock. The town needed a baker. The mountains agreed with my lungs, and I learned to love the quiet. I’ve seen a generation or two grow here since. It’s home."

"I wonder sometimes I really fit where I landed. In Glenfall, I mean. It happened so fast, and it was the first place I went. I didn't choose it so much as fall into it."

The older woman laughed. "Don't be foolish, child. I've listened to you talk about that town all afternoon. About your shop and your friends and your young man.” Felicity shook her head. "A fool could see you fell for that place. It has your heart. There’s nothing wrong with that."

Jane felt a bit of relief at being told it was all right to go with the first option one tried, so long as she liked it enough. She had really known the truth of it herself, but hearing it from someone else settled the issue for good.

It was then, in the middle of the night, that she realized something.

"Uggh. I didn't arrange a place to sleep. I got caught up in things and forgot."

Felicity waved a hand dismissively. "Don't be silly. Just go to the inn and let yourself into a room."

"Really? It's the middle of the night. Won't they be upset?"

"Nobody would begrudge a young girl a bed, here.” Felicity smiled. "Trust me. Just go in, find a room with a made bed, and sleep. You can settle up in the morning."

Jane stood, gathering her bag and her now precious notebook. Before she could reach the door, she turned back.

"Can I come back tomorrow morning? There's still so much I want to learn."

Felicity's expression softened. "Of course you can. But are you sure you want to? I would have thought you'd be tired of such an old woman by now."

"No." Jane felt tears prickling at her eyes. "No, of course I'm not. You have no idea how much this means to me."

The tears were running in streams before she could stop them. Then she felt arms around her, thin and frail but surprisingly warm. Felicity was taller than she was, even stooped with age, and Jane was encompassed in her weak grasp for more than a few moments before she regained control over herself.

"It meant a lot to me, too. More than you know. I spent years on that book, and I never knew if it mattered at all." She held Jane a little tighter. "And then you came and told me you had. It's like I opened a book, and just had to leave it lay until someone came along and closed it for me. You did that."

Finally, Felicity released her and stepped back. Her eyes were wet too.

"Now go, child. Get some sleep. I'll see you in the morning."

Jane wiped her eyes with the back of her hand. "Thank you. For everything."

"Thank you for coming. It's the best gift anyone has given me in a very long time."

Jane walked out into the cool mountain night and walked the quarter mile or so it took to reach the inn. The building was silent and dark, just as Felicity had predicted. Jane unhooked the door and let herself in, lighting a small flame above her hand to light her way.

The common room was tiny and empty, and a narrow staircase led up to the second floor. Jane climbed it carefully. There were four doors at the top of the stairs, and she found the third was what she was looking for. It was a small room with a single bed, made up and ready for guests.

She set her bag on the floor, kicked off her shoes, and collapsed onto the mattress without bothering to undress. The bed was soft enough and the night late enough that even an unfamiliar room couldn’t get in the way of her sleep.
