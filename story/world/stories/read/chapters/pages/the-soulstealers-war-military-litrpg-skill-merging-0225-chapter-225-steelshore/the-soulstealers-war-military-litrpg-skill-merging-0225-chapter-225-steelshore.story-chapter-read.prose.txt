On the sixth day of riding, the chimney smoke of Steelshore appeared on the horizon.

The journey hadn’t been eventful. After I finished my Skill merges, I focused on intaking the Presences I’d received in Hyrdford, and on continuing to sort the most important parts of my memory palace.

After the Skill Merge, my combat memories were smaller, but they hadn’t been erased entirely. I could still move them to different mental shelves, and they often travelled in bundles with the other linked Skills.

Far more interesting was the memories my armour Skills implanted. I couldn’t access them completely, but if I really concentrated on it, it felt like I’d spent a lifetime operating a crane or being a lawyer in an ancient Roman court.

As we approached Steelshore, the foothills grew steeper, and the valleys deeper. A warm wind melted most of the snow, but the mountains loomed ever closer, and glaciers still streaked their caps. Once, we crossed a corner of the boglands, but they weren’t nearly as muddy as I had been expecting for the spring. It was the edge of the bog, and it quickly faded away as we gained altitude.

Every day, more and more seagulls whirled overhead. We couldn’t be that close to the southern coast, could we? I couldn’t see the shore, and we kept gaining elevation. The foothills kept rising and falling.

Our trail merged with a brick road from the old empire, which carried us over bridges and made our progress faster. We crossed over sharp-walled chasms filled with shrubs and waterfalls.

Every village we passed looked the same as Hyrdford had when we first arrived. Worn down, quiet, and long-since given up on itself. The people weren’t nearly as ill, but every so often, we passed a burnt farmhouse or a field that hadn’t been harvested last year. Worse, once, we passed a ranch whose cattle had all seemingly died from exposure. Carrion birds feasted on their half-frozen corpses, outside a barn whose doors hadn’t been unlocked. The ranchers were nowhere to be seen.

Finally, Steelshore itself emerged from the horizon.

I hadn’t realized how close we were to the coast, but as soon as I saw the cliffside abruptly end, I understood. A wall of white limestone formed the southern coast, like the Cliffs of Dover, topped by snow and grass. The rivers shattered the land, paving trenches out to the sea, and creating long, skinny slivers of land at the top of the cliffs like dry mud.

“That’s the Suderled Sea,” Anchor said.

“It is one of the deepest seas in the world,” Sage added. “Not even an Emerald could reach the bottom—that shelf drops off abruptly. There is no beach.”

“In the world?” I asked. “How do you know?”

“She does not know precisely,” Brawl teased. “She would, however, call that an ‘exaggeration’.”

“The point is that it is deep.”

Steelshore began gradually. Its outer structures had no curtain wall to protect them, and the wattle and daub houses were rotting and collapsing as we rode through. Outside the wall, people trudged through the streets or lit fires in alleyways. Every so often, we passed a burnt-out house. I spotted a few cultists in an alleyway who fled when they saw us.

Steelshore spanned across multiple shattered sections of land. Bridges spanned the gorges, though I didn’t trust half of them to support our weight. We stuck to the ancient marble structures left behind by the old empire—they were the busiest bridges, anyway.

After travelling the equivalent of a few city blocks, we arrived at an old curtain wall made of cobblestone. There were no ornaments, and it was barely thrice my height. The buildings inside it were all taller than it, as were the outside walls. If someone wanted, they could easily use the buildings outside to hop the wall without having to lay siege to it.

Sage caught me inspecting the walls and said, “The city hasn’t been attacked since Aelfwine’s reign. These walls have never even been tested, and the city has grown beyond them. But they keep out ruffians, and most of the inner city is insulated from the rest of the outside world.”

We passed through a gate. The guards didn’t bother questioning us. They would’ve been expecting us, and there wasn’t anything they could do to stop us even if they wanted.

Inside the wall, we rode our horses up onto a terrace, which afforded us a view of the southern half of the city.

The city folded over the cliff edge like a blanket hanging off a shelf. Blankets clung to the stone, and piers stretched out into the waves. They were mostly abandoned, and only a few ships sloshed in and out of the piers. The waves, though harsh, were somewhat mitigated by the inlets that the ships docked in.

The ships themselves were tiny. They reminded me of viking longboats, both in size and shape, but their hulls didn’t boast any shields, and their sails were tattered. Crews offloaded supplies onto the docks, and cranes hoisted them up to the city’s higher levels.

“How can a shipping hub be so large if they aren’t shipping anything?” I whispered.

“There are very few actual workers in this city anymore,” Sage replied. “Most of them are bureaucrats for Lord Hinlaw’s government, or ink-scrawlers for South Gate’s larger guilds.”

“War and orcs tend to put a damper on shipping,” Anchor provided, but she ignored him.

“Now, the busiest part of the city is the fortress-palace,” Sage said, pointing to the southwestern corner of the city, where an archway of stone reached out over the sea. A massive structure grew atop it, made entirely of white marble.

Its main structure was made entirely of overlapping towers. At the edges, they were short, but the closer they drew to the center of the complex, the taller they got, until they created two separate peaks joined together by a thin bridge.

It had to be something from the old empire. Gate didn’t build things that large anymore. Vines and moss scaled its edges, and modern buildings clung to its walls as barnacles. As the sun rose, the candlelights in the windows slowly dimmed, but nothing else changed. It was just too massive and too far away to see people working inside it—even for my enhanced Perception.

“Is that where Hinlaw lives?” I asked as we trotted down the other side of the terrace and delved deeper into the city.

“It should be,” Anchor replied.

“Target acquired, then.”

“When are we going to attack, brother?” Brawl whispered. “Are you going to challenge him?”

“No duels. He won’t accept, anyway.” I paused, weighing my options. This phase of training was supposed to take four weeks. But if I waited that long, Hinlaw might get scared and try to flee. That was the last thing I needed.

But if I attacked too soon, Moonhand wouldn’t have time to work his political magic. He may have been an Emerald, but he couldn’t teleport from place to place, either. He was probably only arriving back at Eraen-Kalora right now.

“We wait three weeks,” I said. “Then we—”

A rush of air tickled my cheek, and I ducked. A rotten potato flew over my head, then splattered on the walls of a building on the opposite side of the street. Here, everything had a cobblestone first storey and a half-timbered second level, complete with thatched roofs and chimneys. I couldn’t have found a more standard city for Gate—except, of course, for the scattered few buildings that used overturned boat hulls for their roofs.

I whipped my head in the direction that the rotten potato had flown from. Here, the walls were mostly clean and the streets were clear, and civilians in their dark clothes stood out like sore thumbs. Their attire was relatively clean and well-maintained, and though no one was obese, they were all well-fed.

They crowded the edges of the streets watching us, dressed in clean garments. They wore their hair in perfect bonnets or in neat ponytails, and…well, most of them were old. They had to be in their sixties at least.

“Sir Levi the Barbaric!” someone shouted.

“Princess defiler! Criminal!”

“Get out of our city!”

I didn’t see who threw the potato, and I didn’t care. I was in another thegn’s domain now, and I was subject to their laws. Even if I wanted to defend myself, something told me that wouldn’t go over well.

“Should I be concerned about Hinlaw trying to arrest me?” I whispered. “On account of…I dunno, me saying mean things about the cults?”

“Even he knows that wouldn’t work for long,” Anchor said.

“The sentiment seems popular enough…” I muttered.

People gathered on the edge of the streets, shouting at me, jeering and complaining. Some told me to leave, others shouted, “Send him to a moot!”

Would Hinlaw actually do it? Would he go after a thegn, a sapper, who was here on a mission? His own people might stand for it, but would the rest of Gate?

And if I fought my way out, which I would at least try, what then? It would be pointless at best. I gave it an eighty percent chance of happening.

As the protests grew more fierce, the Drillmaster led our column down a side street to avoid them. A few of the most determined protestors followed us, but we held our heads high, ignoring their complaints.

Finally, we reached a small keep located near the center of the city. Inside, cobblestone barracks lined the walls.

We stopped and dismounted, and the Drillmaster and his remaining lieutenants turned to face us. “This is our outpost for the coming weeks. We will take part in a series of drills involving aquatic training. Swimming to improve your conditioning, seamanship on a boat to improve your teamwork, knot-tying to improve your dexterity.”

As far as I could tell, the Drillmaster had no idea what we planned. If all went well, he wouldn’t even know it was me who did it.

I would face Hinlaw on his own turf, whether he liked it or not.

~ ~ ~

As usual, I made the absolute most of our days in Steelshore. We didn’t have our own wakeup bell, but the city bells chimed in the mornings around the same time that Castle Urcia’s bells would’ve.

I was always awake hours before. I inspected Hinlaw’s fortress, watching the entrances and exits, trying to spot the Ealdorman. He was nowhere to be seen, but when I asked around, no one had seen him leave.

Once I’d identified all the potential exits, I spoke to the guards. Most of them knew who I was by my reputation, but being Dupes, I hoped they would be slightly more pliable to my cause.

I asked them if they would watch the entrances for me and report if they saw Hinlaw, and usually, I received some form of, “You have no power here,” or “You’re still in training—you don’t command us yet,” or “You’re making everything worse for us Dupes, sir,” but finally, one young Dupe agreed to do it for a price.

After my daily scouting missions, I returned to the inner city keep for breakfast. We didn’t have any infused food, and our total supplies were dwindling as it was. Meals consisted of basic dried meat and hardened biscuits. They were filling enough, but we weren’t going to get any miraculous advancement out of them.

Then, as long as we had daylight, we worked in the harbour. There, we practiced on the boats. The first few days, the Drillmaster and the two surviving lieutenants taught us knots and general theories, and for the rest of the week, we practiced.

I couldn’t stop myself from sneaking glances at the Ealdorman’s fortress as we worked.  I hated being cut off from the world, not even having a glimpse of Hinlaw. I hated not getting letters from Alis, or meeting with Moonhand and teasing out information about the wider state of the world.

After we grew proficient at knots and the general concept of seamanship, they took us out on the boats. While, for the first week, they paired us with experienced sailors to ensure we didn’t crash into the cliffs, we weren’t going to have the safety net in the coming weeks.

On the sixth day of the first week, when we were out at sea, and the crashing waves hid our voices, Anchor rushed to the ship’s gunwale and pushed in beside me. Pretending to tie a knot, he said, “I haven’t seen a single Subtle Man this entire week.”

“That’s good, no?” I replied.

“We’re in their southern stronghold. They have outposts all over the city. They even have a System amplifier here. You don’t think it’s odd that we haven’t seen a single one?”

“I suppose. What do you think they’re doing?”

“I have no idea, but something’s not right here.”

I nodded. “Stay alert. One of us stays awake at all times. If we notice anything fishy, you call it out. Got it?”

“Yes, Sarge.”

~ ~ ~

By the end of the second week, we were sailing the boats on our own. We learned fast, and we didn’t need any physical preparation, unlike most newbie sailors. Our sea legs developed faster than most, and all of us had exceptional balance. An experienced crew of mortal men still sailed with us, making sure we didn’t crash the boats, but they didn’t do any of the work.

When we stepped ashore on the last day of the second week, the Drillmaster met us on the wharf. He paced in front of us, arms crossed. “You have all advanced phenomenally. Tomorrow morning, we will begin a day-long voyage, where you will be expected to put all your skills to use, no matter the weather…”

He droned on for a few minutes, but my gaze wandered to Hinlaw’s fortress. The setting sun turned it into a black spire, but a single slit of light burned in a window near the tower’s peak.

I’d never seen that window lit before.

~ ~ ~

“Pack only the most important belongings. We travel light. Nothing burdens the carriage that doesn’t have to,” Hinlaw said, marching around his bedchamber with purpose. His third son, and now his only son, stood in the doorway, arms crossed. Hincar was almost a Gold himself, and he was by far the most motivated of Hinlaw’s children.

It wouldn’t matter. Hinbern had already dishonoured them enough in his defeat. Their family would look pathetically weak, and the Witenagemot was surely already preparing to replace him.

“What’s wrong, father?” Hincar asked.

“Both of your brothers are now dead,” Hinlaw said. “The news arrived this afternoon. They attempted to keep it from me, but even I still have a few ears scattered around the nation.”

Slow messengers, though. He didn’t have enough good men anymore, no one who could travel as quickly as a patrol of determined sappers, apparently.

He didn’t have any good men anymore. He could only muster a force of a hundred cultists to assault Hyrdford. The rest responded only to the Subtle Men, now, and Hinbern hadn’t been able to turn the tide. The sappers had moved fast, and he hadn’t expected all of the Sapper trainees to show up. Just Sir Levi.

Everything was slipping away. He had to get out of here before it got even worse.

“The flash-raven from Frythlid didn’t seem to faze you,” Hincar stated plainly, his face expressionless. Of Hinlaw’s sons, he was by far the broadest, and it was all muscle. He was a better wicca than his brothers, with his preference for unarmed combat—he only used his Great Warbrand if he had to.

“She told me nothing I didn’t know. Sir Levi is planning to kill me? Yes, I ought to have known that.”

“What changed?”

“The Subtle Men have locked themselves in their outposts for the entirety of the past week,” Hinlaw stated. “I sent emissaries this evening, and the Subtle Men turned them away. They have abandoned me, and that can only mean one thing.”

“Father?”

“Your wit may not be the sharpest, son, but Frythlid said she sent two letters. Can you imagine who she sent the second to?”

Hincar swallowed, then nodded.

“He is coming.” It wasn’t wise to talk about powerful Path-walkers with their name or title, not when you were planning on running from them. “His Subtle Men have abandoned us, and they await orders from their one master, now.”

“He will not kill us. We have been loyal to him. We have done everything he has asked.”

“We failed to replace Lord Ceonwin. We failed to stop Sir Levi.”

“Father…”

“Sir Levi is here for us. And he is here for Sir Levi. I do not want to be present when either of them arrive. We won’t survive the aftermath, no matter what happens. Gather your things, and come with me.”
