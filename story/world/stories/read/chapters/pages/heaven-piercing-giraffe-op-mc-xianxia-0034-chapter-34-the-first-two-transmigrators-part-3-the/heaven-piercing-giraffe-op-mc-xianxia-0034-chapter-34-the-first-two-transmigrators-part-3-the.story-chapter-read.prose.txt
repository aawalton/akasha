Chapter 34: The First Two Transmigrators Part 3 (The American's POV)

It had been a month since I somehow became part of the family. I stood at the edge of the field behind the house with my hands on my hips and stared at what I'd done.

Technically, I hadn't done anything. That was the problem.

The field sat at the back of the house, hidden by a huge tree. It was older than the village, with a trunk four people couldn't have circled and branches so low and wide they hung down like a curtain, a wall of leaves between the house and everything behind it. Len had never been much for looking behind the tree. She hunted at dawn, came home through the front, ate, slept, and left the back to me.

I'd planted the vegetables exactly the way Len showed me, in neat rows, and watered them from the river I climbed down to every single morning. Somewhere around week two I noticed the glowing weed I'd also planted had started dropping seeds. I'd meant to deal with it. Then I'd gotten busy.

Now the whole field was weed. Rows and rows of it, faint gold, swaying in the wind with little motes of light drifting in from the air to settle on every leaf. The turnips were dead, the beans were dead, and even the stubborn pumpkin vine that had survived the caterpillar invasion was now a brown, crumpled thing lying under a leaf the size of my hand.

"Sis is going to be so mad when she finds out," Yuto said, standing beside me with his arms crossed.

"Definitely," the other kids chorused behind him. There were five of them now. They had followed me out here the moment I'd stopped moving.

I sighed. "She'll find out one way or another." I squared my shoulders. "Better to man up and tell her myself." I took a step toward the house.

A small hand caught mine.

"What's up, kid?" I looked down.

Yuto's face had changed. The grin he wore like a permanent accessory had dropped away completely, and what was underneath looked older than he was. Behind him, the other kids had gone just as quiet, all of them watching me with the same flat, serious stare.

The wind picked up across the field. It carried the smell with it, sweet, green, heavy, the smell of home. Of a basement closet, a secret garden, an entire childhood.

"Take my sister," Yuto said, "and run."

I stared at him. "Why?"

He pointed up.

A floating island hung in the sky above the mountain, close enough that its shadow had been creeping across our field for the past several days. I had assumed it was just weather.

"Every year they come down," Yuto said. "They take everyone who's old enough."

I didn't say anything. I thought about the village, about the way I'd noticed something off in my first week and filed it under culture shock. Almost no adults. A handful of young people, a lot of kids, and nobody over twenty. I'd assumed it was just a young community.

"If that's the case…" My throat felt dry. "Then Len..."

Yuto nodded.

"Yuto, what nonsense are you telling Juan?"

Len's voice came from behind the tree. The leaf curtain parted, and she stepped through it with a bow slung over one shoulder and a dead hare hanging from her belt. Then she saw the field and stopped.

"What the hell are these plants? Where are the crops?"

I crossed the distance to her in a few long strides and took her by the shoulders. "Len, listen to me—"

She knocked my hands away. "If you're scared, you should go." She looked at me, and her expression was more tired than I'd ever seen it. "Everyone here is accounted for. One missing person and the rest of us suffer for it. You're not on their list." She turned toward the house. "So leave. They're coming tomorrow."

She gathered the kids with a single look and led them back through the curtain of leaves. The branches swung shut behind them, and the field was alone with me again.

I stayed where I was, fists clenched so tight my nails dug into my palms.

A thin rain began to fall, soft and cold, settling on the leaves around my boots. Every one of them glowed a little brighter for it.

I opened my fist.

My nails had cut four neat crescents into each palm, and blood had pooled in the creases and started to drip, mixing with the rain on the leaves at my feet. I looked at it for a second, then wiped both hands on my pants. It stung.

I looked down the mountain, toward the crater I'd left a month ago. Then I went down.

I didn't take the path. The path wound back and forth across the slope in long, lazy switchbacks. Len had said tomorrow. Tomorrow was a few hours away, so I went straight down.

The first twenty meters were fine. Steep, but fine. The rain had only just started to soak in, and the grass held under my boots well enough that I started to think I'd made a smart call.

Then the grass ended and the dirt began.

My left foot hit a patch of wet clay and kept going without me. The world tilted. I threw out an arm for balance and caught nothing but air, and then my hip hit the slope and I was sliding, shoulder first, picking up speed with every meter.

The first tree took me in the ribs.

All the air left me at once. I bounced off the trunk and spun sideways, the sky and the ground trading places twice, and something hard caught my shin on the way past. A root, I think. I felt it more than I saw it, a bright line of pain that ran from ankle to knee.

I kept rolling.

Ferns whipped across my face. Wet gravel peeled skin off my forearms. Then a boulder rose out of the dark and I hit it with my back, and everything stopped.

I lay there with rain falling into my open mouth, listening to my own breathing sound like a leaking bellows.

Arms, I thought. I flexed my fingers. Legs. I wiggled my toes. Both shins burned, but they moved.

I rolled onto my side, got a palm under me, and pushed. My ribs complained in a sharp, specific way I chose not to investigate. I stood, swayed, and put a hand on the boulder until the trees stopped spinning.

I started running again, slower this time, but I did not stop.

***

The crater looked smaller than I remembered. Or maybe it looked smaller because the thing sitting at the edge of it looked so much less like a person.

Wei was still cross-legged, in exactly the position I'd left him in. The berries I'd set beside him were gone, the stems scattered and dried. I'd assumed he ate them. Judging by the rest of him, he'd eaten nothing else.

His robes hung off his shoulders like they were draped over a coat rack. His cheeks had caved into two shallow hollows, and the skin of his hands had pulled so tight over his knuckles I could count every bone. Gold motes of light drifted into him with each slow breath, and he looked like a lantern that someone had forgotten to feed.

I stopped a few steps away. My voice came out smaller than I meant it to. "What are you doing?"

His eyes stayed closed. "Cultivating."

I crossed the distance in two strides and grabbed him by the collar, hauling him upright. He weighed nothing. That scared me more than the rest of it.

"Snap out of it! Humans can't live sitting cross-legged for a whole month!"

His eyes opened, finally. They were sunken and clear and slightly annoyed. "But I lived."

"That's bullshit! If you keep this up, you'll be dead by next month!"

He didn't answer. He just looked at me, unbothered, like I was a fly that had landed on him.

I stared at him for a long moment. Then my grip loosened, and I let go, and he settled back down onto the ground without a word.

"I'm a fool," I said. "Coming here expecting help from you."

Nothing.

"But it looks like you need more help than I do." I dug into my pocket and pulled out the strip of dried meat Len had pressed on me that morning, back when my biggest problem was dead turnips. I set it down in front of him next to the empty berry stems. "And I don't have time to deal with you."

I turned and started back up the mountain without waiting for a reply.

***

It was already dark by the time I reached the field.

It didn't look dark, though. That was the strange part. The whole slope glowed. Every plant in every row burned a soft, steady gold, and the light drifted up from them in little motes that mixed with the rain, so that the drizzle itself looked like it was falling in sparks. The shadow of the floating island hung overhead, huge and silent, but beneath it the field shone like a lit window.

I stood at the edge of it, soaked, bloody, bruised from the descent, and for the first time in a month, completely sure of what I had to do.

I looked at the plants. They looked back, in whatever way a field of weed looks at you.

"Will you help me?"

The wind moved through the rows. They swayed, all of them, in the same slow direction, as if answering my question.
