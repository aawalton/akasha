By the time Eola, Colin, and Roth limped back to Varin’s Academy—with Eola doing most of the limping—she’d come up with a pretty reasonable theory about what had gone wrong with their patrol. The Winterstalk had been a challenging monster, sure. And she’d been a poor match for it when a battle mage or fire-attuned spellsword would have handled it better. And, on top of that, their strategy had been missing a few key elements.

So, after the three of them had written their first Patrol Report, Eola volunteered to drop it off. She took the stamp-printed form they’d spent nearly an hour deciphering and filling out, tucked it into her robe pocket, and headed for the library’s first floor.

Lieutenant-Librarian Fringe’s office sat behind a door Eola had never noticed opposite the locker chamber, just inside the library’s vestibule. She opened it, followed the long, marble-floored hallway past a few dozen doors—each with the name of a librarian and their rank on a brass placard—and knocked on the third from the end, on the left.

“Enter,” the gnome said.

Eola opened the door to an office that, in a word, she’d describe as minimal. No desk, just a four-legged kitchen table. A single bookshelf. Weapons and armor on racks in the corner. No art, no certificates, nothing on the walls—Fringe clearly wasn’t one for making himself at home. The only indication that it was his space came from the magenta-colored lizard sunning itself in a beam of artificial light, safely tucked away in a silver cage.

She sat at one of the two simple stools at the table. “The patrol to Shimmer Grove was successful. We encountered a Winterstalk that had been hunting the village’s goats, and were able to destroy it.”

The yellow gnome’s eyebrow raised fractionally, and Eola forced herself not to react as she continued. “However, we were too late to prevent any deaths. The week before we got there, one of the villagers was killed. Herder Ginger.”

“A danger of being outside the cities and holds,” Fringe said slowly, a tinge of genuine sadness in his voice. “I hope her spirit reaches the beyond.”

Eola waited a moment, then continued. “Lieutenant-Librarian Fringe, I have concerns.”

“State them.”

“First, how long ago did Varin’s Academy know about the Winterstalk? Would you leave a monster threatening a village until a patrol—“

“Absolutely not.” The Lieutenant-Librarian’s eyes narrowed slightly. “If there were no patrols, we’d gather a group of professors to take care of it. We heard about it two days before the three of you left Varin’s Academy. It would have taken two to rally the professors.”

“I’m sorry, sir,” Eola said quickly. “I had to be sure for…my own conscience.”

That wasn’t entirely true. Eola and her friends had done everything they could, and she didn’t feel guilty at all. But she also couldn’t help but think about her parents on the way back to Varin’s Academy. As far as she could tell from the simple letters she received from Seneschal Stoken Dahl, her parents were alive and could be rescued with a concerted effort. But no rescue attempt had ever been made. Hers would be the first, unless something changed.

It had been entirely possible that Varin’s Academy looked at Herder Ginger’s death the same way, and she’d had to know.

“Second, did you know about Winterstalk teeth? About how they freeze flesh as long as they’re embedded?” Eola poured the bag of still-freezing, bloody teeth onto the table.

To her surprise, Lieutenant-Librarian Fringe burst into laughter, then slowly contained it. “I’m impressed, Miss Lemiene. Most students figure out the truth about Patrol Duty by the third or fourth patrol, but you? You got it after one. Yes, I know about Winterstalk teeth. Where’d it get you?”

“The calf and thigh, on either side of my left knee. Roth had to cut some of them out of my leg, and they’re going to—“

“Leave a scar, right? That’s a good reminder for you, then.”

“Of what?”

“That you can’t learn everything you need to know.” The gnome grew serious. “Every Patrol Duty class is designed to do two things. First, what I said it was for—to protect others. The villages, or first-year students who’d stumble on things they couldn’t handle if you didn’t get there first. But second, it’s designed to teach you how to operate without complete information.”

“So, you almost killed me, then?”

“I did. And since you survived it and saw through it, you’re better-equipped to handle that uncertainty next time, aren’t you? And you’ll have a reminder of that fact.”

Eola’s eyes narrowed as she pressed on the raw wounds on her leg with a finger. They ached; Colin hadn’t been able to maintain his ritual while they walked, and when she’d inspected them, the skin around them glowed a dull red. The beginning of an infection. He’d have to treat her again tonight.

She gathered the Winterstalk’s teeth—leaving one behind on the center of the table—and handed their report to Fringe. Then she stood and left, letting the door click shut behind her.

The gnome was right. He was a rat and a clown, and Eola’s respect for him had plummeted in the last few minutes, but he was right. She’d be ready on their next patrol—and so would her friends.

Patrice wasn’t back.

That wasn’t a surprise, since her patrol had only started that morning, but it was disappointing. Eola sighed as she pulled her traveling clothes off, then unwound the bandages in her room. Yep. Infected. She’d talk to Colin soon.

Unfortunately, a more pressing matter sat on her desk.

“Stupid girl. You need to be more careful. If I’d been able to say something, I’d have warned you about the Winterstalk’s frigid poison,” Atta hissed as she arched her back. It had been a long walk back to Varin’s, the blue cat’s tail flicking against her shoulders, and Eola had braced herself for a scolding.

“I thought we handled ourselves well.”

“You think that? If you’d died, I’d be in real trouble.”

“That’s the kindest thing you’ve ever said to me,” Eola said absently. Then she stopped mid-poke, finger inches from the highest puncture wound. “Wait. You’d be in real trouble because you’re bound to me, so you’d vanish, right?”

Atta said nothing. She just stared, yellow eyes locked onto Eola’s. She didn’t blink, and after a moment, she licked her paw gingerly.

“Right. You’ve said all you can say.”

“Exactly.”

Eola groaned. “So, what now?”

“Now, you work on the notebook, like we both know should have been your priority all term to begin with.” Atta curled up, careful not to touch any of the papers they’d recovered from Varin’s notebook. “I’ll be here if you need me, but—“

“You can’t help anyway. I got it.” Eola groaned again, then reached for her copy of Introductory Orantic Dictation and its Shorthands. Instructor Tarik had been very clear that they weren’t supposed to read ahead, and that Extinct Tongues was a confusing enough class without making more work for themselves, but the second and third pages of Varin’s Notebook contained a linking phrase she was pretty sure was Orantic. It had the angular, overlapping look of it, at least.

Y’aer curse Varin for this confusing mess, anyway.

As she slowly translated the second and third pages of Varin’s notebook, flipping back and forth between Introductory Orantic Dictation and its Shorthands and the specific shorthand Varin had used, a coherent message began to emerge.

Archmage Frelanne believes my soul will never be strong enough to cast the spells she does.

Nevertheless, she keeps putting me through soul-strengthening exercises. According to her, it’s the only way forward. But as the days go by, I lose faith.

The next bit was written in Sothron. Eola sighed and added a long blank to her translation, then continued when it switched back to Orantic Dictation.

The reality’s setting in that, at best, I’ll be good for holding Mistress Frelanne’s hat and cloak while she sketches. I’m still working on that mark I copied from her casting. It’s clever. I’m not sure what it does, but it’s not a traditional modifying mark. Unfortunately, I can’t add it to my—

Eola puzzled over the next word—and the last before it switched to Forsooth Script—for a long time, but it wouldn’t translate. No matter what she did, the word had no equivalent in Introductory Orantic Dictation. She scratched Atta’s neck for a while as the cat purred in her sleep, then shut the book, piled the loose pages up in the right order, and thought for a while.

Varin had had Mana problems, just like she’d thought. Frelanne had been giving him instruction in expanding his soul, but it hadn’t been enough. Could the missing word be…

No. No, that was impossible. Eola laughed.

“What?” Atta asked, cracking an eye.

“Nothing. Only, I had this idea that Varin was just like me, but in reverse. Not enough Mana to cast spell-chains. That’d be ridiculous, though. This is Varin we’re talking about, and there’s no way he used spell-chains.” She smiled despite the growing pain around her left knee, then hiked up her robe. The smile changed to a worried frown. “How long was I working on that?”

“A couple of hours? You seemed focused, so I let you work, but it’s nearly dinner time.”

“I think I’m going to skip out on dinner tonight.”

“More reading?” Atta asked, brow rising.

Eola shook her head, staring at the pus oozing out of the bites again. “No. I’m going to need Colin’s help, though. He casts better rituals than I do, and these are definitely infected.”

The next day should have been a typical one, and Eola spent a long time dreading Spellsword Tactics.

Instead, Colin knocked on Eola’s door early, then opened it without waiting for her bleary-eyed response. “Nurse Tremory reporting in. Let’s see that leg of yours, Miss Lemiene.”

“Never call yourself that again,” Eola mumbled.

She tried to bury her head in the pillow, but Colin carefully pulled the blanket away, exposing her goosebump-covered leg. He shook his head slowly. “This is healing well, Eola.”

“Thank you,” she said. “That ritual last night was all it needed.”

“You could have cast it.”

“Sure. But you do rituals so much better than me, and it stayed running all night.” She pointed at the ceiling, where the ritual—though much diminished from its original strength—hung on doggedly, pumping healing Mana toward her leg. “I couldn’t have done that.”

“Have it your way.” Colin couldn’t help but grin, though. “I’ve got a note from Instructor Clearance exempting you from strenuous exercise for the next two days while you heal up. I told her about all the pus and red swelling last night, and she asked me way too many questions about your injury. I thought she’d—“

Eola held up a hand. “You went to Clearance last night?”

“Well, yeah. Of course I did. Your leg smelled like trogs-breath, Eola.”

“Thank you.”

Colin’s smile grew, and he turned a little red. “Anyway, I thought she’d come see, but when I told her about the Winterstalk, she just nodded like she knew something I didn’t and wrote that note.”

Eola accepted the note, then motioned for Colin to step out while she got herself dressed. The dozen or so scabs did look a lot better than the pus-oozing, half-scabbed archipelago of punctures, each surrounded by a sea of red, that she’d encountered the night before. Still, she was grateful when Colin wrapped a fresh set of bandages, each with a Soothing Sigil, over her lower thigh and upper calf.

They were almost to the door when it opened, and a beaming, sweat-covered Patrice barged in. “I’m back! You would not believe how good Ferrin is.”

“Yes, I would. I fought her,” Eola said quietly. “Did she get a chance to cast?”

“She sure did. She’s fast as lightning with her smallsword. Probably better than you, Eola.” Patrice scratched her head. “No, she’s definitely better than you. What’d you do to your knee?”

As they walked to breakfast—with Eola carefully keeping herself downwind of Patrice, who’d paused just long enough to shed her armor before joining her and Colin—they filled her in on their patrol to Shimmer Grove and the fight against the Winterstalk. Patrice winced sympathetically. “Sounds like a battle mage would’ve been a better match-up.”

“Yeah. Or if Colin had pre-cast his fire ritual, that might’ve changed the flow of battle, but we had no idea when the Winterstalk would show up, so that wasn’t an option. We went with the plan we thought would work best, and it worked…alright.” Eola lowered her voice as they passed another group of second-years in the hall. “Patrice, listen. Lieutenant-Librarian Fringe is holding back information on the monsters. He didn’t tell us about the freezing teeth. And when I confronted him about it, he laughed and said it was part of the training.”

Patrice’s brow furrowed. “I’ll let Jeanette know. Thanks. Should we spread it around?”

Eola pursed her lips. On the one hand, telling the others would inevitably get back to Fringe, and he’d probably have something to say about it. And it’d help Catrine—who Eola wouldn’t have imagined helping under other circumstances. But on the other hand, these weren’t training duels, or even a duel of honor. Lives could be at stake, all for the purpose of teaching a lesson. She nodded. “We’ll do it quietly, though. Wait until Third-Day, then spread it to a few people who’ll take credit?”

“That way it doesn’t come back on us? Clever, Eola.” Patrice laughed.

Breakfast was porridge. Not the apple-chunk kind, sadly. Today’s fruit seemed to be a slimy, ground-up peach or apricot or something. Eola couldn’t tell by looking. She scooped up a spoonful, sucked it into her mouth, and shivered. “Oh, that’s gross. It tastes amazing, but the texture is…it’s not something I want in my porridge.”

Colin raised an eyebrow. “Are you sure you’re not feverish, Eola? You’re complaining about food, and you didn’t eat dinner last night.” He reached for her forehead, and she ducked.

“I’m fine. Really.” Eola kept eating, doing her best to mash the peach chunks into something unrecognizable as she did. And as she did, her mind shifted to Patrice.

And to Patrice’s problem.

~Patrice Clerk~

Patrice saw the look in Eola’s eyes.

Y’aer help her, she was about to ask about magic again. How many times were they going to have this conversation? How many times could she survive this conversation?

It was going to be the same cursed thing. Another idea that went nowhere. Another waste of time attempt at fixing her stupid attunement, and another breakdown of the barrier between who Patrice was with a hammer and who she was with her magic. Eola was a meddler—and worse, she was a meddler who’d been empowered by Instructor Tarik. All she had to do was blame him, and she could dig into anything she wanted.

“Can we go to the cells soon, Patrice?” Eola asked.

“Y’aer curse you, Eola,” Patrice muttered.

“Sorry, what was that?”

“Nothing. Only, I don’t think it’ll help at all.”

“I’m not so sure about that. I’ll need to work on a spell-chain, though.” A bit of pulped peach dripped from Eola’s spoon. She looked down at it, distaste written across her pursed lips. “There was a spell Madame Reyanna taught us last year, and if I can figure it out, it might be helpful for seeing exactly what’s going on with you.”

Patrice couldn’t help it. Y’aer, she hated herself for hoping, but she still hoped. Not that Eola would figure it out, but that she’d find something actionable. Maybe if she could control even her Child’s Magic, Patrice could…

Well, Patrice could do a lot of things with just Child’s Magic. She could learn how to cast like Eola, for one thing. It’d be slow, and she was pretty sure her soul was smaller than her friend’s—mostly because Eola’s soul was absolutely gigantic. But she could do it. How hard could it possibly be?

“Which spell?” Colin asked as they carried their trays. Eola’s bowl was still half full, and she moved with an obvious limp.

“It’s called Inner Vision. I think I can set up the spell-chain tonight, then use it tomorrow, but we’ll want to be in the cells so Patrice can cast for me.” Eola kept talking and talking about her idea. “I think there’s something in how the Phantasm in the library fixated on you, as well, and I’d like to try that, too.”

Patrice tuned her out. She’d gone through enough tutors, doctors, Cultists, and Y’aer only knew what else to know how this usually ended. She had hope, yes, but—

“I’ll see you two at lunch?” Colin asked. He looked nervous—Eola obviously hadn’t picked up on it, but he couldn’t stop fidgeting with the hem of his robe. Patrice focused in on it.

“Sure,” Eola said.

“Great.” Colin reached out a hand, hesitated, and then—

Patrice saw it coming an instant before it happened. He leaned down. His lips met Eola’s. And, for the first time Patrice could remember, her two most awkward friends kissed properly.

Then Colin fled down the hall, face red, leaving Eola staring at nothing, a blush ripping across her face and her eyes wide.

And Patrice burst into laughter.
