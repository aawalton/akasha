Nineteen Thousand Years Ago

As much as the God of Thunder hated to admit it, that old bitch was right.

He couldn’t ascend. Not like Stormlord Nalthelas had. There was something about how he’d watched his fellow pupils die—scared, but still hoping that the elf mage would keep his promise to them, that he wouldn’t need all their strength, that they’d still enter a new universe by his side—that felt…wrong. Viscerally, horrifically wrong.

But at the same time, ascension was there. It felt just out of reach. And his portal world, with its massive pillars ready to be filled with Mana, had been unwittingly designed to help him get one step closer.

The God of Thunder didn’t have a stomach. He subsisted on nothing but pure energy, and always had. But even so, the thought of…harvesting…beings’ cores made him sick. What Stormlord Nalthelas had done in his lightning lab was unforgivable. The God of Thunder didn’t understand exactly why, though.

He pondered the pressure on his core. It was almost mild, but relentless. He hadn’t been free of it in years—not since the moment he’d pushed to SS-Rank just before his assault on the elf mage’s lightning lab. It was a constant companion, and with every victory, every skill’s level-up, it increased just the smallest amount. It was, according to the Crone, the true price of power. To step beyond what the universe could tolerate was to stand against the universe itself.

Could that pressure turn him into Stormlord Nalthalas?

No.

He wouldn’t let it.

Simple.

But at the same time, ascension. He wanted it. No. He needed it. That was all that truly mattered. Once he was beyond the barrier between universes, and the door had closed behind him, nothing he did here would matter. More importantly, those two voices—the one screaming at him to kill and kill until there was nothing left, and the one whispering seductively about a newer, bigger universe—would be gone. That would be worth anything.

His stomach roiled.

Anything but what Stormlord Nalthalas had done.

The answer was simple, but it took the God of Thunder a long, long time to arrive at it. The true horror of Stormlord Nalthalas’s lightning lab hadn’t been that he’d harvested cores. That was the natural order of things. Monsters consumed other monsters’ cores after their battles. Delvers stole cores from monsters. The hunters became the hunted, then returned to hunting.

It was the lies. The manipulation. And worse than that, the scale. The Stormlord hadn’t been a predator. He didn’t understand. But the God of Thunder had been one, and he knew about the relationship between predator and prey. There was something intimate about it—almost as close as when that ancient, A-Ranked eel Paragon opened her core to him for bonding. Nalthalas’s operation had sought to pervert that relationship. He had killed dozens of his pupils at once. But how to avoid that?

Once again, the answer was simple. The God of Thunder wasn’t in a rush, after all. He’d take only a single pupil at a time. An interesting one—one who sparked his curiosity enough that he’d forget, for a moment, what the student’s inevitable fate would be. He’d help that student become the strongest they could be, and then, when they reached the higher reaches of power, he’d tell them the truth.

The truth. Three days. And a hunt.

They’d die, of course, but they’d die like prey, not like livestock. There was honor in that.

And there would be honor for him, too.

Stormlord Nalthalas’s aura pressed down on us, a wave of static that smashed through Ellen’s magic and my best attempts to resist it. The Thunder King didn’t bother. Even before the elf’s aura hit him, he was already bent down, his lightning-columned body bowing like he had a hinge built in.

I braced myself. Like the Placid King, Stormlord Nalthalas was on the cusp of ascension. And, in a way, we were on his home turf. This was a fight we’d struggle to survive, much less win.

But though the elf Paragon’s cloak fluttered against my leg as he walked past me, he didn’t seem to notice any of our presences. The blue-black cloak parted, and a thin arm reached out, hand open to receive the core. His skin was twisted and scarred from countless spiderwebbing burns that crossed it, and it shook slightly.

“My lord, this core is only A-Rank. Will it be enough?” the Thunder King asked.

For a brief second, the Paragon’s brow creased in either a frown or a glare. From my angle, I couldn’t tell. I tried to creep forward, but the sheer static in the air was all but overwhelming. “It will suffice. We must hurry, though. The Stormstrike Path reaches its end, my favored pupil, and from there? From there, only ascension awaits.”  His voice shook, and it was little more than a whisper.

The core dropped into Stormlord Nalthalas’s hand, and as he turned away from the Thunder King, it crumpled into nothingness. At the same time, the Stormlord stood straighter. His muscles filled out beneath his skin, and his empty, dark eyes burned with red-orange sparks.

“Yes, my favored disciple, you’ve done well. Please continue to acquire more cores.” It was phrased as a request, but the Thunder King only bowed more deeply, as if he’d been ordered to follow through. Stormlord Nalthalas paused. His eyes roved around the room, and I caught my breath. They lingered on the stairs. “And, if you would, do something about the intruders. We ascend tomorrow.”

Sarah gasped.

Terrel dropped his linked axes and hefted the massive warhammer he’d strapped to his back.

Ellen started casting. So did I.

And the Stormlord vanished in a crack of thunder.

Lightning struck. Blue-white and Green-black bolts filled the room. They split the air in the sprawling, suddenly tiny-feeling butchery floor. My ears rang as thunder echoed from every wall, turning the battlefield into the inside of a bass drum.

Through the center of the storm, the Thunder King straightened to his full height. The column of blue-white lightning cracked and sparked. Arcs reached out to catch every bolt in the sky, pulling them in to his body as he consumed the magic and seemed to grow taller.

Then he spoke. “Stormlord Nalthalas has commanded your deaths. I will—“

A massive hammer ripped through his body. Sparks flew, and he went silent. At the same time, Terrel Young’s armor lit up. A ball of lightning covered him, and the air filled with the stink of burning flesh. But that wasn’t enough to stop the Portal Tyrant—and it wasn’t enough to stop me, either.

Thunderlord. I attacked quickly, the Storm’s Blade darting forward to put two small holes in the Thunder King’s pillar-shaped body. Then I used Stormfall. The Shockwaves on my next three lightning-quick attacks rippled across the Thunder King’s surface.

Ellen’s magic sliced square-shaped chunks off the Thunder King’s body. The Shadow Cell went to work, and she echoed it with Arcane Resonator, punching even more damage in. Then she dropped a Black Hole behind the S-Ranked monster as she moved between the Thunder King and both Sarah and Terrel.

I grinned despite the chaos—or maybe because of it. This was my element. Fighting against a powerful monster. And Ellen wasn’t afraid anymore. Not of pain. She was shielding the healer. I moved to join her. Sarah’s magic rose into a crescendo behind us, and a moment later, Terrel leaped over both of us, gigantic hammer slamming down on—

“No.”

It was the Thunder King’s voice. But it wasn’t. This one was larger. More powerful. The God of Thunder had, however momentarily, intervened.

The word echoed through the room. Magic froze. So did the Thunder King, and all of us.

A familiar aura flickered across the portal world for a moment. Then it vanished, leaving the room intact and the Thunder King rifling through a corpse’s ribcage as he searched for an A-Rank core.

I blinked, sword frozen mid-air, inches from the Thunder King’s pillar-shaped body. “What.”

No one said anything for a moment as we waited for the Thunder King to react. Nothing. He kept working, and after a moment, reached into the carcass and came away with a small core. “Another core for you, Stormlord Nalthalas.”

For a moment, I had no idea what to do. Then Ellen pointed back at the stairs, where no blood dripped down from the carnage we’d created above. “Come on,” she hissed. “I know what’s going on.”

We retreated halfway up the stairs, to a landing where we could press against the wall as Stormlord Nalthalas hurried by, and waited until it was silent.

Then Ellen cleared her throat. “Okay, so. Uh. Remember the Ghostdream? How the Crone used Mind Maiden Enolda as a sort of enforcer presence to handle intruders? It’s a pretty typical behavior for Ghostdream portals, from the few encounters we’ve had. But whatever’s going on in the God of Thunder’s Ghostdream is wildly different from anything anyone else has seen.

“My theory is that the God of Thunder’s subconscious is preserving something about this memory. That it’s core to his existence and his sense of being. He doesn’t have an enforcer like Mind Maiden Enolda, because unlike the Crone, he values these memories too highly to entrust them to a party outside his control. So…”

I waited. Ellen’s eyes were closed, and her brow wrinkled as she tried to decide how to push forward. Terrel cleared his throat after maybe ten seconds. “So, who do we kill?”

“Well, that’s the thing, isn’t it?” Ellen said quietly. “We can’t kill anything meaningful here.”

“We can’t kill anything meaningful?” Terrel asked. “Then what’s the point of us all being here?”

“To figure out what’s so important to the God of Thunder that he’d forge an entire Ghostdream to protect it,” I said.

Something moved below us, and the Thunder King began climbing the stairs. He moved slowly, but smoothly, his lightning-pillar body seeming to slide across the ceramic steps, and when he reached the landing, he turned and continued upward. For a brief, crazy moment, I wondered why he didn’t just turn into a dragon and fly.

Then I looked at the grid of portal metal and copper beams and shook my head. Flying here would be incredibly difficult. “Come on. Let’s follow him,” I said.

The Thunder King climbed slowly upward. As he did, we trailed behind. The maze of portal metal and copper only grew thicker as we climbed, until, by the time we’d reached what felt like the top of the Hall of Thunder, there was only a small gap for someone to walk through, and the rest was a tangled rat’s nest of metal. At the same time, the ceramic walls seemed to grow thicker and to push in more. Part of me wanted to understand the device as a whole—especially when another pulse of thunder and lightning sent unfathomable volts of electricity coursing down the four lightning rods at the structure’s corners.

But the rest of me realized it didn’t matter.

All that mattered was following the Thunder King and not disrupting the memory so we could see exactly what happened.

The God of Thunder stepped through a door almost as massive as the one far below, then faded from view.

So did the rest of the world around us.

When it returned, we stood at the end of a long, narrow room. At the far end, a copper and portal metal cage arced up into a dome-like space. Stormlord Nalthelas stood in its center, adjusting bronze needles and an array of tiny lightning rods around him. The cage—and the conduits connecting it to dozens of unrecognizable devices up against the wall—flowed and arced in that beautiful elven style. Lightning surged into the cage from every conduit.

I knew exactly what I was seeing. The Stormlord was attempting to ascend.

Those weren’t the only conduits, either. Even more tubes of copper and portal metal extended outward toward us, stopping periodically at the border of a similar cage. There were dozens. And in every cage was a Paragon.

A man, eight feet tall or more, with crackling skin the color of burnt paper. A ghostly gray woman whose body seemed made of cloud. Eel-like beings coursing with electricity, and at least two other pillars of lightning—one green-black and one red-orange. And others, their forms obscured by the cages surrounding them.

Every one of them was S-Rank or higher. And every one of them was trapped.

Stormlord Nalthelas began his ritual.

Lightning surged outward from him, then was stopped by the cage he’d placed himself inside. More and more power drained from the Hall of Thunder as he channeled it into himself. But even that wasn’t enough. The sheer power needed to force a path between worlds open, according to the Crone, was more than any Paragon could hold within them for long. It was more than the Stormlord could store in a building like this. He’d need an entire world dedicated to the task.

An entire world, or every minion he had worth spending the time on.

Cultists ran from cage to cage, finishing partially-constructed magic spells, and even more power poured into Stormlord Nalthelas as his former pupils screamed in defiance at his betrayal. I felt for them—but I felt for one voice more than the rest.

The Thunder King was trapped in one of those cages, too.

As the power entering Stormlord Nalthelas’s body reached its peak, his skin glowed red-orange. Then thunder cracked. His cage exploded outward as energy rippled across the room. His core emptied completely, and high above him, a sliver of flickering nothingness opened. His own power vanished instantly, but the machine simply continued to work.

A core snapped, and a wail of agony echoed across the room.

The sliver opened a fraction of an inch wider.

And the Thunder King raged against his cage next to us as Stormlord Nalthelas began to ascend without him.

But none of that mattered. The Stormlord’s ascension had to have failed, because the God of Thunder was still alive. This was a memory. It was core to the thunder dragon’s being, but painful enough to keep sequestered in a Ghostdream of his own design. A critical moment was about to happen—or perhaps it already had.

Either way, I set myself to the task of learning from the God of Thunder’s memories.
