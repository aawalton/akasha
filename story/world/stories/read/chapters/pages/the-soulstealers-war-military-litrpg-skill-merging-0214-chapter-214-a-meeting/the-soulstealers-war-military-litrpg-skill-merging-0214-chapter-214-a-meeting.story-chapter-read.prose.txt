When we reached the surface, our arms were full. Sage, Anchor, and Brawl carried as much wood as they could muster. As for me, I carried the corpse of an Ashdeer.

It wasn’t exactly a monster, but it had found its way into the Labyrinth nonetheless. We’d found it drinking from a lava canal, licking mineral deposits off the surface with its enhanced tongue. Its hide was infused with the ash of the Labyrinth’s lower level, giving it its distinctive gray colour, and it had the perfect texture for a canvas, even without any modifications.

And, of course, the Rawhide was strong on its own. It had taken one of Anchor’s resonance Skills to get through it. Once Drywsone enhanced it, it would probably be even stronger.

We brought them to the blacksmith shop and set the loot down in front of the counter. Drywsone was busy, but Shatter rushed over to greet us.

“Good haul today,” he chirped.

“You’re in excellent spirits,” I replied. “Yes, though. A good haul. Probably one of the last massive resource haul as things start ramping up. The next few weeks, we’re gonna just be focusing on getting Presences.”

“Well, we should be able to get started on the shields. That wood is tough and heat resistant, and the same goes for the hide. They should last for a little while, at least.”

“Sir Levi?” a different, non-Dupe apprentice inside the smithy called. “I don’t mean to be a bother, sir. There was a man around here asking for you. I’ve never seen him before, but the few remaining men on the gate watch let him in. It must be important.”

“Where’d he go?”

“Toward the keep.”

I glanced at my squad, then said, “I’ll catch up later. And I’ll be back soon.”

Then I took off, racing through the castle grounds. When I reached the keep, I looked around for anyone I didn’t recognize. While I didn’t know everyone on the regular staff’s names, I knew their faces and the way they walked, even subconsciously. My Focus helped with that, and even though my memories were still jumbled, my subconscious knew when someone looked out of place.

When I reached the library inside the keep, I stopped. A man stood in the center aisle, holding a book by its spine and leafing through the pages. He wore a bright yellow gambeson, but when I used my Perception skill, I detected no arcane weight from him. He was just an average mortal man.

I didn’t recognize him, but those were Hinlaw’s colours.

“Who are you, and what do you want with me?” I demanded, taking a prepared stance. He probably wouldn’t attack, but this was Hinlaw we were dealing with. He’d used dirty tricks before.

“I am here at my lord’s bidding,” the man said, turning to me. “It was said that you might be in the library, if you weren’t in your room.”

“What do you want?” I repeated.

“My Lord Hinlaw wishes to make you one final offer.” The man smiled in the way a tired customer service worker would. He held out his hands in a calm gesture. “I am merely his messenger, Sir Levi.”

“I hear it’s tradition to behead a rival’s messenger if you don’t like what he says.”

“I hear you are not one for our traditions, soulstealer,” the messenger countered. “My lord’s offer is this: if you surrender your thegnage, step down from the seat of power in Hyrdford, and abandon all ambitions of nobility, he will spare your life and your reputation. If not…he will spread the most vile rumours about you, which you will not be able to escape.”

“Such as?”

“Perhaps that you were improper with the princess, and that you stained her honour. Shall I elaborate?”

“She wouldn’t accuse me of anything.” I crossed my arms.

“She doesn’t have to.”

“People won’t just believe that,” I said. “They’re not—”

“Oh, but they will. Hinlaw has done this before, and he can do it again.”

“What then? I get executed?”

The messenger chuckled. “They would only be rumours, Sir Levi. However, my lord’s network of whisperers is quite extensive, and his people are primed to believe it. They already believe you to be terribly unfair and dishonourable to the peaceful Blood Tear Order.”

“They are called the Blood Tear Order. Blood. Tear.”

“It’s just a name. There are many Orders with unfortunate names—a relic of a more barbarous past. The power of my lord’s rumours to assuage concerns like that is immense.”

I scowled.

“Your villagers will not go unharmed,” the messenger continued. “You have, after all, insulted a protected Order. And should I mention the Warlord’s new decree? Fearmongering about the existence of the Dark Lord will not be tolerated. The Riftforges aren’t burning. The raids on the south are merely bands of disgruntled orcs that the locals are handling poorly.”

“You know that’s not true.”

“I follow only the will of my lord, and he bade me to bring you this news. Hinlaw would be within his rights to attempt to reclaim a rogue thegn’s territory by force—especially after said thegn dishonoured him so deeply at the trial-joust. None of these things on their own are enough, but together, they pose a problem for you.”

My stomach dropped. A few baseless rumours could sting, but they couldn’t ruin me. But could I just let someone hurt my people? “Is his army in position, then, to attack?”

“Perhaps.”

“If it was, you would’ve included that in your threats,” I said. “So Hinlaw hasn’t attacked yet, I figure. And it’ll still take time for him to muster a force.”

The messenger was silent.

I stepped forward, eyes narrow. “Tell him to shove his little decrees and rumours up his ass. Those words precisely.”

The messenger blinked.

“Now get out of my sight before I decide to follow your world’s traditions a little more closely.”

~ ~ ~

As soon as the messenger left, I rushed back to my room and picked up my communication chained plants.

The connection was weak. Some of them had died with the snow, and my calls had grown more and more infrequent because of how difficult it was to contact Hyrdford anymore. The connection probably wouldn’t survive another week. The sapling’s leaves were all gone, and the only thing that still vibrated were its branches.

Still, I said, “Cuthward. Hardric. Can anyone hear me? Is there anyone there? Someone, pick up, pick up…”

I went on for a few minutes, simply rambling, making sure a noise continued to transmit through the plant.

Finally, Cuthward’s voice shrivelled through the plant’s vibrating branches. In a crackly whisper, he said, “Levi, what is it?”

“Something’s coming. Hinlaw is sending an army of some kind.”

Cuthward paused. “Are you certain?”

“He sent a messenger to threaten me.”

“I assume you told him to go grind water?”

“I— Uh, yeah, something like that.”

Cuthward inhaled sharply. I probably wouldn’t have picked up on it without my enhanced Perception, but the branches of my plant gave a faint rattle, simulating the noise. “He won’t have a massive standing army of his own. His militia would be a mixture of mortal city guards and Dupes, located in Steelshore. If he’s sending an army to crush us, though, I would suspect he would use the cultists. They would have to muster from far and wide, unless his force was already prepared.”

I pressed my tongue against the roof of my mouth. “He didn’t sound like he had his force ready.”

“That is what he wants you to think, perhaps.”

“But why would he warn me at all?”

“As far as I’ve seen, Hinlaw always takes the easy way out. Perhaps he underestimated you.”

I scowled. “That doesn’t make sense. He’s seen me. He knows I wouldn’t…just surrender, right?”

“I wouldn’t be so certain that he sees that in you. He has seen you been knighted, which saved you on the chopping block. He saw you target Sir Peofrith with your challenge—a seemingly easy target. He saw you defeat his son. From what he knows, you would surrender if threatened enough. Perhaps your public appearance is important to you, he thinks. That is how he threatened you as well, correct? He has spent his time cultivating lies about you while probing your defenses.”

I gulped. “Correct.”

“We will prepare for the worst. Should he have a force ready, it will take them four weeks or so to march from Steelshore to Hyrdford. If not, it will take longer to muster one. Now, I don’t have time. Our new portreeve is calling for me, and he has been quite displeased with his apparent lack of power here. It’s amusing, but I’ve been keeping him placated as best I can.”

“Good luck. Make sure the city guard is ready. And do your best to contact the Greencloaks.”

I didn’t catch a response from Cuthward. The line cut off.

That was probably the last time I’d speak with him. Four weeks, or eight weeks. Either way, this third phase would last about six weeks. The city could fall under attack before my combat training was up.

Should I go?

I couldn’t miss out on my training. The defenses would hold for a while—not all the cultists were powerful, and my new local guards were well-trained. Once the third phase was over, we would head west anyway, and I could stop by Hyrdford on the way to Steelshore.

~ ~ ~

When Alis’ next letter arrived later that day, I was ready. I threw open the window to our room in the barracks, let Croak in, then plucked the rolled up scroll off his foot. “Good boy,” I said.

I read the letter as quickly as I could.

Dear Levi,

I don’t know if I understand your misgivings about a monarch. It is the way things have always been done here, and the way we have always conducted our rule. However, I will admit, I find it rather amusing and cute.

I stand by my suggestion, however. Perhaps it comes from selfishness. Yes, I would be a queen. I would also choose a man I feel is fit to rule. But you have read the Great Charter, as I understand. The duty of the king is to his people first and foremost, and it is a duty. If we do not wield this power, someone else will. That ‘someone else’ will oppose us. We can wield it for good, and then when we have saved the world, then we can worry about the role of royalty.

The Great Charter is an oath. The king swears the main clauses, and it is a Path Oath. The Warlord does not swear it, and it is not binding in the same way. That is why it is important we have a king.

A tyrant serves only himself. A king serves his people with utmost certainty.

My father is dying. He has a year left, and even that is perhaps an overestimation. However, I will not be free until I pick a suitor.

Whoever I pick must be a man fit to be king. And should the Warlord fall, that man would rule the nation. I must pick wisely, and I will delay as long as possible, so I can make the best possible decision—and so hopefully, the Warlord cannot meddle any longer.

As I understand, you will be heading to Steelshore in a few weeks for your last phase of training. It would be unwise to try to contact you there; a flash-raven would be spotted and caught before it reached you, and there are too many unfriendly eyes in that city. Since I cannot guarantee when I will have the ability to write another letter, this will be my last communication until I am certain that you have escaped Steelshore.

However, the ribbon fastened to this letter resonates with the Path in a frequency only detectable by ravens such as Croak. As long as you wear it, he will be able to find you again.

Wishing you the best,

Alis

I read the letter, grimaced, then nodded. Immediately, I began drafting up a reply. It read:

Dear Alis,

I’ve donned your ribbon as a bracelet. Croak seems very possessive of it, and I’m thinking it will take plenty of treats to convince him to let me keep it.

I concede the point on monarchy. I won’t let my otherworldly sensibilities stand in the way of saving this world. If that is what must be done, then so be it. We can sort it out in the aftermath.

I hope you still have faith in me, despite this. I promise you, I’ll do everything in my power to continue fighting, whether you believe in me or not.

Whenever you feel that it’s safe, send your next letter. I’m looking forward to it.

Still, I suppose I won’t hear from you until after I’ve defeated Hinlaw. Your next letter will either reach an Ealdorman or a dead man.

Kind regards,

Levi

I paused when I reached the end of the letter. At first, I almost crumpled it up and tried again. I almost told her about the dream I’d had, but I didn’t think that would come across right. It could wait until I got back. I tapped my quill on the edge of the page, then shook the letter to dry it, before rolling it up.

I handed the letter to Croak, fed him a double helping of treats, and finally sent him on his way.
