I gazed skyward as the three Fae drank and laughed around me. They paid me no mind and did not include me in any further conversation.

That was good. I could barely manage my own thoughts, much less a coherent sentence.

The gods were Fae. The realms were sealed by Fae. Runes are us calling for help… from Fae.

But not the creatures I’ve heard about my whole life. Not monsters. Good Fae. Ones who watch us from an entire world away and lend their power when we need it.

A good Fae. Just a few weeks ago, the idea would have been laughable. Then tonight I’d met a satyr who wanted to buy my freedom, a nymph who claimed to be a friend to the daughters of men, and a lethian who’d starve rather than hurt a human.

And the figure from my dream… he was good. I knew it to my marrow. Whatever else was true of him, he had been good.

And he died, but an echo of him is still alive in his broken sword. And when he died, or perhaps because he died, the rest of his Court stopped answering runekeepers. They’ve stopped sharing magic, stopped caring about us.

But… not him.

‘This vow did not end in death.’

A tide of questions drowned out the mellifluous voices around me. One hand slid to a pocket and my fingers closed around the silver mirror. I wanted, more than anything, to try and call up his face. To find strength in unseeing twin dawns while I fought for clarity.

What did he vow? Was it to keep humans safe? Was it to protect us even after he died? To keep answering our calls for help? But then, why does he only answer mine? Why does he give me magic, and not anyone else?

Why does it hurt?

It all blurred through my mind as the stars blurred above. Eventually Velidan ordered me to go get more wine, shaking me from my stupor.

Time crept on in a dreamlike haze. Velidan often turned the conversation to questions about tomorrow. It wasn’t hard to see what he was doing. Every new pitcher of drinks fanned my hopes that the other Fae would simply reveal all their evil plans in a drunken stupor.

Sadly, they did not. Embriel remained level-headed and answered every question with a sharp smile and a murmur of, ‘I don’t want to spoil the surprise, Vel.’

The talk fell to things I didn’t fully understand; politics, the states of regions within the Night Court, conflicts among lesser Fae, and how various species were doing. Occasionally they snarled about more powerful, predatory monsters lurking throughout the realm and what would need to be done about them.

Saffine quickly began to slosh and slurr, spilling wine upon the stones and gossiping about other High Fae women. I didn’t understand much of that, either. From what I gathered, organizing reconstruction efforts over tea was incredibly boring, they never wanted to do anything fun, and someone named Alora hated her.

They had food brought up alongside the second pitcher. Velidan’s meal mirrored mine, while the other two ate bowls of something vaguely stew-like. Charred meat and sweet-smelling plants swam in dark, gelatinous broth. It looked appetizing until Embriel bit a mushroom in half and blood oozed from its flesh.

When the endless black above paled to gray and the stars were fading behind steely clouds, the Fae finally left. Velidan watched with an easy smile that vanished as soon as they were out of sight.

He turned a thoughtful gaze towards me, braced both forearms on the table, and spent a long while searching my face. “This was an unfortunate end to the evening.”

I didn’t disagree.

“The cook here makes excellent desserts.” He sounded distinctly annoyed. “I was going to have you try a variety, but we need to leave soon.”

I blinked at him. That was possibly the last thing I’d expected to hear. “You… could have ordered them earlier?”

His expression clearly said that was a ridiculous idea.

“Oh. Right.” I dragged both hands through my hair and sighed, glancing towards the stairs. “I suppose letting me eat chocolate or something in front of your friends would be absurd.”

He chuckled. “I have no idea what chocolate is. And I don’t consider either of those individuals friends, but I’m pretending to support their conquest of your species. Feeding you desserts hardly fits the image.”

My lips thinned. I spent a moment tracing the jagged, sharp-edged cracks along the table, then said uncertainly, “You promised I wouldn’t be hurt.”

“You weren’t.”

“He threw me on the table.”

“Very gently, and I made him stop. You’re welcome, by the way.”

“You-!” I bolted upright so fast the chair nearly fell over, then spent a moment grinding my teeth. “Fine. You made him stop. What if you hadn’t?”

“I imagine he would’ve enjoyed watching you squirm and beg while we drank.” A pitiless gaze was leveled up at me. “That was just a predator sniffing a piece of meat, Brin, and seeing if anything else showed its teeth.”

I gaped at him. Horror and impotent indignance swelled to a bottleneck that made me want to throw one of his salted wineglasses. “I’m not a piece of-”

“You are. In this realm, and in such company, you are exactly that.” He let the statement sink in, then added with an exaggerated eye-roll, “If it helps, most of the other High Fae will ignore you.” His nose wrinkled. “And one will probably spend the day berating me for mistreating you.”

“Good,” I grumbled, settling back into the chair. “Someone should.”

Rich laughter echoed off the stones.

My brow furrowed and my mouth twisted to one side. “And what about this other person? Garruk?”

All traces of mirth died. “You are not to go near him.”

“Saints, obviously! But what’s to stop him from coming near me?!”

He swept a hand up and down, indicating the whole of himself.

“Right. Great.” I sighed. Quiet settled between us as I curled my knees up and rested my chin on them. Below us, the vibrant music and voices of the market were falling to a sparser hum. One finger absently traced zellen on the marble and I murmured, “Was all of that true?”

Endless eyes followed the movement. “Every word. Your version of history teaches that rune magic is divine strength gifted from a deity. It’s not. It’s just some soft-hearted Fae answering a human’s plea.”

I inhaled slowly, letting the scents of cool ivy and bittersweet wine fill my throat. Pale lavender smoke curled about along the wall to one side and a few wisps fluttered by in bright, lazy trails.

I spent a while taking it all in, then gave him a helpless little smile. “You know, if you’d told me all this a moon ago I wouldn’t have believed any of it.”

Full lips twitched. “A moon ago, you wouldn’t have heard a word I said. You’d have been too busy running and screaming.”

“Heh. Maybe.”

He cocked his head to one side. “You’re taking tonight’s revelations quite well, all things considered. I’ve essentially shattered your understanding of the world.”

My chest swelled with another steadying breath. “I… I suppose I might panic later. I don’t know. It… seems silly, but I almost feel like it doesn’t matter. Not really.”

Black brows drew together.

I began to pace. My boots made little taps against the stone and both hands wrung together as I parsed it all out. “I spent my whole life thinking there were gods who loved me. Now you’ve told me that they weren’t gods, they were monsters! But what I know about monsters is wrong, too. Some of them aren’t even monsters at all! They’re kind and noble and… and one of them died trying to save us.” The world around me blurred. “He wasn’t a fool. I don’t care what you say. Not about that, at least. You’re wrong, and it’s just that simple.” I sniffled, ignoring the mocking huff of laughter from one side. “I’m alive because he chose to fight for us. Durst, Marion, Clem… all of them. He saved us. You mocked that woman, too. The lethian. Saints, but she’s… she’s… can you imagine how brave she must be? How strong? I’d like to come back in the future, if that’s alright. I don’t know how often she needs to… er, eat, but I’d like to help her.”

He didn’t answer. A quick wipe of my eyes and a glance over showed a familiar sneer. “You’re confusing stupidity with strength, Brin.”

“And you’re wrong, but that’s alright.” My fingers slid through ivy as I watched the throng below. Many stalls and tents were being packed away. “And the rest of it… the part about runes. I thought my magic was from the gods, but I suppose it doesn’t really matter, does it? When a runekeeper asks for water or fire or light, is it important who answers? If whoever it is cares enough to try and help, what does it matter if they’re a Fae?”

The words sounded very strange, but voicing them aloud made a curious, comforting calm settle over me. Like a heavy cloak draped across my shoulders. “I… I am a little confused, though. Why are you so sure it’s the shard giving me magic? Or… the Fae who made it, rather. The echo of him. Why do you think it’s that, and not someone else?”

Silk swished as his large frame settled beside me, elbows braced on the stone. “Faer’yll Illyrium, the manipulation of a soul, can only be done willingly. If it was another Fae helping you, your rune this morning would have done nothing. Instead, it had its maker’s will behind it.”

I spent a moment pondering that. Then, in a very small voice as one finger traced faer, “So why does he hurt me, then?”

“Some Fae can inflict pain by sharing power.” A frustrated bite sharpened the next words. “But for someone from the Day Court to be doing it, and to you, of all creatures, is… insanity.”

“Me, of all creatures? What does that mean?”

“Well, a few hours ago you were worried about a bee.”

“Oh, I’d nearly forgotten.” I pressed a hand to my mouth. “I hope he’s alright.”

He fixed me with narrow-eyed exasperation. “You also apologized for reliving frightening events, tried to defend a harpy who would have gladly plucked your eyes out, and spent most of your time in an enchanted marketplace shopping for other people. Until you found the mirror, at least.”

“The mirror’s for Durst, actually. His wife passed away years ago. He misses her terribly and I think he’d… I think it would mean a lot.”

“Of course it’s for someone else.” He practically growled it at me, sounding as though he was fighting anger and amusement in equal measure. “That only further proves my point.”

“Which is?”

“You’re the last creature a kind Fae would harm.” Velidan shook his head. “It’s unintentional. It has to be. I doubt he even realizes he’s hurting you. And why you specifically, I can’t fathom. But when you use a rune, he answers by blindly throwing power at a mortal body not meant to withstand it.”

There were a hundred things I could’ve said in response to that. I settled for a quiet, vaguely stricken, “Oh.”

“My thoughts exactly.”

My face puckered. “But… there have been times the shard’s been far away. Really far, like when I gave it to Renner or when Baltha…” I swallowed. “When I summoned you. It was miles away. How could it have known I needed to use luridel or… or yours? How could it have… saints, sent me power?”

Velidan considered all that, then spread both hands. “Perhaps you’re a beacon for it, in some way. Perhaps it’s somehow able to watch you from afar. Perhaps there’s some kind of magical tether between you both.”

“That’s a lot of perhapses.”

“This is all guesswork on an unprecedented situation.” He snorted. “I told you, Brin. You’re a mystery in more ways than one.”

“It feels like a lot of the world is a mystery, tonight.” My gaze flicked upwards. “Or today, rather. Can I ask you something else?”

“I have yet to figure out how to stop you.”

“You said zellen means hive, or swarm, and you knew what faer meant. You said you recognized some of the others, too.”

He went very still, then made a thoughtful sound in the back of his throat.

I plucked at the ivy. “I… I’ve been thinking about my mom a lot tonight. About how she knew what all my runes do. Even y-yours.” Saying that aloud made my voice hitch. I spent a moment steadying myself, then whispered haltingly, “She… said the last rune in my book is… the last thing she s-saw of me. El’phial. Do you know-”

“I do.”

I peered up through my lashes, heart hammering. “Will you tell me?”

Velidan regarded me with pursed lips and furrowed brows. Then the expression smoothed into a familiar, subtle smile.

“Well...” My breath caught as he shifted over, lifting one hand to brush hair behind my ear. “That depends.”

My mouth went dry. “On?”

He spent a moment searching my face. “What don’t I know, Brin?”

My silence, and the flinch that came with it, spoke volumes.

“Come now.” The murmur was silk and smoke, as gentle as the touch trailing down my fluttering pulse. “I’ve answered your questions. I’ve lavished you with gifts and food, let you enjoy yourself, kept you safe. Aside from the intrusion, you had a wonderful night. What was your word? Amazing? Surely you’re feeling more… open.”

A shiver ran down my spine as his fingers graced lower, brushing past my collarbone and settling on a too-heavy jewel. Saints, how can he be so… it’s almost like some twisted version of Renner’s constant attempts to fluster me.

Much more chilling, though; I trusted Renner with my life, and quite enjoyed being flustered by him. This felt more like a wolf sniffing at my throat. Perhaps his teeth weren’t bared, but they were still right there.

Well, trying to fluster Renner back kind of worked. It’s the same principle. I think. And stars’ know he enjoys it when I push back, so…

So rather than follow my instinct to dive off the tower, I caught his hand firmly. Then I lifted both brows, pushed him away, and snipped, “It would have been more amazing if you’d bought me dessert.”

Velidan’s face split into a wicked grin. He chuckled and snaked one palm to my lower back, pressing me towards the stairs. “Mm. I’ll remember for next time.”
