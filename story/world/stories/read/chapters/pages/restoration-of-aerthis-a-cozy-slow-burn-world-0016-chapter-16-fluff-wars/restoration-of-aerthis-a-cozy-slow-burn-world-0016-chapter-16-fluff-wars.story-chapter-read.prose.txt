The distant clang of pots came before the light. Elynara pulled the blanket tighter around her shoulder and buried her nose into the pillow.

Starpuff disagreed with this decision.

It flopped directly onto her ear with a dramatic spin and a soft, indignant trill, then bounced off her collarbone and hovered a finger’s width from her face.

She groaned.

“Traitor.”

The fuzzball leaned closer, blinking its glow in time with the far-off clatter of something being chopped in the kitchen.

There was no point pretending. She swung her legs off the bed and stretched until her spine cracked. Her shirt clung a little from yesterday’s dust, and her hair was doing something indecisive between flat and tangled.

She dressed quickly, cinching her belt one hole tighter than before. Another benefit of consistent fieldwork. The hall smelled of herbs and baked roots when she stepped out, and by the time she reached the common room, two of the youngest were already arguing over who had seen a mole first.

The breakfast table barely had a gap for her to sit. And their questions started before her bowl hit the wood.

“Is it true you talked to a firebug and it listened?”

“Are Bonefangs always that stinky or just the old ones?”

“Is the lady from the guild really scary, or does she just look it?”

Elynara stirred her porridge.

“Alicia only glares when someone deserves it.”

That earned a few impressed nods. The mole debate continued anyway.

Rina leaned over from the other end of the table, balancing a plate with one hand and corralling Finnie with the other.

“You heading out again today?”

“Back to the same area. Going to check for anything I missed and try for cleaner dismantling this time.”

Rina’s eyebrows rose.

“Want someone to haul the carcasses back this round? Aren’s free. And he won’t talk your ears off like the rest of us.”

Elynara took one look at the youngest two arguing over whose turn it was to chase hens and nodded.

“That sounds like the smartest idea I’ve heard all morning.”

Aren gave a noncommittal grunt from the side wall, still finishing his stew in silence. That suited her just fine.

After breakfast, she slipped away through the rear door, passing the vegetable beds and the low stack of cut lumber by the shed. The shrine sat just beyond the fence, where the grass grew a little longer and the air held less noise.

The fruit on the cloth was beginning to dull. No rot yet, but the mana lines had faded, curling into thinner, uneven strands. The carved fox still pulsed lightly. And the bird continued strong.

The dried slices hadn’t changed.

Elynara stepped into the grass, centered herself, and pulled mana from her core. Two smooth arcs, one to each charm. A third toward the two glimmering shapes just behind the standing stones.

The spirits stirred as the energy reached them. Quickly ‘waking up’ to gobble the mana up. She stayed a few moments longer, watching.

Something had changed.

The greens were a touch deeper. The soil held a richer shade. Even the edge of the grass shimmered more cleanly in the light.

It hadn’t looked like this when she arrived.

Her fingers stayed at her sides, but her breath held a small rhythm of satisfaction. The difference wasn’t dramatic, but very noticeable.

She didn’t expect it to spread. It was quite clear her influence only stretched as far as her effort and mana allowed. The mana here still thinned fast once the flow broke beyond the shrine circle.

Still, that was something.

Even if the work stayed small. Even if the land only responded in pieces. It was enough to know the restoration was possible for now.

The grass softened beneath her steps as the farm shrank behind her. Elynara followed the rise toward the forest path with her staff light in her hand and Starpuff floating beside her, quiet for once.

The morning air still held a bite to it, but the sun had crested enough to take the edge off. Light flickered through the trees ahead, shifting with the breeze.

Her thoughts drifted to the system message from yesterday, and her ‘new’ Meditation skill.

She hadn’t thought much of it in the moment.

But when she had sat by the shrine again this morning, it had responded faster. The centering came quicker. It seemed there was value in having the System recognize her skills, beyond just better memory.

That made her wonder how many other things she had done half-right. Things she had practiced often, but never with the focus or frame the system recognized as ‘proper’.

The more she thought about it, the more likely it seemed that other unlocks were waiting. Not behind some grand rituals, but behind subtle thresholds.

Beside her, Starpuff began drifting closer. It nudged her arm once. Then again, more deliberate.

“You’re not subtle.”

It spun a slow circle, then headbutted the side of her neck with the same kind of exaggerated pout it used when the children ignored it.

The message came through loud enough. She had given mana to the other spirits. But not to it.

Elynara shook her head, half smiling.

“Are you sure you need to eat? It’s not like you’re sharing with the land like the others.”

Starpuff trilled in a long, wounded note and tucked in tighter to her shoulder, curling against the strap of her pack.

“Fine, fine, I’m sorry.”

She reached with two fingers, fed a short pulse of mana to the edge of its glow. The reaction was immediate, giving off a brightening flash of color and a smug twirl before it settled into an almost lazy recline in midair, like a child who had gotten dessert early.

She didn’t need a translation for that.

With Starpuff settled, her thoughts drifted back to spellwork.

The fire spell she used had come from instinct and experimentation. Pressing mana against itself, then shaping the pressure into something sharp and hot. The idea had felt obvious at the time: heat through friction and compression, fire through containment.

But if that worked, then maybe the same idea could be reversed or shifted.

If compression made fire, what did expansion lead to?

She didn’t know.

But she could find out. If she could feel it when fire took shape, she could feel when something else tried to. That was all it took: shape, watch, adjust. And eventually, refine.

Not all knowledge came from books. The system responded to reality. And reality responded to effort.

The forest edged closer with each step. The light grew more patchy, the shadows deeper. Her grip on the staff adjusted automatically as she picked up pace, Aren following suit. She almost forgot he was there for a moment.

The morning would be for scouting. The rest of the day, whatever she made of it.

The ground still bore the marks from yesterday’s fights. Tamped grass, broken stems, the long skid trail where the third Bristleboar had fallen. Elynara slowed as she stepped into the open stretch, eyes tracing over the cleared space.

Nothing stood out to her here. No waiting shapes tucked behind the grass as far as she could tell. Still, she didn’t relax fully until they had passed the hollow dip where the Bonefangs had first shown themselves.

Aren walked a few paces behind her, sack slung over one shoulder and a short iron hook at his belt. He didn’t talk or drag his feet. She liked that about him.

Starpuff floated further ahead, drifting low to the ground, occasionally nosing a patch of grass before losing interest and spiraling off.

The edge of the forest wasn’t far. Dense and uneven, the tree line held its shape like a curtain, with shadows crossing each branch. She stopped there and raised her staff slightly.

“We’ll stick to the outer edge first,” she said. “I’ll draw and hunt, so could you two search for herbs? Those ones here.”

Saying so she pulled out the quest sheet. Aren gave a short nod. Starpuff spun once, then zipped off toward the left without waiting for agreement. She followed, shifting her grip as she moved.

The requests were simple enough. Hunt perimeter threats. Search for listed flora. No one had said where the line was between “edge” and “inside,” but she would stay cautious until the outer ring proved useless.

She decided heading southwest would be the best place to start, and trailed along the treeline. Every hundred paces, she cast a short-range burst. Heatless, loud, bright enough to startle. Her true and tried tactic.

The first creature didn’t show up until the fifth cast, another Bonefang that lunged too slow and died too fast to make her pulse spike. The second, nearly forty minutes later, was a lean thing that looked hungry enough to fight anything it heard. She dropped it in two strikes.

Both were hauled off the path and stripped of easy parts. Aren helped silently, shouldering the heavier one while she worked.

Between casts, she scanned the growth just beyond the treeline for the listed herbs. She found two matches. One was half-wilted, the other missing too many leaves to keep.

Aren spotted another.

“Closer to good, but not good enough,” he said simply, holding it out in a folded rag.

She agreed.

By the time two hours passed, she could feel the drain creeping in. Not a crash, but the long burn in her arms that said she’d pushed steady without pause. She sat briefly at a dry patch near a ridge and let her breathing ease while mana refilled.

Aren drank from his canteen, then gestured to the tied corpses.

“I’ll start hauling."

She nodded.

“I’ll help drag them past the bend first. Then I’ll backtrack solo.”

He didn’t argue. They worked together to get the second carcass up onto his sled, lashed it with worn rope, and rolled it toward the farm path. She stayed until they reached the next marker, then turned around.

Her pace quickened as she returned along the route they had cleared. The path felt broader now. Familiarity made things feel safer. She knew where to look now, and nothing stood out either.

Still, she didn’t let her thoughts drift far.

The forest stayed quiet, but not empty.

And if the shallow sweep was any sign, it would take more than noise to get results.

Elynara circled the cleared field with a longer stride this time, staff swinging lightly in her grip. The second sweep would run opposite the first. If anything still lingered near the perimeter, it would be on this side. She had enough mana restored to hold through it.

Starpuff trailed loosely overhead, occasionally darting between tree branches or pausing to nose a low bush. She left it to its wandering. The puffball had earned a little freedom after keeping alert all morning.

Not like she could tell it what to do.

The grass here sloped gently into uneven dips before rising again near a rocky crest. She kept to the lower edges. Every few paces, she sent out another casting to sound things out. A few birds scattered after the second. Nothing else stirred.

Until it did.

The first two monsters she found came in a pair. They were smaller Bonefang types, less ragged than usual, but fast enough to keep her busy for a moment. She dispatched them cleanly, though the second got close enough to leave her cloak billowing where the blast reflected back.

The third came after a long pause. Something like a bloated crawler with tusks, chewing its way through a rotten log. It took two focused strikes, and then a third to finish after it writhed half-alive under the ferns.

She actually didn’t know what that thing was, it wasn’t in her notes. But it died all the same.

Her search continued for a while longer before she found the next one.

It was tucked low in the brush, almost hidden; a flick of mana that disturbed the air around its head, and she had accidentally spotted it. Her shot was fast, reactionary, more instinct than aim. It hit anyway. The Bristleboar barely made a sound as it dropped.

By the time she circled back to the midpoint, the afternoon light had shifted. Sun angled through the grass from above, and the forest edge cast longer shadows across the clearing.

She expected to find Aren again. Instead, his father stood next to him, sleeves rolled, pulling the cart from yesterday, rope already coiled over his shoulder.

“Thought I’d be useful,” he said when she approached. “Figured you would have more than just two beasties to show soon.”

Aren gave a small shrug beside him, as if to say he hadn’t asked, but didn’t mind.

Elynara blinked once, then nodded.

“You were right.”

They moved together easily. Without excess chatter, just callouts for weight and direction, short gestures to shift grips or steer around the thorn patches.

The father didn’t slow once, and Aren worked twice as hard without complaint.

She stepped back after the last corpse was secured and sat with her back against a shallow rise. Her shoulders rolled once before she let herself relax fully.

The hunting request didn’t demand forest entry. Technically, she had already cleared enough to count. But the herb list told a different story.

Most of what remained couldn’t be found out here. Not unless the terrain changed. Yet she also didn’t want to go through the overgrown and bushy forest line.

She thought back to earlier walks. There were a few spots she remembered with open breaks in the tree line, and had neither thick brambles nor twisting roots. Places you could step through without forcing it. One near the north bend looked ideal. Sloped clean, too.

But then there was the matter of casting.

Her fire burst tactic worked well in the fields. It was safe to throw heat into open space, or against any rocks that stood out. Inside a forest, it would be different. Branches, dry leaves, cover above and below. Fire would bring risk. Sound, too, might carry poorly or reflect strangely.

It would still work, but… She needed another option. If not now, then for the future.

Her eyes stayed on the forest edge. It was perception, not sight, that had caused her to notice the movement of the flickering mana in the air.

If she trained that more deliberately, maybe she could stop guessing.

Everything had mana. She had known that for a good while now.

So why keep relying on being loud when she could learn to listen instead?

It was a thought worth testing.

She stood, brushing her hands on her sleeves.

“I’ll head in,” she said. “Not far. Just enough to check for herb growth.”

Aren started to reach for his pack, but the father stepped in instead.

“You stay,” he said, giving the boy a light push toward the sled. “Bring the beasties back and help your mother with the cuts. I’ll go.”

Elynara blinked once.

“You don’t have to–”

“I do,” he said plainly. “Can’t just let a youngun go into the forest alone.”

She opened her mouth, thought better of it, and nodded.

They turned together toward the trees, steps light, shadows pulling longer across the ground behind them.

The forest welcomed them with a mild sort of silence. It wasn’t the heavy kind that pressed in, but the quiet of a place no longer stirred by wind or people. The trees here grew tall but sparse, their roots sunk into dry earth and leafmold rather than thick underbrush. Whatever life had once pulsed through the clearing, it wasn’t strong now.

Elynara moved one step before the father, boots light on the hardened ground. His pace was slow but steady, eyes scanning with the casual familiarity of someone who didn’t need to dramatize caution to be careful. He didn’t speak. She didn’t either.

Branches crisscrossed high above them, thin enough to let the afternoon light filter through in ribbons. Visibility stayed good. The breeze, faint though it was, helped keep the tension at bay.

She still kept her staff close.

Even without any movement or worrying signs, the forest edge had its own weight. Thinned as it was, the space felt different from the open field. Sounds didn’t carry the same way, and direction blurred a bit.

Elynara kept her attention forward.

She could fire off a sound burst here and probably get away with it. The canopy wasn’t thick enough to trap heat or light, and nothing looked dry enough to flash burn. But even so, it didn’t feel right.

The fire booms had worked outside. Big space. Easy targets. Lots of wind.

Still, she had little other choice for now. She just had to make sure her spells didn’t land anywhere weird. And so she fired off, and again a few minutes later.

Nothing came out of hiding.

But instead of reaching for her next spell, she let her mana settle.

Might as well try simply listening. And mana sensing, of course.

She focused on how the mana felt around her. Around the trees. And how the grass under her boots barely held a pulse. Recalled how the spirits by the shrine had stirred at her presence.

The same energy was here. Diffuse, faint, but present.

Everything with mana gave off a trace. That had always been true.

So why not use that?

She didn’t need to invent wind spells. Didn’t need to guess at elemental triggers.

Maybe she could train this, to sense mana more clearly and accurately. She already knew how to feel mana in herself. In objects. And nearby ambient space. Now she just had to apply that same skill outward.

It wouldn’t be flashy. Wouldn’t unlock anything dramatic overnight.

But it could be steady. Quiet. Hers to build.

Her hand shifted slightly along her staff. The bark felt warm where it met her fingers.

The father glanced over once, then forward again. Still nothing ahead.

They kept walking.

Elynara breathed slowly, every sense tuned for a flicker. A pull. Any shift in the thread of the world around them.

It felt like a good start, but it was clear it wouldn’t happen today. Still, she kept at it, in between her fire spells. Any practice was good to have.

And she seemed to learn better testing things out in the world than tucked away in a corner somewhere.

The forest didn’t sharpen the longer they walked, but it didn’t ease either. The further they went, the more the air thinned. Mana lingered behind the bark and root in small threads, loose and pale. Even the leaves overhead looked faded, like the green had drained off weeks early.

For all she knew, it was completely normal to the people around here. With how little mana there was around to feed the cycle.

Still, they kept close to the clearer paths. The trees stood with enough spacing to move without forcing it, but visibility dropped fast around bends.

Elynara scanned the brushline constantly, fingers shifting along her staff. Her fire spell stayed ready, but not charged. She didn’t want to burn her margin early.

The father walked behind her at an even, controlled pace. He didn’t speak. Just making sure she was always in his sight. Keeping up without any trouble.

She appreciated that.

Tucked low behind a half-fallen log, they found their first creature; it was rather small, but fast. It snapped toward them without warning. She hit it mid-lunge, burned a path through its upper torso, and kicked it back before the smoke cleared.

It wasn’t anything she recognized again, but seemed even weaker than Bonefangs were.

The second came twenty minutes later, another Bristleboar. Startled by the sudden explosion, it was just slow enough to respond for her to take it out before it even had the chance to charge.

It wasn’t until a while later when the unexpected happened, and another boar charged out of the thickets nearby. She barely responded in time, dispatching the beast a mere two meters away.

She was shaken but happy it worked. Though seeing how quick Theren responded to the threat, she was never in any actual danger.

Still, it was done.

The father gave no comment on her casting, just picked up the fallen beast. Though his pace picked up after that. Steady and unbothered.

They found the herbs between fights, scattered in clusters across a shaded incline. Most of them she recognized by their leaf-like shape, which had thin, angled veins and soft fringe edges. It was enough to fill up a good chunk of the required amount. The others she marked for tomorrow.

By the time they reached the edge of the forest again, her shoulders had relaxed. The pressure that lived just behind her ears, the one that came from holding tension too long, began to ease.

She was about to turn, ready to thank him and start back toward the farm, when a hand landed firmly on her shoulder.

“You have done the hunting,” the father said, voice level. “Now let’s go and do the work.”

His face made it pretty clear it wasn’t a request.

Elynara didn’t speak. Her brain made a quiet, high-pitched noise that didn’t translate into words. Her legs almost protested on the spot.

But her mouth said:

“Alright.”

The next hours passed in a blur of lifting, sorting, hauling, and more lifting. Bones were cleaned, cuts divided, hides packed. She didn’t keep count of how many times she bent down and stood back up. Her arms stopped registering weight sometime before dusk.

By the time she reached her bed after the evening meal, she could barely even bother to untie her boots.

Starpuff tried to land on her shoulder, missed, and settled on her hip instead.

Sleep hit before her eyes had fully closed.

Not a single dream waited.
