Sinking deeper into the comfy chair inside Uncle Winton’s spiritual gate device, Ferris considered what to ask about next. His mind was still reeling from the qi rope transfer technique.

While thinking about the large stone fort… Ferris remembered the brightly burning coal from the towers.

“Uncle, atop each of the fort’s four towers, I found a bright flame. It appears to be burning a large white-hot chunk of rock or coal, but I don’t know what it is or what I could do with it.”

His uncle grinned and asked, “Ferris, my boy, do you recall how to advance to the second tier?”

“Grow the Qi Pool, unlock meridians, and purge impurities,” Ferris recited. His Qi Pool… had not grown at all. Ferris knew a meridian needed a flow of energy, much like the waterfall in the slimespace. That only left…

“That ‘chunk of rock’ is likely an impurity, a spiritual representation of the toxins and waste in your body. Typically, you have to go out and find them yourself, but it sounds like your demons did the work for you.”

“Then how do I ‘purge’ them? Does burning do that?”

“No,” Uncle Winton shook his head, “burning them is a technique to attract demons. Those four impurities could burn forever and not go out. Think more on the word ‘purging.’ Removing the spiritual representation causes the physical impurities to leave your body. The process is… unpleasant.” His nose scrunched up. “Bluereef has a room at the bath house dedicated to it. I suggest you ask around to see if Hollowfield has the same.”

“How many impurities do I need to purge to advance tiers?”

“It is different for every Adept,” Uncle Winton shrugged, “likely at least hundreds. Those who rely too heavily on pills or other cultivation aids can build up toxins in their body, adding more impurities to clean. The breakthrough to the next tier will purge any that you miss. If there are too many, the advancement will fail, causing a backlash.”

“Then how would I ‘purge’ them?”

“Ha, figure that one out yourself.” The man smiled at him. “I’m not going to give you every answer, that would only stifle your growth.”

Ferris considered that statement. He had a thousand questions, and constantly felt like the information was just out of reach. He understood why Hollowfield put a price on knowledge, but his uncle hadn’t given him anything other than basic instruction.

“Uncle,” Ferris began, “Hollowfield’s library has a series of jade slips on spiritual techniques. They gave me one called First Steps of the Soul, and then recommended I purchase two more: Spiritual Harmony, and Mental Materials. Surely our clan has similar information, could I get it rather than paying Hollowfield?”

Uncle Winton frowned, and then shook his head.

“Think of it like this. You’ve just started workin’ at a restaurant. I’ve worked at the one down the street my entire life. Who should be teaching you the ropes?”

“Someone from the place I’m at,” Ferris said, seeing where this was going.

“Right. I could start teaching you how to cook, but on your first day you would do everything wrong. You wouldn’t know where anything was, and the way they do things would all be different. Someone with a couple years’ experience wouldn’t take long to adapt to a new place, but you don’t have that. Those manuals from Hollowfield are foundational, and all the rest they have will build upon them. Our versions might serve the same purpose, but you’d suffer from minor differences when you buy the next ones.”

Ferris looked down, trying to hide his disappointment. He had expected the answer, but it was still frustrating.

“Don’t feel too bad,” his mentor stood, then came over to pat him on the shoulder, “we make new Adepts work and pay for the same sort of knowledge back home. It’s been shown pretty clearly that giving everything to a new cultivator ends poorly. Too much at once spoils the dish, as they say.”

“We definitely wouldn’t want that,” Ferris said, a bit more sarcastically than he meant to. He leaned back in his chair.

“That isn’t to say I have nothing for you. I was going to wait until we were done talking, but now is as good a time as any.”

Sitting back up straight, Ferris turned to his uncle. The man was pulling a large chest into the center of the room.

“Hollowfield’s library may have a breadth of techniques and abilities that put Bluereef’s to shame, but don’t count us out entirely. We survive just fine out on our own by specializing. You may not be following our martial path, but you are a part of the clan.”

Ferris stood, setting Jade down to walk over and take a closer look at the large chest. It was the size and shape of a typical chest that could be found at the foot of a bed, although ornately decorated with silver trim. The Bluereef Clan’s crest was embedded on the top: a spear thrusting through a wave of water. A heavy lock secured the top closed.

“I had to wait until you were bonded, for stupid reasons. Politics mostly, a few idiots wanted to make sure you didn’t abandon the clan to join some martial tradition.” Uncle Winton said. “But within this chest is your rightful inheritance. The legacy of our clan. Well, most of it anyway.” The man pulled out a large key, then handed it to him.

Ferris looked dumbly at the key for a second, then back at his mentor.

“A legacy?”

“That’s what I said,” his uncle placed the key in his hand, then gave him a nudge. “Go on, open it.”

Ferris knelt in front of the chest, fitting the key to the lock. He’d heard of legacies before, usually in stories. A cultivator would find some deadly challenge left behind by someone who died a thousand years ago. After overcoming all the traps and puzzles, they would be rewarded with a legacy.

They were different than martial traditions, although the two often overlapped. The ‘tradition’ part of a martial tradition implied a group of people collecting and sharing knowledge of a type of bond for generations, while legacies were more generic. Many powerful people who never started their own martial tradition still left behind a legacy.

The key slid into the lock, then turned with hardly any resistance. A clear and precise click sounded out as the lock released. With trembling arms, Ferris pushed the lid up to look inside.

He had to blink several times, looking away and then back at the chest. It was somehow larger on the inside. It reminded Ferris of the bag inside the spirit gem refinement device, although worse. He’d had enough trouble just feeling around inside the bag, whereas here he was looking directly into the chest.

Reaching out, Ferris picked up the item that stood out: a spear. Lifting it out, the full-sized weapon somehow came out of the knee-high chest.

“That,” Uncle Winton said, “is an imprint weapon. It’s for training, not fighting. One of our elders made it specifically for you, imprinting their skill and technique with the spear into it. Go on, try a few practice forms.”

Grasping the weapon, Ferris fell into a ready stance. As he brought the spear up, a distracting sense of wrongness pulled at his attention. Everything from how he gripped the shaft to the position of his feet felt off. Movements that he had practiced for half his life suddenly were clumsy and unfamiliar.

It was almost like the opposite of his bone spear. In fact, it was exactly like that. Where his bone spear had allowed his body to do precisely what his mind wanted, this spear was making every movement feel wrong.

“Uncle,” Ferris said, “recently I used qi forming to make a new spear. It feels… special to use. Like I can fight for real how I picture it in my head. Is it also…”

“An imprint?” Uncle Winton replied, looking surprised. “Possibly. If it is, I bet it feels a lot nicer to use than this spear. Imprint weapons you make yourself solidify your understanding. Ones from another person have a foreign understanding and experiences. You must spend time practicing, learning from it. This spear will show you what you’re doing wrong, but you still need to learn the lesson yourself.”

Ferris looked back at the spear. Then, it was like having one of the elders of the clan personally teach him. His grip tightened on the weapon.

“You’re excited now,” his mentor said, with a chuckle, “but just wait. Training with an imprint weapon is the most frustrating thing ever. If it wasn’t so damned effective, no one would do it. Instead, we just suffer through it, then complain afterwards.”

The man gestured to the chest.

“There are two more spears like that one. The cursed things are at least less terrible when broken up into multiple levels. Once you can wield the one in your hands as easily as a spear you have made yourself, move on to the next.”

Ferris nodded, excited despite the warnings.

“Then, there is one more,” Uncle Winton pulled out another spear from the chest. “This one is a bit different, and… may not be worth your time.”

The man hefted the spear, moving into the same ready stance Ferris had.

“Our clan has refined and improved our forms for generations. We call our style the Bluereef Surging Spear Art, but there are many different styles for the spear out there. You could probably purchase training or a manual for one of the more popular ones from Hollowfield. It would be very different than what we taught you.”

The man glanced at Ferris, then asked, “Which style do you think is better, ours or theirs?”

“Ours,” Ferris said, giving his uncle a big smile. He could see that the answer was a trap, but decided to walk into it anyway.

“Neither,” the man said, rolling his eyes at the cheeky grin. “Saying a style is ‘better’ misses the point, and you know it. Bluereef’s spear forms have evolved to meet the needs of our delvers, becoming the best support for our other abilities. Which means…”

“Water Jet,” Ferris said, losing the grin.

Bonded weapons could typically have a single ability enchanted before they were bound. Bluereef’s spears all had the Water Jet ability, which shot water out the end of the shaft. This was vital for mobility underwater, but Ferris had also seen the ability used extensively to give more power to thrusts.

“Exactly,” Uncle Winton said, then jabbed the spear forward. Mid thrust, a jet of water pulsed out the back, adding more force to the attack.

“We all have the ability,” the man continued, easing out of the fighting stance, “and our style has grown to take advantage of it. Many of the forms are intrinsically tied to the ability. The spear you hold in your hands… I mentioned an elder made it specifically for you. It strips out any use of Water Jet, and gives a complete style without needing the ability. This spear,” he lifted the weapon, “has our unaltered style imprinted.”

Ferris nodded, saddened. His eyes were inevitably drawn to Jade, who was happily rolling around the room. Uncle Winton didn’t say the rest, but Ferris understood. A style stripped of a key element would never measure up to others. If he continued learning Bluereef’s style, he would always have a weakened technique.

But learning a different style…

After a few moments, he forced his hands to release their death grip on the spear. Returning the weapon to its place in the chest, Ferris looked at what else it contained.

Next to the spears was a book, titled Bluereef Surging Spear Art. It appeared to be a reference intended to help him better understand the forms from the imprint spears.

Then, there was a glove tied to a book, which Ferris lifted out.

“That is another imprinted construct, although this time for our hand signs. You know the basics already, but there is a lot more we only teach delvers. The book is a reference, but the glove will help you practice.”

Ferris flipped open the book, seeing each page had a drawing of a hand, usually with arrows describing motion.

“Hollowfield will have something similar, but with different signs,” Uncle Winton said. “Every clan inevitably creates their own codes. Most long-term delving groups even make their own variations. I recommend you learn Hollowfield’s first, then come back to ours in a few years. Eventually, you’ll need to know half a dozen different codes, but stick to one to start with.”

Ferris sighed, returning the book and glove to the chest. Hollowfield would inevitably charge him merit for their hand sign language’s manual.

“The book next to it is mandatory learning: Surviving the Waters of Bluereef, written by our clan’s founder Aston al Bluereef. Read it, reread it, then read it again. Memorize it. I want you to be able to recite it in your sleep.”

The book was thick, and Ferris had to hold himself back from pulling it out and immediately digging in. There would be time, later. Perhaps he would save it for when he was missing home more than usual.

“This,” Uncle Winton tapped on what looked like a carved rock, “is known as a travel marker. Don’t worry about it for now, but after you start learning rift magic, it will let you open rifts to Bluereef Bastion.”

The man looked Ferris straight in the eyes, taking on a more serious tone.

“This goes for everything in the chest, but especially this. Don’t let the travel maker fall into any other hands. Not even for a moment. Best not to let anyone even know you have it. There are techniques to copy them, which could happen without you even noticing.”

He placed his hand on the elaborate chest.

“This legacy chest will only open with the key. Keep it locked at all times, with everything inside. Don’t lose the key. Even I couldn’t get into the chest without destroying what’s inside. Store the key somewhere else. This makes it…” the man paused for a second, “harder for someone to kill you and rip it from your soul.”

Ferris gulped, looking back at the chest. He knew delvers often met out in the rifts, and the rules of society sometimes broke down. It was one of the major reasons for secrecy around one’s cultivation. A potential enemy might hesitate if they didn’t know what you were capable of. Plus, it was best to always have trump cards no one else knew about.

His uncle had mentioned ‘politics’ delaying him from handing over the legacy until now. From a certain perspective, Ferris could understand he was a risk. He was a new Adept, off living in a different bastion. Some charlatan could trick him into ‘showing off’ or ‘comparing notes.’ There were a thousand ways he could be manipulated into sharing secrets.

It probably would have been smarter to wait a few years. Let Ferris mature first before giving him this.

Turning back to his uncle, Ferris gave the man a hug.

“Now, now,” the man said, patting him on the head, “that is an extreme example. Hollowfield is a respectable organization, and people know not to mess with them.”

Ferris just shook his head. The man had mistaken his appreciation for fear, but it didn’t matter. He simply hugged tighter.
