The magic carpet carried the group of four and the half dozen wolves toward the side of the great central spire of Mahādi.

Its smooth surface split open much like the hangar doors of the Vimana, revealing an entrance that glowed with light. They passed through into a much smaller hangar than the Vimana’s, disembarking as Yuma, the goddess of love and compassion, led them inside.

This spire, unlike any other, held a particular weight for Vir. In his own universe, the central spire of Mahādi wasn’t visible, being buried beneath a swarm that clung to it like glue—a living seal where Janak had been trapped. If that were true, though, then why bring him here, to this reflection of their world?

It didn’t make sense.

They stepped into a wide hall that was surprisingly plain by Imperium standards, lit by the faint blue-white glow of the walls, floor, and ceiling. The corridor led them into a dark, hemispherical chamber that immediately reminded Vir of Janak’s primordial chambers.

A moment later, runes and inscriptions flared to life across the walls, much like the chambers Janak had left behind for Vir.

Lines of prana interwove and multiplied. Dozens at first, then hundreds and thousands, until the chamber walls were painted over with intricate sigils connecting in brilliant fractal patterns. It was one of the grandest workings of prana Vir had ever seen.

Maiya’s breath caught. Even Ashani, who had been subdued since their arrival, looked stunned.

“What is this?” the goddess whispered. “How is such an advanced contraption even possible?”

“You may find that much has changed in the past several millennia. This is one of our greatest creations,” Yuma said, admiring the inscriptions. “It was made possible through the efforts of Adinat and Badrak, and a host of others.”

Vir felt a chill. Both names existed in his own world. Both were legendary figures of the Imperium and revered as the greatest gods. Was it a coincidence that they arrived at this particular universe? Or was it by Janak’s design?

Knowing what he knew about the ancient deity, he strongly suspected the latter.

“This entire chamber was built for a single purpose,” the goddess continued. “To separate it from existence as we know it. To detach it from time and space itself.”

Maiya frowned. “Why would anyone do that?”

“To rid ourselves,” Yuma said quietly, “of those most prying of eyes and ears. The Keepers of Fate, as we call them,” she added, and at those words, Vir noticed that Ashani stiffened. She’d been acting strangely ever since they arrived, and now, he was beginning to wonder if it was something more than mere nostalgia.

“We call them the Fateweavers,” Vir said, before adding, “Though Janak was insistent their name never be mentioned out loud, lest it attract their attention.”

Yuma nodded. “And rightly so. They are an entity that can see and hear through the eyes and ears of their agents. The echoes of every motion and every whisper across the fabric of creation. They see the unwritten tapestry of the future and react to that future by altering the past and the present.”

Vir frowned. “Which means they can affect the flow of events. Bend it to their will?”

As they’ve done in our universe… Vir thought.

“Not precisely,” Yuma replied. “Their influence is not willful, at least, from what we've been able to deduce, though their true motive still remains a mystery to us. Through our collaboration with your Janak, however, we've at least learned that the Fateweavers are less conscious beings and more forces of nature. And like nature, they are neither evil nor benevolent. They are, simply, a byproduct of creation itself. They seem to have existed as long as the infinity of universes itself. Not something to be feared or revered, but understood.”

Vir’s jaw hardened. “Hard to feel that way when they’re destroying our universe.”

“All because of Janak’s mistake,” Yuma said sadly.

“What mistake?” he asked, though he had a pretty good guess.

“Janak delved too deeply into the workings of the universe. In his quest to save Siya, he touched upon that which he shouldn’t have. The end of your Imperium,” Yuma said softly. “Though he has shared little with us, what fragments we have gathered tell the same story. Janak’s actions provoked the Keepers of Fate. He trespassed into their domain, that of the tapestry of reality. Their reaction was… instinctive. Almost defensive. It brought the downfall of your civilization and devastation to your realms. And, from what we can tell, they may not have been wrong to react as such. For though Janak now realizes it, at the time, he had no idea that by meddling in those affairs blindly, he could have doomed not only his own realms, but ours and everyone else's. They acted to ensure such a thing never happens.”

Vir tried and failed to comprehend the magnitude of it all. “This is… almost too much to believe,” he said. “An hour ago, I thought our three realms were the whole of existence. That our biggest problem was the spread of the Ash Tears that plague them.”

Yuma chuckled softly, though it was a weary sound. “We felt the same when we learned the truth. It was Janak who showed us that the multitude of universes extends far beyond what anyone could imagine. You were brought here because this world is the closest reflection of your own. But there are others so strange you could scarcely fathom them. Worlds without magic or chakras. Worlds where there are no realms, only planets and stars. Worlds where beings like us exist only in myth and fantasy.”

Vir shook his head. “I can barely comprehend this universe as it is. To think there are so many more… But if Janak isn’t here, then why bring us at all?”

“And if the Imperium exists here,” Maiya added, “then why can’t we open more Gates between our universes? With your help, we could end the blight of the Ash Tears. Maybe even restore our realms to what they once were!”

Yuma’s luminous eyes dimmed, her smile fading. “I’m afraid that is impossible. To do so would guarantee the destruction of both our universes. When Janak first encroached upon the Fateweavers’ domain, it was a small infraction—a wound the cosmos could bear. But when he later attempted what you now suggest—bridging universes permanently—the world he reached was doomed.”

Her tone grew grave. “The Fateweavers reacted like a body fighting infection. The universe he touched became seeded with more and more of their unwitting agents. The world more malleable to their will. Ash Tears eventually opened, and that universe eventually succumbed, losing all intelligent life, as is happening to yours now. Not even we, with all our advancement, can lift a finger against existences that transcend space and time to manipulate the very fabric of reality as we know it.”

A heavy silence fell over the chamber. Vir’s hands clenched at his sides. “So even saving our world risks destroying it.”

Yuma inclined her head sadly. “Such is the paradox of the multiverse. Salvation and destruction walk hand in hand.”

Vir’s eyes glazed over as the implications of Yuma’s words sank in.

“So if Janak had made a mistake with our universe,” he said slowly, “we would’ve just… never have existed.”

“Most likely,” Yuma replied softly. “That is the sad truth. Even this small degree of contact between our universes carries immense risk. A risk we have done everything in our power to mitigate, to be sure, yet the danger remains. Both to us and to you. For once they take note, though it may take centuries or millennia, there is no resisting their encroachment. It is, perhaps, why Janak took so long to find a suitable candidate to continue his work.”

“That still doesn’t answer why we’re here, though,” Vir asked, frowning in thought.

“For one,” Yuma said, “to share truths that could not safely be spoken in your universe. What I am about to tell you may very well determine the fate of your world.”

The chamber seemed to hum with prana as she spoke, as though struggling to contain her words.

“The Fateweavers are capable of possessing near-omniscient awareness, yes, but only where they have sufficient influence. They cannot see and hear where they have no eyes and ears, after all, and then too, that awareness is split unevenly.”

Vir frowned. “Blind spots, you mean?”

“Somewhat,” Yuma said, tilting her head. “Think of them as a slumbering giant. Its mind and body are mighty, but its attention drifts, as is necessary for a being that governs a near-infinity of parallel universes. You may move quietly beneath it, making small motions without waking it, but you must never do anything loud enough to draw its gaze.”

“We never know what they might hear or what might attract that attention,” Maiya murmured.

“Indeed,” Yuma said. “As for you, Maiya… you are marked by them. Your existence seems more closely tied to the Keepers of Fate than any other. Wherever you walk, their will manifests.”

Maiya bit her lip. “I’m aware,” she whispered. “But what can we do about it?”

Yuma turned to Vir, resting a hand against his chest. “The answer lies with you, Ekavir. You are a special existence. One who carries a fragment of Fate itself within your soul.”

Vir wondered what she meant and how that could be possible, but realized he'd known all along.

“It's my ability to reincarnate, isn't it?”

“Exactly,” Yuma said. “This is not natural, not even among our kind. It should be an impossibility. Yet Janak managed to defy Fate, and as such you defy the Fateweavers’ power. You exist as an anomaly they cannot perceive or control. A true blind spot, as you say.”

“So I alone am free of fate’s shackles?” Vir asked, still struggling to believe it.

“To a degree,” Yuma said. “Your actions and those near to you, both physically and emotionally, exist beyond their sight. The farther that bond stretches, however, the more the Keepers reassert their hold. Through others, they can observe you, learn from your actions, and respond accordingly.”

“I see…” Vir said quietly. “Akin to groping around in the dark, I suppose.”

“Quite right. With this knowledge,” Yuma continued, “our hope is that you can divert their attention, turning their eyes away from our universe. How you do so will depend entirely on your circumstances. Our understanding of your world is limited.”

Vir nodded. “Understood. Thank you. For trusting us with this.”

“So, what happens now?” Maiya asked.

Yuma’s expression brightened. “Now,” she said, clapping her hands, “we offer you what aid we can without drawing the Keeper’s attention. And then, we send you to the next universe.”

“The next universe?” Maiya asked. “What do you mean?”

“Have you never wondered?” Yuma said gently, “How your universe still stands, when Janak’s interference destroyed so many others?”

So many others?

Vir’s eyes widened as realization struck. “He’s been cooperating with you, hasn't he?”

Yuma nodded. “Knowing the threat his existence posed to you, he's worked with several other universes from his prison within your Mahadi. All to thwart their attention. To wage an unwinnable war against an invincible foe. All to buy you the time you need.”

“I suppose that explains why we’ve only ever seen projections of him,” Maiya said. “He can't risk being seen by anyone, let alone someone who might be an agent. Kind of a wonder he was able to contact us at all.”

“Precisely,” Yuma said. “Janak repurposed the Vimana of your world to act as conduits between universes—a feat we would never have imagined possible had he not left us clues to decipher the process ourselves.”

Vir exhaled slowly as it all clicked into place. Only the Imperium’s genius could have reverse-engineered such technology from fragments of divine knowledge. Any lesser civilization would have been lost.

How long had it taken Janak to find this universe? How many mistakes had he made along the way? How many worlds had he doomed before getting it right?

Vir forced the thoughts aside before they could overwhelm him. There was nothing he could do about the past. All that mattered now was ensuring Janak’s sacrifices weren’t in vain. The cryptic messages, the secrecy—it all made sense now. Any piece of information could be weaponized to destroy not just their universe, this one as well.

“Now come,” Yuma said, leading them out of the sealed chamber. “It is time I bestow you weapons and armor to aid you on your quest. With these at your side, you may yet defy Fate itself.”

Vir couldn’t help but smile, and he knew Maiya had the same expression on her face. For the longest time, he’d wondered how he’d stand a chance against an enemy that spanned universes.
