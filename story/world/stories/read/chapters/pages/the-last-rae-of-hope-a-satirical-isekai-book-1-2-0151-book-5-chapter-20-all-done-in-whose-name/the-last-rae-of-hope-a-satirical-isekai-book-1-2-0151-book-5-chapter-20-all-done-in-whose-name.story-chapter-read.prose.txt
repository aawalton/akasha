Fighting the heavy dread in my limbs, I took a step forward and called her name.

“Lala… I’m so sorry this happened to you.”

She turned, her tear-ravaged eyes sliding past me to lock onto Olethros.

For a moment, she just stared, her chest rising but not quite emptying as her breath quickened. Her hands shook as if she didn’t know what to do with them. Then her face twisted, and she bent down, snatching up the nearest rock.

“They were here for you!” she screamed, hurling it with everything she had.

It struck Olethros in the right shoulder and clattered to the ground. Another rock followed, and then another.

The worst part was that he absolutely could have stopped them by any number of means, but he made no move to do so. And I knew he didn’t want me to do anything, either.

“You brought those angels here!” Lala shouted. “Damn demons! You always take what little we have!”

Nora kept shifting uncomfortably on her feet, her staff working a hole into the dusty ground.

“They took my sister!” Lala sobbed. “They took our homes, our food—everything! And now you’re here, just standing there like—like—”

Her voice broke, and the next rock went off to the left.

Tetora, closest to the latest trajectory path, took a step forward, his jaw clenched and his fingers twitching at his sides. Before he could take another, a thin black tendril pulled tight around his ankle, holding it in place. After stumbling slightly, he looked down, startled, then over to Olethros.

The demon king met his gaze briefly before shaking his head and waving him away.

More villagers had gathered now. Some picked up a stone; others were already similarly armed. The first few were thrown at Olethros, but when he didn’t react the way they wanted, their aim widened.

I turned, using Faith to block the one aimed at my shoulder. Nora smacked one back with her staff, eyes blazing.

For her, that was enough.

She tapped her staff, and a translucent purple barrier engulfed us with a low, resonating hum. Several more stones came faster now, all of them bouncing harmlessly a few feet away.

“They’ll get tired or run out eventually,” I said, uneasy at the possible counteractions my mind was already assembling.

“Doubtful,” Nora replied, her fingers already half-clawed for a second spell.

A loud, booming voice cut through the mob’s shouts.

“Lala!”

She paused mid-throw and turned.

Father Baram, the first priest we encountered in Speranza, pushed past several villagers, his exhausted, dirt-streaked features tight but oddly neutral.

“Lala…” he called again.

She dropped the rock she had and ran to him, collapsing against his chest with a loud wail. He wrapped his arms around her, pulling her head into the crook of his shoulder to whisper.

The other townspeople slowly lowered their hands and dropped the rocks, moving closer to offer additional comfort.

“Everyone,” Father Baram boomed. “We are to take refuge in the church. We will be safer there.” He then paused and held up his hand, encasing our barrier with another made of gold.

“You will not follow,” he declared confidently, as if Euphridia herself stood behind him. He even managed to look Olethros straight in the eye, his ram’s horns lowered in almost aggressive anticipation. Then, he turned his attention back to the others and roused his flock to leave toward the eastern hills.

“Keep up your barrier,” Olethros whispered to Nora.

“Is he that powerful?” Nora whispered back.

“Pfft!” was his only response.

And so, the townspeople left in haste, with those more injured being helped by those spared. A few looked back suspiciously, but we held still for so long that we became unsettled a second time.

In the end, we did absolutely nothing to help anyone here.

Maybe we even made it a little worse.

Olethros clucked his tongue. “I would disagree. You’ll see.”

“Huh?” Nora asked, confused over his sudden statement.

“Oh. You can drop your barrier now,” he advised with a stretch. “They’re out of sight.”

Nora gave him a weird look, but then she nodded, and her spell dissipated.

“Um…” I looked up at the gold sparkles still surrounding us.

Olethros walked over to the edge of gold and tapped it with an index finger, popping it like a large soap bubble. “Clever,” he admitted. “A glow without bite.”

“So… it’s an illusion?” I asked. “I thought that’s only associated with animus.”

“It’s light,” he answered. “But I’ll let you decide whether its application here counts as an illusion.” He smiled casually, and I became instantly aware he was doing his best to distract. “The priesthood would call you a heretic if you did. But don’t let that stop you from acknowledging that the perception of the light’s color is a mental construction… one that can be manipulated.”

I puzzled over that for a moment before Nora huffed.

“It’s like duality isn’t really at odds with either half; everything magic-based is just perception-based misdirection!”

Olethros blinked several times, then grinned. “Well now. That’s a consideration. I wonder who would benefit from proscribing magic by perception and understanding…”

“I can think of three beings off that bat,” Nora grumbled. “But I also think my list of suspects will keep growing…”

Tetora let out a loud, defeated snort. “Forget magical nonsense. Let’s go back.”

Olethros shook his head. “That priest wants to talk to us alone. I think we’ll want to spare him the time.”

“Maybe we could salvage a little while we wait?” I asked, gesturing back at the rubble. “Leave what we pulled out for them to find later?”

Tetora’s eyes crinkled sadly. “Still my Gold Dragon, I see. But it would be best to leave it all alone. Anything we touch, they’ll discard. They’ll tell themselves we cursed or poisoned it. Trust me. I’ve been on their side in the past.”

“Oh…”

Evening crept up slowly at first, then pounced, bringing with it a stiff, windy chill and a lone Father Baram. He stopped a short distance away, folded his hands, and took a series of deep breaths.

Olethros bobbed his head for me to go first. Father Baram also dipped his head in a similar gesture when I approached, then waited for the rest of us to join him.

“I will need some guidance on my actions today,” he said slowly. “For most assuredly, I will need to sell the story in a way that protects us here.” He reached up and began to scratch behind his ear. “I plan to say I encountered a demon and a false hero attempting to recruit hybrids while the town was vulnerable. That I was scared, before being divinely inspired to lead my people to safety.” He glanced at Tetora rather ruefully. “The only reason we escaped was because of the loud tiger proclaiming everyone here was too weak to be of any use before storming off.”

We all exchanged a few glances, some saying more than others.

“Do you think that will suffice?” Father Baram prompted. “Or should I add a few more lies? If so, please be as specific as possible. I’m sure I don’t understand it all as a humble, rural priest.”

“Father Baram…” I said with a soft sigh. “I’m so sorry to make you do this—even though it sounds like your best move, all things considered.”

I saw a bit of hurt betrayal in his eyes just before he shrugged. “After seeing and thinking about it…” he murmured. “Shorter hair suits you.” Then he glanced at Olethros. “Are you…”

“I don’t think you want me to answer that,” Olethros replied. “No matter which name you were going to call me.”

Father Baram swallowed sheepishly. “And you answered it anyway. My sin for raising the question in the first place.” He straightened considerably. “I’m here, telling you my plan because I know this isn’t the end of it. Rule-based fear doesn’t stop in tiny towns.”

“We’re well aware of that,” Tetora replied with a morose sigh.

“Right. Hybrid to hybrid,” he added with a strange gesture before folding his arms. “A broadcast went out about the ‘demon purge’ here as it happened. By the time I made it into town, it was all over. Some witnesses say over 50 angels descended, but accounts varied widely.”

“What else about the broadcast?” I asked. “Did he have anything specific to say, other than demons are evil?”

Father Baram nodded. “That he is aware that our town is in shock over the idea that demons could have been lurking in our close-knit community. But rest assured that every sentient being taken will be thoroughly investigated, and those that are not found to be demons will be returned.”

“Huh?”

“He’s sending a message, Captain. If you don’t comply with his ways, you will be labeled a demon and removed. It’s as simple as that.” With a bow of his head. “If you were going to ask us to fight back, know that we cannot leave, if for no other reason than our need to wait for the return of our loved ones, no matter how slim the chance is.”

“That rat bastard!” Nora swore. “He’s…. He’s--!”

“Going to do this to every place we've visited! Anyone who’s met me!” I shouted. “He’s doing this because he knows I need others to oppose him!”

Father Baram nodded. “As soon as I saw you here, it all made sense. I hope you find stronger allies than us out there. I am sorry we cannot be of any service to you.”

Nora took a deep breath to calm herself. “Father Baram. One last favor, then we’ll leave you be.”

“If it is within my power?” he hedged, looking as if his nerve would break at any moment.

“Can you show me how you bless something? Oh, not any of our stuff. But uh…” she started to look around. “Um…”

Tetora picked up a nearby stick. “This?”

“That’ll do!” she chirped. “Explain how a blessing works? I get that certain materials hold the blessing better. But tell me of the process itself.”

Father Baram considered the stick. “Blessings call upon Euphridia,” he explained. “But they also require giving something of yourself.”

“Does it have to be Euphridia?” she asked.

“Who else would—” He stopped. “I… have always assumed… But… she isn’t here, is she? And she has been gone for…”

Nora wrinkled her nose. “Don’t try it by calling him. How about you try it by calling my God.”

“Y…your God?”

Nora nodded. “Mmmhmm.”

Father Baram looked as though he was going to jump out of his skin. “What… What is your God’s name?”

“Abraham.”

Oh! That’s just God from Earth. Right?

Wait, no, the saying is God of Abraham.

Father Baram tilted his head. “Abraham?”

“Well…” she then leaned in and whispered, “His full name is Abraham Abrams.”

“Abraham Abrams…”

Wait. President Abrams?

His first name is Abraham?!

Nora started to roll back and forth on her heels. “He knows Euphridia. Like a father to her. Grandfather, even. He’s very old. Wise. You know the type.”

Wise?!

The only person staring harder at her than me was Olethros himself.

Father Baram considered us all, and then the stick in his hands. “I…” Then he looked at me. “Captain?”

I took a deep breath, mainly because I had run out of air. “I... I briefly met him in the next world. He loves Euphridia very much. I don’t think you’d be committing a sin by asking him to help us out. And no. Relias has no relationship with him.”

I hope I’m right on that one.

And so, Father Baram, bravest of all priests, called for the blessings of Abraham Abrams. Nora watched closely as the stick in his hands quivered, an electric blue glow coating its length. Father Baram gasped and dropped the stick, taking several steps back.

“Well, what do you know! It worked! And hey! None of us even got smited!” She then considered her dual orb staff. “Hmm...”

I glanced at the still-horified Father Baram. “I don’t think he’s up for a second round,” I advised.

“That’s fine,” she said absently, hefting the staff. “I think I get it now.”

And with that, she started to glow purple. She glanced up at the dark sky and shouted. “Hey, Gramps! Take it out of petty cash, will ya?!”

And I’ll be damned if her staff didn’t glow electric blue.
