Forty-five seconds.

I slammed through the portal. Lightning welled around me. Ellen was out of position—and maybe even on the wrong side of the portal, in the God of Thunder’s world. That didn’t matter. We’d planned for this.

All around me, the Earth shuddered in pain.

There wasn’t time to take stock.

We’d come out right where Jessie and I had guessed. The Governing Council had known for a long time that specific portal worlds tended to connect in similar places. That didn’t mean that every Warren would always connect in the same place, but if a specific Paragon connected their world to Earth, then—barring intent on the part of the Paragon—they’d connect in more or less the same place the next time. Most of the time, that intent didn’t exist. We’d banked on it here.

Most of the portals connecting the God of Thunder’s world to ours had opened to the Desert Wind Guild’s building, in the northwest corner of Surprise.

And we had a surprise waiting for him.

Forty-four seconds.

I charged the God of Thunder. The timer ticked down. Laminar Blade crackled to life, water coating my sword. The dragon’s tail whipped by, but I’d pulled my aura in as far as it’d go. The Stormshade Anvil tightened my reflexes, and the thrashing lightning tail missed me by inches. It ripped across the Storm’s Blade. A jagged cut appeared in the monstrous tail; lightning vomited from it in a torrent that consumed an entire house across the street before his Health kicked in, healing it almost instantly.

Forty-three. Forty-two.

The tail’s backswing caught me. No delver could have dodged it, and I didn’t try. The impact drove the air from my lungs. Thunder boomed as I flew out of control down the street—the shockwave obliterated windows across Surprise. But the Stormshade Anvil was still running, and as I slammed into the ground, I hit perfectly to roll. Asphalt ripped into my skin, opening raspberries across every inch of exposed flesh. On top of the blistering skin, it hurt.

I didn’t care.

Forty-one.

Pain was an abstraction. It didn’t matter in the face of the timer. Lightning Dance. I surged down the street, flickering from corner to corner as my five charges of lightning-quick teleportation popped me back and forth. Thunder ripped across the street. Stoplights and lamps popped and exploded, echoing across Phoenix.

Forty seconds.

All around us, the 303 Wall’s defenses went active. Towers of Scripts shot into the air, suppressing the God of Thunder slightly and pouring extra speed, strength, and toughness into the delvers across Phoenix. Auras flared as every delver fighting in the streets got a sudden second wind. But the main trap wasn’t the Scripts, or even the dozens of Bindings that tried and failed to gain purchase in the God of Thunder.

It was the cannons. Hundreds of Traynor-Olverholz Cannons opened fire. Every single one of them was aimed right at us.

I parried a claw. The impact tore a muscle from bone, and pain ripped through my arm. But I needed the Rainfall Charge. The shells flew through the air, seemingly in slow motion. As they closed in, I used Mist Trance and vanished, immaterial.

The God of Thunder’s tail slammed into my body a moment later. Asphalt shattered to sand below me, then superheated into a sheet of glass. That glass shattered as dozens of round cannon shots thudded through me and into the God of Thunder. Each of those dented the road; some slammed through empty houses, turning them into so much timber and roof tiles. Others destroyed palm trees and cacti. The air around us turned to a mulched, shredded suburbia.

Then the explosions started.

All across the God of Thunder’s body, shells detonated. Shockwaves rippled, and he roared in pain. The guns had hurt him. The plan was working. The mulch in the air redoubled, red and pink gravel joining the deadly shrapnel whirling around us.

Thirty-five seconds.

Ten seconds had already vanished. We’d hurt him, but whether we’d done enough to evict the God of Thunder from Earth on time, I couldn’t say.

Mist Trance expired. Shells kept slamming down around us as the 303 Wall’s guns went into rapid-fire, burning through their ammunition as quickly as they could.

And the God of Thunder erupted upward through the explosions and shrapnel.

“This is your idea of allies, kid? Let’s see how they handle true power.”

His body flailed through the air as his massive, world-spanning wings beat, and he aimed for a building far to the west. Sky Harbor. Where, if everything had gone according to plan, one Ophelia St. Vrain waited for him.

As he moved, spells whipped up at him from the low-ranking delvers fighting in the city streets. None of them were enough to stop him, or even to hurt him; he didn’t even bother with an active response. Nor did he need to. Below him, lightning ravaged the city streets, shredding delvers with his passing.

I followed. Stormrunner kept me close enough to keep up the pressure, but even with every Script in the 303 Wall running, I couldn’t possibly catch up or stop the God of Thunder.

Thirty-two.

Across Phoenix, a building collapsed. A ten-story apartment complex, deep in the center of downtown. One moment it stood strong. The next, a tremor buckled the ground, and it was nothing but liquid glass and metal. I closed my eyes; hopefully, the evacuation had pulled everyone out. But I couldn’t do anything except pursue the God of Thunder.

Thirty.

Things happened quickly. We were both moving faster than anyone had ever moved through Phoenix’s streets. Indian School Road turned to molten tar and gravel from the heat of our movement. Rain—rain, in Phoenix—turned to steam long before it touched my skin.

Twenty-eight.

Twenty-seven.

The God of Thunder stopped. Not under his own power, but like he’d hit a wall.

We stood outside of Sky Harbor. Asphalt burned, the scent both acrid and strangely cloying. Gigantic icicles had formed like spears sticking out toward us, their melting points jagged and dripping. In the distance, a winged serpent’s corpse and a flaming egg told me exactly what had happened. The Phoenix Reborn had fallen, but he—or someone—had killed a Paragon here not minutes ago. A Paragon of ice and snow.

But none of that had the God of Thunder’s attention.

Twenty-six.

“Please take your fight elsewhere. There are innocents here, and by order of Guildmaster Gerald, I must protect them,” Ophelia St. Vrain said in her usual madwoman monotone.

The God of Thunder stared at the woman standing in front of him. She was shockingly thin. No muscle. No fat. A loose black dress, hair in a bun. No ornaments.

But there was something wrong with this woman. He couldn’t place it. And he didn’t have time to.

By his count, the world had twenty-four seconds before he caused real damage. A minute and six seconds before he ended the city and turned the desert around it to rubble. One minute thirty-four to destabilize the planet. Already, his storm spread out against the barrier around Phoenix. It would find a way out, and when it did, it’d devastate the world.

This woman—with her dead eyes and even more dead voice—was slowing him down.

And slowing down was agony for him.

The universe itself was too small for the God of Thunder.

This world was even smaller. The pressure it exerted on him, and that he exerted back on it, was painful. Not debilitating. Far from it. Nothing this world had done up until one second ago had been enough to slow him down in any way that mattered. But frustrating. Painful, like a mosquito he couldn’t slap. He hated it. He couldn’t be here. Not for long.

He shouldn’t have had to be. But this woman…this rail-thin woman he could have crushed in the time it took to blink…had stopped him. Not without strain. Blood ran down her face from her left eye, where a vein had popped, and she strained to keep the barrier up against his bulk. Every limb shook; her shoulder and elbow on her right side had dislocated from the impact, and she coughed blood. He could pop her like a grape. Kill her instantly, collapse the barrier, destroy the molten egg that was clearly one of Kade’s fallen allies, and force Kade to start making sacrifices.

It’d be easy.

It’d be fast.

It’d even be the lesson Kade needed. Once he sacrificed the people beyond the barrier—or his fallen ally—the God of Thunder would turn his attention to Phoenix’s center. The kid’s sister was his strongest friend and ally. He could feel Jessica Gerald’s touch on everything he’d run into so far.

Instead of crushing the barrier, the God of Thunder turned his attention back to Kade Noelstra.

Twenty-three.

Twenty-two.

I couldn’t lose.

Not here.

But the God of Thunder attacked with a fury I hadn’t seen before. His jaw slammed shut inches from my face; the thunder from his bite crashed against me, sending me spinning across the tarmac back to the east. Lightning ripped from his maw even as I recovered. I recognized the spell. Hand of the Thunder God. Asphalt and gravel, sand and sagebrush—anything in the beam of blue-white lightning vanished, burned to ash. Then the ash burned behind it.

Claws ripped trenches in the ground around me as I dodged. Lightning Dance threw me back and forth, barely missing a decapitating claw only to be thrown aside by another shockwave.

Twenty.

Nineteen.

I didn’t give him the initiative willingly. This was a gambit on his part, and the best thing I could do was not fall for it. Every second here clearly hurt him; my job was to make it hurt bad enough to force him out. As shells hammered the ground and explosions ripped across the airfield, I lashed out. Stormfall sped me up. The Storm’s Blade left burning, blue-white cuts all across the God of Thunder’s body. Windfall ripped into him, slowing him down. I used Cloudburst to stop his next Hand of the Thunder God, only for him to pivot out from under it and send another blast skyward.

I didn’t give it to him willingly, but he took it anyway.

As we hammered at each other, the Stormshade Anvil giving me the preternatural speed and reaction time I needed to keep up with an SS-Plus-Ranked monster like him, the wounds stacked up. Deep cuts that gouged through my gauntlets and shredded my arms’ skin and muscles. Burn marks across my face and scalp. A bruising blow from his tail that shattered ribs and sent me tumbling. Stamina poured into the wounds. My delver’s healing slowly repaired them, sped up by Torrentborne’s innate healing.

Fifteen.

Time was running out. Buildings groaned and shuddered all across Phoenix. The 303 Wall shook. The God of Thunder’s presence in the world had to be removed. Otherwise…

A screeching scream of what sounded like agony rose from behind me. I took the quarter-second to look, then turned, back to the God of Thunder, as the Governing Council’s tower collapsed in a cloud of dust.

“You have to leave,” I said.

The God of Thunder nodded. “I do. Will you make me?”

Ten seconds. Time was running out.

I shifted stance to Cyclone. Rolling Thunder. The whole Hand of the Thunder God combo. But instead of the Hand, I cast Lightning Chain, aiming it right at the God of Thunder. It wrapped around his body, pinning a wing to his long, snake-like torso.

Nine.

“What are you doing, kid?”

“Making you.” I used Stormbreak before he could react. The lightning flowers blossomed around a dozen different delvers who’d gotten nearby. I couldn’t exclude them all, but I tried. Then I held the attack back as the God of Thunder broke through my empowered Lightning Chain like it was nothing.

The storm broke.

Six seconds.

More buildings came down around us as the God of Thunder pulsed with electricity. His Mana drained into a never-ending blossom of lightning that lasted maybe two seconds, or maybe less. But in that time, I’d shifted stances again, this time to Thunderlord. Laminar Blade and Shockwave covered my sword as I attacked the monstrous dragon’s face. Cuts opened, and this time, Stormbreak’s lightning poured into the wounds.

Three seconds.

There was no time. Not to do anything but attack. The God of Thunder’s lightning claw ripped across my stomach. Burning hot pain followed. He’d opened it up, and blood poured from the wound. I didn’t even bother redirecting Stamina. Instead, I poured on the attacks. Thrusts and lunges punched deep wounds into the God of Thunder’s face—wounds that burned and cauterized instantly, even against the storm dragon’s lightning body. I threw myself into the attack even harder as my last seconds vanished.

Two.

One.

The God of Thunder vanished.

I whirled, looking for him. Where had he gone? With only a second remaining, the city was as good as destroyed, and the rest of Earth was soon to follow. I’d…

A golden portal hung in the air in front of me.

He’d left.

The pain hit me. The pain, and the exhaustion. My sliced-open stomach screamed in agony. The cuts on my arms, the blisters on my face, and the scorch marks on every exposed scrap of skin all burned as I came out of the Stormshade Anvil’s focus state. My muscles, if anything, burned more than that. I’d been moving at a greater-than-S-Rank speed for forty-five seconds, never once dropping below a full sprint.

But I’d won. Not the war. Not even the battle. But a small fight. I’d forced the God of Thunder out of my world.

Another building collapsed far away, dozens of stories folding in on themselves and crashing down into the streets far away in Downtown. The 303 Wall looked gutted and broken, with chunks calving away like icebergs from a glacier. All around me, the airport and city streets we’d fought in had been utterly destroyed.

And, as I sucked in superheated, ozone-tainted air and knelt on the burning ground, I caught a glimpse of a handful of delvers. The city’s S-Rankers—or some of them. Not all of them had been able to keep up. A handful were almost certainly dead; the God of Thunder clearly hadn’t focused exclusively on me. Every one of them had taken wounds that’d keep them out of any real fight. But they’d been there to defend Phoenix.

I had allies.

Ellen, waiting for me on the far side of the golden portal, where she’d almost certainly been hard at work. Jeff. Sarah Cullman and Nathan Anders. Jessie—holed up somewhere safe, hopefully. The Desert Wind Guild, and the other guilds in Phoenix. Even the Guardians were fighting against this.

But before I could say anything, Terrel Young pointed at the portal. “We’ll clean up here. That worm’s hurt. You can finish this.”

“I know.” I gritted my teeth and forced myself to stand even as my wounds slowly scabbed over. The God of Thunder’s claws had destroyed my abdominals, and I kept myself upright through sheer force of will. But that’d be enough.

My allies had my back against the rest of the God of Thunder’s assault on Earth. I had to be the spearhead in our pursuit of the monster. But I wouldn’t be alone.

Like I said, Ellen had almost certainly been hard at work.
