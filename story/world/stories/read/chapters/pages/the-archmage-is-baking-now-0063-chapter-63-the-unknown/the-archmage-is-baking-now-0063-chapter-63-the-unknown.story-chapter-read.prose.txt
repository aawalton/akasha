Jane ended up working well into the quiet dead of the night. The town outside her windows went dark and silent as she experimented, trying one meat-and-seasonings combination after another in search of something approximating the recipe she had in her mind. Most were failures, but a few of them worked. A bit more fine-tuning of the successful ventures finally brought her pretty close to what she had been imagining.

The resulting bun had a bit more bread and a bit less filling than Hattie’s fare, and the filling itself was lighter and more vegetable-based. It wasn’t better or worse than what Jane had enjoyed in Trailor, but she thought it would probably fit the tastes of her customers better.

Trailor was a mining town. A miner wanted something he could eat in a few minutes that would sustain his pick-swinging for a full day. The people in Glenfall, though, were more commercial than industrial. And while Hattie’s buns were delicious, they were heavier than what the average Glenfallian ate on a day-to-day basis. Jane wanted to make something people would buy whole baskets of and use as family snacks for days.

She wrapped up the successful fillings and stored them in her cold box, ready for the morning’s cooking. Then, after a quick clean-up, she finally went to bed to eke out what few hours of sleep she had left.

—

The next morning was hell, but it was a hell she had chosen for herself. Jane went through the motions of her baking routine sleepily. Letting her muscle memory make up for the sleep she had missed, she made loaves, keln, cookies, cakes, and all the normal staples her customers had come to expect.

Once those daily batches were in the ovens, she got to work on the buns. She divided the dough into dozens of pieces, rolled the pieces out, and piled on the filling. After sealing each bun, she baked them until they were golden brown. She ate a few of them herself for breakfast, hot out of the oven. They would work better as a lunch, but even so, she loved every bite.

Her customers had already begun to filter in, buying the breads they would use with their breakfasts and lunches. The day stretched on as Jane’s goods steadily disappeared into her delivery box or her customers’ shopping bags. By late afternoon, she was almost entirely cleared out.

With a certain amount of displeasure, she noted that the new buns hadn’t really moved. There had been questions about them, but none of her customers were ready to integrate them into their day just yet, apparently.

“Miss Jane!” Dave appeared at her door, his hand resting on his pouch. He looked far more awake and energetic than he had seemed in Trailor. “I’m sorry it took me so long to get down here. I had to get settled at Frank’s place, and then talk to some people about work. But now I want to buy some of your bread.”

He looked around the shop, and Jane watched all his excitement evaporate as he realized just how little bread was left for him to buy. “Oh. I guess I’m a little late. Sorry, Miss Jane.”

For a second, Jane thought he might actually cry. She took pity on him instantly. Reaching behind the counter, she retrieved a loaf she had been saving for her own dinner and set it in front of Dave. She slid a bread knife over to him as well, and then turned to her cold box and began withdrawing meat and vegetables.

“You cut that. I’ll just fry these up, and we’ll have a whole dinner of it.”

“Miss Jane! I wanted to be a customer!”

“You will be, some day. Today, you have to eat with me, or I’ll be bored. I still have a good hour before I can leave.”

That wasn’t quite true. Jane’s work day was essentially over. She only had the buns left to sell, and it was becoming increasingly obvious that they wouldn’t, at least not today. She could leave any time she wanted to.

But she had promised Peter she would look after his son. So far, she had done next to nothing in pursuit of keeping that promise. Getting Dave’s stomach filled in good company seemed like a step in the right direction.

Jane pulled out a pan and set it over the fire, adding oil and waiting for it to heat. She tossed in some of the vegetables she had left over from the night before, along with strips of chicken and a heavy dash of pepper and spices. The smell filled the shop in seconds.

"That smells good, Miss Jane," Dave said, watching her work. “Real good.”

"It's nothing fancy." Jane stirred the mixture until the chicken was cooked through and the vegetables had softened. She cut two thick slices from the loaf Dave had prepared, set them on plates, and scooped the hot filling onto each one. "Here. Eat."

They dug in with little fanfare. This wasn’t a masterpiece of a meal, but it was good, hearty, and savory in a way that didn’t take many words to enjoy.

Between bites, Dave started talking about the town, telling Jane all about his initial impressions and experiences.

"There's so much happening here," he marveled. "I was never much for mining, but that was all there was in Trailor. Here, there’s everything. I met a blacksmith, and a woman who raises bees for honey, and her husband who makes candles from the wax. And your friend Allen. He said to say hello."

Jane looked up. "You met Allen?"

"Frank had me bring him a broken hinge to fix. We talked for a bit." Dave took another bite and chewed, paying little attention to manners as he continued his full-mouthed conversation. "He seems like a good person. I'm glad about that, Miss Jane. You deserve that."

Jane wasn't sure what to say. She settled for nodding and focusing on her food. He was probably right, she admitted to herself. There was nothing wrong with knowing she deserved good things, as unnatural as it was to hear it said out loud.

Dave noticed none of her discomfort. Finishing his last few bites of dinner with enthusiasm, he sat back. His eyes wandered around the shop and settled on the basket of unsold buns sitting on the counter. "What are those?"

"Something new I'm trying. Take one."

He reached over and grabbed a bun, biting into it without hesitation. His eyebrows shot up.

"These are great. Like Hattie's, but lighter." He paused. "Not that I like them better than hers."

"That's fine. They aren't meant to be exactly the same thing."

"Why do you have so many left?"

Jane sighed. "They didn't sell. People asked about them, but nobody actually wanted to buy one. I figure I'll have to learn how to explain them better."

Dave stared at the basket for a moment, thinking. Then he stood up and grabbed the whole thing.

"What are you doing?"

"I'm taking these." He was already heading for the door. "I'm going to get you some customers for them. Make sure you make the same amount tomorrow. You'll need them."

He was out the door before Jane could figure out how to respond. She stood there for a moment, blinking at the empty space where the basket had been.

He had looked so eager to help, she decided to ignore the fact that he had technically walked off with several coins’ worth of food. She finished her own dinner, put the few unsold products into the cold box, and stepped into her backyard. Sitting on one of the stone benches, she watched the last bits of the afternoon work themselves out.

Allen showed up a short while later, just as the sun was starting to get serious about setting. Jane motioned for him to sit down on the bench, then went inside to make him a plate, scooping the last of the vegetables and chicken onto bread and bringing it out to him.

He kissed her hello, took the plate, and moved over so she could sit next to him on the bench. Jane tucked herself against his side as he ate, watching the sky turn red above them.

They sat like that for a pleasant hour or so, not talking much more than was needed. Jane was tired, and once again glad to have someone who was more than willing to sit with her in silence when the situation called for it. She got the impression he’d had a hard day, too, and eventually found the energy to ask him about it.

"How was your day?"

"Tiring." Allen rolled his shoulders, stretching them. "I've been helping Brit in the shop. His work is heavy labor over a hot forge. I'm not used to it."

"That was kind of you."

"A big order came in, I guess. He needed the help.” He nudged her shoulder gently. How about yours?"

"Fine. Just another day baking." She yawned. “Same as always.”

"Isn't that what you like?"

"Very much." Jane paused, trying to put into words a feeling that had started gnawing at her as soon as Dave ran out of her shop with a basket full of purloined goods. “I don't know. As glad as I am to be back, it almost feels like it's time for me to have some sort of trouble. Like I'm in the calm before a storm, even though there's no reason to imagine anything is wrong at all."

Allen turned to look at her. "You are sure you don’t know what’s wrong?"

"Nothing. Really, nothing that I know of. I'm just worried, and I don't know why." She shook her head. "It's silly."

"Maybe," Allen said. "Maybe not. Sometimes people have feelings about things for a reason." He pulled her closer against his side. "But whatever it is, whenever it rears its head, I'll be there for you."

Allen left shortly after dark. It hadn’t been a long visit, and as Jane watched him walk away down the street, she found herself counting the hours they had actually spent together since she got back into town. Only a few. Early in the relationship, that would have seemed like plenty. At the moment, it felt like not nearly enough, even as pleasant as those hours had been.

She decided she would make more time for him soon. Once she was properly settled back into her routine, once the shop was running smoothly again, she would find a way to see him more. He deserved that, and so did she.

For now, though, she was tired. She went inside, washed up, and fell gladly into bed.

—

The next morning, though not hell, was still busy.

The baking went more smoothly now that she had a full night of sleep powering her efforts. It wasn’t much faster, but she wasted fewer ingredients on stupid mistakes and felt more confident about the final products.

Jane thought briefly about vowing never to stay up late before a workday again, then decided it just wasn’t practical. She needed sleep, yes, but she also needed to live. Staying up late sometimes was part of the kind of living she wanted to do.

Today, the customers didn’t need to notice she was back. They knew it from yesterday. The word had even gone out to the regulars who had temporarily shifted their business to other bakers. The smell of fresh bread wafted through the streets, carrying back Jane’s business, person by person. By midmorning, she had sold through most of her stock, though it looked like she’d make it without having to turn away too many hungry latecomers.

The only exception to her success was the new meat bun. She had made another batch, following the same recipe she had developed before, and they looked just as appealing as everything else as they sat in their basket. But her regular customers walked right past them, buying their usual loaves and pastries without any thought of experimenting with a new unknown.

Jane tried not to let it bother her. New things took time to catch on, she told herself. People were creatures of habit, and they bought what they knew. She would figure out how to introduce the buns properly. Eventually.

I shouldn’t let it get to me. If this is my biggest problem, I’m a lucky girl.

Shaking her head, she turned away from the disappointingly full basket and began prepping her latest dinner hold order.

Still… it’s not like it ISN’T a problem, right?
