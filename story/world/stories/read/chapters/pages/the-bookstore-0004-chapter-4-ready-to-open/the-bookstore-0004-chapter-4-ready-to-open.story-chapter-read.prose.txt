Scrubbing, ripping, sanding, and nailing planks back into place, Anne spent the next week repairing furniture, walls, doors, and windows. The result was... acceptable.

She had to learn that word quickly.

Anne was not a professional carpenter, mason, or general repair worker, and perfectionism was a luxury she could not afford. After two full days of redoing the same pieces only to end with results that were barely any better, she finally accepted a painful truth. Good enough now was better than an excellent that might never exist. When the bookstore could afford it, she would pay proper craftsmen to fix everything the way it deserved.

Until then, acceptable would have to do.

Fortunately, the week passed without serious problems. No beasts came sniffing around the building at night. No thieves wandered in from the road. No curious townsfolk arrived to pry before she was ready. Being away from Mountainend and close to a more desolate stretch of the forbidden forest had disadvantages, but it also gave her something precious—peace.

For sleeping arrangements, Anne chose one of the first-floor rooms. The bed itself was hardly worth the name, but with one of her thick furry coats spread over it, it became tolerable. Some of the other rooms still had mattresses, though time, dust, and old dampness had ruined them beyond recovery. She ignored such inconveniences. She only had to endure it a little longer. Once her books and appliances arrived, the real work would begin.

She also cleaned portions of the second and third floors, just enough to keep future trouble from piling up, but left both levels empty for now. There was no reason to waste effort on spaces she could neither use nor maintain properly.

Her main task became the bookshelves.

Once again, fortune favored her just enough to keep hope alive. The former owner had left behind an absurd amount of furniture, and much of it could be dismantled and reshaped into shelving. It was not ideal wood, and the pieces had not been made for this purpose, but they were solid enough, and most importantly, they were free.

That last detail gave them considerable charm.

With her old schematics and years of familiarity guiding her hands, the shelves proved easier to make than the rest of the repairs. Not pretty, certainly. Nothing intricate, elegant, or fine enough to impress a traditional craftsman. But the sizes were workable, the planks close enough in measure that they only needed trimming and smoothing, and Anne knew what mattered most—stability, depth, support, spacing, and the small practical details that protected books from neglect.

Her hands paid for the work. By the middle of the week, roughness and small cuts had started to cover her palms and fingers. Still, each new shelf made the tavern area look a little more like a place worthy of knowledge.

The backyard was also doing better than expected.

The blessed seeds had taken well to the soil. Perhaps it was the nearby magic of the forest, or the special underground water, or simply the quality of the seeds. Whatever the reason, the first shoots were rising fast. The soul tea tree in particular made her check on it far more often than necessary, each inspection feeding the same stubborn satisfaction.

The well connection worked exactly as intended, too. By activating the transfer array indoors, she could pull water from the well without carrying bucket after bucket through the building. Better still, the personal energy cost remained low because the bookstore circle carried most of the burden once the link had been established.

That was not the only pleasant surprise.

The circle beneath the building was absorbing energy far faster than Anne had predicted.

She had expected strong results because of the Wall’s proximity and the surrounding environment, but this was better than strong. The central crystal gathered energy at an impressive pace, its steady glow growing fuller each day. Had she chosen a lesser crystal, it might already have cracked from overload. As it stood, the situation was more exciting than dangerous. More storage would be useful. Very useful. If the bookstore survived its early months, buying two or three more quality crystals would have to become a priority.

Piece by piece, shelf by shelf, the public room began to take shape.

That was when another problem finally cornered her.

The name.

It seemed almost ridiculous, after all the years of preparation, after the cost, the study, the travel, the planning, the risks, that she still had not chosen a proper name for the store. But there had always been something more urgent to occupy her attention.

Now, however, it could not be delayed.

A bookstore without a name felt unfinished. The name would be the face she offered the world. Worse, it would be the face remembered later. Anne had no intention of changing it after opening. That would feel wrong, almost like admitting she had not known what she was building in the first place.

At first, she tried the obvious routes.

History offered enough examples. Most bookstores of the old days had carried either the family name of the owner or some elegant reference to their specialty. But both paths felt ill-suited to the present age. Her ancestral line meant nothing to most people now. As for specialty, what use was such a distinction in a world that had forgotten what a real bookstore even was?

So the name had to do more.

It had to carry purpose.

It had to mean something to the few who still remembered, attract those who did not, and establish a place in the minds of future customers before they ever stepped inside.

Anne filled a scrap sheet with possibilities and crossed them out one by one. Some were too old-fashioned or too arrogant in the wrong way. Others were too plain.

Then, somewhere between frustration and tiredness, the answer appeared so simply that she almost dismissed it on sight.

She stared at it.

Laughed once.

Then stared again.

“Moruh,” she said, not looking up from the paper, “what do you think of naming my bookstore... ‘The Bookstore’?”

The familiar, who had been floating broken tiles from upstairs into a neat pile for later disposal, stopped in the air. He turned slowly toward her with the expression of a creature reconsidering every decision that had led him to this moment.

“Mistress,” he said carefully, “is that not a little... too simple?”

“It is,” Anne admitted. “That is part of the appeal.”

Moruh narrowed his eyes, clearly bracing himself for whatever explanation would follow.

Anne set down the charcoal nub and leaned back, thinking through it as she spoke.

“Almost nobody remembers bookstores properly anymore. Most people will assume I sell magic artifacts, scroll bundles, or some decorative nonsense. If I call it ‘The Bookstore,’ they will have to notice it. They will have to wonder what kind of confidence, or audacity, it takes to use such a name.”

She was warming to the idea quickly now.

“And more than that,” she continued, “the name does not only describe the business. It claims meaning. To most people, it will help recreate what a bookstore is supposed to be. To those ancient enough to remember, it will mean something else—the bookstore still standing, perhaps even the only real one left. And if the craft truly revives...” Her lips curved with rising pride. “Then later generations may look back and see it as the first bookstore of this era. The one that brought the meaning back.”

Moruh continued staring at her for a moment, then looked away with weary patience.

Anne did not mind. By then, the name had settled inside her with weight.

And thus, The Bookstore was born.

Three days after her expected delivery date, Anne was still watching the road with bookmancer focus.

Her estimate had only been the minimum likely travel time from the province capital. Realistically, the courier could arrive any time within the week and still be considered punctual. She knew that, and reminded herself of it repeatedly. But it changed nothing.

Every hour felt too slow.

Every distant shape became suspicious.

Every breeze that stirred dust across the road made her step outside again.

By then, the shelves she needed for the initial layout were ready, but Anne kept making more anyway. She told herself it was because the extra ones would be useful later when the upper floors were opened. That was true. It was simply not the main reason. The real one was that her hands needed work while the rest of her waited.

So she moved in and out of the building through the morning and into the afternoon, checking the road, adjusting shelves, checking the road again, then pretending not to be disappointed each time she saw nothing.

Then, at last, in the middle of the afternoon, she spotted movement.

Small at first, distant, and then clearer.

Anne froze, took a deep breath, and forced her expression into something resembling composure. Her books were finally coming. Her books. After all the planning, the bargaining, the sleepless work, and the humiliating arithmetic of expenses, the true bones of the bookstore were finally arriving.

She refused to greet them looking like a fool.

The approaching shapes resolved into a simple coach followed by four large carts. Each cart was covered with the hide of some sturdy beast stretched tight over a reinforced frame. Anne knew exactly how much that sight had cost her, and thinking of it still made part of her heart ache.

The mercenary company from the capital had been reliable, but reliability at that level was never cheap. One gold for the service itself, then forty-five silver for each premium cart. Those carts had spatial arrays worked into them, doubling the useful carrying space, and the beast-hide covers protected against storms and rough weather far better than common canvas ever could. In total, with additional gratuity and unavoidable practical costs, she had spent around three gold.

A more ordinary cart would have cost a fraction of that.

It also would have been a disaster.

Common carts offered almost no protection against rain, and her collection would have required nearly twice as many. That meant more animals, more handlers, more time, more risk, and more hands touching books they had no right to handle carelessly. In the end, the expensive option had been the sane one.

As the group came closer, she recognized Dorgh riding with them and, beside the convoy, the hazel-haired mercenary in charge of the delivery.

Ceres.

A month ago, when Anne had signed the contract, she had already marked the half-beast woman as a possible future customer. Reliable, practical, visibly strong, and clearly experienced in travel—such people often needed something, and more importantly, often came back.

Perhaps, Anne thought, she might recover at least a little silver from this relationship eventually.

By the time the carts reached the building, Anne had arranged her expression into her best business smile—warm enough to welcome, restrained enough to suggest composure, and not so bright that anyone would guess how close she was to running straight to the cargo.

“Welcome, Mr. Dorgh. Ms. Ceres,” she said. “I hope you had a pleasant trip. How about coming in for some rest and refreshments before we unload the books?”

Dorgh looked at her with mild surprise.

“Ms. Anne, you look well. Ms. Ceres reached town around noon, and I invited her to eat and rest there first, but she wanted to finish the job quickly, so we came straight away.” He glanced toward the building, then back at her. “Would it not be better to unload first and take our guests into town afterward?”

The question was sensible.

He had seen the state of the place before. Even after repairs, he likely still imagined it as barely habitable. On top of that, as a town official, he probably felt a lingering duty to receive visitors properly—especially mercenaries and merchants who might bring business into Mountainend.

Ceres offered Anne a polite greeting of her own, her manner open and straightforward. The bronze-tier mercenary looked travel-worn but alert, the sort of person who preferred to finish important matters before indulging in comfort. Her earlier impression of Anne had likely improved on sight alone. Young, respectful, well-spoken, and clearly serious about her enterprise.

Anne answered before Dorgh could keep pressing.

“There is no need, Mr. Dorgh. The bookstore may still be a little shabby, but I have enough space and enough food to receive friends. Provided, of course, that Ms. Ceres and the others do not mind water and bread for now. Later, they can always go into town for something more generous.”

She spoke lightly, making it clear that she took no offense at the concern while also setting the limits honestly. No feast, and no false promises. Only basic hospitality.

Yet it was not the offer of bread and water that caught their attention.

It was the word.

The bookstore.

From now on, people would come. And when they did, Anne intended to greet them the same way.

Welcome to The Bookstore.
