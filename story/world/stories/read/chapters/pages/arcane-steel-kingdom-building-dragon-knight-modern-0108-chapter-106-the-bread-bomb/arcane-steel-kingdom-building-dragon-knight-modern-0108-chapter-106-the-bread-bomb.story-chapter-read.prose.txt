We hit the deck of The Vengeance hard.

The landing wasn’t graceful. It was a scramble of claws against reinforced steel, a blast of displaced air, and the smell of ozone burning off Kaelus’s scales. The wind up here was vicious, tearing at my uniform and whipping Nyxia’s hair into a frenzy. We slid off the dragon’s back, my boots ringing against the deck plates, my heart still hammering a rhythm that had nothing to do with the flight and everything to do with the insane promise I’d just made.

Nyxia smoothed her midnight-blue gown, her hands trembling just slightly. The adrenaline of the escape was fading, replaced by the crushing reality of what we’d done. She looked at me, then at the sprawling, militarized city of steel around us, and finally at the ring of Mark IV-S Specters guarding the perimeter.

“I need a moment,” she said, her voice tight. “To… adjust.”

“Guest quarters are prepped,” I said, pointing toward the command tower. “Standard protocol. Hot water, secure comms if you need them. No bugs.”

She shot me a look that could have stripped paint off a bulkhead, then turned on her heel and marched away, flanked by two silent automata.

I exhaled, a long, shuddering breath that rattled in my chest.

Kaelus shrank down instantly, popping into his cat-sized form and landing on my shoulder with a dissatisfied huff. That was dramatic, he projected, sounding bored. Also, I’m starving. You promised snacks.

“I promised nothing,” I muttered, walking toward the main lift. “I promised we wouldn’t die. Snacks are a bonus.”

You have terrible priorities.

We descended into the belly of the ship, moving toward the Combat Information Center—the CIC. It was the brain of the fleet, a room usually buzzing with a hundred officers and analysts.

But tonight, I’d cleared it.

“Tes,” I murmured as the blast doors hissed open. “Seal the room. Omega-level clearance only. No one enters but the inner circle.”

[Acknowledged. Room secured. Atmosphere recycled. Welcome back, Master.]

The CIC was a cavern of cold light and humming servers. The main holographic table dominated the center, currently displaying a wireframe map of the entire continent. Standing at the far end, bathed in the blue glow of a tactical readout, was a figure encased in matte-black armor.

The Mark VI-S. The Director’s suit.

Nyxia walked in a moment later. She’d changed into something more practical—a dark grey jumpsuit provided by the ship’s quartermaster—but she still moved like royalty. Her eyes immediately locked onto the armored figure.

The tension in the room spiked.

“So,” Nyxia said, her voice dripping with a mix of respect and venom. She walked around the table, keeping her distance from the black-armored ghost. “You are the elusive Director. The Shadow Prime.”

The figure didn't move. The opaque black visor reflected nothing.

Nyxia circled closer. “You’ve been the bane of my father’s existence for three years. Your network… It’s terrifying. We called you the Shadow Order. We thought you were a separate entity, a rogue guild operating out of the Dominion. But the patterns… they always led back here.” She stopped, staring at the helmet. “My father respects you. He fears you. That is not an easy thing to achieve.”

The voice modulator in the Mark VI-S crackled, emitting a distorted, gender-neutral rasp. “I am nothing special. The tools my Lord provides are what make us unparalleled. I simply apply them.”

I cleared my throat. The sound echoed too loudly in the quiet room. Before the interrogation went any further, I had to drop the bomb. It was better to do it now, while Patricia was encased in armor, than later when she had access to sharp objects or hot tea.

“Director,” I said, my voice cracking slightly. I winced. “Before we proceed with the debriefing… There is a development.”

The black helmet turned toward me slowly. The movement was smooth, predatory.

“A development, my Lord?” the distorted voice asked.

I swallowed hard. I looked at Nyxia, who was watching me with narrow eyes, then back at the armored figure.

“Yes. A significant update to the… organizational chart.” I took a breath. “Nyxia and I… we’re married.”

The silence that followed wasn’t just quiet. It was the sound of a vacuum.

The armored figure froze.

It didn’t just stop moving. The suit went rigid. I heard the faint, high-pitched whine of servos locking up, as if the operator inside had stiffened so violently the machine had engaged its emergency stabilizers to keep from toppling over.

Whirrrr-clunk.

Patricia didn’t speak. She didn’t move. She just stood there, a statue of black steel, staring at me through that soulless visor.

But I knew. I knew exactly what was happening inside that helmet. She wasn’t processing the political ramifications. She wasn’t calculating the shift in house alliances.

She was thinking about my mother.

She was visualizing the sheer, apocalyptic fury of Duchess Seraphine Wight when she found out her baby boy had eloped without a wedding, a guest list, or—gods forbid—a cake. Patricia was calculating the blast radius of that anger, and she was realizing we were all standing at ground zero.

“Married,” the voice modulator rasped. It sounded like a death rattle.

“It was… tactically necessary,” I added weakly.

The helmet tilted just a fraction of an inch. It was the most judgmental movement I had ever seen a machine make.

“Tactically,” the voice repeated.

“Yes.”

A long pause. Then, a sigh hissed through the helmet’s external speakers—a sound of profound, weary resignation.

Nyxia narrowed her eyes. She was probing, looking for a crack in the armor, a hint of the person beneath the legend who seemed so rattled by a wedding announcement. “Modesty? From a spy? Unlikely. Take off the helmet. If we are to be… allies… I want to see the face of the person who outmaneuvered House Black.”

The armored figure reached up. There was a hiss of depressurizing seals, a mechanical click. The black helmet lifted and retracted.

Nyxia braced herself, expecting a scarred veteran, a demon, or perhaps another exiled elf.

Instead, a cascade of brown hair tumbled out. Patricia blinked, her face perfectly composed, looking exactly as she did when she fluffed pillows in the castle—though her eyes were currently wide with the thousand-yard stare of a woman who has seen her own doom.

“Would you care for tea, Lady Nyxia?” Patricia asked, her voice returning to its normal, polite cadence, though it was slightly higher than usual. “I believe the chamomile blend helps with shock. And I need a cup before I face Her Grace.”

Nyxia froze. Her brain seemed to stutter, reboot, and crash. She looked at the lethal, high-tech power armor. She looked at the woman inside it.

“The… maid?” Nyxia whispered. Her voice rose an octave. “The maid? My father’s network was dismantled by the head maid?”

Patricia stepped out of the armor. The suit opened up like a mechanical flower, allowing her to step free in her crisp, pristine uniform. She walked over to a small kitchenette built into the wall of the CIC and began boiling water with aggressive, jerky movements.

“I prefer the term ‘Executive Assistant,’” Patricia corrected gently. She slammed a jar of sugar onto the counter. “Sugar?”

Nyxia slumped into a chair, rubbing her temples. “I need something stronger than tea. I need to rethink my entire life.”

Kaelus snickered in my mind. She looks broken. I like her.

I walked over to the main console, hiding a smirk, though my hands were still sweating from Patricia’s reaction. “Focus. We have work to do. Tes, bring up the Oracle feeds.”

The center of the table lit up. The wireframe map vanished, replaced by high-resolution video feeds streaming directly from the satellite network. It wasn’t magic scrying; it was raw optics, unblockable and persistent.

Nyxia looked up, her despair momentarily forgotten. “This… this is real-time?”

“Delayed by point-zero-four seconds,” I said, tapping the glass. “We see everything.”

I swiped a hand through the air, bringing up a specific feed. It was the interior of a lavish bedroom in the Cinderfall Palace. The image was crisp, the audio clear.

Prince Ignis was pacing. He was red-faced, screaming at a young woman cowering by the bed—one of his concubines.

“Too hot!” Ignis shrieked, snatching a teacup from a tray. “You useless cow! Can you do nothing right?”

He threw the cup. Boiling liquid splashed across the girl’s face. She screamed, falling to the floor. Ignis didn’t stop. He kicked her, shouting about incompetence, about stress, about the indignity of his position.

The CIC went dead silent.

I winced. I hadn’t expected this. The Oracle was tasked with monitoring command decisions, troop movements, and strategic data. Instead, it had spit out a raw, unfiltered view of a monster in his bedroom. It felt intrusive in a way that mapping a coastline didn’t—a jarring, ugly glitch in my sterile war room.

Beside me, Nyxia stood up. Her face was granite. No tears. No trembling. She walked to the console, her eyes locked on the golden-haired prince as he kicked the sobbing girl again. She looked like a statue carved from ice, watching a fire burn.

“Lord Wight,” she said. Her voice wasn't soft. It was flat. Dead.

I looked at her. Her expression hadn't changed, but the air around her felt colder.

“Yes?” I asked, my voice cautious.

She stepped closer to me. Too close. I could smell the jasmine in her hair, feel the heat radiating off her. She didn’t look at me; she kept her eyes glued to the screen.

“You are not allowed to have concubines,” she stated. It wasn’t a request. It was a law of physics being written in real-time.

I gulped. “Understood. Noted. Logged.”

She nodded, satisfied, and pressed a button on the console, changing the feed.

The image shifted. Now we were looking at a dimly lit office. Her office. Or rather, the office she used when visiting House Black’s territory. The feed was coming from a microscopic perspective, likely an insect-sized drone clinging to the ceiling.

Nyxia froze again. She recognized the room. She recognized the angle.

She turned to me slowly. “Wight.”

“Yes?”

“Why is there surveillance in the guest wing of Black Manor?”

I felt the blood drain from my face. The room suddenly felt very warm. Even Kaelus, sensing the danger, floated backward and hid behind Patricia’s leg.

“Ah,” I said, my brain scrambling for a logical, strategic defense. “Well. You see. Intelligence gathering is paramount. We needed to ensure the security of the diplomatic channels…”

“You bugged my house,” she said flatly.

“We bugged everyone’s house,” I corrected quickly. “It wasn’t personal. It was… comprehensive coverage.”

“Did you have one in my room?”

The silence stretched. It stretched until it was thin enough to snap.

“The mesh network creates a web of nodes,” I babbled, using big words to hide the truth. “It requires equidistant relay points to maintain signal integrity. The architecture of your manor is quite complex, structurally speaking, so we had to—”

“So you tried to spy on me.” Her voice dropped to sub-zero temperatures.

“No! No, look,” I stammered, realizing I was being accused of being a pervert when I was actually just a paranoid warlord. “It wasn’t possible anyway! Your room… House Black’s internal wards are incredible. Top tier. We couldn’t get a clear signal. The interference was… impressive. Really. Kudos to your security team.”

I thought the compliment might save me.

It did not.

Nyxia’s eyes narrowed further. “So you tried,” she whispered, “and you failed. And then you gave up.”

Wait.

I blinked. Was she… offended that I hadn’t tried harder to stalk her?

“It was completely strategic,” I said, trying to regain the high ground. “As a fellow tactician, you understand. Low yield, high effort. It wasn’t an efficient use of bandwidth. It implied… lack of strategic interest.”

The moment the words left my mouth, I knew I had dug my own grave.

Lack of interest.

Nyxia stared at me. The air in the CIC seemed to crackle. She wasn’t mad I spied. She was mad I stopped spying because I decided she wasn’t interesting enough to break the encryption.

I looked at Patricia for help. Patricia was aggressively stirring her tea, refusing to make eye contact. Even Kaelus had covered his eyes with his tail.

You are so dumb, Brother, Kaelus projected. So, so dumb.

Nyxia took a deep breath, smoothing her jumpsuit. “Fine,” she said, her voice clipped. “Strategic efficiency. I understand perfectly. You have no interest.”

“That’s not what I—”

“Moving on,” she snapped. She pointed at the map. “The Aegis. It’s still in the Neutral Zone. If you want to use it for command or attack, you’re violating the treaty. The headmaster can overlook a lot, but parking a warship in a sanctuary is pushing it.”

I seized the topic change like a drowning man grabbing a rope. “Right. Yes. The ship.”

I turned to the main interface, tapping in commands with shaking fingers. “Tes, initiate propulsion. Move it out of the Neutral Zone. Keep it close enough for emergency extraction if the family needs it, but legally clear.”

[Acknowledged. Thrusters engaging. Moving to international waters.]

A deep thrum vibrated through the floor plates as the massive engines of the flagship spooled up. It was moving.

“Now,” I said, my voice steadier. “We aren’t just moving the ship. We’re mobilizing the fleet.”

I pulled up a new schematic on the main table. It wasn’t a weapon. It was a package. A silver, vacuum-sealed brick with runic script running along the edges.

“The war isn’t going to be won by killing soldiers,” I said, looking at Nyxia. “It’s going to be won by feeding them.”

Nyxia frowned, leaning over the table. “Food? You’re talking about supply lines?”

“I’m talking about Magical Meals, Ready to Eat. MMREs,” I explained. “My automated factories have been churning these out for months. High-calorie, nutrient-dense, and thanks to a little preservation magic, they actually taste like food. Roast chicken. Beef stew. Fresh bread.”

I gestured to the map, where hundreds of blue dots were lighting up across the ocean.

“The Hegemony is starving. Theron took the grain. The peasants are eating boiled leather. If we march in there killing people, we’re just another conqueror. But if we drop food? If we fill the bellies of the people Theron abandoned?”

Nyxia’s eyes widened. “You turn the populace.”

“We create a revolution,” I said. “But distribution is the nightmare. We can’t drive trucks through enemy lines. So we’re not going to.”

I swiped the screen, revealing the hangar bays of the fleet.

Nyxia gasped.

Rows upon rows of aircraft sat ready. But these weren’t the sleek Wyvern fighters she had seen before. These were monsters of burden.

“The Kantonov K-225,” I introduced the massive, six-engine cargo planes. “Heavy lifters. Each one can carry 250 tons of cargo. I have fifty of them.”

I highlighted a smaller, more aggressive-looking plane. “The Centurion C-130. Gunships for close air support, but also configured for precision airdrops. They can put a pallet of food into a village square from ten thousand feet.”

And finally, the dark, menacing shapes of the C-52 Strategic Bombers.

“And these,” I said, tapping the glass. “Usually, they carry cruise missiles. Today? They’re carpet bombing.”

“Carpet bombing?” Patricia asked, pausing with the teapot.

“With bread,” I said. “With rice. With medical supplies. We are going to flood the Cinderfall countryside with survival.”

Nyxia stared at the screen, her mind racing to catch up with the logistics. She knew war. She knew sieges. But this… this was industrial-scale benevolence used as a weapon.

“You have the capacity for this?” she asked, her voice hushed. “To feed… millions?”

“We stripped the Obsidian Dominion bare,” I said. “Every farm, every fishery, every grain silo. We processed it all. We have enough food stored in the sub-dimensional holds of the fleet to feed the entire continent for three months.”

I looked at her. “This is why we win, Nyxia. Not because I have a dragon. Not because I have lasers. We win because I have logistics. And I have intelligence.”

I tapped the screen, bringing up the live feed of the starving village I had seen earlier.

“Tes,” I commanded. “Launch the fleet. Operation Manna is a go. Turn the sky black with wings.”

[Acknowledged. Launching all heavy lift assets.]

On the screens, the hangar doors of the fleet opened. The roar of engines, even dampened by the hull, began to bleed into the CIC.

One by one, the massive K-225s lumbered down the runways and lifted into the air, defying gravity with brute force. The C-130s followed, their props blurring. And then the C-52s, dark shadows rising to block out the stars.

Nyxia watched them go, her hand resting on the console. She looked at the feed of the starving village, then at the planes carrying their salvation.

“They won’t know what hit them,” she whispered.

“No,” I agreed. “They’ll look up expecting fire, and they’ll get a feast.”

I sat back in the command chair, the adrenaline fading into a dull ache. Kaelus hopped onto my lap, curling up.

Bread bombs, he mused. Weird. But I guess it’s better than burning them.

Ideally, I replied.

Nyxia turned to me, her expression unreadable again. The anger about the spying seemed to have been filed away for later use.

“You’re a terrifying man, Alarion Wight,” she said softly.

“I’m just an engineer,” I said, closing my eyes for a second. “I just want to fix the machine.”

“And the machine is the world?”

“Something like that.”

The door to the CIC hissed open again. This time, a young elf in a cadet’s uniform stepped in, looking terrified.

“Lord Commander! A message from the Agis! Priority One!”

I opened my eyes. “Put it through.”
