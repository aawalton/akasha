The yellow sun had barely crested the city wall, bathing the West Gate in a golden hush. The guard tower seemed to exist just outside the noise of the waking city.

I stepped into Garo’s room as the familiar scent of incense wrapped around me. Earthy and sweet, ambergris again, or something similar to it. A thin trail of smoke curled toward the ceiling. The old man sat cross-legged on the rug, steam rising from the two cups of tea already set between us.

“You’re early,” he said without looking up.

“You said dawn. I don’t break appointments with people who can fight monsters.”

He chuckled and gestured to the spot across from him. “That’s a good habit to have. Sit and relax.”

I joined him on the rug, accepting the tea without a word. After a moment of shared silence, he spoke.

“Tell me,” he said. “What do you think Elemental Bloodline powers are?”

I blinked. “Energy and intent, right?”

“Not a bad answer,” he said, nodding. “But let’s build it up from the ground.”

He held up a finger. “First, every living creature has a soul: plants, beasts, people, and even monsters. That soul is the root of identity. It contains the Self, memories, and experiences. It defines affinity and determines how magic resonates in the body.”

I nodded slowly, filing it away.

“The Soul-Dealers from the Soul Realm can extract remnants of souls,” he continued. “Soul-Scribes can write books that let someone else use that soul’s power. That’s what your lightning book is: a fragment of a creature’s power, sealed in ink, paper, and intent.”

“And the creature, is it trapped in the book?” I asked.

“No, you can’t act on the true soul,” he said, shaking his head. “Only what’s left behind.”

He took a sip of tea, then pointed at me.

“Now, what you get from the book depends on where the creature came from.”

I leaned forward.

“If it came from the Elemental Bloodline Realm, you get something similar to an innate Bloodline power, a magical expression tied to a specific element or force. If it came from the Kindred Realm, you get physical traits like a gorilla’s strength, a troll’s regeneration, or a dragon’s scales.”

“So to master lightning...” I said slowly.

“You train as if you were born with it,” he finished. “The same way a Bloodline child would. With will and intent.”

He raised his hand, and a puff of smoke curled up from his fingers. It twisted into a spiral, then unraveled.

“Every Bloodline power works within certain rules, a set of limited behaviors. Some fire-blooded caster might throw fireballs. That’s easy for them. But if they want to make a bird out of flame?”

He tapped the burner lightly.

“That takes more will, more intent, and a lot more mana. While others, for some freak reason, can only cast flames in bird form and find it difficult to do simple fireballs.”

I nodded. “So magic resists you when you try something outside its predefined shape.”

“Well said. And that definition is written on the soul.”

He set his cup down and looked at me with his full attention now.

“Let’s talk about aura.”

With a push of his will, he engulfed the room with his presence. A heavy pressure settled over me, making the air feel thick and my vision narrow, as if he were suddenly the only person in the universe.

“Can you feel it?” he asked.

I nodded again.

“That’s my aura. It’s the manifestation of my soul. Aura expands as your soul grows, but it can also be trained. Aura is how your magic knows where it is allowed to act. Any external power you use, like lightning, can only manifest inside your own aura.”

“So if my aura is small... my spells won’t go far?” I asked.

“Exactly.”

He waved a hand slowly. A puff of smoke drifted to the far end of the room, then vanished as if it had struck an invisible wall.

“Intent defines the form, and aura gives it territory.”

“And mana?” I asked.

“That’s the fuel,” he said. “Everyone has some. You burn it when casting. It slowly recovers when you breathe, eat, and sleep.”

“Can I grow my mana pool?”

“Yes. It all depends on the strength of your soul,” he nodded. “Meditation, discipline, and overuse all help. Even training your body can grow your mana.”

“Until you hit your talent cap, that is.”

“My what?”

He nodded again and took another sip of tea.

“Your maximum aura strength and mana pool depend on your birth. Think of it like fruit. With proper care, fruit grows. But some people are born the size of grapes, others are apples, and some lucky bastards are watermelons.”

“And there’s no way to fight beyond our birthright?” I asked.

“Some people can break that cap. Kindred and Bloodline can evolve. Knights and Clerics can ascend. Dreamers?” He shrugged. “I have no idea how they do it.”

“I understand Holy ascension, but how does evolution work?”

“I don’t recommend chasing evolution yet,” Garo said, shaking his head. “You are far too weak for that.”

“No, it’s just... I met someone younger than me, a Kindred girl, who claims to have already gone through two evolutions.”

“Ah, you’re talking about Nakera.” He nodded. “I’ve heard of her. Her parents were civilians with zero training. The poor girl was born with one of the weakest talent caps imaginable. Do you understand what that means?”

“It means no matter how much she trains, she could never punch a tree down with her fist, unlike some other Kindred.”

“Exactly.” Garo smiled. “You can’t break the talent cap through training alone. You need an evolution, and that only happens in life-or-death situations.”

“Does that mean people can replace training by constantly living on the edge?”

“No. To break the talent cap, you first have to reach it. It takes a lot of hard work just to become eligible for evolution.”

Garo stood and stretched, then gestured toward the center of the room.

“Enough talk. It’s lesson time.”

He took a long breath through his nose, then exhaled with focus.

“Sit. Close your eyes. Feel the lightning inside you. Don’t call it. Just... listen.”

I did as told.

The world dimmed. The incense curled through my breath.

“Good,” Garo said, his voice steady. “Now cast a spark. Not strong. Just a flicker. Feel where it lands. Feel where it fades.”

I closed my eyes, drawing on that thread of crackling energy I had begun to recognize. It danced across my fingertips like dry static.

The tiniest spark leapt free. It fizzled out about ten centimeters from my fingertips.

“Push it,” he said flatly. “That’s too weak. Push it again.”

I focused harder, summoned another spark, held it a moment longer, and forced it outward. It crackled, arcing through the air, and this time it flew twenty centimeters before dying with a hiss.

“That’s better,” Garo muttered. “But that’s still not the edge of your aura. Not even close. Come on. Push.”

I gritted my teeth and tried again.

The spark formed, then dropped straight down and popped against the floor, like a wire shorting out on stone.

“What was that?” Garo barked. “Why did you push it down?”

I opened my eyes. “I don’t know. Maybe because that’s where it’s supposed to go?”

Garo stared at me.

I hesitated, then clarified. “Electricity always tries to ground. That’s how it works. Air is a terrible conductor unless it’s ionized first, so it follows the path of least resistance. Usually down. Into the earth.”

A beat of silence passed.

Garo slowly dragged a hand down his face and let out a long sigh. “Girl. You’re creating lightning out of mana. Why in the hell are you worried about how it behaves in nature?”

I opened my mouth, then closed it.

“I have seen someone create a lightning sphere the size of a house and throw it like a rock,” he continued, waving his hand for emphasis. “I’ve seen lightning crack sideways into the sky, curl like a snake, and shatter armor from the inside out. Never once have I seen it care about the laws of nature.”

“…Right,” I said weakly.

“You’re not summoning real weather, Alice,” he said, leaning forward. “You’re using willpower to give mana shape. You want lightning to go sideways? It goes sideways. You want it to hum like a songbird? It hums.”

I nodded, embarrassed but strangely relieved.

He sat back again. “Now. Forget grounding. Forget nature. Focus on what you want it to do. Magic doesn’t care what the world thinks it should do. It only listens to you and your intent.”

I took a breath, cleared my head, and raised my fingers again.

This time, I didn’t picture wires, clouds, or anything scientific. I pictured a crackling arc of energy leaping outward, obedient, clean, sharp. The spark jumped from my fingers and flared half a meter before blinking away.

Garo’s grin returned. “There we go. That’s a good start. However, you are still nowhere near the edge of your aura. You still need more training.”

The next day, I traded incense for bruises.

Edmund’s spear class met at sunrise in one of the guild practice fields. The other students were three boys and one girl, all younger and fitter than me. At least at first glance.

I noticed everyone stretching, so I joined in until Edmund handed me a weighted spear.

“Run,” he said.

“For how long?” I asked.

“Until you drop.”

I laughed.

He didn’t.

It turns out running with a weighted spear is exactly as miserable as it sounds. My arms ached, and my legs begged for mercy. Sweat poured off me like someone had uncorked my skull, and whenever someone slowed or faltered, Edmund shouted at them to get up and keep running. I collapsed twice, and each time he made me start again after three minutes of catching my breath.

And all of that happened before the lesson actually started.

Edmund had a special kind of cruelty where he waited until you looked half-dead before beginning instruction.

Stance, grip, strike angles, and footwork.

Again. And again. And again.

By the time our group looked fully deceased, he finally stopped and turned to us.

“There is one secret to fast growth,” he said. “You grow when you push beyond the limits of your body. That is how you strengthen your soul.”

“I can’t...” I panted. “Push... past... this...”

He raised an eyebrow at me and smiled.

“If you were facing a dragon right now, would you just kneel over and die? Or would you fight harder and find hidden strength inside yourself?”

Slowly, he turned and raised his warpick high into the air.

His voice boomed across the stone like thunder.

“RESOLVE!”

His aura erupted like a banner catching wind, broad, bright, and golden.

It didn’t feel like pressure or heat.

It felt like presence.

Like certainty.

Like my shaking limbs had suddenly found a second wind.

The world steadied.

My hands stopped trembling.

I gripped the spear again, and this time, it held steady.

Edmund’s voice followed, low but clear. “This is a Holy miracle from the Knight class. It doesn’t heal you. It doesn’t protect you. It simply reminds you of your strength.”

We began the drills again, starting everything from the beginning.

Thrust, step back, rotate, and guard.

I could feel my muscles burning, but exhaustion no longer dragged me downward. It pushed me forward instead. I couldn’t even feel bored by the repetition. I was far too energized for that.

Every time I thought, This is too much, my resolve whispered that I still had more to give.

Edmund didn’t stop us, not until the drills became automatic and our bodies had memorized the motions through sheer repetition.

And finally, when it was over, and my sweat had soaked through my tunic, I collapsed backward into the dirt, gasping.

Edmund crouched beside me.

“You feel that?” he asked.

“Pain?” I wheezed.

“No,” he said with a smile. “Growth.”

I blinked at him.

“Your physical limits are also affected by your soul. This kind of training strengthens your aura, too,” he explained. “And Resolve gives you the will to keep pushing when you would normally quit.”

I sat there, blinking up at the sun, the taste of iron lingering in my mouth.

“That’s not a miracle,” I muttered. “That’s cheating.”

He laughed and stood. “Every miracle is cheating. Do you think it’s fair when a cleric drags someone back from the brink of death while others don’t get the same chance?”

I stayed sprawled in the dirt for another few minutes, panting.

Whose idea was it to do this…  right, it was mine, I fancied myself a hero from a fantasy story… Those montages lied to me, time slows when you are training…

I forced myself to stand; my legs shook, and my spine cracked, but I was able to do it.

If what Edmund said was true, then that meant...

I raised my hand and cast my Lightning.

A small spark arced across my fingers, brighter than yesterday.

I focused and pushed harder.

The arc jumped.

I watched it dance almost a full meter past my fingers.

I couldn't help but smile.

Maybe this pain was worth it after all.

A day in the Contested Realm has twelve hours under the yellow sun, or what the locals call Firedays. Then twelve hours under the blue sun: Waterdays.

For the next few days, my life flowed to this new rhythm. Firedays meant training, magic, spear work, and pain. Waterdays meant books, baths, and hanging out with friends. But mostly books.

Every first, third, and fifth day of the week, as the yellow sun climbed over the West Gate, I found myself sitting cross-legged across from Garo Agame. The room always smelled of incense and tea. The relaxing atmosphere only gave way to painful lessons.

We started each lesson with meditation. The aim was to teach me to perceive my aura and better control my mana flow. This was the easy part, since I already had some experience with meditation from my old world.

Next came the aura drills. I would spark lightning from my hands and test how far the current could arc before fizzling into the air. This was my life for the first week, until I was able to consistently push beyond two meters.

Then he had me cast within his aura.

It was a miserable experience, having him loom over me with a suffocating dome of dense pressure. My lightning sparks could barely reach past my skin. I had to actively will them into existence. The more resistant the space became, the clearer my intent had to be.

“Your willpower can only be as strong as your soul,” he told me once. “Push and grow. That’s the only way.”

We also did shaping exercises. At first, it was simple. Sparks turned into rings, then cubes. Then we escalated to more complex shapes. I made a thunderbird, a bull, and a cat.

Once I could hold shapes reliably, he made me move them after casting. That meant keeping them inside my aura while shifting their position, like trying to puppeteer without strings. I couldn’t just throw them out and forget them. I had to maintain control while also moving my own body.

It felt like trying to walk while counting backward and forward at the same time.

Then it got worse.

He started making me give the shapes velocity, not just guiding them gently through my aura, but actively launching them outward. Once they left my aura, they were no longer under my control and only lasted a few seconds before blinking out.

“Your aura is your anchor,” Garo said. “Past that, you’re trusting the shape and momentum to hold it together.”

By the final week, he had me drop all the shaping and just focus on range, throwing raw lightning spheres like baseballs.

There was no need for form or frills. Just power and direction.

And it worked.

My aura had grown to about five meters by then, but with the right push, I could send a lightning sphere four times that distance, twenty meters of clean, controlled throw. That was a major range advantage, especially since I could only arc lightning within my aura.

And at the end of each session came the hardest part.

After my mana was completely drained, he had me cast one final time despite being dry.

It was torture. I would either faint or collapse with a nosebleed.

Those sessions ended with my brain feeling like it had been crushed by a hydraulic press. I would stagger back to the dorm with a migraine that felt like the entire sky had collapsed onto my frontal lobe.

Worse, I kept doing it voluntarily.

On the second, fourth, and sixth days, it was a different kind of torture under Edmund’s regimen.

Wood clashed against wood, sweat hit the dirt, and muscles screamed.

Edmund’s training was less metaphysical and more, “Here’s a stick. Now swing until your arms fall off.”

After running, we rotated through drills, spear guards, sweeping arcs, quick thrusts, and weapon retention against grabs. My palms had new calluses before the first week ended.

We ran, we climbed, we lifted, we jumped, and then we held weighted spears for extra drills.

Sparring was the final reward or punishment.

I was physically stronger than the girl and one of the boys in our group, roughly equal to another, and weaker than the third, but it didn’t matter. All of them had better endurance. All of them had cleaner footwork. Every one of them had an extra edge I lacked. They had been raised in a world where physical hardship was normal. I had been raised in relative luxury and peace.

Still, I had one advantage.

The gravity in Hano was lighter than back home. Only slightly, but enough that over time I started hitting harder, jumping farther, and holding my ground just a little longer.

“You have good instincts,” Edmund had said after I finally scored a clean hit in week three. “Your body will start listening to them eventually.”

On Waterdays, under the serene blue sun, I rested.

I would retreat to my room, the temple, or the officers’ lounge, quiet spaces where I could stretch out with a book and try to forget that my legs were made of boiled noodles and my brain had turned into soup.

I swapped recipes with Louis, focusing on learning local ingredients so I could improve my diet, since I had been eating far more than usual with all the training.

I talked medicine with Vena, who was finally free to spend time with me after weeks of sweeping the slums and healing people.

I introduced chess to Justicar Gray, and he showed me a similar game, something like Go, except pieces could be eliminated rather than captured, and the loser was the one who ran out of pieces first.

I watched Yon train his scrappy group of misfits and eventually noticed that Kan was among them.

I got drunk with Nakera and Kuru and gambled away a few dozen bronze coins on newly learned card games and deeply unlucky dice throws.

And whenever one of the girls was free, Louis, Vena, Nada, Nakera, Kuru, or even Sara, I would take them shopping or exploring around the city.

This wasn’t just learning magic.

I was learning how to live in a new world.

By the end of the month, I found myself standing in the east training yard, sweat already clinging to my skin before I even stepped into the sparring ring.

Across from me stood Rell, one of the quieter boys in our group. Polite, focused, and frustratingly safe. He never took risks during a match and never overextended. He just kept his guard tight and waited for you to make a mistake.

Which, to be fair, I usually did.

Not today.

Edmund gave the signal with a sharp whistle.

Rell approached with his usual caution, spear held in a perfect mid-guard, his steps steady and measured, not a hint of aggression.

I mirrored him. Kept my breathing level. Waited.

Rell came at me with a probing thrust, testing range. I shifted my footing and let it pass just outside my hip, then jabbed forward with a quick counterstrike. He caught it with the shaft of his spear, pivoted, and brought the butt of his weapon around toward my knee.

I ducked back. He followed, closing the distance.

He feinted with a jab.

Not today.

I didn’t react, so when the real strike came, a forward thrust aimed at my shoulder, more probing than serious.

That was when I moved.

Instead of deflecting, I snapped my left hand forward and caught the shaft of his spear just beneath the head. His eyes widened.

I didn’t hesitate.

I twisted hard, pulling him slightly off-balance, then stepped in close and drove my boot straight into his chest.

It wasn’t elegant, but it worked.

He hit the ground with a surprised grunt.

“Match,” Edmund called.

Rell coughed and pushed himself upright, rubbing his ribs. “Okay,” he said, not unkindly. “Didn’t see that coming.”

I offered him a hand up. “You should really take bigger risks.”

He chuckled. “Noted.”

As we cleared the ring, Edmund walked over and gave me a slow nod.

“That’s two wins today. Good read. Good timing. And a clean reversal.”

“But also two losses. I’ll take it,” I said, still catching my breath.

“You should.” He paused, watching the next pair prepare for their match. “You’re ready to train on your own now. You’ve got the basics. You’ve got instincts. What you need next is experience.”

“What kind of experience?” I hesitated. “I don’t see myself fighting in wars.”

He looked back at me. “Guild missions. Monster culls. Maybe patrol work. Anything where you have to apply what you’ve learned, rather than just repeating it.”

I nodded, still gripping my training spear. It had weight and balance, but it wasn’t a real weapon.

Edmund must have noticed the way I looked at it.

“You should buy a proper weapon,” he said. “Something with a real edge. Learn its shape, its sound, its balance. Make it part of your body. It won’t magically win fights for you, but it might stop you from losing one.”

I exhaled and nodded again, more certain this time.

He rolled his shoulder and leaned lightly on his warpick. “Also, you don’t have to train alone. I’ve seen some of your friends.”

I blinked.

“Try Yon’s group. He’s solid for formation drills and close-quarters strategy,” he said. “And Nakera?” He smirked. “Slippery as an eel. Good for learning how to fight someone who doesn’t fight fair.”

I raised an eyebrow. “Are you keeping tabs on me?”

“I’m a freelancer sergeant,” he said. “I keep an eye on all my juniors, especially the one who paid me a full silver for training.”

“Fair enough.” I laughed.

He gave me one final nod. “You’re not finished. But you’re no beginner anymore. Keep training. And don’t die on your first mission. That would reflect badly on me.”

“I’ll do my best,” I said.

And for the first time, I believed I actually could.

I didn’t realize it was my last lesson with Garo until we were already halfway through.

We started with range drills. Out behind the West Gate, a row of battered training dummies had been set up. Most were half-charred from previous attempts. One was missing an arm. Garo simply waved a hand toward them and sipped his tea.

“Throw.”

I summoned the familiar crackle of mana and shaped a tight, compressed sphere of lightning. My aura stretched about five meters now, farther than most people without innate talent. I focused, compressed the charge further, and launched it.

The sphere hissed through the air and slammed into the chest of the second dummy, leaving behind a deep black scorch mark.

“Again,” Garo said.

I had learned that I could throw about ten good lightning spheres before my mana became too low for strong attacks. I had also noticed my attacks weakened noticeably after twenty meters.

When I asked Garo about it, his answer was simple.

“Inside your aura, your power has structure. Outside it, you’re throwing whatever you have already formed, and nature starts eroding it the moment it leaves your aura.”

I nodded, breathing hard. “So the range is longer but weaker. Does that mean lightning attacks within my aura are stronger?”

I stepped a little closer to one of the dummies and threw a lightning arc at it. The attack was noticeably stronger.

“Try that again,” Garo said. “But on me.”

I didn’t hesitate. After a month of training, I had learned one thing with absolute certainty.

I could not hurt him yet.

I stepped closer to Garo and launched another attack, but my lightning faltered as soon as it entered his space. I could physically feel the pressure of his aura resisting mine.

“Getting this close to your enemy makes the surrounding area contested. Most of the time, that means the stronger soul wins,” Garo explained. “But attacks thrown from outside your enemy’s aura can finish forming first, and those can no longer be interfered with.”

“I see,” I said with a nod. “Can I try something else?”

“Sure.”

I approached him and raised two fingers into a V shape.

I focused my intent on turning each finger into an electrode, one positively charged, the other negatively charged, then tried pulsing current between them like a taser.

A small arc of electricity crackled to life.

I looked up at him.

“Can you stop these small arcs from forming?” I asked.

He tilted his head. “No. I can’t. What did you do, girl?”

“I limited my mana control to inside my body rather than extending it through my aura.” I smiled, pleased that the idea had worked.

“Ah,” he said with a small nod. “Just like how Fire Blooded heat their fists before punching.”

After that, we returned to aura training, this time with me attempting to suppress him.

Of course, it failed every time.

Still, I could feel some faint improvement.

“My aura has been growing steadily,” I said. “Do you think it’s because of my sky affinity?”

Garo stroked his beard, smoke coiling faintly from his wrists as he thought.

“Could be. A sky affinity that high isn’t common, especially since you’re not from the Bloodlines. Most people with that kind of natural growth also develop an innate power of their own. Elemental Bloodline powers usually flare during puberty at the latest. You haven’t, which means...”

“I don’t have innate powers?” I offered.

“Or maybe you have something more esoteric,” he said. “Something hidden. You might want to get a proper soul map drawn. It could tell you what kind of magic is locked inside you.”

“I heard those cost a gold coin.”

“They do,” he said with a slight smile. “And they’re not essential yet. But later? If your aura ever stops growing, discovering your power might be your next way forward.”

I mulled that over quietly.

My aura had grown, yes. But there was still so much I didn’t understand.

I turned back toward him. “So that’s it?”

He nodded. “You’ve got control. You’ve got power. Keep practicing, and keep pushing. You’re on the path now. You don’t need me hovering over your shoulder anymore.”

I stepped forward and gave him a small bow.

“Thank you, Garo. For everything.”

He waved me off lazily, incense smoke curling behind him like a signature.

“Any time, girl. If you’ve got questions, come back. I’m not going anywhere.”

Training was over, and I was exhausted.

After my final session at the West Gate, I limped back to the guild, soaked in the guild bath until my skin wrinkled like a prune, then collapsed into a nap that was closer to unconsciousness.

When I woke, the blue sun was already high.

I slung my empty backpack over my shoulder and made my way toward the temple.

The courtyard was fuller than usual. Locals and pilgrims gathered beneath arched walkways while sunlight filtered through colored glass, scattering dappled patterns across the stone. At the altar stood Lady Sana, dressed in white and speaking with her usual calm certainty.

“The bravest knights often fall young. The purest clerics may never know a lover’s touch. And so it falls to the rest of you to carry the world forward. It is not shameful to bring life into this world. It is sacred. It is necessary. The wilderness still covers most of this realm. Monsters continue to grow stronger, unchallenged. We remain outnumbered. Still scattered. The world has room for more children, and the future demands them.”

It was a softly spoken sermon, but the weight in her voice made every word land like scripture.

I waited near the back and let the crowd move first.

Everyone seemed to want something from her, a word, a smile, or a brush of her hand.

She gave each person the same warmth.

Eventually, the last lingerers drifted away, and I stepped forward.

Sana turned toward me and smiled.

“Alice. I see your posture has improved. Training agrees with you. You look more dangerous.”

“I would hope so,” I said. “My bruises should count for something.”

She gestured for me to follow her.

We made our way into her study, tucked beside the altar. The air inside was cooler and smelled faintly of peaches and pressed herbs.

After exchanging pleasantries over tea, I casually mentioned something.

“If we perfect the vaccine project, child mortality might plummet. Eventually, maybe people won’t feel pressured to have so many children.”

She tilted her head and smiled, not dismissively, but with the same gentle tone she used when correcting people during sermons.

“Alice... ninety-nine percent of the world is still wilderness. Vast, untamed, and full of monsters. We are not running out of space. Not even remotely.”

Then she chuckled.

“But I doubt you came here to discuss birth rates.”

“No,” I admitted. “I came to ask for my devices back. My laptop and my phone.”

She nodded once, stood, and retrieved a locked wooden box from a tall cabinet.

Inside sat my phone, neatly tucked beside my closed laptop.

“Still in one piece,” she said. “Though I admit, I was tempted to poke at the smaller one for more music.”

“Sorry about that,” I said, carefully placing both devices back into my backpack. “But they’re depleted of magic. I think I found a way to charge them again.”

I thanked her again and made my way back to the freelancer dorms, slipping into my room just as the bells signaled the blue sun reaching its peak.

I sat cross-legged on my bed and pulled both devices out.

Time for science.

I booted the laptop first.

The battery still had eighteen percent left, just enough to access the encyclopedia app.

I searched: battery, lithium-ion, charging methods.

After thirty minutes of study, I scribbled down a few notes.

Phone and laptop batteries are lithium-ion.

They store energy through chemical potential, and overcharging can destroy them.

Power enters as low-voltage direct current; it should be smooth, steady, and predictable.

Too much heat, too much power, or unstable pulses? That’s how things explode.

I stared at my phone.

Sorry, little buddy, but you are the guinea pig.

I placed it on a folded blanket, steadied my breathing, and reached for mana.

Instead of force or flash, I focused on creating a gentle current. A smooth flow rather than a surge. I visualized electricity entering through the charging port like a thin thread.

For several seconds, nothing happened.

Then the screen lit up. 1%. 2%. 3%.

I grinned. It wasn’t a total victory yet; I still had to maintain the charge and keep the current stable.

I held the intent steady for ten full minutes until the battery climbed to seventy percent.

Then I stopped and waited.

I gave it a touch, no heat. I gave it a sniff, not smoke, or even the smell of burnt rubber. And best of all, the phone was still alive.

Now for the real challenge. Losing you would be devastating.

In a way, the laptop was easier, since the battery was removable and I didn’t have to worry as much about frying the hardware.

Also, because the battery was larger, I used both hands and charged faster while carefully monitoring for heat.

The task required intense concentration, but surprisingly little mana.

I used more mana throwing a single lightning sphere than I did charging both devices.

By the time the blue sun neared its zenith, both devices were fully charged and undamaged.

Main objective complete.

As a bonus, I had accidentally learned how to fight in order to achieve it, and now, I could finally fall asleep listening to music.
