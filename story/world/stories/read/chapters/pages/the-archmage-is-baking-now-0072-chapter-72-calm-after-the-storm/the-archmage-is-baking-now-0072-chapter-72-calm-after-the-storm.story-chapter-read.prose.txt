The Underbridge Market was even busier than usual. The queen’s eyes widened when the sprawling collection of stalls and workshops, which had grown up in the shadow of Glenfall's largest bridge, came into view.

Jane had been down here many times before, mostly to visit Allen, but also on her own business. She, too, had once found it overwhelming. She immediately felt obligated to act as a tour guide of sorts for the queen.

Especially on a day like today. Word of the king's visit had spread. Every Underbridge crafter seemed to be putting their all into their goods, which Jane took as a sign that they had not yet settled their own showcase selections. It wasn’t just the work, though. The morale boost of the visit seemed to have brightened the whole market, both figuratively and literally. Stalls that had always been cluttered with half-finished projects were now neatly organized and spotlessly clean.

"This is remarkable," the queen said, looking around with undisguised interest. "I had no idea places like this existed.”

Jane wasn't sure how to respond. The Underbridge Market wasn't a type of thing that was unique to Glenfall. Most cities of any size had similar places, informal communities of crafters who couldn't afford proper shops or who preferred the company of other tradesfolk. She supposed the queen had simply never had reason to visit one before.

"It's where a lot of the real work happens," Bella said. She had caught up with them outside the restaurant, apparently unwilling to miss whatever adventure was unfolding. "The fancy shops up in town are nice, but this is where you go if you want something made to your specifications. The stalls here supply a lot of those shops, anyway."

They made their way deeper into the market, weaving between stalls and dodging helpers carrying armloads of materials. The queen attracted some attention, though less than Jane would have expected. Her traveling clothes were fine, certainly, but not so obviously royal that people stopped what they were doing to stare. Most of the crafters here were too busy with their own work to pay much attention to passing customers, even well-dressed ones.

The crafters who did notice seemed pleased by her interest. The queen listened attentively as they explained their wares, asking questions that suggested she understood more than Jane would have expected.

Eventually, they found their way to Allen's spot.

His workspace was less a stall than a proper open-air workshop. There was usually enough room to work on larger projects without getting in anyone's way. Today, that job was a little harder, given how crowded the space was with extra bodies.

Allen and Brit bent over the workbench, their heads close together as they studied a large piece of paper covered in sketches and notes. The king and Brand stood nearby, watching with obvious enjoyment. They both looked more engaged than Jane had seen all day. Several of the guards had positioned themselves around the perimeter, trying to look casual and mostly failing.

"There you are," the king said as the three women approached. "We were wondering if you'd gotten lost."

"The queen wanted to see the market," Jane said. "We took our time."

"It's extraordinary," the queen added.

The king smiled. "I agree. Did you know there's a man here who makes nothing but hinges? Dozens of different types of hinges, each one suited to a specific purpose."

"That would be old Marcus," Allen said, not looking up from the sketches. "He's been making hinges for forty years. Nobody else comes close. I try every once in a while, though. Mostly special orders he doesn’t want to take."

As he spoke, he began pulling out materials from a chest beneath the workbench. He selected a large piece of leather and began working it over, adding smaller strips of leather with an awl, needle, and heavy thread. Jane quickly lost track of what he was trying to accomplish. Whatever it was, it was a complex object, something with clasps, buckles, and layers arranged just-so.

"The tricky part is making it comfortable," Allen explained. "It has to sit against the skin for hours at a time. If it's too stiff or too tight, you'll want to take it off. Then it's useless."

"You've made these before?" the queen asked.

"Oh, no. Not something exactly like this. But most things people need to wear for their work are the same. You don’t get any use out of something you won’t bring with you, they say.”

At the forge, Brit was heating a bar of steel. He turned it in the coals until it glowed a dull red, then pulled it out with long tongs and began to hammer it on a small anvil. Each strike was precise and controlled. The metal flattened and lengthened under his blows, slowly taking on the rough shape of a blade.

"The prince mentioned he's had some combat training," Brit said between strikes. "What style?"

"Formal fencing, mostly," Brand answered. "The kind they teach at court. They have me drill a few types of strikes over and over again, with a lot of emphasis on not getting hit."

Brit grunted. "Daggers are different than rapiers. Closer quarters. You'll need to practice.”

"I intend to."

The conversation drifted as the work continued. The queen asked questions about the forging process, which Brit answered with surprising attention as he continued to work on the orange-hot metal.

It wasn’t a short process, but the hours passed more quickly than Jane had expected. The work itself was interesting, and the royal family’s interest made it even more so.

“That’ll do it, I think,” Allen eventually said, stepping back and wiping his hands on a rag. “Looks like Brit’s done, too.”

Two small daggers lay on the workbench, their blades still warm from the forge. They had been polished to a mirror shine. The handles were wrapped in thin strips of leather, wound tight and secured with small brass pins.

"Beautiful work," the king said, picking up one of the daggers and testing its balance. "The weight is perfect."

"It should be." Brit sounded pleased, but not surprised. "I've made enough blades over the years. Though, to be honest, my trade usually leans more towards horseshoes."

Allen’s leather pieces had been assembled into something Jane finally understood when she saw it laid out flat. It was a sleeve of sorts, meant to wrap around the forearm and be secured with a series of small straps. Two narrow pouches were sewn into the inside, each one sized to hold a single dagger.

"Try it on," Allen said, handing the assembled sheath to Brand.

The prince took it carefully, examining the construction with obvious appreciation. He rolled up his sleeve and slid the sheath onto his left forearm, adjusting the straps until it sat snugly against his skin. Then he rolled his sleeve back down.

The transformation was remarkable. The sheath was completely invisible, hidden beneath the fabric of his shirt. Jane couldn't even see a bulge where the daggers rested against his arm.

"Now draw one," Brit said.

Brand reached across his body with his right hand, his fingers finding the handle of the upper dagger by feel alone. The blade slid free in one smooth motion. He brought the dagger up and executed a series of quick, precise cuts through the air.

Jane was surprised by the skill. Court training or not, anyone would have been limited if they hadn’t practiced enough to back up their learning. The prince clearly wasn’t limited. The dagger flashed in the late afternoon light in a way that indicated hard work. She appreciated that, and respected him more for it.

"Excellent," the king said. "Just what we wanted."

Brand sheathed the dagger just as smoothly as he had drawn it. The blade slid home with barely a sound. He flexed his arm experimentally, testing the range of motion.

"It's comfortable," he said. "I hardly notice it's there. This is exactly what I was hoping for." He paused, looking at the sheath more closely. "But why are there two slots? I only need one dagger."

Brit shrugged. "In case you lose the first one."

"Lose it?"

"If you ever need to draw a hidden dagger, things have probably gone very wrong. And when things go very wrong, they tend to stay wrong for a while. You are the prince. I figured you might as well have a spare."

“To be honest, I hope I never need even one.”

"So do I," Brit said. "But if you do, you'll be glad you have the second."

The prince drew the other dagger, tested its balance, and sheathed it. Then he looked up at Allen and Brit with a genuine smile.

"Thank you," he said. "Both of you."

"Don't thank us yet," Allen said. "You need to practice with it. Do that, and we are square."

"I will." Brand hesitated, then added, "And thank you for your time. Really. It's been good to meet people who treat me like a normal person. That doesn't happen often."

Jane caught Bella's eye across the workshop. Her friend was smiling, and Jane found herself smiling, too. Whatever else this royal visit brought, at least it had given Brand a few hours of genuine connection with people who didn't care much about his title.

The king stood and stretched, his joints popping audibly. "Well then. I believe we've imposed on these good people long enough. Shall we head back to the inn?"

The royal family gathered themselves to leave. Handshakes and thanks were exchanged, and then they were gone, trailed by weary-looking guards who Jane sincerely hoped would be off their feet soon.

Jane watched them go, then turned back to her friends.

"Well," she said. "That was something."

A different sort of silence immediately settled over the workshop. It wasn't uncomfortable. It was more like the quiet that came after a storm had passed, when the air was calm simply because the danger had moved on to somewhere else.

Jane found a crate to sit on. Bella claimed a spot on the workbench itself, her legs reaching all the way to the ground as she put her full weight on the sturdy building surface. Brit and Allen busied themselves tidying up the scraps of leather and metal that had accumulated during the prince's commission.

Nobody spoke for what felt like a long time. Jane let the silence stretch and watched the market traffic flow past the edges of Allen's workspace. It seemed strange that everything could be so normal when she had just spent the better part of a day in the company of the king and queen of the realm.

Was that tension there the whole time? she wondered. I didn't notice it while they were here. But now that they're gone, I feel like I've been holding my breath for hours.

The royal family had been friendly, even surprisingly normal. But they were still the royal family. Stories about them and lessons about their importance to the country had been pounded into everyone’s head for years. That wasn’t the sort of thing that could be mitigated simply by finding they were also pleasant company.

"That was exhausting," Bella said finally. "A good experience to have had, I think. But exhausting."

"Agreed," Brit said. "I don't know how court people do that every day. I'd need a nap after every conversation."

"Maybe they do take naps," Allen said. "Maybe that's why royal schedules don’t seem that intense."

The moment was broken by the sound of approaching footsteps.

A young man appeared at the edge of Allen's workspace. He wore the simple clothes of a market runner, someone paid to carry messages and small packages from one end of the city to the other. He wasn’t Dave, but he almost could have been, judging by his clothes and age.

He approached cautiously, as if making sure someone wasn’t there. Jane thought she probably knew who he was afraid of, but decided not to mention it.

"Miss Jane?" he asked, his eyes darting between the four of them.

"That's me."

"I have a message for you, miss." He stepped forward and held out a folded piece of paper. "From the town council. I'm sorry I didn't get here earlier. I was trying to find you, and then I found you, but the king was here, and I couldn't quite work up the courage to approach. I hope it's not too late."

Jane took the paper from his hand. "It's fine. Thank you for finding me."

She unfolded the paper and read it quickly, her eyes scanning the neat official script.

"What is it?" Bella asked.

"The showcase schedule." Jane read the relevant line again to make sure she hadn't misunderstood. "The bakers are first. Tomorrow, late morning."
