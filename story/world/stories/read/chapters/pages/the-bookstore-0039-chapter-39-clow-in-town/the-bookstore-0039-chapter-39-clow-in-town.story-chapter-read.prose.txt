“Are we close?” Yana asked, her irritation no longer hidden.

“A few minutes,” Ailen replied with a polite smile that did not reach her eyes, while Asha stared at the potential enemy across from her with a strained, uncertain expression.

Yana was not blind to the obvious delay. For the last two weeks, the elves had stretched every errand, stop, and courtesy just enough to slow the journey without openly resisting it. She could guess their reason easily. Not that she cared very much.

The two weeks prior to leaving the Province Capital had been dreadful. Running urgent little errands for officials who pretended she mattered had been mind-numbing. The only real amusement had come from watching poor Asha struggle under a little playful pressure. Even so, Yana had humored her hosts’ stalling because it cost her nothing. Contrary to what they feared, she had no great interest in catching local rebellions or dragging hidden acts of treason into the light.

Yana had no illusions about what she was. She was not an overseer in any meaningful sense. The title was a convenience, not a promotion. Either her family wanted her out of court because her presence had become undesirable, or they meant to send her somewhere distant so that one unfortunate accident might clean her name from their records forever. Most likely both. Of course, placing pressure on the elven court by reminding them how tightly the Clows held one of their promising girls also suited the family nicely.

Still, leaving the Clow nest was not entirely a loss. For the first time in years, Yana could breathe without wondering which cousin might try poison before the week ended. That did not erase the humiliation of being formally discarded, but humiliation was hardly new to her. She had spent enough years being despised to build a durable hide over the softest parts of herself.

If this exile was meant as punishment, she would treat it as opportunity.

Across from her, Asha knew better than to provoke the girl openly. The problem was that she still had no idea how to behave around her.

The minister’s coercion on the castle balcony had been the first open display of neuromancy Asha had truly suffered. Everything since then had moved too quickly for her to recover the right footing. Was Yana not here to throw the Clows’ weight around? Was that not the point? Then why discard the name that made doors open and people kneel?

Yana answered the question before Asha could even frame it.

“In public,” she said, “you call me Yana of Emperor Seat. Do you understand?”

Her purple hair and eyes were gone. Or rather, hidden. Holomancy had given her common brown hair, brown eyes, and the look of an unremarkable noble girl from elsewhere. The command in her voice, however, remained unmistakable.

“Yes, Lady—” Asha caught herself. “Miss Yana.”

Ailen only inclined her head. Asha cursed herself in silence. She had already brought enough danger to her family, town, and perhaps more. The least she should have been able to do was adapt faster. Her mother would have. Ailen had been placed under worse pressure and never once failed to act as though the situation was controlled by her rather than the Clows. She had not flinched when Asha came back shaken from the balcony. She had not hesitated when Yana casually cast an illusion inside the carriage without a single spoken word. And somehow, through means Asha had not even been allowed to witness, she had already sent warning ahead to Mountainend before they ever left the Seat.

Ailen Treefehl was infuriating as a mother, but she was flawless when duty called.

Yana’s little performance was not difficult for her to read either. Taking a false name, wearing a common face, refusing the obvious display of Clow authority—it would confuse many people. To Ailen, it looked more like the sort of defiance privileged girls mistook for strategy.

What interested her more was the craft itself. The illusion was excellent. Better than excellent. The transformation had nearly escaped her scrutiny at first, which was no small thing. Ailen genuinely could not understand how a family obsessed with power could treat such a useful art as undesirable. Then again, royal families often lacked common sense precisely where it would have helped most.

On the opposite bench, Yana was privately pleased with herself. Her decision cut both ways. By using her despised craft to refuse the title and appearance expected of her, she insulted the court that had sent her away while also shielding herself behind an entirely reasonable excuse. Undercover observation sounded much more useful than parading a Clow name through frightened provincial streets. Publicly, she was accompanying Asha and helping her understand her gift. Officially, she was observing the girl and watching for rebellion. In truth, she had been removed from the court, and the Treefehls had been reminded of the rope around their necks.

It was a neat little rebellion, and Yana enjoyed neat things.

The carriage entered Mountainend and rolled toward the town square, where a small welcoming party waited at the entrance of the Town Hall. Once they stopped, the driver straightened himself, hurried down, and opened the door.

Ailen and Asha descended first. The countess exchanged a warning glance with Ehran before anyone else could see it clearly. The mayor stood waiting beside Dorgh and a handful of local officials, all arranged with the sort of careful dignity provincial towns adopted when dealing with power from elsewhere.

Then Yana stepped down after them. Now brown-haired and ordinary-looking, she earned exactly the confusion she expected.

Ailen moved quickly.

“This is Miss Yana of Emperor Seat,” she said smoothly. “The daughter of a friend. She will be staying in town for some months. I trust Mountainend will show her proper borderland hospitality.”

The message landed. Ehran’s surprise lasted only a moment before he concealed it. His eyes flicked over the gathered officials, and the warning in his glance was sharp enough to silence any foolish curiosity before it could surface. Then he stepped forward.

“Welcome, Miss Yana,” he said. “I am certain you will find your stay memorable.”

He gave something like a bow without offering the full thing, which suited Yana perfectly well. The others echoed his greeting with a careful politeness that also made one thing clear: they understood exactly who she really was.

Ehran led them to a guest house near the Town Hall, small but far more luxurious than the town deserved at first glance. In a place like Mountainend, a distinguished guest could only be received in one of three ways: at the mayor’s own residence, in the home of a wealthy merchant, or in an official guest house set aside for such occasions. The banquet prepared for her was equally predictable.

Yana refused it. She asked instead for the food to be sent later and dismissed most of the waiting servants with minimal courtesy. She allowed only two to remain—one for cleaning, one for driving the coach. There was no point filling the house with obvious eyes and ears if she could avoid it. She could not reject the guest house itself, nor all of the servants, because she lacked better local options. Bringing attendants from the Seat would only have invited more dangerous snakes into her orbit.

But she could strip away as many of Ehran’s spies as possible. That much, at least, was easy.

The day passed. Only the next morning did Yana finally leave the guest house to inspect the town for herself.

Ehran had likely expected scrutiny of records, questions for clerks, and the usual formal nonsense people prepared when they thought Clow interest would come. Yana ignored all of it. Instead, she walked. She visited the market, passed the tavern, watched the pace of the streets, and the gossip that formed naturally when people did not yet know they were being questioned.

That proved far more useful. By noon, she had already found the town’s current fascination.

A store. Not just any store, if the rumors were to be believed. An unknown shop that had somehow transformed a failed local hunter into a small success story, drawn the interest of adventurers, and even caught the attention of passing mercenaries. The name alone made her arch a brow.

The Bookstore.

To Yana’s city-bred instincts, it sounded like some strange frontier version of a magic emporium. That raised immediate questions. Why would any respectable business of that kind choose such a forgotten place? And how could it be producing real change where so many others had failed for years?

Of course, she knew better than to trust common gossip outright. Rural people could turn coincidence into miracle before the tea cooled. Still, gossip pointed toward pressure points, and pressure points were worth examining.

Yana smiled. At last, something interesting.

She summoned the coach driver without hesitation and ordered him toward the famous store by the forest road.

A storm was coming to The Bookstore. Whether Anne’s roots ran deep enough to endure it remained to be seen.
