Chapter 122

Hanging by a Thread

I’m a lunatic riding a flying barbell toward the hospital at a speed I can barely control. Lake Erie vanishes behind me. The invasion is over. That should mean something. There should be a moment where I feel the victory settle into my bones, where I breathe in the morning air and think, Wow, Joseph, you just punched a shark king into cloud-clearing confetti. You’re a freaking hero, dude!

But the victory feels hollow and lifeless. There’s just too much loss piled on top of it to resemble any kind of win I care to recognize. Right now, my only focus is on the hospital. There’s only one word, banging around inside my skull like a loose bolt in an engine.

Dad.

The soles of my feet press down harder onto the Full Metal Staff. The steel bar radiates a warmth like I’m standing over a vent, humming with stored power and the engraved [Fly] spell, which I’m pushing to its limit. My boots are planted on the bar, knees bent, cape snapping behind me hard enough to sound like gunfire.

I feel sick.

God dammit, Dad. What the hell?

No—worse—I feel helpless.

It’s that sick feeling that sits behind your ribs and makes every breath feel like you’re inhaling through wet cement. My mind is a junk drawer full of razors. Every thought cuts. Every memory comes with a little hook buried in it.

Back to the hospital, after the fight against the Imp King. Mom’s face. Liv holding Jelly Boy. The slime’s two, intelligent but wet eyes filled with so much sadness and empathy that I thought was for me.

The room going quiet.

“Joseph…”

Mom’s voice had cracked on my name.

“It’s your father.”

I hear it again.

And again.

And again.

“Cancer.”

That’s the word that followed. Or at least the next one my find was able to focus on.

“Cancer?” I echoed. It sounded like the punch line in some stupid joke. We have System-powered healers that can re-grow arms. How could something so common from the world before still be so devastating?

Turns out, the power radiating from open Gates could complicate certain pre-existing illnesses. Make it even harder to treat, even with the use of magic.

He’d had it for a while, apparently.

A while.

How could I be so fucking stupid not to have recognized all of the signs?

It started with stomach issues. Loss of appetite, nausea, some pain. My mind can’t help but to see all the half-empty plates in front of him. Then, there was the weight loss. The tumors that spread to his stomach eating the little he could keep down. Then, weakness in his legs. Trouble standing too fast. Trouble walking too far. Eventually, trouble walking at all. It had come slow at first, then seemingly all at once. But now it seems all too obvious.

A rare type of blood cancer, Liv explained.

One of the new ones resulting from the Gates’ ambient energies. The medical community didn’t know what to do with it. Not really. They had protocols. Much of the same as treatment had been before the System, though supported through the use of healing magic and artifacts.

I grit my teeth so hard my jaw aches. I couldn’t go see him. Not at first. Not while Nereus was tearing a hole open in Lake Erie and the world was down two of the most powerful Guilders who had been sent to stop the catastrophe from beyond the Gate.

I had to go. I was the only one left.

That thought doesn’t comfort me. It makes me want to vomit over downtown Cleveland.

I was the only one left, and because of that, I made my dying father wait.

Maybe he isn’t dying. Maybe. The word is thin and pathetic and I hate it. Funny how hope can make you feel so pathetically weak.

I bank hard around a glass tower, the staff tilting beneath my boots. Wind rips tears from my eyes before they can decide whether they’re emotional or just from the wind lashing my face.

A notification blinks in the corner of my HUD. A couple of Level Up notifications I never closed out after my fight against Nerus. I close it without reading.

“Not now,” I snarl.

The notification vanishes.

I pump more of my muscles’ vitality into the staff. The barbell surges forward.

Pain flashes through my legs and core, a familiar bite of Stamina being wrung from meat. My body is still recovering from last night. From Reshma burning herself away behind me, letting my body tear itself apart over and over again, until I had enough power to save the world in a single strike. I don’t ignore the pain. Instead, I sink my teeth into it and let it drown out as much of the madness roiling within me.

Dad. Just hold on. Please. Just hold on.

The main campus of the clinic is a maze designed by someone who hated sick people and wanted them to prove they deserved medical care through navigation puzzles.

There are three towers. Four elevators banks in random locations that don’t go to the same floors. Signs with arrows that point toward departments named after donors instead of useful things. A skybridge that is apparently the only way to get to one part of the hospital’s campus. Not to be confused with the underground tunnel. A gift shop selling stuffed bears with tiny casts on their arms, which feels either adorable or psychologically hostile. I pass that gift shop three times before I find the correct elevator bank.

I almost cast [Locate Ally].

I actually get as far as flexing my fingers and thinking about the spell before I stop myself. I can find my father’s hospital room without using magic. Probably.

After asking two nurses in route to the cafeteria, I finally find the room. My hand hovers over the door handle. For a second, I just stand there. Why am I so scared to open the door? I think. I’ve grown so accustomed to walking through portals to other worlds, and now I’m afraid of opening a simple door. Stop being a coward!

I push the door open.

Dad is lying in the bed, propped up to a half-seated position. He has the usual smile plastered on his face. It’s as though I just walked through the door back home to see him at the kitchen nook, greeting me and asking me about my day. But my [Perception] is too sharp now. I can’t help but take in the IV. The thinness in his cheeks. The wrinkles on his neck and at the corners of his eyes. When did he get so old? How did he grow so frail? Was I too absorbed in my own little world to not notice the cracks beginning to show?

Jelly Boy is sitting on his lap like a blue therapy animal with no spine and limited table manners. They’re watching TV together. Some daytime game show where people clap too much and guess the prices of furniture nobody under eighty owns. A tray of picked-at hospital food sits off to the side. Gray turkey, mashed potatoes with the texture of wall patch, and green beans that look like they’re allergic to seasoning.

A chocolate pudding cup floats inside Jelly Boy’s body, slowly dissolving.

Dad turns his head when I come in.

“Well, look who it is,” he says, voice warm and a little raspy. “The flying underwear wizard. Can I get your autograph? A Make-a-Wish?”

Jelly Boy buzzes in greeting, pudding cup wobbling inside him like a doomed aquarium decoration.

I try to smile. I really do. But something breaks.

There’s no warning. The floodgates just open, and I start crying like a kid.

Full-on ugly crying. My face is hot and my throat is closed tight, causing my breath to come in jagged pieces.  I cross the room fast, almost tripping over the chair by the bed, and lean down, wrapping my arms around him.

Carefully. Like he’s glass.

Dad makes a soft sound, surprised, then pats my back.

“Hey now,” he says. “Hey, Joey. I’m right here.”

That makes it worse. Because he’s comforting me. He’s the one in the hospital bed. He’s the one with the rare, aggressive blood cancer. He’s the one who should be angry, scared, screaming, anything.

And instead he’s comforting me. His scared, crying little boy.

Shame hits me hot and awful. I hold him tighter anyway.

“I’m sorry,” I choke out. “I’m sorry, Dad.”

“For what?” he asks, like he genuinely doesn’t know. Like there aren’t a thousand answers.

For not noticing. For being gone when I should’ve been home with you. For becoming strong enough to punch gods’ toys into craters but not strong enough to help you. For making you wait.

The answers all come crashing in on me, but I can’t say anything.

He pats my back again.

“Careful there,” he says. “Are you trying to squeeze the cancer right out of me?”

A laugh bursts out of me. It sounds broken and wet and terrible.

Dad chuckles. Jelly Boy buzzes softly, then extrudes one tiny slime pseudopod and pats my shoulder, too. The pudding cup continues dissolving inside him, plastic cup and all, filling his body with little bubbles.

After a minute, I force myself to stand upright. I wipe my face with both hands like that will make me look less like I just lost a fight with my tear ducts.

It doesn’t.

“Where’s Mom?” I ask, voice rough.

Dad nods toward the door. “Sent her to get some better coffee.”

“There’s better coffee here?”

“No,” he says. “But it gave her a mission.”

I breathe out through my nose.

That is Dad. Right there. Managing Mom’s panic by sending her on a side quest.

I drag the chair closer and sit beside the bed. Jelly Boy shifts on Dad’s lap, making a content little blurble as the pudding cup finally collapses in on itself and vanishes into blue goo.

Dad looks down at him.

“Don’t tell your mother I gave him my pudding,” Dad says. “She told him three times he wasn’t allowed.”

Jelly Boy freezes.

“Pudding? What pudding?”

Dad chuckles softly.

A tiny pocket of normal opens between us, fragile as soap film.

Then. I ruin it because I have to.

“What’s the prognosis?” I ask. My voice catches on the last word. “Do they know anything further yet?”

Dad leans back against the pillow, smile softening into something gentler.

He laughs. Just a little puff of amusement, like I asked whether the Browns might make the Super Bowl.

“They’ll try some things,” he says. “Doctors have ideas. System doctors have weirder ideas. Got a specialist coming in. Maybe some treatment from Columbus. Maybe something experimental.”

I nod too quickly. “Okay. That’s good. Experimental is good. Right?”

His eyes meet mine.

“But it’s probably more about making sure I’m comfortable.”

The room goes very still.

I don’t know how he says it so casually.

I don’t know how those words can come out of his mouth without breaking every window in the building.

Comfortable.

My mouth opens.

Nothing comes out.

I search for something. Anything. My brain grabs at possibilities like a drowning man grabbing reeds.

“A healer,” I say suddenly. “There has to be a healer. A really good one. Someone better than the clinic. Pegasus had—”

Reshma.

The name slams into me and cuts that thought in half.

Then another memory follows, quieter and worse.

Liv’s face.

Her tense expression. Her eyes full of exhaustion.

I tried, she had told me.

This wasn’t an ordinary wound. It was his blood betraying him from the inside, and magic had touched it and found no handle to pull.

I sink back in the chair.

Dad watches me, and there’s no accusation in his face.

That almost makes me angry.

“You don’t have to fix this,” he says. “We all have one body and no warranty policy.”

I look away because I can’t handle him being kind about it.

“I want to fix it though. I want to make this right,” I mutter.

“I know.”

“No,” I say, sharper than I mean to. “I mean, that’s what I do now. That’s the whole thing. Monsters show up, and I hit them until they stop being a problem.”

Dad nods. “That’s a very specific résumé. Right above Save-Some-Bucks. You’re a well-rounded guy, son. I’m proud at how dedicated you are.”

“I’m serious.”

“So am I,” he says. “You’ve always been that way. Even before all this.”

I glance back at him.

He shrugs.

My throat tightens again, and I hate it.

Jelly Boy buzzes softly, like a tiny malfunctioning refrigerator.

For a while, neither of us says anything important.

Which is good. Important things are dangerous. So, we talk about small things instead.

The coffee in the hospital. The fact Mom snored like a chainsaw. The Browns. The Guardians. Mom’s ongoing war against the parking garage payment kiosk. Jax and the Toms. Dad asks if I really killed a giant shark-man that morning, and I say yes, and he says that sounds like something that should get me a coupon for free seafood.

I tell him the shark-man monologued.

Dad shakes his head. “Never monologue.”

“Rookie mistake.”

“Classic.”

Eventually Mom comes back holding two coffees and looking like she aged ten years during the elevator ride. Her eyes flick to me, then Dad, then to his food tray, then Jelly Boy.

“He ate your pudding?” she asks.

Dad points at Jelly Boy. “He’s been a model citizen!”

Jelly Boy vibrates innocently.

Mom gives a tired laugh, and for a moment she looks like herself again. Exhausted, wrecked, but herself.

We sit together. We talk more. We don’t talk about dying.

After a while, I know I have to leave, even though every part of me feels like leaving is a betrayal. Dad gets tired. His eyelids droop between sentences. Mom keeps touching his hand like she’s checking that he’s still there.

I stand.

Jelly Boy reluctantly oozes off Dad’s lap and into my arms, smelling faintly of chocolate and hospital blanket.

Dad looks up at me.

“You come back later,” he says.

It’s not a request.

“Yeah,” I say. “Of course.”

“And don’t scare your mother on the news again.”

Mom makes a sound that is both laugh and sob.

I nod, because promising that would be a lie and we both know it.

“I’ll try.”

Dad smiles. There it is again.

I lean down and hug him one more time.

This time, I don’t break. Not all the way.

Then Jelly Boy and I leave the room, stepping back into the maze of white walls and humming lights, while behind us my father keeps smiling like the world hasn’t just become something I don’t know how to fight.

By the time I get back to my apartment, I feel like someone scraped out the inside of my skull with a grapefruit spoon and replaced it with static. I set myself on autopilot, scarfing down a pre-prepped meal with precisely calculated macros, before taking a scalding hot shower.

When I finally make it to the couch, I’m wearing sweatpants, a soft shirt, and a mantle of exhaustion.  Jelly Boy sits beside me. He oozes into the cushion next to me, a pseudopod is extended from his body, formed around the remote, and the TV is on.

Some reality show. I think it’s Big Brother.

I stare at the screen for about nine seconds and absorb none of it.

My phone buzzes. Then buzzes again. Then again.

It’s sitting on the coffee table, facedown.

I pick it up. Hundreds of unread messages. Most are from people I haven’t exchanged texts with in years. High school acquaintances. Old coworkers. Gym people. A distant cousin I’m pretty sure once stole my Game Boy Advance charger and never admitted it. Someone named “Derek Finance Drinks???” who I do not remember but apparently “always knew I was built different.”

There are also seventeen missed calls from unknown numbers and one voicemail from a local radio station asking if I’d like to come on tomorrow morning and “talk about the jorts.”

This is all too much to deal with right now. I silence all notifications. The phone goes blessedly dark.

Jelly Boy blurps without looking away from the TV. One of the reality show contestants is crying in a pantry over vetoes or something.

I need a distraction. Any distraction.

I open my System messages.

There’s the level-up notification from the shark-man leader still waiting in the corner. Two move levels gained. I allocate my new stat points with a mental flick.

I don’t linger on the battle summary. Nereus, the Trench King, gets reduced to tidy text, and that makes it feel even more ridiculous. A giant monster crawls out of Lake Erie declaring conquest, and the System files it under “encounter resolved.”

I move on to the next notification window.

STAGE TWO: THE BLESSED FEW

[Opt-in] / [Opt-out]

Time Remaining for Selection: 4 Days, 6 Hours, 22 Minutes

Reminder: If a Participant fails to make a selection in the allotted time, they will automatically be opted out of the God Game.

I stare at it.

A little over four days to decide whether I keep playing the God Game or let the System remove me from the board.

Earlier this morning, when I was still in the hospital completing my recovery, I thought maybe opting out made sense. Maybe it still does. The consequences seem almost minor, considering the past System punishments for failing to progress in the Game.

No longer able to access Gates. Level locked.

Does that matter?

I’m over Level 40. I have multiple physical resistances, a flying staff, absurd magic fists, a slime familiar who watches reality television, and a Guild contract that pays more money than I knew what to do with even before the entire country started calling me Muscle Mage. I could have an excellent life. Handle Gate breaches for Harvest Guild. Save people. Get paid. Train. Build something. Maybe help Liv. Help Mom. Spend time with Dad while there’s still—

The thought catches.

I swallow hard.

Then another thought rises.

What was the prize for reaching the end of the God Game?

The winner gets whatever they desire, right? A wish granted.

My whole body goes still.

The reality show keeps playing in the background. Someone shouts, “You lied to my face!” and the camera cuts to the female host, standing before a studio audience who all gasp like this is the most important betrayal on Earth.

Could that save my father?

The thought is dangerous. I don’t want to touch it. I don’t want to hope around it. Hope is a bear trap when the universe has a sense of humor.

But it’s there now.

The opt-in option is suddenly a door. An opportunity.

And behind it is death.

Because the consequence of opting in and failing is not removal. It’s culling.

The last time the System ushered out that consequence, I ended up standing inside a room painted with my boss’s insides. The System can dress it up however it wants, but I’ve learned its vocabulary.

Dad wouldn’t want that.

He wouldn’t want me risking my life for him. Not like this. Not for a maybe. Not for a wish at the end of a divine tournament where only ten parties advance and gods start handing out Blessings like sponsorship deals. There’s still so much I don’t understand.

He would say, “You’ve got your whole life ahead of you, Joey.”

He would mean it.

And I would hate him a little for being right.

My hands curl into fists. The couch cushion creaks under me. Jelly Boy finally turns away from the TV, his surface rippling. He buzzes once, low and worried.

“I know,” I say, though I don’t know what he said. My mind is too much of a mess to decipher it right now. “I know.”

The decision sits in my chest like a stone.

Opt out, live, help where I can, be there for my family.

Opt in, risk dying, chase a wish that might be the only thing powerful enough to save my dad.

There’s no good answer. There’s just fear wearing different hats.

I close the selection window.

The blinking icon retreats to the corner, still waiting.

I’m too emotional right now. Too raw. Too stupid with grief. This is not a decision I get to make while my brain is still replaying Mom’s voice and Dad’s smile and the words making sure he’s comfortable.

I need to talk to people.

Liv. Veronica. Jerome. Clyde.

I rub both hands over my face and lean back into the couch.

The paranoid contestant in the pantry is now eating cereal from the box while whispering something about betrayal. Jelly Boy buzzes approvingly.

“Good for her,” I mutter.

I need another distraction. One that doesn’t involve cosmic stakes, cancer, dead Guilders, or people from high school texting me fire emojis.

I open my User Statistics.

The blue-white panel unfolds in front of me, clean and clinical.

With a simple mental command, I expand the windows. A haptic tingling tickles my frontal lobe as the blue-white window separates into several compact summary screens. A neat little inventory of everything the System thinks I am.

For once, I’m almost grateful for it.

Because numbers don’t cry. Numbers just sit there, waiting to tell you how much stronger you’ve become, and how far that still might not be enough. Number tell you how much further you have to go.

Your Flexible Casting Trait has been triggered by a recent Level Up!

FLEXIBLE CASTING: You have 1 Modified Spellcasting available. Please select one Spell to see available options.

Another chance to reshape part of my arsenal. Take something I already know and twist it into something better. Something more versatile. Maybe I could modify [Wizard’s Fist] again. Maybe [Slimy Shield] for more defensive flexibility. A dozen ideas flicker through my head.

I would typically be excited by this opportunity. But right now? I’m just tired.

“Nope,” I mutter.

I close the notification without choosing.

It drops back into the corner, alongside the Opt-out option for the next Phase of the God Game.

I’m sitting on that too. I need to consider my options carefully, and with a clear mind.

Jelly Boy vibrates beside me, fully invested in his TV show. He’s pressed against my hip, warm and cool at the same time, which makes no sense biologically or physically but is deeply comforting anyway. His surface ripples gently with each buzz, like the surface of a lake on a windy day.

I reach over and pat the top of his head. Or what counts as his head, at least.

My hand sinks in slightly before bouncing back, his slime surface giving way with that familiar soft resistance. He makes a tiny pleased noise and nestles closer, oozing against my side until he’s tucked there like he belongs. He does. I leave my hand resting on him.

I don’t check another message. I don’t look at my phone. I don’t think about the opt-in timer, or Dad’s hospital room, or Reshma dissolving into gold light, or Matt vanishing against Terminal Tower, or the giant red eye in the sky.

I let my thoughts blur.

The edges of the room soften.

The apartment becomes couch, television glow, city noise through glass, and the gentle vibration of Jelly Boy against my ribs.

The world keeps ending in complicated ways, but for a little while, I just sit there with my slime, watching nothing.

And I let myself disappear into it.
