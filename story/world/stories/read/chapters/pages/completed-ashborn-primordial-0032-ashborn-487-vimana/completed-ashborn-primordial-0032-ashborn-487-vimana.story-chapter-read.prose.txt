The hallway stretched deeper into the compound, leading the group farther into the bowels of the Vimana.

“Hard to believe we’re on a floating object,” Maiya muttered in a hushed voice, as though in deference to the ancient beings who built this floating marvel. “It doesn’t move at all. Completely unlike being on a skyship.”

“I can’t even begin to imagine how much such a thing must weigh,” Vir replied. “You know how bigger ships sway less in the ocean? I imagine this is similar in principle.”

“Astute,” Ashani said with an appreciative smile. “However, in addition to their prodigious weight, all Vimana possess inertial dampeners, stability thrusters, and several other devices to cancel any feeling of motion.”

Though Vir didn’t know half of what Ashani had said, he knew better by now than to ask. Some Imperium magic was simply beyond his capacity to know.

Several doors opened and closed as they passed, granting them glimpses into the depths of this colossal machine.

Yet, as Ashani had said, instead of ancient machinery or mysterious artifacts, what they saw inside was surprisingly mundane in nature, if exotic in execution. Rooms that looked like they might have once been offices, communal halls, and more than a few bedchambers. Albeit with chairs that looked nothing like any chair Vir had ever seen, desks that floated, and beds that one could see through.

Everywhere they looked were the trappings of everyday life. Exotic, Imperium life.

“You really could live here,” Maiya said, eyes wide. “Can you imagine having a home that moves? A home like this?”

“You don’t need to imagine,” Vir said dryly. “Or did you forget that you quite literally live at the top of a spire built by the Prime Imperium?”

Maiya turned away, flushing. “Of course not! I still can’t believe I can even set foot in the Ash, let alone Mahādi. Let alone live there. But the Ash is…”

“A wasteland,” Vir finished for her.

“Yeah,” she said softly. “Can you imagine living in a floating fortress like this? One that moves around the Human Realm? We could be anywhere we wanted. In the summer, we could fly north to Kartara, and in winter, we could bask in the warm waters of Rani. It would be marvelous. And the views—you must be able to see the whole world from here! The clouds passing beneath as you travel…”

Vir opted not to mention to her that even with their limited set of Gates, traveling across the Human Realm—or even between realms—took no more than a few minutes, and a few hours in the worst cases.

“It’s quite an incredible daydream,” he said. “But that’s all it is. Let’s not lose sight of why we’re here. We’re on a mission to end the Ash Tears that ravage our realms. To fight those who cannot be fought. This is no time for relaxation.”

Maiya deflated. “Right. I know.”

They continued deeper inside. Vir had fully expected to encounter automata or other defense mechanisms, but to his surprise, they met none at all.

“I imagine the Vimanas were abandoned during the Fall,” Ashani said. “A precautionary measure. Though I must admit, I was quite surprised to learn that the humans had built their cities under them.”

Vir felt the same, though he supposed it made sense. Why not build under the protection of a godly relic?

Finally, they entered what could only be described as the Vimana’s grand central chamber, yet as Vir and Maiya craned their necks, the word ‘enormous’ didn’t even begin to describe it.

The chamber stretched the entire height of the Vimana, a massive sloping space no less than half a thousand paces across. Mirroring the ship’s vaguely pyramidal structure, the walls of the chamber tapered toward the top, coming to a point.

All along its slanted walls ran lifts on tracks running up and down in a fashion unlike anything Vir had ever seen.

And then there was the gold.

Brilliant, shining gold adorned every surface that wasn’t made of the flawless white Imperium material. From floor to ceiling, the walls were covered over in impossibly intricate carvings that crisscrossed in dizzying ways.

And that was to say nothing of the prana. It flowed in and out of the carvings, weaving patterns through the golden latticework like veins.

“How long do you think it would take,” Maiya asked in awe, “for even the most skilled artisans of the realms to create something like this?”

“Decades. If not longer,” Vir said without a doubt. “Assuming it’s even possible.”

“My people always did enjoy the art of creation,” Ashani said with a tight smile. “Though, I fear they might have taken it a bit far. This is an early model Vimana. The later ones were even more gaudy and ostentatious.”

Vir felt that only a member of the Imperium could show disapproval at such a wonder.

He felt no such thing. All he saw was a creation that defied time itself. A monument to gods who had once attained heights that would never be seen again. A thing to be celebrated, revered, and remembered in awe.

Yet as impressive as the intricate decorations were, they weren’t what held his attention. What truly drew his gaze was the dense tangle of prana swirling high above, concentrated near the summit of the vast chamber.

Vir pointed upward. “I believe that’s our destination?”

Ashani nodded. “This way, if you please,” she said, leading the group to one of the walls. “The Vimana appears to be in standby mode. It is a power-saving measure meant for extended periods of hibernation. As such, most of its systems are offline, though I suspect the lifts remain operational.”

The entourage followed Ashani into one of the cylindrical capsules attached to the slanted walls. Opaque from the outside, the lift’s interior afforded an impossibly clear view, as if made of glass finer than anything crafted in the realms.

It was so seamless that Vir had to tap its surface to assure himself there was actually a wall there.

“This is incredible,” Maiya breathed, her eyes wide as the lift began to ascend. It moved with such perfect smoothness that Vir couldn’t tell they were in motion. Only the distant floor shrinking below told him they were rising. Within moments, the chamber beneath was just a speck of gold and white far below.

The lift slowed as they reached the top and came to a gentle stop, and from this high vantage, the chamber was even more breathtaking.

They stepped out onto a triangular walkway that mirrored the Vimana’s shape. Where the base had been cavernous, the space here narrowed to a triangular catwalk in a much more modest space—its only feature a pair of ornate double doors set into one of the slanted, pyramidal walls.

The doors were sealed tight, but Vir quickly recognized the same sort of lock he had used on the outer hull. Channeling prana into the embedded slots in the correct order, he watched as the doors slid open without a sound.

Beyond lay a hemispherical chamber oddly similar to the primordial chambers Janak had created for the primordials.

Why such a thing would exist here, he couldn’t say, and Vir was about to slip into his chakra mindscape to learn more about the space when the lights abruptly dimmed, plunging the room into darkness.

Stars ignited, blazing brilliantly across the curved ceiling and the floor beneath their feet.

Suddenly, it was as if they were suspended in the heavens themselves.

Below stretched a vast plane—a nearly-infinite landscape glowing faintly with prana. Vir realized at once what it was.

“The known realms,” he whispered. “The Demon Realm, the Ash, the Human Realm… our universe.”

“This is our universe as it once was,” Ashani murmured, her voice reverent. “Before the Fall.”

“Indeed it is,” thundered a booming voice.

They turned to see a towering figure of white and blue prana materialize before them. An ancient man with older eyes and a flowing beard.

Vir immediately recognized him. How could he not?

“We meet again.”

“There is much to discuss,” said Janak’s avatar without preamble. “For the journey that awaits you is greater than any you have yet faced. Though I speak not in the flesh, your presence here means you stand upon the cusp of revelation.”

He paused, the weight of eternity in his tone. “You are, however, not ready. I fear that all is lost.”

Vir’s expression darkened. “It’s true, then. The Lost Ultimate Arts?”

Janak shook his head. “Not here. Our words are not our own in this place. We must meet in person. Face to face.”

“Just how do we do that?” Vir asked, already dreading the answer. “I’ve spent years trying to get through that Prana Swarm around Mahādi’s central tower.”

“That,” Janak said, his voice fading, “is something I cannot tell you, for my own safety. You must discover it yourself.”

Vir understood now. He hadn’t before, but he’d learned much of late.

The Fateweavers were always listening, their reach extending even here, into this sanctum crafted by gods.

“I look forward to our meeting, then,” Vir said quietly. “But how do we take the first step?”

Janak gestured toward a corner of the chamber, where space itself rippled and tore open. Like an Ash Tear, yet profoundly different.

Vir felt it instinctively. This was no ordinary rift.

This was not merely a gate between realms. No, this was a gate to something much, much further. Something incomprehensible.
