[Time]: Autumn Semester, Day 76 — 10:15 PM[Location]: Yggdrasil Academy · Dormitory [Golden Bough] · Room 302

Hathaway had the lumen-globe cranked to a punishing white and a sheet of parchment in front of her that had been blank for two hours, which meant it was now the most expensive object on the desk.

She hates cats.

Evangeline's intelligence had been sitting in the middle of her workspace for three weeks, radiating the specific toxic warmth of a logic trap that had been left running on purpose.

She hates them because she watched them that long.

Both branches had died weeks ago. Leave the cat out and she submitted a landscape that would not lose a point and would not gain one either. Put a cat in and she filed into the most crowded category in that vault, and a boring insult was the only kind that ever got punished there.

Hathaway chewed the end of the quill and let her developer brain pace the inside of her skull.

When both branches of a conditional throw, you build a third button.

She sat up so fast the chair complained. The parchment came across the wood, the ink came uncapped, and the quill went down before she had finished having the thought, because the thought had arrived pre-assembled out of a lecture delivered by a woman standing inside a snow globe of her own tobacco smoke with tears running unwiped down both cheeks.

Emotion is the non-linear feedback of the soul's circuitry to external stimuli. That which is unattainable exists in a superposition of high value. The moment it is observed, and entirely possessed, the wave function collapses.

That was an autopsy, and the body on the table belonged to the woman grading this assignment.

Emilia Lucas had offered total possession in a single transaction, with the paperwork already signed.

The wave function collapsed. The Fifth Seat filed it, thanked her, and closed the door with impeccable courtesy.

Hathaway's inner producer had the whole design document open now and was writing fast. A woman who lost that way does not want a polite trinket to carry across a room. She wants ordnance. She wants something that gets a reading off a face that has never once let anything through, and she does not care whether the reading is pleasant, because the alternative is another decade of not knowing.

The quill hit the parchment, and Hathaway drew a containment lattice: a soul circuit wired through a spatial anchor, running a stability algorithm whose entire function was to refuse, permanently and by design, to resolve.

Not a pet. A state machine.

With the backend locked, her otaku muscle memory shouldered its way to the terminal and took the controls, because a masterpiece needed more than airtight physics. It needed art direction, lore, and an interaction loop. It needed a persona. Character design sold the banner, and everyone in her old industry had learned that the hard way, usually twice.

The brief specified brilliant gold, true white, luminous silver. The safe read was a white cat with silver trim, and Hathaway got two strokes into it before her inner art director threw the palette out the window.

In the Inner Sea, white cats were decorative. The black ones were the omen: wealth, fortune, luck arriving from a direction nobody had budgeted for.

She took Irene's coin out of the desk drawer and held it under the glare.

A matte black body. Twenty-seven points of gold sun stamped through the middle of it.

Why compromise?

A chassis of pure black that took the ambient light in and declined to return any of it, and two eyes of luminous gold set into the dark. The most auspicious object a witch could put on a shelf, rendered exactly in the client's own colors.

Hathaway grinned at the sketch, pleased with herself in a way she would have found embarrassing in daylight, and moved on to the part that broke her.

The behavioral matrix.

She closed her eyes, and the file surfaced without being requested: the Eastern Courtyard, the summer, an Aspect of Ovelia working a garden full of apex predators the way a professional works a room she has already bought.

Allison had put her hand out for a formal greeting, pulled it back before contact, and drawn two manicured nails across the center of the Fifth Seat's open palm. Hours later, at the edge of the same courtyard, Irene's thumb had curled in and pressed hard into the exact center of that palm.

Was that revulsion. The question came out of her threat arrays with Evangeline's fingerprints all over it. Is that what disgust looks like on a woman who never lets it reach her face?

Hathaway groaned at the ceiling and put both hands in her hair.

Because the other reading was sitting right there, and it fit the same data exactly, and there was no experiment she could run on either one.

And she could not get her thinking back on the road, because Allison at that party had been the design. A fraction more and it was cloying. A fraction less and it was cold. Every witch in that courtyard had spent the morning suspended at the exact distance Allison had chosen for them, and not one of them had held the dial.

Then the second file loaded, and made it worse.

Avenka, at the Wisteria, crossing the Greed Umbrella's suite to seize the wrist of Irene and announce that cat wanted to buy books. Irene's golden eyes had widened by a fraction of a millimeter.

And then she had sighed, and nodded politely to the room, and gone.

Hostages do not sigh and nod goodnight to the room.

Hathaway pulled the two frames up side by side: one had withheld contact at the last instant, and the other had imposed it and refused to hear a single word on the subject.

Neither of them had asked.

The feature is not the distance. Her design brain wrote it down in block capitals. It is who holds the dial.

Flawless work that nobody can argue with is work that nobody argues with. Hathaway looked at the core of her matrix. Emilia wants a design she cannot put down, and she will take the faults that come with it.

And if it landed wrong, if the thing genuinely made Irene Habsburg uncomfortable in her own vault, Hathaway was not going to pay for it. Irene did not punish people for aiming correctly. It was possibly the only thing about that woman she would have bet the house on.

The quill flew.

Aloof. Unhelpful. Maliciously patient.

Ignored, it manifested at the periphery: sprawled along a shelf, draped over a priceless antique, golden eyes half shut, demanding nothing from anybody. Glanced at, it stayed, and permitted the look. Watched, it tolerated the attention with visible boredom, and the tail moved once. Watched past that, it declined to go on being there.

Reached for, the spatial anchor engaged and the chassis phased sideways out of the world, and the hand closed on cool air, and the sensation that arrived one second later, unfailingly, from somewhere across the room, was of being looked at.

It always came back. She wrote the guarantee into the specification in plain language, because it had to survive an appraisal: return assured, interval undefined, no input that shortened one. The circuit read intent. Waiting was an input.

An artifact that could be owned, catalogued, insured, and displayed, and never once held. Or kept.

Hathaway set the quill down and rolled her wrist until it cracked.

The blueprint lay finished under the lumen-globe: spatial mechanics, sensory illusion, and doll-making philosophy soldered into one architecture with no precedent anywhere in the index.

It was the best thing she had built all semester, and entirely original.

She should have gone to bed.

Instead she pulled a fresh sheet off the stack, dipped the quill, and started drawing the housing: the shell, the hinge, the friction of the latch, the precise angle at which the lid would have to open so that the gold in those eyes caught the light before anything else in the room did.

None of that was worth a single point.

She was wrapping a present.

[Time]: Autumn Semester, Day 81 — 3:45 PM[Location]: West Campus · Yggdrasil Academy · Wondrous Item Hall

Emilia Lucas closed her case, and the latch went off across four tiers of seating like a rifle.

"From today," she said, "I will answer no further inquiries regarding the client. If you have not understood the parameters of that vault by now, you were never meant to enter it."

She was through the faculty door before the acoustics had finished with it.

Forty-five students packed up in a silence with unexploded ordnance in it. Hathaway slid her notes into her ring and watched the room come apart along lines that had not existed eight weeks ago. Surtrina shouldered her bag and left without looking back. Spectra went into the shadow of the exit arch and stopped being a separate object from it. Yenna gathered her things with flat precision and moved for the doors alone.

There had been a version of this corridor, in the first month, where the five of them dismantled the lecture all the way to the dining hall and got told twice to keep it down.

Hathaway came out into the hall with Victoria holding station at her usual half-step, and caught the gloved hand, and squeezed the leather once.

"I want to talk to Yenna," she murmured.

Victoria stopped. The pale blue eyes moved across her face, and then she gave one microscopic nod, released her hand, and put her shoulders back against the stone wall to wait.

Hathaway broke into a jog.

"Yenna."

The golden ears rotated to track her before the rest of Yenna did. "Hathaway."

Hathaway went into her coat and came out with the frost-warded vial, and held it so the corridor light went through the thick pearl-white liquid and did not all come out the other side.

"My aunt handed me a box of six in one go," she said, pitching it as a woman complaining about her mail. "Apparently our house buys them straight out of Renavelle. I had no idea your family ran that kind of supply chain."

Yenna's gaze dropped to the glass, held for a beat, and came back up.

"That is not my family's industry," she said, level as a poured measure. "Renavelle is the ancestral seat for most of Milan'thir's fox witches. My house is five generations out from that bloodline."

Hathaway adjusted her grip on the vial. "Right. Well. I just figured that even in the enormous clans, the good juniors get handed the good stuff." She shrugged, going for light and not quite making it. "Given what you put on the board."

"I did drink one," Yenna said.

Hathaway choked. "One? Is a fox witch's constitution honestly that efficient? You cap the skeletal reinforcement on a single dose?"

Yenna tilted her head and corrected the misunderstanding with the frictionless economy of a spreadsheet closing a column.

"Resources are limited."

The corridor did not have anything to say about that.

Hathaway's inner accountant looked down at the object in her own hand and priced it, correctly, for the first time. A dynasty had handed her six because her ribs had inconvenienced her. Yenna had been handed one, once, and told to go and compete against a cohort of girls who drank legendary assets with breakfast.

She swallowed the pity before it reached her face, and looked straight down the empty hall instead.

"Why did you tell us?" she asked. "The sunflowers. The valleys. That was vault-grade data and you handed it to a room full of your direct competitors for nothing."

Yenna's three golden tails traced slow curves behind her knees.

"Do you want the truth, or the lie?"

Is there a build where I ask for the lie, Hathaway thought, and then recognized the cadence, because it was a dialogue wheel and it was being offered by someone who only ran that particular subroutine on friends.

"I'm a completionist," she said. "Both."

"The lie," Yenna said, "is that our friendship is an unbreakable bond, and your high score is my high score, and I could not bear to watch my dearest companions struggle in the dark."

She did not make it to the end of the sentence before the laugh got out.

Hathaway clapped a hand over her sternum and staggered into the wall. "Ouch. Straight through the plate, Yenna."

Yenna's ears flicked. "The truth is that even if you used it, you could not build a better meadow than I can."

Hathaway blinked, and her developer brain took the parameters, ran them, and returned a clean proof.

It was a debuff with an area of effect. Four overachievers, handed one highly specific and deceptively simple aesthetic, all four constitutionally incapable of leaving a good prompt alone.

Something in Hathaway's chest that had been carrying a debt for three weeks quietly put it down.

"Then I suppose I should say it's a good thing I didn't use your idea," she said, grinning.

Yenna laid a hand over her heart. "It is genuinely heartbreaking," she said, in a voice of absolute theatrical fraudulence, "to watch a friend throw my goodwill away like that."

Hathaway held the vial out.

"Deiner's final," she said, and the banter came off her voice like a coat. "Five minutes of live fire, whole cohort at once, and last year's average was forty-nine seconds. I'm opening a channel tonight. Five of us. Separate from the other thing. The other thing stays shut."

Yenna looked at the vial and did not take it.

"I invest in my friends," Hathaway said. "You take that, you owe me a favor. Call it the buy-in."

Her social engineering thread shoved a line at her: my family just buys me another one, it's nothing, take it. Every word of it was true and all of it would have converted a contract into charity in under a second. She killed it before it cleared her teeth, and heard how thin the rest of it had sounded, and pushed the glass another two inches anyway, because she had nothing better.

Yenna looked at the pearl-white liquid. Then at Hathaway's face, which was holding, with some effort, the expression of a woman conducting business.

The golden ears came down a degree.

She reached out and closed her fingers around the glass.

"I accept."

Hathaway walked back down the corridor with her hands in her pockets, letting her accountant thread leave the ledger entry open. One vial out, one undefined favor in. No set parameters, no expiration date. Any rational witch running a short-term valuation would have concluded she had overpaid by a margin visible from orbit.

But a blank marker had no fixed ceiling. Its true yield depended entirely on the heights Yenna eventually reached, and what fraction of that future authority she decided the debt was worth.

Angel investment, Hathaway decided, entirely comfortable with the long position, and closed the file.

Ahead of her, Victoria was still against the wall where she had left her, in no apparent hurry, and had been for exactly four minutes.
