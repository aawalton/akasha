The brewery road had started to feel colder. It did not feel cold yet, instead it felt refreshing, which Alric considered a warning sign. He looked toward the building as they approached, trying to see it from Captain Rein’s point of view.

The enlarged roof frame had sections of the ceiling open, Steam rose and drifted through them. The carters were currently loading the handcarts, there were more of them than Alric remembered. Someone shouted for a clean bung. Someone else shouted back that if he wanted one he should stop losing them in places only idiots looked.

The brewery had not become less busy because the weather had changed. It had simply added cold to the list of things it intended to ignore until failure made that impossible.

Beside Alric, Captain Rein watched the yard without speaking.

Alric studied the Captain properly this time, they had met earlier but conversation had made that difficult.

He was not old. Not young either. Somewhere in that useful middle where a man had enough years behind him to distrust easy answers and enough strength left to make other people regret testing him. Mid-thirties by Alric’s guess. His hair was dark, clipped short beneath the edge of his helm, and his face had the lean look of someone who had eaten too much army food.

There was a title attached to him.

Vale had mentioned it quietly before the man arrived. Minor noble. Some family out near the border. Old enough to matter to heralds, not rich enough to impress anyone already seated at court.

Captain Rein did not wear the title like armour. If anything, he treated it like an old cloak someone else insisted belonged to him. His uniform mattered. His rank mattered. His orders mattered.Alric found that reassuring.

“These three buildings?” Rein asked pointing at the three large warehouses nearby.

Alric nodded and began pointing. “Main brewery, the left building is where we store and clean casks, and the stone building on the right is where we put beer before it becomes beer, we call it fermentation. We own all three.”

“Own?” Rein asked with his eyes wide.

“Well, no. Rent. Although I haven’t had Vale, my clerk, explain to me what changed, since becoming a royal factor, other than its now cheaper.” Alric glanced at him. “I pay money and get headaches. That part remains the same.”

One of the guards behind him made a very small sound.

Rein did not smile, but his eyes shifted with amusement. “We’ll start with the main building.”

“Of course.” Alric said, he gestured to it. They walked.

The two guards followed a few steps back. Not too close. Close enough that Alric remained aware of them every time he changed pace. He was beginning to understand what Seren had meant about guards who knew his habits. These men were good. Professional. Alert. They watched the road, the rooftops, the workers, the gaps between carts, and occasionally him. His own guards were with them, his own people spent more time sizing up people than anything else.

That made them both comforting and irritating. Inside, the brewery was heat, movement, and the familiar smell of grain turning useful. Alric felt himself taking a deep breath of it, realizing he missed it.

The warmth hit Alric next, stirring paddles moved through mash with heavy rhythm, Hal stood nearby watching carefully. He spotted Alric and the captain, giving his greeting as a nod. Men carried sacks of grain to the men operating the boilers, Hal wrote something down. Steam gathered in the rafters before finding its way out. The boilers worked along the wall, broad and hot and increasingly ordinary to everyone except visitors.

Rein stopped just inside the entrance. Alric waited for the usual reaction. There was always one. Some people saw the size first. Some saw the steam. Some saw the men. Some saw the rows of casks and immediately began making calculations in the privacy of their own greed.

Rein saw the doors. Alric knew because the captain’s eyes moved from one entrance to the next, then to the side passage, then up toward the rafters, then to the boiler line, then back to the men working between all of it. Not impressed rather assessing.

“How many entrances?” Rein asked glancing at the obvious ones.

“Three proper ones. Main road. Yard at the back. Door coming off the one wing where cooled casks go to the other building.” Alric explained pointing them out.

“I count everything that opens.” Rein said with a frown.

“There are a lot of shutters, they are barred.” Alric pointed to one nearby

Rein nodded, still glancing around. “Night watch?”

“We have adventurers mostly. They do have shifts.” Alric said looking around trying to spot them.

“That is not the same thing.” Rein said with a frown.

“I am learning that.” Alric said not seeing them.

They moved past the boiler area.

Alric explained the flow because that was easier than standing silently while a military man discovered all the ways his brewery could be attacked. Grain came in. Water came from the wells, was moved overhead. Mash was heated. Wort moved outside to cool, then the fermentation building. Casks were cleaned, filled, sealed, watched, moved, lost, found, cursed at, and eventually sent out into the city where tavernkeepers complained, paid, and then complained again.

Rein asked questions. Good ones.

Not brewing questions. He did not care which stage made flavour. He cared where the grain sat before it entered the building. He cared how many people could reach the boilers. He cared whether workers were assigned by task or wandered where needed. He cared where spoiled batches went and who decided they were spoiled. He cared who could enter the cellar.

Alric answered as honestly as he could.

“This place is remarkable,” Rein said at last, he leaned back, put his hands on his hips and took it all in.

Alric blinked at him, one brow lowered. “Thank you?”

“I don’t mean the beer.” Rien clarified, shaking his head and making a waving gesture with his hand.

“Uh...” Was all Alric said trying to understand what else could be remarkable to the captain.

Rein glanced toward the workers. “Any fool with enough grain can make bad beer. Some can even make good beer in small amounts. This…” He gestured, not widely, but enough to include the building. “This is order.”

Alric looked around the brewery. It did not look like order to him. Henry was shouting at a man because a cask had been placed where a cask had no business existing. Hal was arguing with someone as a barrel was lowered through a roof. It looked like wet boards, hot stones, men sweating, ledgers growing fat, and everyone discovering fresh ways for casks to ruin their day.

“You may be pushing it,” Alric said giving him a suspicious glance.

“No,” Rein replied, he gestured to the boilers. “Other cities will try to copy this, they will do it very badly.”

Alric shrugged following his gaze. “Probably.”

Rein began walking again, he pointed out things as he walked. “They’ll see the boilers, the casks, the amounts you make. They’ll ask how many men you use and how much grain you buy. Then they’ll put twice as many men in half the space and poison a district by spring.”

Alric did not like how easily he could imagine that.

Rein said turning fully on Alric. “They’ll copy the copper, not the discipline. They’ll copy the size, because size is what fools understand first.”

They left the main building through the side door.

The yard between the brewery and the next warehouse was narrower than Alric liked now. It had been a perfectly normal gap between buildings once. Then the brewery had eaten it. Boards had been laid across muddy sections. Casks moved through it daily. Workers used it as a passage, a waiting space, a temporary staging area, and occasionally a place to stand while pretending they had not been sent somewhere else.

They entered the fermentation building. This one did not impress anyone at first. It was not meant to. It held casks becoming beer. This was Hesta’s domain, it did carry a lot of spares, sealed ingredients, bundles of hoops, lengths of wood, a row of bungs sorted into shallow boxes, spare cloth, chalk, charcoal, and several things that had been purchased because someone had said they might become useful.

The longer Rein looked, the less relaxed he seemed. They continued through the building. A worker lifted a full cask and placed it on the built racks, he stopped when he saw them, shifted awkwardly, then tried to decide whether he should bow, salute, move, speak, or become part of the wall. He settled for stepping aside.

Rein watched the unfortunate worker. “Do workers know their guards?”

“I don’t think so.” Alric admitted, feeling uncomfortable on the workers behalf, who was shaking his head and trying to be invisible.

“They will need to, you know. My men will also need to know which workers belong where.” He said still watching the worker in question, amplifying his discomfort.

Alric nodded although he disliked the calm way the captain said it. This was getting complicated and complication seemed less threatening when people were at least annoyed by it. They stepped back out into the cold yard and crossed toward the third building. This building held casks coming back from the taverns and new ones. They were washed, then steamed, then left to dry before the process got repeated.

Inside, it smelled of wood, stale dust, and faint alcohol. Casks rested along every wall in neat rows. All empty and all waiting, some labelled so carefully that Vale had clearly been involved. A corner had been cleared for tools. Another held bundled straw. The floor had been swept, but not with the same obsessive violence Seren or Hesta directed at the main buildings. Rein walked the length of it, then returned without speaking. Alric found himself touching one of the window sills with a small smile.

The captain looked at the walls. Then the doors. Then the space outside through a narrow window. “You expanded sideways.” The captain said although Alric couldn’t be sure if it was a question.

“It made sense,” Alric explained, just taking in the building and smiling slightly. “We needed space. These buildings were available, well I say that but it’s more the previous owners could be motivated to move.”

“Well…” Rein began, his gaze moved over the racks of empty casks, “Moving like this creates edges, then gaps, then work for someone like me.” He frowned as he looked at a back doorway and the staff quarters.

Alric looked at the warehouse again. “I am starting to see that.”

They finished the warehouses and Alric lead them to the wells, it was the thing that alarmed the king the most after all.

The wells stood just beyond the brewery yard, not far from the main building. They had never looked dramatic to Alric. Useful, yes. Annoying, often. Dangerous only in the ordinary way holes in the ground were dangerous if one was stupid around them. Alric took a moment to remember this was a brewery.

Now Rein stopped in front of them like he had reached the actual reason for his visit. The captain stood still for a long moment. The two guards behind him did the same. Alric felt the yard shift around that silence. Workers still moved. Carts still rattled. Someone laughed near the far door and was immediately told to carry something if he had that much air to spare. But around the wells, the world narrowed.

Rein walked around the first one. He looked at the stone lip, the cover, the barrel lowering mechanism, the drainage, the path from well to brewery, the overhead lines connecting the frame, the windows nearby, the places where a man could stand without being noticed if people were busy enough.

Then he looked at Alric. “I understand the king now.” Rein said frowning at the wells.

Alric did not like that sentence.

Rein rested one gloved hand on the well’s stone edge, he gestured to the well with his other hand. “This is the brewery.”

“The wells?” Alric asked, while he was impressed the Captain understood the importance of the water, he wanted his perspective.

“The water.” Rein clarified, before turning to Alric again.

Alric said nothing, he wondered how much the captain knew about beer to make that statement.

The captain looked toward the brewery, then back to the well. “A man does not need to burn your brewery if he can make you afraid to drink from it.”

He had known it. In theory. The king had said as much, though not the same way. Poison the wells, poison the stores, poison the drink, poison the city. It had been a large thought then, too large to hold properly. They had Tarrow in place as well as the filters, to catch such details but it was impossible to explain that properly.

Rein nodded, he pointed at the road. “The road changes first.”

The captain turned and pointed along the brewery road. It ran between the cluster of buildings and out toward the broader street, used by carts, workers, neighbours, passers-by, curious children, delivery men, and anyone who had discovered it was a useful shortcut. Alric followed his gesture and felt his stomach sink.

“I thought you were going to say gates.” Alric asked, glancing at him sideways.

“I will say gates after this.” Rein grinned as he said it.

“Walls?” Alric asked, his gaze trying to see where it would all go.

“Not any time soon and not fortress walls.” Rein explained, making complicated hand gestures Alric did not understand.

Rein looked at him again. This time the smile nearly survived. “The road will be closed. Brewery carts, workers, deliveries. Crown guards. No casual passage.”

Alric looked down the road. It was not a grand road. The cobblestones had lifted in some sections. It had ruts, stains, corners where water gathered, and one patch where carts always bounced badly enough to make drivers curse in new languages. It was still a road. People used roads. Closing one felt larger than the road deserved. “There’s another warehouse that needs to use the road.” Alric said slowly.

Rein followed his gaze. “The one at the end?”

“Yes.” Alric said, he looked over at it.

“Who owns it?” Rein asked, following his gaze.

“I don’t know. Vale knows. When we got the cask building, we only did because the owner of that warehouse was trying to gouge us.” Alric explained with a small shrug.

Rein’s eyebrows rose.

“It was a while ago,” Alric added, making a dismissive waving gesture.

“How badly?” Rein asked, displaying his grin proudly, the two soldiers behind grinned too.

“Badly enough that I remembered it while already owning too many buildings.” Alric said with a frown.

The captain gave a short laugh. “That is useful to know,” he said.

Alric narrowed his eyes. “Why?”

“That is where the barracks will go.” Rein said with a wide grin. Alric stared at him. The captain looked down the road again, already measuring it with his eyes. “Good position. Controls the end of the closed stretch. Near the storage buildings. Enough space for beds, arms, a duty room, reports, small yard. Everything I’m going to need. I wondered if there would be objection.”

“There will probably be objection.” Alric said, letting out a deep breath from his nose seeing the paperwork coming.

“From someone you dislike.” Rein said with a smile as if that was the only part that mattered.

“I didn’t say dislike.” Alric clarified, glancing at Rein who seemed to be enjoying this.

“You remembered him with detail.” Rein said raising one finger with the same grin earlier.

Alric looked at the warehouse at the end of the road. The captain’s gaze moved past him. Alric turned slightly and saw Mara standing near the edge of the yard.

She was not close enough to interrupt. She stood beside the corner of the storage building, hands folded together, shawl wrapped tight around her shoulders. Her hair was pinned back, though the wind had begun loosening a few strands. She watched him with that expression staff would develop when they had something important to say but recognised that their boss was currently being managed by someone official.

Rein noticed the direction of his attention.

“I’ll be looking over the road, you can find me there.” Rein said politely, he nodded to Mara before walking off with the two guards. He did not leave Alric alone exactly. The men remained close enough in the broad sense, and the yard was full of workers. But the captain gave him a small space, which Alric appreciated more than he expected.

He crossed to Mara. “Mara.” Alric said with a small nod.

“Alric.” Mara said with a forced smile, she had a small cough.

“You all right?” he asked, noticing the smile that didn't reach her eyes.

“Yes.” She paused and put on the fake smile again. “Mostly.”

That was not yes. Alric waited. The wind moved through the yard, catching at the edge of her shawl. She pulled it tighter.

“We need to have a meeting about winter,” she said, glancing away from the wind, she brushed a loose strand of hair out of her face.

Alric glanced at her shawl, wrapped more tightly. Alric had expected a meeting soon as they would need to switch to lager sooner rather than later. Alric drew in a breath. “We should gather Hesta, Hal, Lessa, Seren, Vale and yourself of course. Before the first proper frost. Tomorrow afternoon at the inn?”

“That would be best.” Mara nodded at him, again with the small smile.

He nodded. “Done.” He said hoping to reassure her but he didn't notice a change.

That should have been the end of it. Mara did not leave. Alric became aware of the yard again. The sound of casks. The captain somewhere behind the buildings asking a worker a question. A cart wheel striking the bad patch in the road and giving the familiar wooden complaint. Steam leaving the brewery in a slow white breath.

Mara looked down at her hands. “There is something else,” she said, while avoiding eye contact.

Alric’s attention sharpened, he listened carefully. “All right?”

“I would like a private meeting.” She said not looking at him again.

“With me?” Alric asked bewildered, he felt his mind spinning up.

“With you and Seren.” she clarified. Her head was still down, turned away from the wind.

His mind reeled at various implications of what it could be that needed both him and Seren. Alric kept his face as still as he could. He was not sure he succeeded. Mara had never requested a private meeting. Mara brought problems with solutions attached. She did not ask for quiet rooms and Seren. “Is something wrong?” he asked carefully.

Mara’s fingers tightened around each other. “No. Not exactly.” She looked up at him finally. “It can wait until evening.”

“That is not the most reassuring way to say that.” Alric said, he moved as if to put a hand on her shoulder then thought better of it.

“I know.” She said with a small nod, she turned away again.

“Mara.” Alric probed, although he felt a small twinge of guilt at trying to push for an answer.

She smiled again, but it did not settle properly. “It can wait,” she repeated. “This evening would be better. Just the three of us.”

Alric studied her. She was not frightened. Not in the way people looked when something had gone badly wrong. She was unsettled, though. Fidgety in a way that Alric could not recognise. Her eyes moved once toward the brewery, then toward the road, then back to him.

“Tonight then,” he said. “Come for supper at the inn?”

She hesitated. That was also not good. “My office rather?” he asked, one brow lifted.

Mara nodded, the fake smile returning. “That would be better.”

Alric did not ask why. He wanted to. The question pressed at his teeth but she had been careful to chose privacy, forcing the matter early often got a worse answer or no useful answer at all.

“All right,” he said, nodding slowly with a worried expression. “My office then. After supper.”
