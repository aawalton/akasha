As Samantha was reviewing her items, she blinked. She had completely forgotten about an item in her possession, such was the craze of things since Caneboro. She pulled out a plaque made of thin marble, framed in what must’ve been petrified cane. It was beautiful.

Old City Memories Memorial Plaque

“This city has been destroyed and built on top of uncountable times, for the wants and needs of mortals know no quit, and the tide cannot be denied. It washes ashore again and again, crashing and bringing tales of wonders afar. We will hear them, we will seek them, and we will ever build toward them.

Whatever name these streets bear, the identity remains as blood and soul soaking below. These elements cannot die. Whosoever steps foot here, step lightly! Whosoever thinks to rule here, beware! Handle these, the ‘uncouth’; handle these, the ‘villeins,’ with fairness and care.

Independence will persist here, or nothing. Defiance will make a grave again, and the foot of the tyrant will be marred to step upon us. Many such have thought us destroyed, but it is we who’ve outlasted.

You have been warned.”

When placed in the proper city location, a citizen of the Old City may touch this plaque and form a link. Whenever they summon memories of the ‘place before,’ it will bestow them with intangible strength, energy, and hope, usable when truly needed.

In addition, the names and faces of any slain loved ones (during the destruction event of the last incarnation) may be recorded into the plaque, and seen publicly by anyone again, with a touch.

Samantha was stunned and struck silent to read it. Her mind went rather hollow, as her body went through the motions of shifting to the Bridge of Edges and heading straight for the underground location in Caneboro.

She first had to be amazed by what she saw on the other side. It had been thoroughly cleaned up and restored to what looked like a well put-together audience chamber. No one was there, of course. She made her way through orderly halls underground, eventually to the above-ground city. Now called, officially and ridiculously, ‘Catboro.’ Good luck getting the residents to call it that, though.

There was already something of a memorial she’d had built, looking something like a church, but containing various preserved or recreated artifacts of the city that the Dominion’s onslaught had so quickly demolished. Half-demolished, anyway.

It was early morning, and no one was in there. She went in disguised in the typical attire of a traveler, not wanting any scenes in case she was recognized. It felt inappropriate to be. With that, Samantha set about rearranging the facility's central interior so she could make the plaque the new center, atop a prominent dais and well-lit by sunlight from above. At first, she made the stand that the plaque would sit on out of gold, but she abandoned that quickly. Caneboroans would probably steal it. Or think it was fake?

With some amusement, she made the stand out of reinforced cane. If someone really wanted the damn thing, they’d have it, but she didn’t think that would happen.

Once it was cemented in place, she touched it.

Nothing, of course. Just a hand touching a plaque. She was not a citizen. That meant something special to Old City, and she was not it.

Nor does an abandoner like me deserve to be. I failed this place.

It struck her that this city and this memorial were nothing new. She had other names come to her and realized they were clones of it in her other lives. To Rallindin it was Farenal, to Innogen, Norwost. The names hardly mattered: it was Old City. It was never new, and it always was, across many shadows of time and space. It was a place of suffering and the ever-persistent darkness of humanity, little changed even here, in the world she’d come to so late. This was the border city on the edge of entropy — the edge of despair. She hadn’t merely failed them in one world. She’d failed them countless times, again and again, like an unhealable wound. Like the simple balance of reality. If there lived a monster, there was always someone living next to it, defining the border. Becoming prey.

Tears came to her eyes, and she collapsed on the lovely, marble dais steps she’d just manufactured by her own power. She wept, as she had told herself she would stop doing for these sorts of things. Pathetic. She mourned for the city and the people, and felt awful for all that she’d wrought yet again.

She felt like all the pressures collapsing continually down on her would tear her apart, regardless of how many times she told herself she had to keep it together. She could’ve simply Invoked her classes again, sobered up, and gone about her business.

But at the same time, she couldn’t.

Deep at the core of her, she was just the girl, even now — arguably, the whole point of all that was done was that she was just a girl. She was ‘Sammy,’ even as she’d abandoned the cutesy moniker by the necessity of being a ‘respectable’ deity. Adulthood. She’d arrived in Calrenazzod a thousand times more like a girl, no doubt. A child, really.

The time lived since, however short on the surface, was much, much longer. With every passing day, she lived more and more actual minutes for every real minute. Since acquiring the Fate Domain, more so. Since acquiring [Multitask Magician], more so. Since the amplification of levels, more so. [Light Speed]? Again. Compressed time, years and years, from her past lives, particularly Ozzy and Rallindin, injected into her, separate yet merged, invariably still her lived experience. On and on these things went; her brain was flying like some monstrous AI, in multiple processes.

What was Jeeves, even? An extension of her. A daemon composed of her power and energy.

What was everyone? An extension of them. It seemed likely that the entirety of Earth was drawn from her. A vast, disparate amalgamate that also equaled One. That one was her. Not ‘chosen,’ not ‘special,’ in the sense of being picked out of the crowd. No. Always the keyholder. The source. Like the womb, perhaps — like ‘woman’ itself, and thereby ‘mother,’ and thereby, ‘lifegiver,’ and thereby ‘goddess.’ What man or woman escaped it? All came from it, and that spiritual reality was inseparable, even after the surgeon’s snips cut the umbilical.

From her, life came, whatever form it took after. And the coin came to her because it had to. It meant nothing to anyone else. It was likely just a coin to all but who planted the seed of its transformation in the first place.

But even so, even with this glaring truth, the idiotic thought arose, as if she, held small-minded in the paralysis of grief, were unable to think in any other way.

Why me?!

There was no answer, of course. Because it was dumb. Irrelevant. Like asking ‘Why is the world flat?’ It was the wrong question. It belonged in the dumpster of history along with such questions as ‘Which of the four humors caused this illness?’

Some part of her wanted to come to a conclusion, there in the grief of her failures and sins. The thousands of the dead at her feet. The sea of corpses her arrival had wrought. It was the correct conclusion to sober up with something pithy and motivating. She’d done it before — not even all that long ago — and she’d probably do it again.

But on the dais, weeping, hugging her twisting gut, she rejected that. She didn’t want it. She deserved to feel miserable, just like the rotten, marred, forever broken city under her boots. She held to it, suffered with it, and under its oppression, feeling its judgment heavy on her shoulders. She absorbed that most terrifying of weapons: blame.

“Still fresh, isn’t it?” came a woman’s voice.

Samantha nearly flew off like superwoman as she jumped and spun around awkwardly, just stopping herself from doing any obvious supernatural shit. It almost made her chin eat marble, but thankfully, her reflexes were too acute for that, now. She caught herself, if a bit wobbly for it.

Through blurry eyes, she saw an old woman in the conservative garb a typical Caneboroan matron would wear to a respectable place. A light but covering dress. A thin summer shawl. Attractive patterns, a bit of color, but nothing too extreme.

She had a sympathetic look on her face, but also one that had just come out of awe. She probably was impressed by the hall.

Wiping her eyes, sitting back down, swallowing, Samantha nodded and finally managed to say, “Yeah…I was late…” She affected a mild Southlands accent, since she was supposed to be a common traveler.

The matron formed a mild smile and walked around the circumference of the dais' bottom, eyes cast around once more in wonder. “A lovely place, though.”

“It oughta be,” Samantha replied. “Small apology as it is, for what happened.”

“Oh? You reckon it’s the guilty what made it, then?”

“I do. Who else?”

The old woman looked around with squinted eyes and a considering expression. “Must be. But then, we’d have to say the whole city was remade by the guilty, ay? It’s all done up, after all. Like magic. I suppose not ‘like,’ at that.”

“I suppose. Some of it was the cats.”

“Aye.” The matron nodded, and a silence persisted.

Trying to collect herself, Samantha wiped her tears and snot with a handkerchief. She caught the woman’s eye and gestured up at the nearby plaque. “This is something special. For natives…”

“Oh?” Curious, the woman ascended the dais and inspected it, soon looking quite affected as she read the physical text. “Oh my… that’s passing strange…” After another few moments, she added, “Familiar, too.”

“In the past lives kind of way, you mean?” Samantha asked, legitimately curious.

The matron nodded absentmindedly, blinking as she finished reading. “At least, I think so…” Her hand reached out to touch the plaque, and then gasped, her hand jerking back as she stumbled a bit.

Samantha shot up like a lightning bolt to steady the woman and make sure she didn’t fall. “Are you okay?!”

The woman looked stunned. She looked down at Samantha’s hand, then at her face. “My word! Y-you were just sitting down, lass! Can you teleport?!”

Samantha gently let her go and smiled sheepishly. “Oh, it’s nothing, you were just out of it for a moment, so didn’t see me rise…”

Collecting herself, the woman nodded reluctantly, then her eyes went back to the plaque. “People should know about this. How do they not? How haven’t I heard?”

“It’s new. I mean, I think? Apparently. I’d suggest telling others.”

The woman gave a subtle indication of agreement. “Who did you lose, dear? Tell me about them.”

“I-... it doesn’t matter.”

“Of course it does, lass! You know how the plaque works, hmm?” At Samantha’s curt nod, she continued, “I see you’re a traveler. Not a ‘citizen’ by this confounding thing, I bet. Well, tell me who you know. If they’re not a loved one to me, you can make me one. We’ll get their name and face in here in a jiffy, I do swear it. You’ll know they’ll be remembered before you leave here.”

Samantha stared incredulously at her. She thinks this is why I was crying. Tears began to well in her eyes anew. “You’d do this for a stranger?”

The woman half-scoffed, half-chuckled. “Oh, come now! We already met. Never met a lass with such a soft heart that I didn’t get along with, besides. I’m sure you and yours are neighbor enough. I’m Kira, by the way. You?”

Samantha finally broke her stare with a swallow, as tears rolled down her cheeks. “I-... P-perhaps another time.” Suddenly feeling a pressure on her, like a ‘phony’ who didn’t belong in that place, she awkwardly turned away and walked off down the steps, stumbling slightly but not slowing down by any means.

“Miss?!” the old woman called after her. “Miss, it’s alright, really!”

As soon as she was out of sight around a corner, Samantha went invisible and rocketed out of the place via flight. She didn’t look back and did not plan on ever returning. It wasn’t her place.

It just wasn’t.

Not anymore.
