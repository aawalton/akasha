[Time]: Autumn Semester, Day 56 — 8:20 AM[Location]: Yggdrasil Academy · Dormitory [Golden Bough] · Room 302

Forty minutes before the Sunday shift, Hathaway unlocked her terminal and opened the group thread, which was the only crime scene she had ever visited voluntarily.

Five members. Created the evening after the strategy session in the empty classroom, with a charter short enough to fit in the description field: open-source the misery. Whatever anyone shook loose about Emilia Lucas's final went into the channel, and the channel had been, for a while, the loudest object in her life.

Then the updates began to blur. Still compiling. Running into a routing error. Direction might be wrong.

Then Victoria posted a spatial-dynamic sketch for a gemstone housing, which had cost her something to give away, and nobody replied to it. Hathaway had read it twice.

Then nothing.

Nobody had typed I quit, and nobody had picked a fight, and there had been no moment anyone could point at afterward and call the end. There was one commission, and none of the five of them had ever once, in any context, been willing to come second.

Hathaway typed a greeting three times that morning and deleted it three times. She had something to put in there. She knew with the flat certainty of a systems architect sitting on proprietary code that she was not going to send it.

She scrolled up instead, past the dying week, past the duplicate catalogue entries, past a joke about Surtrina's relatives that had been funnier at the time, all the way back to the beginning. Everything Yenna had ever typed was cross-references.

She likes sunflowers.

Hathaway set the terminal face-down on the desk and pulled the dragon-leather tome across the wood. She had the page memorized and opened to it anyway.

She opened the drawer, took out the enchanted coin, and fed a thread of mana into the black metal.

The connection engaged.

"Hello?"

Hathaway's hand stopped moving.

It was the Fifth Seat's cadence exactly, every consonant landing where Irene put hers, and it was coming out of a different instrument. Warm. Unhurried. Every hard edge sanded off, and a charm so effortless it registered somewhere below the level of hearing.

Not her. Her pattern matcher ran the sample twice and returned the same contradiction both times, and she had no category to file it under, so she gave her name and laid the business out cleanly instead: [Ovelia's Evil Lantern Cat Sunflare Burst], a structural impasse, a request for consultation.

"My schedule is open," she added. "Whenever it suits you."

The line went quiet. Somewhere well back from the receiver, porcelain touched wood.

When a voice came back it was a different one, the warmth traded out for something serene and sovereign, and this one she knew.

"Saturday," Irene Habsburg said. "Two-ten in the afternoon. Come to my estate."

Hathaway's spine came off the chair.

That was the block: the single stretch of the week that had survived a Mon-to-Sat teaching load and a Sunday laboratory shift.

Deep in her sensory array a threat monitor came up amber and began composing a report.

Hathaway pressed it closed without reading it.

"Understood," she said, and her voice came out a half-step tighter than she had authorized. She got a grip on the edge of the desk. "About the consultation fee. How should I handle the invoice?"

"We will discuss it later."

Her accountant thread began screaming into a stairwell. Undefined technical debt, no ceiling, no maturity date, held by a Grand Witch. It was the single most ruinously expensive instrument on the server, and she had just accepted one over breakfast.

She hesitated with her finger over the disconnect, and found nothing intelligent anywhere in her.

"Thank you, Irene."

A soft laugh carried through the coin. "You are entirely welcome. And Hathaway. The formatting on your new paper is exceptionally elegant."

The connection clicked shut.

Hathaway sat looking at the coin in her palm for two full seconds.

Sophistry had not gone to print. The proofs were sitting on a desk in a building she had never been inside.

She put the coin back in the drawer and made the executive decision to be a person who had not noticed that.

[Time]: Autumn Semester, Day 62 — 1:50 PM[Location]: White City Center · Snowsheen Lake Sky-Island

Snowsheen Lake was not zoned for anything.

It hung dead centre in the metropolitan airspace, a mile of cascading water and old alpine forest suspended over the White City, developed a century ago as a dynasty enclave for families whose names came with pronunciation guides. Nobody on the board had ever modelled the scenario in which a single witch decided she wanted a base camp within walking distance of the shops, outbid a syndicate of property barons without visible effort, and converted the entire landmass into a residence. The trams still stopped at the perimeter. So did the long-haul coaches.

Hathaway came down out of the stratus layer on matte-black wings and landed at the visitor's gate ten minutes early.

An attendant received her with silent, immaculate courtesy and walked her through an archway of frosted quartz into a waiting room.

It was a very nice waiting room.

The low tables were cut from old-growth spirit cedar. The carpet took her boots to the ankle in dense Kirin fleece. The light came from deep-sea lumens suspended in floor-to-ceiling water columns, which meant it arrived from below and never once caught her in the eye, and the far wall held a polished bookshelf stocked end to end with illustrated storybooks, domestic and foreign, the ones children actually ask for.

Hathaway sank into an oversized sofa with a gold-rimmed porcelain cup in both hands and tipped her head back to audit the vaulting, because it was genuinely good vaulting.

Two-oh-seven.

Click.

The door swung inward and a Silver Knight came through it in a dragon-horned helm and a cloak of liquid silver, posture a full course in martial discipline, delivering her line in an accent you could have hung a portrait on.

"Miss. I trust the refreshments have been to your liking. Hm?"

The knight stopped dead.

The three-tier dessert tower on the table had been, twenty minutes earlier, a structural achievement. It was now porcelain. Beside it stood a five-litre dispenser framed in rare mineral and set stone, the transparent barrel of it engineered to display a slow-brewed aromatic tea to its best advantage, and it was dry to the seal.

The knight's eyebrows went up. Her extremely sweet face performed something complicated.

"...You ate all of it?"

Hathaway felt a clean line of guilt come up under her collarbone, smothered it, and produced a smile of unbothered professional courtesy.

"It was very delicious."

And what, exactly, was the alternative? Her defence counsel was already on its feet. You spawn premium assets on a table, you leave a girl alone with them for seventeen minutes and zero available inputs, and then you act surprised when the assets are processed.

The knight's eyes moved from the bare plates to Hathaway's entirely serene face, and back.

"Lady Irene is ready to receive you," she said, quickly.

Hathaway stood, drained the last swallow, set the cup on the tray, and inclined her head. "Thank you. Please lead the way."

The knight turned for the corridor, made it halfway through the pivot, looked back at the five-litre dispenser, and could not do it.

"I believe our head chef will be deeply appreciative of your... thorough appreciation."

They were, without qualification, the finest butter cookies, fruit brûlée, six regional matcha macarons, egg tarts, parfait cups, black-and-white chocolate nibs, and shortbread with ice cream that I have processed across two entire lives, Hathaway's inner critic reported, filing with the department that handled her only unimpeachable opinions.

"Please pass her my compliments," she said. "All of them."

The Silver Knight's eyes narrowed to slits and her cheeks came out, threatening to convert a career of martial bearing into a steamed bun.

A long second went by.

"...Understood," she managed, pasting on a strained and gallant smile. "I am certain she will be thrilled. Follow me."

Hathaway fell in behind the silver cloak with her eyes forward and her hands to herself, declining to appraise a single tapestry in a corridor that was clearly worth more than her liquid holdings. A short walk, a set of double doors, a small bow, and she stepped through into the drawing room.

Irene Habsburg was sitting by the window.

She was wearing a full open-front Lolita gown layered over a high-collared blouse, gold-stamped patterned stockings, an extravagantly ruffled bonnet, and a complete matched suite of jewellery, and the aesthetic density of it arrived like weather.

Hathaway navigated to an enormous sofa and lowered herself onto it with care.

The upholstery took her weight with a deep buoyant give, soft past any reasonable definition of soft, and then held her spine like bridge cable. It was [Goldfloss]: literal gold, woven through a process one woman on the continent understood, indifferent to centuries. Throw it in a furnace and it came out as perfectly ordinary bullion.

One-way conversion, her developer brain noted, running a hand along the nap. No reverse recipe.

She left the thought where it was and sat up straighter.

Irene smiled, radiant and entirely without mercy. "So. How does such a formal informal visit feel?"

"The desserts were spectacular," Hathaway said, reaching for diplomacy and finding it right where she had left it. "Though I have to admit I'm not fond of the sensation of sitting in a waiting room. You know."

"Alienation. Distance." Irene's laugh was light and offhand. "The two finest methods available for ending a friendship."

The sentence hung in the sunlight for a moment.

Then Hathaway felt the heat arrive in her ears, shifted on the Goldfloss, and made her shoulders come down.

Irene began brewing tea. She warmed the pot and emptied it and warmed it again, let the dry leaf sit a while in the heat, poured from a height that served nothing at all. Hathaway watched her do it and forgot, for a while, what she had come for.

A cup came across. She took it in both hands and drank.

She had flown here for a code review. Sitting in the sunlit room with the steam coming off the cup, her threads began to drift.

A woman who had taken a Saturday afternoon out of a life measured in continental economics to pour tea for a second-year.

In the curl of the scented steam, a name got out ahead of her filter.

"...Evangeline."

Irene's hands did not stop. One eyebrow rose. "Hm?"

The eyebrow pulled Hathaway's eyes to it, and they settled a little lower, on the beauty mark beneath the golden eye.

Cute.

She got rid of it at speed and covered the gap by talking.

"Does she break in and steal your desserts often?"

Genuine, delighted amusement surfaced in Irene's face.

"No. That was the only occasion." She set the kettle back on its stand. "The Ghost Witches, on the other hand, are frequent uninvited guests. I am given to understand that my pastry cabinet holds a firm position in the top three of their internal guide."

Objectively correct, Hathaway's food critic ruled. Verifiable. Reproducible.

Then the rest of it landed.

She was sitting on the victim's sofa, having personally cleared the victim's dessert tower, discussing a burglary ring whose product she had eaten.

She was, by any reasonable standard, a repeat offender, and her most recent offence had concluded eleven minutes ago.

Hathaway drank her tea and made a serious professional commitment to finding out who held the top two spots, from Spectra, later, in a room with no witnesses.

"You know," she said instead, "listening to the two of you talk that day was genuinely terrifying."

The gleam in Irene's eyes sharpened.

"Were you not curious why Evangeline never refuted a word of it?" She took a slow sip. "If she had simply told the truth, right there, the entire aesthetic of the great fraudster would have collapsed on the spot."

Hathaway laughed out loud before she could stop it, and the last of the formality went out of the room with it.

"I heard the Fourth Seat also knows this spell, and my aunt says she learned it from us." She shrugged, and her curiosity finally came off its leash. "So why is Marianne's version different from the original? I mean, she's her."

Irene set the teapot down.

"Marianne can reconstruct anything she has ever seen." The smile that came with it was small and went a long way back. "What she has never once been able to reconstruct is a thing that holds the right to decide whether it wants to arrive."

Hathaway's heart missed a stroke, and she was forward over the table before she chose to be.

"Then I need a fallback path," she said. "If she doesn't come, the matrix degrades gracefully to the procedural build and I still walk away with a yield."

Irene shook her head.

"If you design the architecture from the first node in the certainty that she will not come, and you build your contingencies around that absence." Her voice came down almost to nothing. "Why would she ever arrive? It is an invitation, Hathaway. It is not a summons."

The word sat in the room like a struck fork.

Hathaway did not trust her voice with anything. She reached into her ring, drew out the working notebook with three months of revisions crammed into it, and slid it across the Goldfloss.

Irene took it, laid it flat on her knee, and read the way a woman reads her own handwriting.

"Your containment shell." A polished fingernail traced the outer boundary. "Absorb, disperse, vent outward. This is the industry standard for a blast shield. You catch the load and you throw it as far from yourself as the geometry permits."

"That's the whole design," Hathaway said.

"You have built the correct half and you did not know what it was for." Irene turned the page. "You are treating the heat as exhaust. Heat is the sun you are asking her to remember. When you vent it, you tear up the contents of the invitation and post her the envelope."

She tapped the parchment twice.

"Every joule the envelope takes has to be routed back to the centre and held there. That hundred-and-fifteen-metre boundary is not a wall keeping the detonation in."

She looked up.

"It is a door closed to keep the warmth in."

Hathaway's quill was already moving, her developer brain tearing the entire backend out and reinstalling it facing the other way.

"And the hundred and fifteen metres," Irene added. "You have been reading it as an input. It is a result. Marianne specifies it and hits it perfectly, and mine is measured afterward, and both of us read a hundred and fifteen."

"Then what do I set."

"Nothing. That is the last condition, and it is the one that will cost you." Irene's register changed, into the flat clinical caution of an instructor naming the failure that actually kills people. "Once the matrix closes, you do not add mana. Not a single M-Unit."

Hathaway's quill stopped.

"For how long?"

"That is not yours to know, and you will not get one." The golden eyes held steady. "You maintain the routing and you add nothing, and every instinct in a body built like yours is going to scream at you to push."

She let that sit exactly as long as it needed to.

"Pushing is a summons."

Hathaway wrote it down twice.

"Oh," Irene said, leaning back into the Goldfloss with the tone of a woman recalling where she had left her keys, "and a node sequence on the third page of the original is out of order. Ovelia never bothered to clarify it."

Hathaway's quill snapped to a halt. Her mouth twitched.

"Are you telling me my grandmother blew herself into a fine mist over a typo?"

Irene covered her mouth with one lace-gloved hand, and her eyes creased with quiet, private delight.

"You are welcome to go down and ask her yourself, one day."

Whatever pressure was left in Hathaway's chest went out and did not come back, and the reckless confidence that rushed in to fill the vacancy was, in hindsight, a known hazard of this room.

"If I die on your property," she said, chin on her hand, "do I get a discount on the toll?"

Irene blinked once.

"No."

Hathaway opened her mouth to litigate the pricing of a monopoly.

"I will wait," Irene said gently, "until you have thrown yourself into the River Styx in righteous fury and stayed long enough to hear the end of the gossip. Then I will fish you out personally, and announce that your bill has been waived."

Hathaway shut her mouth.

She lifted off the visitor's gate at a quarter past four with the notebook sealed in her ring and the ledger open in her head.

One consultation, uninvoiced. One death toll waived in advance, as a joke, by a woman who did not make jokes about money. Three tiers of pastry, five litres of tea, and a boxed replacement of both that the Silver Knight had put into her hands at the gate and then declined, with enormous dignity, to discuss.

Total outstanding: unknown. Terms: payable whenever she feels like naming them. Her accountant thread ran the exposure, held the file over the liabilities column, and then did something it had not done once across two entire lives.

Approved.

Below her the White City came up gold through the afternoon haze, and she flew the whole way back one-handed, because the box was warm, and she was not putting it in the ring.
