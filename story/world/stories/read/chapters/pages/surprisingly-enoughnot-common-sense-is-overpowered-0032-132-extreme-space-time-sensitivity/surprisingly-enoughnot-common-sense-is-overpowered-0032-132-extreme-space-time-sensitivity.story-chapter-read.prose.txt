It was late at night, when yin spiritual energy was at its strongest and yang had begun to recede.

Perfect for what Ning needed.

He stood alone in the clearing behind his hut, the night air cold against his skin, and slowly exhaled a long, steady breath before flowing into motion.

After some experimentation, Ning had discovered that the Jade Skin and Icy Meridian Technique was most effective around this hour. He already knew that certain cultivation techniques benefited from being practiced under specific conditions, and for this particular body-refining art, the dead of night provided exactly the kind of dense yin energy it needed.

It made perfect sense.

Didn't make it any less miserable, though.

“Is this how shounen protagonists feel during those ice-bath training arcs? Truly, they had balls of steel,” Ning muttered as another chilling breeze swept across the clearing.

The Jade Skin and Icy Meridian Technique was divided into five levels:

Level One: Cold Adaptation.Level Two: Skin Refinement.Level Three: Marrow Strengthening.Level Four: Bone Tempering.Level Five: Internal Organ Fortification.

The first level laid the foundation for everything that followed. Before he could circulate increasingly powerful cold qi, strengthen his meridians, or refine his body against frost, he first had to teach his body to accept the cold rather than instinctively fight against it.

Of course, it was easier said than done.

Compared to qi refinement, body refinement was far more painful. Much of the process involved placing the body under controlled stress, allowing it to recover, then repeating the process until flesh, bone, and organs gradually surpassed their natural limits. In ancient times, body cultivation had flourished partly because spiritual materials were more abundant, and partly because apparently there had been no shortage of cultivators willing to put themselves through absolute hell for greater strength.

It was a rather masochistic path, but then again, this was the cultivation world. People trained beneath waterfalls, meditated inside volcanoes, shattered boulders with their foreheads, and did all manner of nonsense in pursuit of strength.

Compared to them, Ning's training was practically civilized.

He sank into the first stance, the Frost-Settling Frame, extending his arms while regulating his breathing according to the method recorded in the scroll.

Inhale for three counts.

Hold.

Exhale for six.

From there, he transitioned into the Jade Drip Shift, rotating his torso while his fingers traced the prescribed paths through the air. There were thirty-six movements in total, each paired with a precise breathing pattern and designed to guide the chill progressively deeper into his body.

At first, his movements were stiff and his breathing uneven as the cold worked its way through his muscles and joints until even his bones seemed to ache, yet Ning gritted his teeth and continued, slowly circulating the rudimentary cold qi through his meridians exactly as instructed.

The principle behind the first level was simple enough.

Guide the cold. Do not resist it.

Fight against the cold qi, and the strain could damage his meridians. Accept and guide it properly, and that same pressure would gradually strengthen and expand them.

Basically, hydraulic stress-testing, except the pipes were his qi channels and replacing one after it burst wasn't exactly an option.

Ning's breath emerged as a steady stream of white mist while he continued through the thirty-six movements, allowing the chill to enter with every inhale before guiding it through his body and releasing the excess with every exhale.

Again and again, he repeated the cycle until his muscles trembled and his skin had gone numb.

Normally, Ning would have questioned the wisdom of deliberately freezing himself in the middle of the night.

Fortunately, numbers were very persuasive.

[Jade Skin & Icy Meridians (Level One): 32/100]

Ning stared at the increase for a moment before nodding.

"No wonder people say suffering builds character and strength."

With his nightly dose of voluntary misery completed, Ning made his way toward the spiritual field, where rows of Frozen Breath Plants had already entered their budding stage.

[Hidden Ice Mist Technique]

Cold qi gathered within him before leaving his mouth as a stream of pale, icy mist that rolled across the field. Ning had acquired the spell through the Spiritual Plant Hall, and while it hadn't been free, the price had essentially been cut in half, leaving him seventy-six spirit stones poorer.

He had admittedly been a little disappointed about not getting a freebie, but hey, half price was still half price. Ning would take what he could get.

As the mist spread over the Frozen Breath Plants, Ning noticed once again that the spell felt noticeably stronger after his body-refining practice, carrying a deeper chill while consuming slightly less effort to maintain. It was another useful discovery from his experimentation: the cold qi cultivated through Jade Skin and Icy Meridians was naturally compatible with Hidden Ice Mist.

“Just one month left,” Ning murmured as he inspected the budding plants.

Once the Frozen Breath Grain matured, he would finally have a steady supply of cultivation food suited specifically to his body-refining technique. If everything worked as expected, he should be able to speedrun at least part of the early stages.

After giving the field one final sweep of icy mist, Ning returned to his hut.

Training a yin-aligned body-refining technique wasn't as simple as stuffing as much cold qi into himself as possible. Too much accumulated cold could damage his muscles, hinder the circulation of qi, or, in the worst case, injure his meridians from within, which was why Ning had prepared something specifically to restore the balance afterward.

He lifted the lid from a clay pot simmering over a low flame, and a rich, savory warmth immediately filled the hut.

Inside was a thick bone broth made with small cuts of Fire Chicken, a low-grade yang-attribute spiritual beast whose meat naturally carried a gentle warming qi. Ning filled a bowl, raised it to his lips, and took a long drink.

Warmth flowed down his throat and spread through his chest, gradually melting away the lingering chill buried within his muscles and meridians until his entire body seemed to loosen at once.

Ning closed his eyes.

This was almost on the same level as drinking water at three in the morning.

He took another mouthful, savoring the warmth as it spread through him.

So good.

If this were a food show, his shirt probably would have exploded by now. Truly, this was a healing dish.

Cold training during the most yin hour, followed by warm nourishment rich in yang qi. Rather than allowing either extreme to accumulate, he used one to temper his body and the other to restore its balance afterward.

Ning looked down at the steaming bowl with newfound appreciation.

Who knew the profound Dao of yin and yang would eventually lead me to chicken soup?

Archery.

The execution seemed simple; it really was just shooting a pointed stick from far away.

But that was far from the truth.

Precision, breath control, balance, awareness, timing, and a calm mind that didn’t waver even in intense situations were all essential.

Fortunately, “Ji Ning” came from a martial family where archery was drilled alongside basic forms. After all, archery was one of the go-to methods for hunting, an indispensable skill for any martial practitioner. Especially since mortal martial artists were incapable of using qi.

So, although he wasn’t a master, “Ji Ning” knew the fundamentals. For example, precautions like never storing a bow with its string attached were basic knowledge.

Now that Ning had inherited that foundation, it felt less like learning something new and more like rediscovering muscles that had simply been asleep.

As a cultivator, his control over his body surpassed any mundane archer. After all his body-refining training, his senses were sharper, his muscles steadier, and his meridians more responsive.

And contrary to what many assumed, a bow wasn’t just a dexterity-based weapon; it required considerable strength. Fortunately, this was a cultivation world, where tearing apart continents bare-handed was something high-level experts casually did. Ning was nowhere near that level, of course, but his body-refining was still far from mere decoration.

He stepped into the clearing behind his hut, bow in hand. His fingers curled around the bowstring and drew it back in a smooth, unbroken line. His muscles tightened.

Thrum.

The arrow shot forth, slicing through the air and embedding itself in a tree trunk with a crisp thwack.

Ning lowered the bow slightly, feeling the faint vibration humming along the wood.

“Mm. Not bad,” he murmured.

It wasn’t exactly what he had aimed at, but the direction and form were correct. A cultivator’s body adapted quickly; their muscle memory was frighteningly strong.

After all, simply knowing how to handle the weapon was the foundation of martial arts. All the fancy qi enhancements like qi light differentiation, qi tracing, qi piercing, came only after that.

He nocked another arrow. This time, the target was closer.

The improvement was steep, and the reason was simple:

Spatial awareness.

Ning had a faint, innate intuition for space and distance.

And suddenly, Ning understood why.

Extreme Space-Time Sensitivity.

That was the talent shown on the panel. Until now, it hadn’t seemed useful.

But after reaching the fourth stage of Qi Condensation, Ning had begun sensing faint spatial shifts. When someone approached, even quietly, he would have a vague awareness of it.

That was his spatial sensitivity.

Even from the name alone, Ning could tell the talent was anything but ordinary. Space was one of the most profound and difficult forces for cultivators to comprehend, and those who possessed an affinity for it were exceedingly rare.

There was a saying in this world, after all:

Space and time are king, while destiny reigns supreme.

Ning had once assumed the talent came from his transmigration. But now that supernatural powers were in play, he wasn’t so sure. Even though the catalog claimed that twin supernatural powers were unheard of, Ning wasn’t bound by rigid thinking. In a world as vast and strange as this, rules were more like guidelines.

So Ning was genuinely curious about the origin of this talent. It was so dormant that if not for Know Thyself, he would’ve never realized he possessed it.

He continued shooting without pause. Another arrow flew.

It hit the branch he aimed at, snapping it cleanly.

Simply put, in DnD terms, when Ning rolled for awareness, he was hitting 10+ by default.

It was clearly some kind of prototype “mind’s eye” ability. As his senses grew stronger, the talent would presumably grow as well. In the end, he might even be able to sense the disturbance in the Force.

“Still… time sensitivity feels even vaguer,” Ning muttered. His talent’s full name was Extreme Space-Time Sensitivity.

He had a faint perception of space, but time? Almost nothing. Still, judging by how the spatial side awakened, the temporal side would likely follow eventually.

Maybe he’d end up being able to tell crop maturation times down to the hour.

'Anyway, it seems choosing the bow was a good choice.' Ning smiled.

His reasoning was simple.

Using a bow and arrow was kind, or rather, forgiving.

Unlike swords or spears, where one mistake meant immediate death or dismemberment, arrows fired from a distance offered room for error. He could retreat, reposition, and adapt.

Of course, that safety came with a cost. Archers needed a secondary weapon for close combat. Ning didn’t mind. If anything, he enjoyed the learning process.

There were practical issues too: low-tier bows and arrows had limited effectiveness, and he would eventually need better equipment. But this was true for nearly every weapon. Unless one wielded a growth-type weapon, first-tier gear only worked reliably on first-tier foes.

As for truly unorthodox weapons, chains, darts, strange whips, Ning avoided them for one reason:

Inheritance.

Bows and arrows weren’t mainstream, but they weren’t obscure either. Generations of predecessors had created techniques, manuals, and paths he could follow.

Compared to starting from scratch, that alone made the choice worthwhile.

'It seems the future is promising.' Ning smiled.
