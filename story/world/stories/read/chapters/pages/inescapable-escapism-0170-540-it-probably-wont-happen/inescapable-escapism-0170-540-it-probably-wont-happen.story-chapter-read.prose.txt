I could barely breathe as I followed the vet along the corridor. My heart was thudding in my ears, drowning out all other sound, and I could feel my hands shaking. I clenched them into fists, trying to stop the trembling, but it didn’t work.

What was she going to say? That was the question that kept bouncing through my head. Was it bad news? She had smiled at us when she came out to bring us into the room, but was that a smile of sympathy? Perhaps she always wore that expression before telling people the thing they were dreading.

“Just in this room at the end,” the vet said with another inscrutable smile as she gestured towards a door in the distance.

Why was the veterinary practice so big? There were so many examination rooms, and surely, they couldn’t need them all. It wasn’t a large town, and hardly anyone had come in during the entire time we were in the waiting room, so it made no sense to me.

I should have asked to go with him, I told myself, my thoughts jumping around. When the vet came out to collect the dog, I should have asked to go in with him for the exam. The vet had expected us to, but Mom had said we’d wait out by reception, and as soon as I let go of the dog, I regretted it. He kept looking back at me as the vet led him away from us, and that made me feel awful.

“Take a seat, please,” the vet said as we reached the room, and she held the door open for Mom and me.

The dog wasn’t there. I expected us to be brought to an examination room where the dog would be waiting, but the room we’d been shown to was more of an office, and the dog was nowhere to be seen.

That couldn’t be a good sign, a small voice in the back of my head whispered. Maybe the vet had found his microchip and scanned it. She could have called his owner, and they’d already come in to collect him. Perhaps he was on his way back home as I stood there, too scared to walk any further into the room, where Mom and the vet were already seated.

We would have seen them, though, wouldn’t we? Mom and I were in the waiting room the whole time, and if someone had come in to claim him, they would have had to walk right past us. There was no way that I would have missed the dog leaving the vets, and I knew that.

So maybe something else had happened. Perhaps he wasn’t in the room because he’d had to be put down. The cuts on his back had stopped bleeding by the time we reached the vets, but that didn’t necessarily mean anything. They could have been much deeper than they looked, or maybe they’d caused some kind of internal damage. The dog seemed fine, but he could have been in so much pain that it was cruel to keep him alive.

Or could he have bitten someone? The vet didn’t have any obvious cuts on her. She was seated behind a desk now, but I hadn’t noticed any bite marks when she’d come out to get us. Maybe he’d attacked one of her assistants, though. If he did, I was certain it wasn’t his fault. He was probably just scared or in pain, and he didn’t know what he was doing!

“Are you not going to sit down, Grace?” my mom asked in a tone that made it clear I should not refuse.

I dropped into the last empty seat, my hands wringing in front of me as I stared at the vet.

“Is he okay?”

The question tumbled out before I could stop it. I was too scared to hold it back, and out of the corner of my eyes, I saw my mom’s lips purse.

“Yes, he’s completely fine,” the vet said with a reassuring smile. “We’ve just taken him into the back to get prepped for the procedure.”

The brief moment of relief that I’d felt was quickly whisked away and replaced with a fresh wave of fear.

“Procedure?” I repeated, but it came out as more of a squeak.

“It’s nothing to worry about,” the vet was quick to say. “We’re just going to sedate him so we can sew up his cuts. It’s the normal procedure for all injuries of that size. No matter how well-behaved the dog is, it’s just safest for them to be sedated for something like this to ensure they don’t wiggle around too much and make it harder for us to take care of them.”

“Oh…”

That made sense, but it still worried me. It was better than the things I’d been panicking about, though. At least he was okay. He was still alive, and no one had come in to claim him yet.

“Does he have a microchip?” Mom asked.

“No, he doesn’t,” the vet responded, glancing at the computer screen on the desk in front of her. “We’ve not received any calls about a missing dog so far this morning either, but we’ve taken some photos of him that we’re going to post on social media in case anyone’s looking for him. That’s how most missing animals are located these days.”

“That makes sense,” I muttered.

It was selfish, but part of me wanted to ask her not to. I was torn between not wanting anyone to have ever lost their dog and not be able to find them, whilst also hoping that no one claimed him so that we could adopt him.

“Were you able to figure out anything about him?” Mom asked. “Where he came from, or how old he is?”

“Yes and no,” the vet said, cocking her head to the side slightly. “Obviously, first things first, he is in fact a boy dog.”

“Well, I could have told you that,” Mom muttered snidely.

“Without his microchip, we’re not entirely sure where he came from,” the vet continued, seeming to pretend she hadn’t heard Mom. “But he’s not malnourished, and his hair wasn’t particularly matted, so I don’t think he was on his own for too long. I think it’s unlikely he travelled too far.”

“Mmmm,” Mom replied.

I could tell she was thinking about the family who live at number seventeen again. She’d already decided the dog had come from them, and what the vet said only confirmed that.

“And we think he’s fairly you still. Probably just under or around a year. It seems like he’s still growing, based on the size of his paws compared to his body.”

“How big will he get?” I asked as the memory of the towering beast from another world flashed behind my eyes.

There was no way he’d get that big, though. That thing was the size of a bear, and he was just a dog. It wasn’t possible.

“Well, we don’t know his breed for certain, so we have to guess. Based on his physical characteristics, we believe he’s a spaniel border collie cross—”

“A mutt,” Mom muttered, before adding, “Just like my last dog.”

A slight frown had been pulling at the vet’s lips, but Mom’s explanation had helped smooth it away. I wasn’t sure whether she was telling the truth or not, though. I could have sworn she’d said something before about only having purebred dogs when she was a kid, but I couldn’t remember whether she actually had or not. I might have been misremembering it, or maybe she’d lied to me when she said that.

“Yes, he is technically a mutt, we think,” the vet agreed. “Normally, spaniel border collie crosses get to about forty to fifty-five centimetres tall, and they tend to weigh about eighteen to twenty-five kilograms, so he’s probably going to be a medium-sized dog when he’s fully grown. Obviously, he might be a little bigger or smaller than that. It’s just an estimate.”

I looked down at the floor, trying to work out how big forty to fifty-five centimetres, but I had no clue. Even ‘medium-sized’ didn’t really help me. I didn’t know what counted as a big dog and what counted as a small dog, so I just smiled at the vet, hoping she wouldn’t ask me anything about it.

“I see,” Mom said. “So, what happens next?”

The vet seemed to hesitate for a moment as she glanced between my mom and me.

“Oh, did Hannah walk you through our procedure for stray dogs when you first came in?” she asked.

“She did not.”

“Ah, okay. That’s fine. I can run you through it now,” the vet said with a smile. “So, first we’re going to get him stitched up, and we’ll monitor him throughout the night to make sure he’s doing okay, but we’re also going to reach out to the local authority to alert them to a missing dog.”

“Is that really necessary?” Mom asked.

For once, I agreed with her. Surely, they didn’t need to do that. They were going to post about him on social media, so it seemed extreme to also have to contact the authorities, and who would they even speak to about that? I assumed the vet would know better than I would, but was there a person whose job was to take reports of missing and found animals? If so, perhaps I wouldn’t mind doing that job.

Hesitation slammed into me. I wasn’t sure whether that thought had come from, but animals generally scared me. They made me nervous, and I probably wouldn’t have to actually go near any if that were my job, but it was still a concern. Plus, I’d only been thinking about how nice it would be to call people and tell them we’d found their dog, but hearing reports of missing animals all the time must get so depressing.

“Yes. Unfortunately, the law states that we need to inform them of all animals that might have gone missing or been stolen that are brought into us,” the vet told Mom.

“Then what will happen?” I asked before Mom could say anything else.

I was a little worried that she was planning on arguing with the vet. There was a look in her eye that made it seem like a possibility, and that concerned me. As much as I just wanted to be able to take the dog home now, I knew that arguing with the vet wouldn’t help, and I was scared that it would make her think we wouldn’t be good owners. I wasn’t sure whether that was her decision to make, but she may have been able to influence it.

“Tomorrow, as long as he’s recovering nicely, he’ll be transferred to the nearest rescue centre. Luckily, there’s an RSPCA centre not too far from here. It’s about a… twenty or thirty minute drive, depending on traffic,” the vet explained. “Then, they need to hold him for seven days, by law. It’s just to give his past owners some time to get in contact with them, but after that, they can rehome him.”

The more the vet spoke, the more I felt my heart sink. Before we’d brought him in, it had seemed so likely that we’d be able to adopt the dog. I’d allowed myself to get too attached and to fantasise about what it would be like to own him. I’d imagined taking him out on walks or going on runs together, with him pushing me to run faster. It was foolish of me. I actually thought it could happen, but that hope had been destroyed, and I was heartbroken.

He would be claimed. There was no chance he wouldn’t. He was such a good dog and so cute. Whoever he’d run away from before finding himself in our garden would call up the rescue centre, and then I’d never see him again.

That thought made me want to cry. Even though he wasn’t my dog, it felt like I was losing him, like he was being ripped away from me. I had no right to feel that way. I’d only known him for less than an hour, but I already loved him, and my heart was broken.

“And will they be the ones in charge of the rehoming?” Mom asked. “If his past owners don’t get in contact, that is.”

Despite the tears welling in my eyes, my head snapped up. Mom’s tone had been casual. It sounded like she was just asking about the weather or something, but I knew there was more to it than that. Perhaps I was just grasping at straws, searching for any flicker of hope, no matter how faint, but I was certain Mom would not have asked out of curiosity alone.

“Yes, they will be,” the vet said. “But if you’d like, we can pass along your name and contact details? That way, they can keep you up to date, if needed?”

I held my breath, watching my mom out of the corner of my eye. It felt like every single muscle in my body was tensing as I waited to hear what she would say. I knew it was stupid to get my hopes up again, and it still felt far too likely that his previous owner would find him instead, but…

“We would appreciate that, wouldn’t we, Grace?” Mom replied, glancing at me.

The vet smiled as I nodded eagerly, unable to help myself.

“Okay, great. In that case, what’s the best number to contact you on?” she asked.

I stared at my mom as she began reciting her mobile number, barely able to believe what I was hearing. Did that mean that Mom intended to let me adopt the dog? Surely, she wouldn’t have given her phone number if she didn’t.

Unless she was just planning on dangling it above my head for the next week. Any time I didn’t do exactly what she wanted me to, she might threaten to call the rescue centre and tell them she changed her mind, and we didn’t want the dog. It would work. I’d do whatever she said as long as we still had a chance to adopt him.

Was that something she’d do, though? I honestly wasn’t sure. It seemed cruel, and surely she’d only do that if she actually intended for us to adopt him at the end of it. Otherwise, that would just be too mean, even for her. She could be bad, but she wasn’t usually that horrible.

Mom didn’t meet my gaze as the vet confirmed her phone number. She must have felt my eyes boring into her face, but she didn’t look away from the vet once, but maybe that was a good thing, I realised. It gave me a chance to compose myself and hide my excitement. If Mom saw how I truly felt, how hopeful I was, it might make her change her mind.

“Okay, fantastic,” the vet said. “Well, I’ll make sure that’s passed along, and they’ll give you a call if they have any news or if he’s still available at the end of the seven days.”

“Great,” Mom replied before asking, “Is that everything?”

The vet looked at her screen again, scanning through the information there.

“I believe so! Thank you for bringing the dog into us, and hopefully, you’ll hear from the RSPCA shortly,” the vet said as she started to stand.

My mom did the same, and I leapt to my feet as panic flared within me. I glanced between my mom and the vet, a question building in my chest.

“Will he be okay?” I blurted out.

The vet’s smile turned kind as she looked at me.

“He will. We’re going to do some X-rays once he’s sedated just to make sure, but it seems like the cuts on his back are his only injuries, and they look worse than they are,” she told me. “They shouldn’t take too long to heal, and then, he’ll be back to normal.”

“Okay, good,” I said, nodding to myself. “Can I… can I see him? Just to say goodbye?”

My voice almost broke on that last word, but I just about managed to hold it together. If I was never going to see him again, if his owner did claim him or something happened to him, I wanted to be able to hug him one last time.

A grimace appeared on the vet’s face, and I had to look down quickly to hide my sadness and disappointment. I already knew what she was going to say.

“I’m so sorry,” she said. “He’s already being prepped for his procedure, so we can’t let you go back there. We didn’t think you’d want to see him…”

“Oh, that’s okay,” I muttered. “Thank you.”

“I’m really sorry,” the vet repeated.

“It’s fine,” my mom said. “Shall we, Grace?”

I didn’t meet the vet’s eye as I followed Mom towards the door. I knew she felt bad about me not being able to see him, and for some reason, that just made me feel worse. How was she meant to know? We hadn’t gone into the examination room with him, so for all she knew, we didn’t really care about the dog. That was a completely reasonable conclusion to come to, but I was still heartbroken.

My gaze remained on the floor as we followed the vet back along the twisting corridors towards the waiting area. I just couldn’t bring myself to look up. It felt too hard, and I was scared that it would cause my barely-restrained tears to break free.

“Well, thank you again,” the vet told us as we reached the reception again.

“Not at all,” Mom replied. “It was only right to bring that poor injured puppy in to you. It could have gotten hit by a car if it hadn’t been caught!”

Her voice was loud, and I assumed that was for the sake of the people in the waiting room. It had filled up in the time we’d been speaking to the vet, and Mom probably wanted everyone to know that she had selflessly rescued a stray dog. It reflected well on her, making her seem like a hero.

The woman sitting closest to us, clutching a cat carrier, smiled at Mom. She was probably just being polite, but Mom sent her a gracious look, and I knew she was thrilled someone had responded.

“And it’s not even that late,” Mom said, glancing at the clock on the wall behind the receptionist. “We still have time to go shopping.”

I forced a smile onto my face as Mom marched out of the veterinary clinic, knowing that Mom would notice if I didn’t, but it was difficult. I was dreading going shopping with my mom, but that wasn’t what made it so hard.

Pain burned in my chest, and it seemed to be growing stronger with each step. It felt as though I’d left a crucial part of myself in there, and my body was begging me to turn around and retrieve it, but I couldn’t. I couldn’t go back for the dog.

His cuts needed to be stitched up, and then he had to spend seven days in the rescue centre, I told myself. It was the law, and even if I wanted to break it, the vet didn’t. I doubted the rescue centre would be willing to do it either, so I’d have to wait and just hope that no one claimed him in that time. I wanted to believe that they wouldn’t. I still had a feeling that he had no previous owner, but I couldn’t quite bring myself to do it.

My body was still in agony as we reached the car and got in. A wince contorted my face as I twisted to do up my seat belt, and my eyes flicked towards my mom, checking to make sure she hadn’t seen, but she wasn’t even looking at me. Her eyes were fixed on the steering wheel in front of her, and she turned the keys in her hands, the movement seemingly unconscious.

“His owner will probably see the post the vet puts up,” she said after a few seconds.

I winced again. I wasn’t sure why she said that, whether she was actively trying to hurt me, but it had worked.

“Probably,” I agreed miserably.

“Or they’ll have already registered him missing, and the rescue centre will call them as soon as he arrives,” she continued.

“Mmm,” was all I could say to that.

Silence stretched out between us, broken only by the occasional jingling of her keys as she continued to fiddle with them. I didn’t mind the quietness. It allowed me to wallow in the pain, and that was all I felt capable of doing.

“But,” Mom said suddenly, causing my breath to catch in my throat, “if not, it seems only fair that we adopt the poor thing.”

My eyes snapped to her, and I felt my mouth drop open. I’d been hoping she’d let us adopt him, but I didn’t think she’d just announce it. I expected her to at least pretend to consider it all week before making her decision, and she’d taken me by surprise. I didn’t know how to react or what she wanted me to say.

“Really?” I asked eventually.

It felt like a trap, somehow. It was too simple, and I didn’t quite trust that Mom had just made her mind up like that. She must have known how much I wanted to keep him. She could have forced me to act perfectly all week, to tidy the entire house and do all of the chores, in the hopes of maybe being able to adopt him, but instead, she’d just… told me we would.

Had she changed? Had something happened to make her want to act differently, to treat me differently? Maybe it was seeing the dog again. It could have reminded her of when she was a kid and made her remember how horribly her parents had treated her. Perhaps she’d realised she did the same to me and wanted to fix that.

Or maybe I was just getting my hopes up about another thing that would probably break my heart. I’d thought Mom had changed before. A couple of years ago, she’d been in a good mood for almost a full week. She hadn’t shouted at me once in that time, and I don’t even think she and Dad had fought at all. I started to get used to it, to think that it might be her new norm, but it didn’t last. It never did, but I still hoped it would one day.

“He will be your responsibility, though,” Mom said, meeting my gaze. “You’ll have to take him on a walk every single day, even when you’re tired after school.”

“I will,” I said quickly.

“And you’ll have to feed him in the morning,” she added. “You won’t be able to sleep until gone midday at the weekend anymore. He’ll need to be fed before that.”

“Okay!”

“You’ll have to let him out and pick up after him too. Every single time. I do not want to be climbing over a minefield of dog poop whenever I go outside,” Mom warned me.

“You won’t be,” I promised, feeling hope fluttering within me, despite how foolish I knew it was.

“And you’ll need to do something about your room. It’s a pigsty, and that’s not safe for a dog. He could eat something he shouldn’t and be dead before you even get home from school. You’ll have to do that this week. And your lounge too. The whole house needs to be spotless before we pick him up. If we pick him up,” she corrected herself.

I’d been expecting her to say something like that, and I nodded quickly.

“Okay, I will.”

Mom let out an exhale through her nose as she examined me. I had no clue what she was thinking. It seemed like she was thinking hard about something, but she just nodded and looked away.

“Alright. It probably won’t happen, but if we get the call about him next week, we’ll adopt the dog.”
