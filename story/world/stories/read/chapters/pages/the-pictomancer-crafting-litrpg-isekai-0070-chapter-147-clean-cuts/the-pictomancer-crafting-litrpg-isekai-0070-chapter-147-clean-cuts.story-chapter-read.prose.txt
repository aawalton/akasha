The back room smelled like cold iron and earth. Blood and sawdust dotted the room, while chunks of bloody meat hanged from hooks. It looked every bit like how Clive imagined a butcher’s room to be. He half expected Greymane to announce ‘Fresh Meat’ when he entered.

The boar was laid out on a broad wooden table, a big one, maybe two hundred pounds, already gutted and bled out but otherwise whole.

Before Greymane moved to the block, he stopped at the head of the table. He set both hands flat on the wood, not touching the boar. He closed his eyes.

The words were low, more breath than voice. Clive caught fragments — your strength becomes ours, something about the ground receiving what was given, a short phrase repeated twice that he didn't have the vocabulary for.

Then Greymane opened his eyes and was quiet for a moment.

"Old custom," he said, without looking at Clive. "My grandmother's grandmother started it. Every animal that comes through this room fed something before it fed us. Deserves to be acknowledged."

Greymane moved to the block and pulled a boning knife. Held it out handle-first.

"You said you knew meat," he said. "Show me."

Clive took the knife with confidence. He'd broken down chickens before, the occasional rack of ribs from a grocery store, and of course the game that he hunted for Azura. He knew joints existed, knew they were easier than bone. He set his hand on the boar's shoulder, pressed along the ridge of it until he found where the socket had to be, and cut.

The knife skipped off something hard and stopped.

He backed up, re-angled, tried again. The blade sank a little further this time before getting stuck. This was harder than he thought. The boar meat was tougher than what he was used to.

Greymane watched without speaking.

Clive worked at it for another minute. Eventually, the shoulder came partway free before binding up, the cut face jagged, meat torn where it should have separated clean.

Greymane glanced at the cut and shook his head. "You eat that?"

"What's wrong with it?" Clive said. "It's a little rough, but once you cook it, no one can tell."

Greymane picked up the shoulder and turned it so the cut face caught the light. The muscle was torn in two places where the knife had skidded, the grain split wrong, fat smeared across what should have been a clean surface.

"You can tell," Greymane said. "Anyone who's eaten good meat can tell." He set it back down. "Torn muscle holds less juice. Cooks uneven. You end up with dry patches and wet ones and nothing where you wanted it." He tapped the smeared fat. "And this runs off in the pan before it has time to render. Flavor goes with it."

Clive looked at the cut with fresh eyes. He'd thought of it as a presentation problem — cosmetic, something heat would fix. He hadn't considered that the damage went further than what showed.

"I've been cooking it wrong my whole life," he said.

"Most people have." Greymane set the shoulder aside without ceremony. "They think cooking fixes mistakes. Sometimes it does. Mostly it just hides them from people who don't know any better." He picked up his own boning knife and nodded toward the other side of the table. "Watch. Then you try again."

Greymane moved to the opposite shoulder. He didn't pick up the knife yet. He set his palm flat on the hide the way he had at the head of the table and pressed along the joint line with two fingers until he found what he was looking for.

"Feel before you cut," he said. "You went straight to the knife. That's the first mistake."

His fingers traced a line from the top of the shoulder down toward the armpit. "The joint isn't where you think it is. Most people assume the center. It sits back, closer to the spine. You were cutting toward the ribs the whole time."

He picked up the knife.

"Second thing. You were trying to take it off in one motion. Big sweeping cut, all the way through." He positioned the blade at the top of the shoulder. "You don't cut the muscle first. You follow the seam. Trace it all the way around before you go deep. The joint wants to open. You're just showing it where the door is."

He cut. Small, certain strokes, nothing dramatic. The knife moved along a line Clive couldn't quite see but could track by watching where Greymane's wrist tilted. There was almost no resistance. The shoulder began to separate from the body the way a page separates from a book.

Greymane paused halfway and looked up. "Put your hand here."

Clive leaned over and pressed where he indicated. He could feel the seam now, the way the joint had loosened without fully opening.

"That's where you're going," Greymane said. "Everything else is just getting there without making a mess."

He finished the cut in four more strokes. The shoulder came away clean, the face smooth and unbroken, fat intact along the cap.

He set it on the board and stepped back.

"Now you," he said.

Clive moved to the remaining shoulder. He made himself stop before picking up the knife.

He set his palm flat on the hide the way Greymane had done it. Pressed along the joint line with two fingers. The ridge was there — firmer than the surrounding muscle, a buried architecture he'd missed entirely the first time. He traced it down toward the armpit, felt where it shifted, where the socket sat back further than he'd expected.

Closer to the spine. Not the center.

He picked up the knife and found the seam at the top of the shoulder. Small strokes. He felt the difference immediately — instead of the blade skipping off bone, it followed something, a natural channel, the path of least resistance. His wrist adjusted without him fully deciding to adjust it.

The shoulder began to separate.

It wasn't as clean as Greymane's. He had to pause twice to reorient, once when the knife drifted and once when he went slightly too deep and had to recover. But he traced the seam all the way around before going deep, the way he'd been shown, and when the joint finally opened it opened without force.

Four more strokes. The shoulder came away.

He set it on the board and looked at the cut face. Smoother than his first attempt. The muscle grain was intact, the fat cap unbroken along the edge.

He stared at it for a moment.

Greymane was watching him. "Problem?"

"No," Clive said. "Just — something clicked."

"It does that," he said. "Usually takes people longer. You’re a natural at this."

He picked up the shoulder and turned it in the light, the same way he'd examined Clive's first attempt. This time he set it down without comment, which felt like more than anything he could have said.

"Other side," he said. "Again."

By the time Greymane let him go, the light coming through the back room's single window had gone amber. Clive's shoulders ached in a way that was different from weapons training, yet still just as exhausting. His hands were clean. Greymane had made him wash them twice.

He carried the cuts back to the inn wrapped in cloth, stacked in a borrowed crate. It was more meat than three people needed for one evening and he knew it, but Greymane had been generous with the division and Clive hadn't argued.

Azura was sitting on the inn's front step when he came up the lane. She saw the crate before she saw him.

She stood up.

"Meat," she said.

"Good evening to you too."

She fell into step beside him, eyes on the crate. "That's a lot of meat."

"I'm aware."

"Is it all—"

"You're getting a fair share," Clive said. "You're not getting all of it."

She accepted this with visible effort.

Jill was in the kitchen when he brought the crate through. Clive didn't know how she'd managed it—he didn’t think you could just waltz into a hotel and use their kitchen— but there was a fire going and a pot of something on, and no sign that anyone had argued about anything. Things in this village seemed to become available when Jill was nearby. People just quietly made room.

She looked up when he came in.

"You're cooking?" Clive asked.

"You were gone all day." She turned back to the pot. "It seemed fair."

He set the crate on the counter and started unwrapping cuts. "Greymane's thorough."

"He is." She stirred once, tasted, set the spoon aside. "How was it?"

"I learned that I've been cooking meat wrong my whole life."

Jill glanced at him. "Wrong how?"

"Every way, apparently."

She almost smiled. She came over and looked at what he'd laid out. Then she picked up one of the wrapped bundles and moved it to the far end of the counter without asking.

"Loins go last," she said. "If you cook them now they'll be gone before anything else is ready and someone will eat them cold standing over the pot."

Clive looked at Azura, who was leaning in the doorway.

Azura looked back at him with an expression of complete innocence.

They worked in the way that people work together when they have different knowledge of the same task — Jill knowing the fire, knowing the timing, knowing which herbs grew in window boxes along the lane and which the innkeeper kept in jars above the counter. Clive knowing the cuts now, knowing which pieces needed time and which would tighten if they waited too long. There was a brief disagreement about the fat cap that resolved itself when Jill tasted the rendered version and didn't say anything, which he decided to count as a point.

Azura ate two portions before the rest was plated.

Nobody commented on this either.

They ate at the small table by the kitchen fire rather than going back upstairs. The inn had gone quiet around them. Overhead, shade-lings drifted along the ceiling beams in their slow, purposeless way, their ambient glow softening the firelight into something dimmer and more diffuse, like eating by the light of a dying sunset.

Clive watched one drift past the window and pause there, its formless edges bleeding into the dark outside.

"I'll never get used to them," he said.

Jill looked up from her plate. "The shade-lings?"

"It feels like being watched. All the time. By something that doesn't blink."

Jill looked up at the ceiling. A cluster of three had settled into the corner above the hearth, overlapping at the edges, their glow pulsing faintly out of sync with each other. "I felt the same way at first. Like living inside a haunted house." She turned back to her food. "After a while you stop noticing. They're just — there. Like weather."

"How long did it take?"

She considered this. "About a month before I stopped checking the corners every time I walked into a room." She paused. "Two months before I stopped checking the ceiling."

Clive looked back up at the three in the corner. One of them drifted an inch to the left for no discernible reason.

"So three months," he said.

"Give or take."

Jill ate slowly after that. She had a way of being still at a table. Never hurried, always fully enjoying those little quiet moments they had together… Just like the old days.

He looked down at his plate before she could catch him looking.

It wasn't the same, he told himself. Different world, different lives, different versions of both of them shaped by years that hadn't overlapped. The table was wrong, the light was wrong, there was a dragon eating in the corner who had strong opinions about salt.

But the fire was low, and the food was good. Jill reached across without asking and broke off a piece of his bread because she'd finished hers, exactly the way she always had.

He didn't say anything about it.

Neither did she.
