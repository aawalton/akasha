Chapter 144

A gate left open does not always mean surrender. A man could have already counted every road that leads to the gate and had them all covered. He does not need to bar the door. — Codex of the Long Watch, Watcher's Commentary

The party climbed up to the Acropolis under the harsh sunlight. Nobody had proposed a concrete plan back in the kitchen. They had precious little information except that people were taken. This climb was as much to gather information as it was to see if they could twist the general's arm.

"The Periclean walls ran the whole way from here to the city," Steve said, half to himself, watching a stretch of old foundation stone poking through a modern retaining wall. "The Long Walls. So Athens could feed itself from the sea no matter who held the ground in between."

"Did they hold?" Donna asked.

"For a little over fifty years, until the Spartans made the Athenians tear them down themselves."

"So not really, then."

"The walls held for fifty years, Donna."

"And then they didn't. When you build a big impressive wall, someone will eventually come and knock it down," Donna said, adjusting the strap across her chest without breaking stride.

Steve opened his mouth to argue the point, then looked up the hill at where they were going and understood what she meant.

Sarah was walking a half-step ahead of the rest of them, her hands jammed in her jacket pockets, and her jaw set. Arthur fell into step beside her.

• •

Donna lifted one hand and stopped the party where the western approach narrowed below the Beulé Gate.

The crossbows came into view before they had reached the table set in the middle of the path. Two teams, separated by enough ground that no single rush could reach both in one movement, occupied sandbagged positions above the path. A man carrying a staff stood with each team.

"Is that man carrying a staff? Staff needs living wood." Steve whispered.

Ether saturation on the planet has exceeded levels that would make a wooden staff a viable focus. Barely. Archie said.

There were more prepared positions on the roofs below the approach and another behind the parapet higher up. The firing positions were set to cover overlapping ground between them, though the lines did not all point down at the main approach. Some faced across one another, covering the stairs and locations an attacker might get to, if they managed to breach the initial defence.

A relief file came down from the gate carrying empty water jars and a sack of something metal in it, while another went up with full jars and a bucket of wrapped bolts. A folded stretcher hung between two of the men, ready for anyone who might need it.

A rope barrier had been strung across the path where it turned towards the Propylaea. A corporal stood beside it with a roster book and four soldiers with spears behind him.

"Arthur Ashworth. Donna Callas. Steven Parr. Sarah Vassilou," Arthur gave out their names.

The roster already had their names and the man put a mark against each.

"You are expected. The general has agreed to receive you," he said in accented English as a runner left the checkpoint and went uphill.

"Your weapons remain slung or lowered while you are inside the Acropolis."

"Fair enough," Arthur said.

Before they could clear the checkpoint, Captain Karras came down to meet them.

"Keep the weapons lowered and there will be no difficulty."

"There is already a difficulty," Sarah said.

"Then the general is waiting to discuss it. You will come with me," the captain said curtly and turned around.

Arthur had seen the Acropolis from nearly every direction since arriving in Athens. From below, it was a huge mass of pale stone and broken columns. Through the western approach, it was a fortification.

The Beulé Gate stood above them, west of the Propylaea, its ancient blocks now threaded with newer work. Sandbags filled the shallow places where a defender would otherwise have to expose himself. Wooden screens broke up any clean lines of approach.

"Don't look only uphill," Donna said quietly.

Arthur glanced back towards the lower roofs behind them. They held two more positions, both partially screened with salvaged timber and sacks. One covered the checkpoint and the other the road beyond it. A man stood near a chimney with a rod like Steve's, though it did not carry blue markings like Steve's.

"Another caster," Steve murmured.

"There are three I can see. Possibly four," Donna murmured.

Another caster stood behind a low barricade farther up, beside a crossbow team. The separation seemed deliberate: a single strike could take the crossbows or the caster, but not both. A runner sat on the steps near the gatehouse watching the path. Another stood at the inner gate beside a hand bell on its post. The distance between the two was perhaps forty metres.

Fourteen separated firing positions from this approach alone, Archie said. Six of them carry caster signatures. This was not raised in a week, and it was not raised by an amateur.

The soldier guiding them stopped at a cleared terrace below the wall, and from here Arthur could see the harbour, the coast road, the rooftops of Piraeus spread out in the afternoon haze.

The view was the kind a tyrant would want, his dominion arrayed below him. They stopped near a canvas awning that had been stretched between posts. Under it was a wooden table, five chairs and the man who had taken Athens.

A captain stood two paces behind the general, a major to the captain's left and a lieutenant further back.

Mavros looked up at them and said in careful English.

"Mr Ashworth. Ms Callas. Mr Parr. Dr Vassilou." He gestured at the chairs. "Sit, if you would prefer to. I find a conversation goes better sitting, though I understand if none of you would rather."

Nobody sat.

"Coffee, then, at least." He started pouring himself a cup. "You have had a difficult crossing home, and a worse morning since, I think."

"You have my brother," Sarah said.

"I have forty-three names on a requisition list, of which your brother is one." Mavros set the carafe down with unhurried care. "I did not phrase it that way to be…" He paused for a moment to think. "…callous, yes. I did not phrase it that way to be callous. I phrase it that way because it is accurate, and I have found that accuracy wastes less of everyone's time."

"Where are my brother and sister-in-law?"

"Petros Vassilou is quartered in the eastern barracks with the other essential workers. His wife is in the leather workshop on the north terrace. Both are unharmed, fed, sheltered and performing the work this city requires. The former Council leaders are being held for their safety and for the stability of the interim administration. I can arrange a supervised visit with your family in a few weeks."

"They are prisoners."

"They are contributing. The harbour needs someone who understands how a harbour operates. The forge needs a man who can work iron. The stores need someone who can manage supply. Forty-three people are inside this garrison, and every one of them has a skill the city cannot do without."

"It doesn't change the fact that they were taken against their will to be gang-pressed into service," Sarah said.

"What of the soldiers who die every day to protect the civilian population? Isn't it the duty of the civilians to help those who are putting their lives at risk?"

"You are here because I have your friends. I know this. The men on the wall know this. I'm giving you time from my schedule because I want you to understand the reality on the ground." He gestured at the chairs.

"The Council could not hold the coast," he said. "Four voices cancelling one another out while my soldiers died clearing sites. Antoniou would sign a requisition and then sit in a room debating whether to sign another. Nikephoros prayed over the dead. Papadakis catalogued them. I built."

Steve shifted uncomfortably in his chair.

"Do you know how long it took the Council to agree on a grain-distribution schedule? Eleven days. Eleven days while the granaries were half empty. I signed the order on the first morning and the grain moved that afternoon." He picked up his cup and took a sip. "One man deciding is faster than four voices agreeing, and speed is the difference between a coast that holds and one that does not."

"Redundancy always looks like money thrown away until the load hits. The extra thick oak beam seems like wasted cash, till the earthquake hits and then everybody is grateful that they listened to the builder," Arthur said. "The slowness you hate is a brake. It drags on the good decisions and the catastrophic ones at the same rate. You didn't remove the waste; you removed the brake."

Mavros considered him for a long moment, and something close to respect crossed his face. "Redundancy is a peacetime virtue and this world has no peace. You want to instruct me on structures built to survive a shock? I've spent my life inside such a structure. When the line breaks, an army does not convene a subcommittee. One man decides, and the rest live or die on it. That is the structure."

"Except you, that is not true. An officer might decide in a second, but he decides inside a system that is built so that a bad decision doesn't end up losing the war. There is a man above him and a man beside him. That is why you have two keys for the nuclear codes," Donna said, heat rising in her face. "What you have done is a good old-fashioned coup."

"A coup? Against what? A Council that was never elected? The Council took power because they survived the change, not because the people voted them in. The chair was empty. You cannot coup an empty throne."

"The Council can be wrong but when it is wrong, it is wrong loudly, in front of people. That is the whole purpose. When it made the wrong call, you could see it, name it and change it if need be. You've just locked up the only people in this city whose job was to tell you no."

"You are mistaking me for Dr Papadakis. I'm a soldier, not an academic, and I did not agree to this meeting to debate philosophy."

"Good, then we can avoid wasting time and you can let everyone out," Donna said.

"No."

Donna's attention kept moving between the positions above them and the gate behind the awning.

"You know we can take this position," she said.

"Yes," Mavros said, matter-of-factly.

"I could reach the first crossbow team before the caster beside them took a breath."

"I believe you."

"And Arthur could put this table through your chest."

Mavros glanced at Arthur's hands resting on the wood and smiled. "I believe that as well."

"Then perhaps you should believe me when I say you will give them back," Sarah said, irritation plain in her voice.

Mavros leaned forward.

"You can kill me. I believe this honestly. You are extraordinarily capable people. I've known that since the container yard incident. Perhaps you can kill every soldier you see. I do not mistake any of my soldiers as your equals. I've read some of Dr Papadakis's notes on what you have been doing. They were very interesting."

"Then why are we still having this conversation?" Donna said.

"Because defeating the men you can see does not release the people you cannot. The men standing beside your friends are not here. They are inside the walls, in rooms you have not seen, behind doors you have not mapped."

He paused and pushed the cup to one side.

"When the alarm reaches them, and it will, they will hear that four foreigners have attacked the Acropolis and killed their general. Some of those men will wait for orders. Some will panic. Some may decide to avenge me against the people these foreigners came for. Who is to say how a man reacts in a moment of panic."

Donna gripped the edge of the table, and her knuckles turned white.

While Mavros spoke, Sarah had gone still. Arthur had seen this before, in every triage and every injury assessment. He readied himself for action, but Sarah blinked a moment later and her focus returned.

Mavros continued without appearing to notice.

"A broken wall drops stone on the people sheltering behind it. Violence in a closed space does not choose who it touches. It reaches who is nearest."

Arthur looked at the walls.

The Propylaea had been designed to funnel a processional crowd along a single axis, and Mavros had turned the funnel into a choke point. Any forced entry through the main gate would drive them into a corridor where fire could come from three elevations at once.

A breach through the south wall would require demolishing two metres of reinforced masonry, and the rubble would block the route to whatever rooms lay beyond. The interior beyond the gate was a warren of old chambers and new partitions, and he had no way of knowing who was kept where.

"We could take you hostage. Do to you what you did to the forty-three people in there," Donna said.

Mavros smiled and gestured to his side. "Have you met Major Sotiriou? He is my second-in-command."

The major gave the party a nod.

"You have already met Captain Karras, who is Sotiriou, second-in-command. There is a reason why there is a chain of command. You can take me hostage and it would change nothing. I'm a soldier, Ms Callas. Do not think you can intimidate me in my own garrison."

"But more importantly to you, I am not standing in the room where Petros or Maria sleeps. Neither is my second-in-command, nor the sergeant who holds the keys to it." The general's voice did not rise in anger or challenge. "If you take me from this table, by force or by threat, none of that changes who is standing in that room five minutes later. You simply become the foreigners who took the Acropolis, and we become the men who tried to defend it."

"If you had no intentions to negotiate, you could have refused to see us," Arthur said.

"Refusing would suggest I have something to fear from this conversation. I do not. A few months ago, you would have been deported back to your country for meddling in Greek matters, but I understand that the world has changed."

Mavros got up, and the other officers shifted to cover him.

"I agreed to meet you so that you could understand there is no clean extraction and that everyone, including your friends, would be better off if you understand that."

"The strong do what they can and the weak suffer what they must. That's a line from an Athenian general too. Thucydides wrote it down," Steve said.

Mavros opened his mouth to say something, then smiled and decided against it.

"This meeting is finished. Have a good day."

They walked back down the path without speaking while the crossbow teams watched them go. Each position they passed was exactly where it had been on the way up, the same men in the same postures, the bolts resting on the parapets. The corporal at the lower barrier lifted the rope and let them through.

When the last position was behind them, Sarah spoke.

"His Matrix was too dense," she said in a low voice. "I could have held him for a few seconds, but not long enough to compel anything. He is not low-level."

"So the walk up and hit them with a stick plan is buggered," Arthur said.

"It was not much of a plan to start with," Donna said, moving the party into a side street. "It's the same bloody Baron thing all over again."

"The Baron wanted our cores. Mavros wants Sarah's family. It will be a cold day in hell before I let him have Petros and Maria."

Donna and Steve gave him a quick smile and a nod.

"We go back home and figure out how to bury him inside his own fort."
