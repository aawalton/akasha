One of the often underappreciated aspects of necromancy, setting aside the morality issue, is how great the undead are at manual labour.  They don’t eat, other than brains, and they don’t sleep.  They usually have some form of dark vision, so you don’t even need to illuminate the worksite at night.

Nor do you need to fret about health and safety.  The dudes are already dead.  How much worse could their situation get if they lose a finger or even an arm?  Or, as a random example, if they accidentally walked off a ledge that didn’t have a bright orange safety barrier to protect the terminally stupid from fulfilling their destiny?

I had hundreds of workers who were literally impossible to work to death, and whose treatment even the great goddess Karen, may she be blessed in triplicate, couldn’t complain about if the working conditions were less than ideal.  This may sound like a not-good setup, karmically speaking, but seeing as they were all shadow or evil-aligned assholes before I tore their souls out of the great beyond and put them to work, I figured it was neutral at worst.

Hell, I was probably doing the multiverse a favour.  Not everyone got reincarnated; in fact, most seemed to go to whatever their preferred form of afterlife might be, but for those among my undead throngs who might have been sent to another world, I was saving that world from an aspiring villain.  Truly, I was a noble dragon.

However, the zombie dwarf that had splatted down next to me a moment ago was the third this week.  It was starting to impact construction rates.

“How bad is it?” I asked.  The arena, the word amphitheatre did not do Kat’s vision justice, was spread out around me.  The tiered seating for the financially challenged rose nearly a hundred metres above me in all directions.  I stood in the stands, being made for the wealthy elites, thousands of seats on short wooden structures that I desperately hoped were suitably sturdy for rich bastards who’d paid to sit on them.

“Not good,” Otto muttered.  In light of the suicidal tendencies of some of the undead, I sent over a skilled dwarf builder to get things back on track.

“We can still make the deadline?  It looks like we will still make it.  The major earth-moving stuff is done, right?”

Otto spat on the wood and rubbed at it with a metal-shod foot.  “Ja.  Overseers are making sure the zombies don’t off themselves again. Issue is the word has spread.”

“Among the undead?”

“Not so bad now we’ve stopped them talking, but that—” he nodded at the broken corpse lying twenty feet away, “—is still happening a couple of times a day.  All of them accidents, of course, but if you want an accident to happen… they tend to happen a lot.”

“Suicidal zombies were not on my shit-I-have-to-deal-with-today bingo card,” I admitted.

“It is largely under control.  But we will need to hire some skilled workers to make up for the loss in corpse-power.”

“You seem pretty chill about ordering around zombies, Otto.  Your lot had a hate boner for the undead last time I checked.”

He shrugged, his leather overalls rising and falling, but not very much.  He was still a dwarf after all.  “We are coming around to the idea.  They are basically a labour-saving device, like a shovel or a flamethrower.”

“Flamethrower?”

“Do you want to chop down your enemies and get your arm all tired, or just set them on fire?  Less work is always good.”

“Very wise, although most of them still have souls, so I’m not sure about relegating them to the status of a pickaxe.”

“You created them.”

“To win battles, and usually in the heat of the moment.  I’m not arguing about this with you.  So we can make the deadline?”

He sucked in air through his teeth, and anyone who has ever dealt with any tradesperson knows that this is a bad sign.  As this often presaged a massive increase in cost to the customer, my draconian avarice flared, and I gave him a “don’t push your fucking luck” look.

“Two days is doable, but we need a hundred skilled dwarven builders.”

“Why not humans?  Humans are cheaper,”

“Well, if you want it to fall apart after thirty years, humans will do a passable job. If you want to build it to last, dwarves are the better option.”

“You being a dwarf doesn’t colour your opinions in any way?”

“Nein!  Everyone knows dwarves build better.  Are you going for a slapdash arena that will crumble in thirty years or a true monument that will last for thousands, that your great-great-grandchildren can enjoy and marvel at?  There is nothing like having your descendants appreciate the effort you put into constructing something.”

“How do you know?”

“What?”

“Your great-to-the-sixteenth grandchildren aren’t even born yet.”

“Ja, but every time I see the Grandelspitz monument made by the ancestor, I feel great pride.  It fills me with vim and vigour for my own works to know that the hands attached to the penis that made me did such work.”

“The hands were attached to…”

“Not at ze same time!” His accent got thicker as he became upset, I noted with a faint sense of amusement.  “But my ancient forebear made somezing vunderful, and I can still appreciate it and remember his work.”

“So, in honour of you remembering your great grandad rubbing one out, I need to spend… about four thousand gold to hire your buddies?”

“When you put it like that, it sounds bad, but I assure you I am being entirely professional.  I take pride in my work.  It is not such a large sum in comparison to your personal wealth, nein?”

Wrath wanted to turn this stumpy ginger into a brief but illuminating example of how protein and fat burn quite fiercely, but I wrestled control of myself away from my darker instincts.  Trying to console myself that murdering my foreman would not only delay construction but also make it much harder to recruit a replacement, I refrained from killing him.

“I think a thirty-year lifetime for the building is acceptable,” I ground out through clenched teeth.

“Okay.  You want to be cheap, you can be—”

The punch wasn’t hard.  It was maybe twenty percent of my maximum punching strength, but it was enough to send him bouncing across the woodwork like a rag doll.

“Now that that is settled, I’ll be off.  You can hire two hundred humans to help out, just make sure they don’t hurt the zombies for fun.  Failure to meet the deadline will result in my asking Mrs Sanderson to handle the case.”

He had partially risen, fury on his face, but the mere mention of the bishop of Karen set him to obsequious nodding.  Name-dropping Mrs Sanderson seemed like an effective strategy that I should use more often to settle minor issues like greedy dwarves being greedy assholes.

A portal appeared next to me, and I stepped through into Tim’s workshop.  It was almost reassuring to see that some things simply refused to change.

The gothic vibe of the candles and skull-encrusted decor blended jarringly with the techno-svelte appearance of the modern work benches across the other side of the cavernous room, but something about that mismatch felt strangely familiar. The dentist's chair where I’d summoned and banished Moonslight repeatedly brought back happy memories that helped settle Wrath down and bring me some semblance of internal peace.

“Are they ready?” I called out, making the big green man jump slightly.

Tim turned and blinked at me with massive eyes before he snatched the multi-lensed goggles off his head.  He gave me a big, froggy smile and patted something covered by a piece of cloth on his desk.

“Sure do, Bob. Not going to lie, this one was a lot of fun to make.”

A familiar buzzing noise, followed by a wong-wong as he swished the legally distinct laser sword back and forth, filled the heart of thirteen-year-old me with joy.

“Gimme!”

“Hold on there!  Couple of things you need to know.”  The blue blade cut off, and he unscrewed a cap at the base of the handle.  “It will recharge slowly from ambient mana, but it burns through the juice pretty quickly.  You can add mana crystals in here and keep it going.  Prolonged use will drain it in about half an hour, so don’t expect it to last through any epic slugging matches.”

“I don’t care.  I get a lightsaber, so this is a good deal.”

There were alternatives at my disposal, any number of the weapons and armour I’d purloined from Bulb’s repository could serve the same function and level modest towns at the same time, but I was going to be incognito.

Vanity wasn’t terribly happy about that whole aspect of the mission to bring the elves on board.  At all.  My sense of self was deeply offended by the end result of my elf-face.  Lengthening my jaw, reshaping my nose, for some reason, even though the end product had received a thumbs-up from Esme, Vanity wasn’t happy.  Turns out my own face was the best face.

So, in addition to looking like a treehugger, I couldn’t use my own magic or my stash of overpowered goodies.  I had turned to Tim to cover this weakness, and boy howdy had the mad scientist delivered.  He tossed the silvery cylinder to me, and I caught it smoothly.  My thumb settled on the power stud, and the azure blade sprang to life.

“Any chance you can tone down the heat coming off this thing?” I asked, wonging it back and forth happily.  “It’s screwing with my heat vision.”

“It’s a plasma beam.  It is, by its very nature, hot.”

“That’s a no?” The beam vanished, and I clipped the weapon to my belt like a proper Jedi.

“Yep.”

“Yep no or no yep?”

“I’m not playing that game with you, Bob.  Here.  The rest of your order.”

I glanced around nervously as I caught the second item I’d requested.

“You didn’t have to make it like that,” I muttered, holding the item of clothing up in front of myself for a second before vanishing into my storage dimension.

“I kind of did.  If you want full body coverage, the spell locus needs to be around the centre of your body.”  Tim shrugged.  “So undies it had to be.”

“Yeah, but that’s a goddamn thong.”  The chainmail buttock flosser had not looked comfortable.  “What’s wrong with an honest pair of boxer shorts?”

“You asked for full protection, but with the power focused on the front.  The shield spell is embedded into each of the links, so most of the links had to be set to the front.  Even accommodating for the modifications you made to your undercarriage, there would have been too many links to your rear if I hadn’t.”

“If I have to shift back and forth from dragon to human, people are going to ask questions.”

“You’re being a sneaky dragon, which I approve of, so that won’t be an issue.  I suppose you could wear your regular pink boxers under the Cod Piece of Shielding.”

“Oh, and that would look so much better.”

“Who cares?  One, it shouldn’t come up.  Two, you’re a fucking dragon.”

“You raise a valid point.”

“Of course I do.  Look, I’m kind of busy.  Otto has me working on anti-self-termination collars for the zombies, Simeon wants me to look over his bunnytitan designs, and Kat is nagging me about sound systems…  Are we done?”

“Bunnytitans?” I cocked my head to one side, intrigued and horrified in equal measure.

“Think six BB-209s merged into one body.  Multiple pilots, one massive mech.  I like the idea.”

“Me too, you’ve got a green light on that one from me.”

“The problem is they keep breaking out into different songs, the pilots, I mean.  And Billy Jean being blended with Killing In The Name Of just sounds wrong.”
