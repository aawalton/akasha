“This tea...”

Ceres stared at the cup in her hands with open curiosity.

After the horses had been settled and the mercenaries allowed half an hour of rest, the unloading began in earnest. Heavy book crates were carried into the studying area one by one, boots thudding over the floorboards while low voices and the scrape of wood filled the room. Dorgh had already returned to town, leaving Anne alone with the delivery party and the first real visitors ever received by her bookstore.

For that reason alone, ordinary water would have been insufficient.

Anne had prepared two cups of her precious soul tea and brought them to the counter, where she and Ceres could speak without getting in the workers’ way.

“It is soul tea,” Anne said, watching the mercenary’s reaction with quiet satisfaction. “It calms the nerves and helps with focus. Usually it is quite expensive, but this one is home-grown.”

She did not bother hiding its value. On the contrary, she wanted Ceres to understand that this was not a casual courtesy. Giving customers something good at the right moment had its own power. It made them feel seen. Better yet, it made them remember.

Ceres raised the cup again, this time more slowly. The hazel-haired half-beast had the straightforward face Anne preferred in a potential customer—nothing hidden too deeply, no elaborate mask, no habit of smiling while planning something. Surprise, appreciation, caution, fatigue, interest—all of it passed openly across her features.

Not a simple woman, Anne judged. Just an honest one. Which was the best kind.

The mercenary drank again, more attentively now, and Anne let the silence stretch just long enough to make the tea itself part of the conversation.

Then she struck.

“Actually, Ms. Ceres, I wanted to speak to you about another business opportunity. A somewhat different one from escorting goods.”

Ceres lowered her cup and looked at her with immediate interest.

Anne returned the look with a composed business smile.

“Well, I already explained the general idea of the bookstore. I will be selling and renting books and scrolls. As you have seen, I own many already... but far from enough. I will need more. A great many more. So I need someone who can acquire them for me.”

That earned the exact reaction she expected. Surprise first, then consideration, ending with signs of disappointment.

“Ms. Anne,” Ceres said carefully, “would that not suit a merchant better?”

“Usually, yes,” Anne admitted. “But merchants rely on fixed goods and fixed routes, and books do not behave nearly so well. The ones I want are often found in strange places, and at strange times. Antique shops. Estate sales, fairs, forgotten storehouses, old study rooms people clear out without understanding what they have. Research scrolls interest me as well, and those can often be acquired through academies or their contacts.”

She could already see the mercenary’s polite refusal gathering itself, so she continued before it fully formed.

“I am not asking for a permanent contract,” Anne said. “Only this: when you travel, if you come across books or research scrolls that fit my interests, you buy them if the price is fair and bring them back whenever your path allows. If you pass through this region, all the better. If not, I can arrange for delivery from Riverend so long as the cost remains reasonable.”

Ceres listened with visible reluctance.

“Hum... I do travel a lot,” she admitted. “And I have seen books like that before. But I do not like depending on luck. And coming here would not be part of my normal route. If this were Riverend, maybe. But Mountainend...” She shook her head, embarrassed rather than dismissive. “It would not be very profitable for me.”

That much was fair.

Anne took another sip of tea as though considering the problem for the first time, though in truth she had already expected it.

“Well, then let us remove the uncertain part,” she said. “Forget most books for now. We can limit the arrangement to research scrolls. Those are not rare, if you know where to find them. I can give a detailed description of the kind I want, who is likely to sell them, and what prices are acceptable. As for this town, I am not asking you to make special trips for nothing. If you are ever going to Riverend, you can send someone from there to deliver what you found. I will cover the cost.”

The proposal was better. Anne could tell. Still, Ceres did not immediately agree. Which meant it was time for a different approach.

Anne set her cup down gently.

“Ms. Ceres,” she said, softer now, “you seem to be having trouble advancing to silver tier.”

The half-beast woman stiffened immediately. The reaction was bad enough that Anne continued before irritation could harden into offense.

“I am sorry for intruding,” she said, making sure the apology sounded genuine. “But this is my work. Collecting and recording are only preparation. A bookmancer’s true task is to guide people to knowledge. Those books you see there contain thousands upon thousands of fragments of experience, methods, failures, techniques, and paths. Most of it will never matter to any one person. But some of it will. I have yet to meet someone for whom nothing useful could be found.”

That eased the tension, if only slightly. So Anne pressed on.

“Advancement is something nearly everyone wants, which means there is a great deal written about it. Mixed-race paths especially tend to be poorly understood. People inherit bodies that do not always match the examples left by either side of their ancestry. If you wish, I may be able to help.”

There.

The offer was no longer about work, but need.

Ceres’ first instinct was still resistance. Anne could see it in the set of her jaw, in the way her fingers tightened around the cup, in the proud reluctance of someone who had spent too long surviving without asking much from others. But the tea had softened her, and Anne’s tone had not been mocking or opportunistic enough to trigger immediate distrust. More importantly, hope had been placed before her at precisely the kind of angle that made it difficult to ignore.

At last, Ceres looked away.

“I...” She inhaled slowly. “My mother was human. My father was wolfkin. Both were executed when I was ten. Because of intolerance.” Her mouth tightened around the word. “In the capitals, people often do not care enough to act on such things. But in smaller places, they do.”

She drank, gathered herself, and continued.

“After that, I ran. Survived in the province capital. I chose a warrior path because I had strength, and because that seemed better than starving.”

Anne said nothing. The mercenary had not reached the right point yet.

“Copper tier was easy enough,” Ceres went on. “Training and fitness. The usual fighting drills. Bronze...” She gave a humorless breath of laughter. “Bronze took me ten years. Jobs for the company, wilderness fights, constant strain, taking whatever chances came. And now...” She looked into the tea for a moment. “Now twenty years have passed, and I am stuck.”

The admission came out low and controlled, which made it land all the harder.

“I know what the problem should be,” she said. “Everyone says it. Improve the energy in the body. Strengthen circulation. Learn to sense what enters and what leaves. But I have always been nearly blind to magic energy. I can barely feel it.”

By the time she reached that part, the confession itself had already steadied her. Moving from pain to training made the mercenary more comfortable again, as if talking about effort was easier than talking about grief.

Anne waited a moment before answering.

“Beastkin naturally form an energy core,” she said. “Or at least, they move toward that condition more easily. Their bodies interact with energy in a direct way. Humans, on the other hand, are not naturally magical in that sense, except for certain exceptions and specific bloodlines. Most have to build the habit through training and repeated absorption.”

Ceres listened with fierce attention now.

“In your case,” Anne continued, “I do not think you formed a true core. If you had, you would know it. But your body should not behave like a purely human one either. It should accept energy faster, though perhaps less stably, and likely in a way that does not match the standard instructions written for ordinary warriors.”

She paused, letting the thought settle.

“The key, I believe, is not merely absorbing more energy. It is learning to work with better energy quality and forcing it through the body before it dissipates. Not passively, but deliberately, and repeatedly. You need a path that matches what you are rather than what others expected you to be.”

Ceres’ eyes sharpened.

Hope, Anne concluded. Good.

Now close.

“There is a book in my collection called The Knight of Bellary,” Anne said. “It tells the story of a half-beast knight in a forgotten kingdom. More importantly, it records his struggles, methods, and the way he trained through those same inconsistencies. I think it may help you.”

Ceres leaned forward before she seemed to realize she was doing it.

“I can lend it to you,” Anne said, smiling slightly. “But not today. It is the only copy I have, and I am not prepared to lose such a book to bad weather, careless handling, or roadside accidents. I will need to copy it first. Come back tomorrow, and I will have a version ready for you.”

She did not mention price.

Not because the book was worthless. Quite the opposite. But coin was not the only way to profit from a customer.

The effect was instant.

Ceres’ smile broke open, warm and genuine, different from the polite expressions she had worn until now. She thanked Anne once, then again, then a third time in a rush that carried more feeling than dignity.

And, as Anne had expected, an agreement came of it.

“If the book helps,” Ceres said, “then I will do it. I will keep watch for books and scrolls that fit your descriptions. Especially research ones. And if I can get a courier job toward this area, I will come back myself.”

Anne moved quickly before gratitude cooled into second thoughts. She produced parchment, listed acceptable prices, described the categories of scrolls that interested her most, named the sort of sellers worth asking, and set clear limits on what should not be bought without her approval.

Then, after only the briefest pause, she placed two gold coins on the counter.

Ceres stared.

“This is for acquisition costs,” Anne said. “You will not be expected to spend your own money on my inventory.”

Trust, when displayed at the right moment, had a way of making people want to deserve it.

The mercenary hesitated only a little before accepting. That was another good sign. Refusing would have meant pride was stronger than reason. Accepting meant she understood the relationship Anne was offering. Not charity. Not debt. Business with room for mutual benefit.

By the time the last details were settled, the unloading outside had already finished. The final cart was being prepared to leave, and the mercenaries were beginning to gather themselves for the road back.

Ceres promised again that she would return within a few months if she could arrange the route properly.

Anne walked with her to the entrance.

Then, standing beneath the signless front of her not-yet-complete shop, she watched her first true customer depart with the empty carts and the fading sound of hooves.

Only when they had gone far enough that no one could hear her did Anne clasp her hands together and declare, with bright certainty,

“A satisfied customer becomes a regular. I am sure she will be back.”
