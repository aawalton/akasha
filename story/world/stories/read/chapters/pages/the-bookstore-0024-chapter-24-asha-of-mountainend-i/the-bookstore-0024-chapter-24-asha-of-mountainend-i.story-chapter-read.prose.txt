The autumn breeze moved gently through the garden of the mayor’s villa, stealing the first leaves from the trees before their time. It was too early for them to fall on their own. But then, time did not care much for what was early.

Asha knew that better than most. She had now lived through twenty-five autumns, which by elven standards meant it was time to choose a path. By human or beastkin standards, that was absurdly late. Someone of her age from those races would already have years—perhaps more than a decade—of serious training behind them. Elves were given more time, and more room to decide.

And still she had nothing.

Asha rarely felt fear. But the pressure to choose a path—a life that others could judge and compare—tightened like a knot in her chest. Deep down, she felt with stubborn certainty that her real calling had not arrived yet.

Her mother disagreed, of course. According to her, nature had already called.

It was not an entirely foolish argument. The proof looked back at Asha from every mirror. Elven blood always carried a blessing of nature, but hers had manifested more strongly than usual. Her parents both had blue eyes, though not the same kind of blue. Her father’s was linked to rivers and lakes. Her mother’s to springs. In elves, that sort of thing was less inheritance than inclination. Each one carried their own connection.

Asha’s eyes were green.

Not the soft common green of shallow growth, but a darker one, deep enough to suggest old shade, roots, and the life that rose stubbornly out of earth. Trees, plants, and wild growing. Even among elves, it marked a powerful tie.

Everyone around her treated that as certain.

Nature had not called her in the way her mother liked to claim. It had always been there. It was part of her, as inseparable as breath or temper. That was not the same as destiny. She could have chosen the easy direction and become an anthomancer. Or a herbmancer. Or a woodmancer. No one would have questioned it. In fact, most would have nodded with relief and declared it most sensible.

But Asha wanted none of them.

Her father had spent years mediating arguments between mother and daughter by blaming Asha’s stubbornness on the wildness of her blessing. There was truth in that, perhaps. Her nature was restless. It did chafe against fences, manners, and tidy expectations. But that still did not make it some outside influence. It was not something acting upon her. It was simply herself.

Even now, when annoyed or distracted, she would bend branches without meaning to. Leaves turned toward her. Vines shifted. Bark creaked in small complaint or welcome. Her anthomancy moved instinctively, not because she had embraced the elven path, but because it lived in her whether she cooperated with it or not.

That only made the situation more complicated.

Asha spent the whole morning in the garden staring at the trees and not finding answers among them.

Eventually she grew tired of her own thoughts and left.

Mountainend looked the same as ever. Quiet streets, modest shops, and the same lingering sense that the town had once expected more from the world than the world had given back.

And yet it was not the same.

Asha slowed.

She could feel it before she could name it. The people moved differently. The stores did not look richer, and the inn certainly did not look more impressive, but the rhythm of the place had changed. Faster, tighter, and more directed. As if the town had begun leaning toward something instead of just enduring itself.

She searched her memory for the cause.

Then she found it.

The bookstore.

It had only been open for a short time, but even Asha could see the effect. Adventurers, however weak, had started stopping in Mountainend because of it. Trade had stirred in small ways. Rick, of all people, had somehow become competent enough to create commotion in the market. That part still felt faintly ridiculous. Asha had known him for years. He had not seemed like the sort to improve suddenly and dramatically.

Which meant the store was doing something real.

Maybe, it could help her too.

Asha laughed under her breath.

The idea sounded childish. It was a shop, not a shrine of revelation. Whatever the bookstore sold, it sold for profit, not to guide confused young elves through what her mother dismissed as meaningless struggle.

So she pushed the thought aside and kept walking.

Not long after, a commotion near the market square caught her attention.

Most of the merchants and shop managers had clustered around the center, calling figures over one another in rising voices. At first Asha thought there had been some sort of dispute. Then she saw Rick at the center, bargaining with traders in a way she would once have found impossible to imagine.

The three adventurers were with him. The girl in robes—likely the mage. The sturdy man in poor armor. The foxkin in a simple tunic. Together they stood over the remains of a hunt, dividing work with more competence than glamour. Rick and the mage argued prices with traders, the big man weighed cuts of meat, and the foxkin gathered coin.

Asha watched long enough to understand the crux of it.

Three wolves, already processed. Wind-essence beasts, judging by the color and character of the cores. The quantity of meat would not match Rick’s earlier boar haul, but that mattered less than people thought. Earth-essence meat was heavier. Wind-essence meat lighter, and therefore more valuable by weight.

By the end of the bargaining, the three wind cores sold for twenty-four silver, and the meat for twenty-eight more. They had already set aside ten kilograms for themselves before the final sale.

Seventeen silver each. The remaining coin, apparently, was destined for drink. That last part made sense the moment the group turned toward the inn.

Asha hesitated only briefly before following.

She did not drink much herself, and certainly not with enthusiasm. Still, it was not as though the inn were forbidden ground. Besides, curiosity was a good enough excuse when one had nothing better to do and no desire to return home yet.

By the time she entered, the old place smelled of ale and dead wood. Old Saul was behind the counter pouring another drink for a man who did not seem to need it. A few tables held familiar faces, local men already drifting toward drunkenness with remarkable commitment considering the hour.

Several of them noticed her and waved her over.

Asha declined with practiced politeness and escaped before any of them mistook courtesy for interest.

She found Rick and the trio at a side table.

“Hi, Asha. You—” Rick began.

Then he forgot how to continue.

Iris rolled her eyes before she could stop herself.

Rick tried again, somehow making it worse.

“Sorry. Asha—I mean, please sit.”

That earned him a sharp look from the mage.

Asha, however, took no offense. Her appearance here was genuinely unexpected, and Rick had never been especially graceful when surprised. If anything, the awkwardness made him easier to deal with.

After greetings were exchanged, she learned the others’ names properly.

She did not hurry to her real purpose. There was etiquette to consider, and she had no intention of approaching strangers at their own table only to turn the conversation into an interrogation. So she waited until the drinks and food had been served before easing toward the subject that interested her.

“I saw you in the market,” she said. “You caused quite a commotion. Did you go hunting again?”

The question was aimed at Rick, but she spread her glance over all four of them so that any of them could answer without feeling excluded.

“Well, yes,” Rick said. “But this time I was more of a guide than a hunter.”

“You looked like a damned good hunter to me,” Iris cut in at once. “You killed one beast and captured another.”

Rick coughed and looked faintly embarrassed.

Asha hid a smile.

“Well, improving is nothing to be ashamed of,” she said. “You should be proud of it, Rick.”

Then, with studied lightness, she moved where she truly wanted.

“I also heard you’ve been going to that bookstore. I’ve been curious about it for a while. What exactly do they sell there?”

Rick brightened immediately. That, apparently, was a safer topic for him than accepting compliments.

But before he could answer, Iris leaned forward with sudden interest and interrupted him.

“We can tell you plenty about the store,” she said. Then she reached into her things and took out a purplish crystal. “But first, would you mind doing me a favor?”
