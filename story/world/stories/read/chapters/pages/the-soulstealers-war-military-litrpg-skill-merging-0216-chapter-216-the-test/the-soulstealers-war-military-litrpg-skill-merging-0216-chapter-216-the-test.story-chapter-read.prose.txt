After four weeks had passed, my thoughts centered on my lands. I hadn’t heard anything more from Cuthward, but that was to be expected. Still, I was desperate to know. Had the cultists attacked yet?

In my individual sparring session with Moonhand, on the first day of the fourth week, my spear trembled. Right now, people could be trying because of me, and I wasn’t there to help them. I would stick by my choice to tell Hinlaw to pound sand. The alternative was much worse. That certainty didn’t make me feel any better, though.

For the first round of sparring against Moonhand, my Focus was split, which wouldn’t have normally been a problem—I could afford to split my attention with my Focus stat so high—but when my mind told me to focus entirely on the people who were dying because of me, and not because of the man right in front of me with a spear, it didn’t work. Ninety percent of it went to problems I couldn’t solve at the moment.

Within a few hits, Moonhand knocked the spear out of my hand. It clattered to the ground and bounced across the sandpit.

“I would say that was a foolish mistake,” Moonhand grumbled. “However, I know you to be better than this, no matter how far you still need to grow.”

“Sir—”

“The rumours are likely true. The front line may be collapsing. But at the moment, you are not capable of changing anything. Do not worry about that, and focus on what you can change, so that one day, you may make a difference in the larger war. You are responsible for yourself, and for controlling yourself and your mind.” He paused. “You think your station demands more from you, thegn? Don’t get too full of yourself. You’re not that important yet.”

I stayed quiet. He had misinterpreted my concerns. The rumours about the front line’s troubles bothered me, of course, but that wasn’t my primary concern. We had sent some of our best out to hold the line, and I trusted Owl to do what he could.

But Moonhand was still correct in broad strokes. I was responsible for controlling my own mind, and if I didn’t succeed here, my lands would suffer anyway.

I needed to hit Moonhand.

~ ~ ~

Three days later, with a clearer mind (and more practice stowing away my anxieties to concentrate on the fight), I decided to make my attempt.

Today was the day. I couldn’t wait any longer. There might not have been much time left, and I needed to fulfill my obligations to Arnulf and Alis sooner than later.

In the afternoon, when Moonhand summoned me to the second sand pit, isolating me from the rest of the training group, I took a fighting stance almost instantly. I promised myself that I would strike Moonhand.

He usually tried to give some advice before we began, something for me to try fixing, even if it was as small as “Move your left foot back an inch.”

Today, he only said, “Hm,” then settled into a fighting stance as well. He used a training spear of his own, its blunt wooden tip facing me. It meant he wouldn’t be holding back to spare me from a strike of his powerful weapon.

“No corrections?” I asked.

“Did I give you any?”

“No.”

“Then begin.”

I couldn’t give him a chance to take initiative. Lunging forward, I kept myself guarded with my shield. His initial probes clacked off my shield, bouncing away harmlessly.

My temptation was to use an Art to enhance the speed of my attack, but I only had so much Presence, and I couldn’t afford to waste it all so early, especially when every Art cost my three points.

Right now, I have a hundred and seventy points of Presence. With my Art, that translated to about fifty-six uses of a single-well Art, or half that many for the double-well form. In my fights, I’d been mostly relying on the single well form.

I watched Moonhand’s defense. Today, he’d started low, holding his spear down, a form of the Fool’s Guard. I met him there instead of taking the bait, using the Rising Leaf style to attack low.

I matched his spear’s swipes and landed a few scrapes on his shield, then slowly drove his guard higher until I could unleash attacks on his midsection. It was there that I began using my Art.

With my attacks now coming at a slightly downward angle, I used my Art to improve their speed. When I lunged, I envisioned a horizontal enhancement dragging my arm forward, a gravity well just in front of my spear on my target, precisely where I wanted to hit.

The column tugged on it, practically wrenching my arm forward, but I maintained control. My spear cracked into Moonhand’s shield with a boom. Reverberations rolled back through the spear and rumbled in my chest, and my entire skeleton quaked.

But it wasn’t the first time I’d struck that hard, and it wouldn’t be the last. A hit on Moonhand’s shield, no matter how direct, didn’t count the same as a hit on his body. He was fast enough to protect himself. Almost like he was reading my mind, he was always ready to intercept my attacks.

Whether it was speed or something else, I had my own way to counter that.

With my Art active, I envisioned what he’d do next. I framed him in my vision, watching his spear swish back across before it even happened.

I cancelled my Art by using it again—this time, I aimed directly at Moonhand and weighed him down, impacting directly from above.

It didn’t slow him for long, but it let me pull my spear back and avoid a costly bind—or worse, disarmament.

I spun away, leapt, then triggered my Art a third time as I transitioned to the Comet Lance style. As I spun, I leapt, moving through the air, then dragged my spear down toward Moonhand with an Art. It struck with immense force, and again he protected himself with his shield.

I attacked from above, switching between three different highly offensive styles, unleashing strike after strike, all of them empowered by my Art to give them additional power. To keep Moonhand on his toes, or to prevent his counterattacks when I envisioned them coming, I sometimes used a multi-directional Art to pull on his back, dragging him closer to my spear. It didn’t actually move him—he was far stronger than that—but it did prevent him from pulling away as quickly.

But just because I could envision what he was going to do before he did it didn’t mean I could react in time.

When I pushed him to the edge of the pit, his spear blurred in front of him. I needed a different style to react to it. I used my Focus. I pushed all of it into this one moment, trying to sift through my jumble of modern memories and find the right one.

It was a jumble. No matter how high my Focus was, it couldn’t fix that mess in a matter of milliseconds. I settled on using the Falling Leaf style’s pull-back-and-let-you-opponent’s-spear swish-past method.

Wrong choice.

Moonhand caught me as I pulled back, flinging me down to the sand.

“Not good enough,” he accused. “You’re still sluggish. Switch styles. Switch your mental tracks. Think about what you’re doing.”

I wasn’t defeated yet, though.

Moonhand marched toward me, whirling his spear. “Get up. Try again.”

Instead of standing, I clenched my teeth and envisioned my memory palace. It was now or never. I had to score that hit today. I’d promised myself.

The imaginary sphere appeared above my soul. It bubbled up from the ground, forming in layers like someone was 3D printing it in my mind. It slowed at the middle section as the rings grew wider. My lungs pumped, my heart hammered. Soreness etched through them. Ignoring it, I continued.

The sphere kept losing momentum. Its elasticity kept pulling it back, trying to shrink it faster than I formed it.

And Moonhand was still approaching.

If I failed now, what would Alis think?

I could always try again—

No. I’d promised myself. I couldn’t think about pleasing others. This was about keeping my word. Duty and diligence. All of it would mean nothing if I couldn’t keep a promise.

What if I’d overestimated how far I’d come?

Even if I had, I couldn’t let that be what ruined me.

I pressed my teeth together hard enough that I worried they were about to crack. The muscles in my jaw spasmed.

Finally, the sphere finished forming.

That was just the outside of my memory palace. Like I’d opened a floodgate, a storm of imaginary iridescent sand flooded into the sphere. Each was an orb, like the Skill orbs but much smaller, that represented a memory. There were too many to count.

I had to move quickly if I wanted to make this work. I couldn’t afford to sort them all right now, but I could extract the ones I needed.

The most recent memories were near the top. Using my Focus, I sifted through them in a matter of seconds, drawing out the most recent fighting styles I’d learned. That would have to be enough.

I jumped back to my feet to dodge a plunge of Moonhand’s spear, then transitioned to the Snapping Pheasant style, a combat style that relied heavily on combining a force-based Art with a spear, and was best at counter attacks.

My spear cracked against Moonhand’s thrice, each emboldened by an Art. Finally, he leaned forward, projecting a swell of silver maegen out of his shield. An Art of his? It struck me hard in the shoulders, sending me skidding back across the sand, but I stayed upright.

Switching to my crossbow, I launched the bolt I’d save up, buying myself time and forcing Moonhand’s shield into the right position.

Then I raced forward, using an attack from the Comet Lance style, before effortlessly switching to a low undercut from the Rising Leaf style. Three straight jabs from the Aurora Wind style, each one forcing his shield lower, before finally, a finishing blow from the Comet Lance style.

It plunged down, racing between his spear and his shield, slipping in with a perfect resonance, and struck him in the chest.

He barely flinched. I didn’t pierce his tunic. Still, he glanced down at the spear, eyes satisfied.

“Excellent work, Sir Levi,” he said. He lowered his shield and spear.

His voice shifted and trembled. For a brief moment, it reached the same augmented, hidden tone as Arnulf’s. A blur rippled over his face, hiding his features temporarily. It disappeared in an instant. He said, “You have passed my test.”

He was Arnulf.

“You?” I breathed. “But—” I cursed myself silently, shaking my head. “Of course. But how?”

Moonhand knelt in front of me. “Lower your voice, and meet me immediately after dinner. Our usual spot. Do not be late.”
