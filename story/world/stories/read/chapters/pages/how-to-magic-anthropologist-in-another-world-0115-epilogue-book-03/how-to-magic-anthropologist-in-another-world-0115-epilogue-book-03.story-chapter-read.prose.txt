Two hammocks had been strung side by side beneath a canopy of two peach trees that had no business growing on an airless rock, except that Laurel really liked peaches. They had always been her favorite fruit, and they offered a link to a time before she was accidentally turned immortal. So, of course, she made it so that her new home would have peach trees.

Kurt lay in his, one arm slung over his eyes, looking like a sulking child who had been denied sweets.

"You're doing the thing again," she said, not looking up from the small silver bowl balanced on her stomach. Inside it, grains of sea salt obediently arranged and rearranged themselves into shapes at her idle whim: a castle, a sword, a girl with a bident spear.

"What thing?"

"The thing where you sulk for hours as if the universe has personally wronged you, when we have it on record in most history books that it's you who is in the habit of wronging the universe."

Kurt lowered his arm just enough to glare at his wife before his eyes softened when they landed on the planet hanging above them, vast and blue and absolutely beautiful.

"She called me a groomer," he said.

Laurel did not laugh. Through sheer two-thousand-year-old discipline, she managed not to.

"Alice?"

"Yes, Alice," he confirmed, the man had survived two apocalypses and yet somehow was acting as though this was the worst thing that had ever happened to him.

"How did you know she called you a groomer?" the Lady asked. "I don't remember sharing that detail with you, and unlike me, you can't hear when people are talking about you."

"It was in her margin notes. In a history book. About us."

"Kurt, you can't go about reading a girl's private notes," gasped Laurel.

"Hey, I had to do something selfish to balance out all the free food you have given her," Kurt justified. "If anyone is to blame, it's you, not me."

"You have a justification for everything, don't you?"

"Of course I do. That's why I resent the grooming allegations."

"She's not wrong that there's a conversation to be had."

"We were both nineteen when I met you in the past. The math works out fine."

"Grooming, from what Alice understands, carries a connotation of preparing someone for a role," Laurel argued. "Which you kind of did."

Kurt made a protesting noise.

"I was laboring under the restriction of a living myth myself and was not at liberty to inform you of everything. Telling you the truth would have been a paradox, since you didn't know about it in my time. And, well, we both know that temporal paradoxes aren't a thing that happens, not even in the Sealed Realm, meaning a convoluted thing would have had to happen for you, a being with perfect memory, to have forgotten what I told you."

"You say that," Laurel laughed, tilting the bowl so the salt-girl's little salt-spear caught the light, "as though you aren't the architect of everything, including the living myth that restricted you."

"Well, I was just asking for some grace, especially from a girl living under a myth herself."

"Living under a myth can be a dangerous thing," Laurel nodded. "I helped the rise of a religion with the premise that Faith shouldn't be defined by the whims of the Gods, and then I accidentally became the god of that godless religion. I have watched entire kingdoms shape their laws, their art, their children's names, around guessing my approval. I have lived as a story longer than I have ever lived as a woman. If anyone in the Seven Realms understands what it means to be swallowed by your own myth, husband, it is me. And for that, I will be eternally grateful to you for freeing me." She paused. "That, however, doesn't change the fact that you groomed me to save me."

"That's fair," Kurt admitted. "I guess."

"For what it's worth," Laurel said, letting the salt collapse back into formless grains with a small sigh of her will, "I think her protest is about an abstract principle of consent first and foremost, and not a deliberate attack on you."

"This is what I was complaining about." Kurt groaned and pressed both hands over his face. "I am all for consent. I am the avatar of the self, and of the right to exercise free will. The way I found you, you know, when you first recruited me, back when I was fifteen, you were a mere husk of your true self, just a manifestation of Holy Will. I would say I helped restore your consent, not deprived you of it."

"The avatar of self," laughed Laurel, "getting salty over being called a variation of selfish."

She threw a handful of salt over him.

Kurt caught the grains out of the air, their movement in the low gravity letting him catch most of them, before tossing them into his mouth.

After a few moments of silence, Laurel added, "There's real power in being trapped in a myth," quieter now, her gaze drifting back up to the slow-turning planet, to the Mythic Realm, where right now two Holy Messengers were preparing to transport a Paladin and an Inquisitor to Hano. "Provided you don't let the myth overwhelm you like it did to me."

"The real power would be in being true to oneself," Kurt nodded. "Shaping Belief rather than being shaped by Faith."

"In this, Alice might call you a groomer all she wants. She is closer in temperament to you than she is to me. This is why I am confident she will come out on top."

The festival had been going on since before midday, and by the look of the crowd gathered beneath the Great Rift Tree, it meant to go on well past dark. Drumbeats carried from near the roots, an overlapping rhythm rather than a true melody. Vendors moved between bonfires, selling skewered meat and fermented plum wine, and dancers wound through the crowd in loose, spiraling lines, their steps following the drums.

At the center of it all stood the Tree.

It was a landmass that could be spotted from dozens of miles away. Its trunk was huge, the size of a city block, its bark the color of cast bronze, its roots cresting the ground like the limbs of a leviathan, and nestled where its lowest branches met, suspended like a raw wound on reality itself, was the Rift Gate to the Soul Realm.

Above it all, and the cause of today's festivities, was a single fruit hanging from a branch as thick as a tower. It was the size of an elephant, red and plump, almost bursting. A wooden platform had been built directly beneath it, heaped with cushions soft enough to catch anything that might fall.

From the private viewing booth overlooking the square, none of that mattered much to either man seated inside it.

The father sat with one leg crossed over the other, two letters resting in his lap. The first was stamped with Hano's crossed-swords sigil, while the second bore a tree with an eye, the sigil of the Tree Gate Council.

His son sat across from him, running a polishing cloth down the length of a sword that didn't need polishing anymore. But he had always been like this, too attached to his sword, bordering on a fetish. Sometimes the father wondered where he had gone wrong with this one.

Both father and son had flawless cyan hair that marked them as pure Soulit nobility. And both of them acted like it.

The father finished reading both letters, and a smirk filled his face.

"Well?" the son asked when the silence had gone on long enough.

"Hano's leadership is inviting Tree Gate to discuss some important matters." The father turned over the letter, showing Elder Okar's own stamp.

"And the council is sending you?"

"The council is sending me."

The son let out a short laugh and went back to the sword.

"Let's hope Marina'a doesn't stab you in your sleep, old man."

"She wouldn't dare kill her own father."

"I didn't know torturing orphaned girls is what they call fatherhood these days," laughed the son. "Maybe I too can call myself a father someday."

The father didn't so much as blink. "That was three decades of successful integration ago. She's had time to make peace with it." He let out a thin, satisfied smile. "Besides, sending me is a message. It tells Hano we're willing to talk. It also tells them exactly who we're willing to talk to: General Ta'o's faction, not the Freelancer Guild."

"I'm just saying, don't count on me to act as your bodyguard when we get there."

"Why is that?"

"Isn't the Longest Night Festival happening around then too? That means fighters from across all Seven Realms will be gathering in Hano's arena." His eyes shone with anticipation. "I am not missing that for anything."

"Then prepare your luggage. We leave in the morning."

The son reached for a small brass bell resting on the booth's railing and rang it once.

A girl entered a moment later, quiet enough that she was already three steps inside before either man fully registered her arrival. She had black hair, cropped short so no one could grab it.

She had thin scars across her arms and legs, and she wore a plain tunic and a black leather collar set with glowing ember runes. Her sharp eyes were moved once around the room, cataloguing exits, before settling on the young man.

"Okain," the son said, not looking up from the sword. "Prepare our travel kit. We're going to Hano."

She bowed once, said nothing, and left exactly as quietly as she had come.

The father watched the door close behind her and sighed.

"What a pity," he said. "I bought you a beauty like that for your earthly needs, and you've gone and made her a training dummy instead."

"Have you seen her killing instinct?" The son finally looked up, something close to reverence in his voice. "It would be a waste putting her to any other use. The way she picks up combat Soulbooks without suffering from the confusion of competing instincts, I've seen people go mad with a quarter of what I've jammed into her soul."

"You really don't know how to use a woman, my son."

"I know how to use a weapon." The son shrugged. "Without her as my sparring partner, I would never have perfected my sword technique."

Below them, the drums shifted into a new rhythm, faster now, and the crowd's spiraling dance tightened toward the base of the Tree as a crack began to appear in the skin of the fruit.

The whole crowd gasped in anticipation, drawing the father and son's focus to the event as well.

A moment later, the fruit burst in half, and a man fell naked and screaming into the cushions.

"Do you recognize who that is?" asked the son.

"I think that's Lord Li'e's butler. I don't remember his name."

"Pfft," scoffed the son. "Three legendary Soul Shapers died this month, and a butler beat them to resurrection."

"That is the will of the Siphon," smirked the father.
