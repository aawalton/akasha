Chapter 130

The granary burned, and by evening the reeve and the priest were each explaining to the village how it had been the other man’s lamp. The flame is brief. The accounting of whose fault it was outlasts the harvest it took.— Codex of the Long Watch, Field Observation, 12th Century, Wessex

The archbishop’s coffee had gone cold and he had not reached for it once.

The party told the story in pieces, the four of them passing the details around the table in the order that made sense rather than the order in which they happened. Steve had laid out the theology and the history behind it, what Lykaon meant and what the sanctuary on the hill had borrowed from a mountain in Arcadia.

Donna had laid out the operation: the recruitment house, a cart of people going up the hill, the forged papers, the armed escort of fourteen men wearing tabards marked with four cuts. Sarah had described the followers and Arthur had described the killing.

Antoniou sat with his pen between his fingers, turning it end over end without writing anything down. He listened quietly to what he thought was a civic and military problem turn into something far worse.

Nikephoros had known parts of his congregation had walked out to the coast. He had not known what they had walked into. When Steve spoke about the ceremony of the lifted, and how the wolves had come from them, the archbishop’s hand closed slowly around the pectoral cross on his chest and stayed there.

“I did not think you meant the word,” Nikephoros said, without looking up from the table, “when you said fed.”

His hands were placed on the table, the heavy ring against the grain.

“Po po po,” Antoniou said, but quietly, to himself, without any of the performance the phrase usually carried.

“Papa Damianos,” Nikephoros said, shaking his head. “I ordained him. Twenty years ago, in the cathedral at Aghía Triáda, with his mother in the front pew.” He lifted one hand from the table and then placed it back down again. “When he left the parish, I’d assumed the changed world had taken his belief. It had for many.”

“He was running a congregation on that hill,” Steve said, “with structure, supply lines and a liturgy built around the Lykaion myth. That takes longer than three months to assemble.”

“The iconostasis,” Nikephoros said. “You are certain.”

“Four cuts on every face,” Donna said. “The same four cuts on the tabards the men wore.”

Nikephoros turned the coffee cup a quarter-turn on its saucer without lifting it. Arthur recognised the gesture from Antoniou.

“You asked us to handle it quietly,” Arthur said. “We could not.”

“Yes, that was unfortunate,” Nikephoros said.

“There are twenty-three people sitting in the square,” Sarah said. “Every one of them saw the compound, the communion and the screen. There is no way to keep this quiet.”

Nikephoros thought for a while and then nodded slowly. “There was not,” he said. “You brought them home. I had not thought to ask for that, and you did it anyway.”

“How many of them came back?” he said.

“Twenty-three,” Sarah said. “All did not. We brought the ones who would come. There are people still on the hill who would not leave the body.”

“Then that much can be mended.” He unclosed his hand from the cross. “The Church can gather them back. Quietly. At least I know how to do that.”

“I would rather you gathered them compassionately than just quietly,” Arthur said.

“Both can be true,” Nikephoros said, looking up at Arthur. “You have done the city a service this day but do not presume to tell me about Church business.”

Arthur did not respond. He suspected the heat in Nikephoros’s voice was more to do with the fact that twenty-three witnesses had walked down a public road into a public square and the Church now had limited options on how to handle the fallout than what he said.

Antoniou had sent for Mavros the moment the party had mentioned the cult’s recruitment house in Athens. The general arrived fifteen minutes after the archbishop.

He came in a crisp uniform and looked around the room in displeasure. He did not appreciate being the last to know about something.

“Christos,” he said to Antoniou. “Despóta.” He gave a tight nod to the archbishop. His eyes moved across the party but did not stop on any of them.

He counted you before he greeted the other two. Four armed unknowns, seated at a table with two of the three men who run this coast. He is deciding what you have sold them.

Antoniou pushed a chair out. “Sit. Coffee is coming. We have a situation that touches all factions and I wanted you here before we decide what to do about it.”

“Where is Papadakis?” the general asked.

“I stand corrected. Not all factions. I meant the three of ours,” Antoniou said.

“Summarise,” Mavros said, and sat.

Antoniou summarised it in the manner of a man who had been condensing bad news for committees his entire career, and Mavros listened without interrupting. When Antoniou finished, Mavros looked at the party for the first time as people rather than furniture.

“The fighters in the church compound,” he said. “How many.”

“Eight and the priest,” Donna said.

“Levels.”

“Mixed. The strongest was eighteen. Two were casters. All had techniques.”

“And the four of you cleared nine.”

“Yes,” Arthur said matter-of-factly.

Mavros absorbed this without expression and turned back to Antoniou. “The papers.”

The party had found a number of papers in the church that were requisition orders for men and material. Steve had kept two as samples. These were given over to Antoniou while the party told the story of the hill.

“Ela, this is the piece that touches you, Dimitri,” Antoniou said. He pulled his reading glasses down from his hair and picked up a sheet from the table. “Real Council requisition papers. A real stamp. The cult used them to move people through our own checkpoints without being stopped.”

“Someone in the administration was providing authentic papers for a cart full of people being driven to a hill to die,” Donna said. Though her voice was flat, the sentence carried its own weight.

The silence in the room had a different quality now. Arthur watched Antoniou set the sheet down and flatten it with his palm while the other two Council members watched him.

“You are telling me,” Mavros said, “that a clerk in your building sold authenticated transit papers to a death cult.”

“I am telling you that someone with access to the seal produced papers we cannot distinguish from our own,” Antoniou said. “It could be someone here or someone in your camp. I will find out who, and I will deal with it.”

“You will deal with it.” Mavros repeated the words with venom. “The way you dealt with the wolves on that hill. The way you dealt with the settlement at Vari.”

“Be careful, Dimitri,” Nikephoros said.

“I am being careful.” Mavros turned to Nikephoros. “Your clergy built a cult that fed people to a dungeon. His administration printed the tickets, it cannot be someone from my camp. In both cases the institution responsible discovered the problem when four foreign civilians walked into this room and told them about it.” He gestured towards the party. “How long had Papa Damianos been operating?”

“Months,” Steve said. “The names carved into the sanctuary wall were in the hundreds. The compound infrastructure was not built in a week.”

“Hundreds,” Mavros said, and let the word sit in the air. “Hundreds of people walked out of this city to a hill south-east of the harbour, and neither the Church nor the Council noticed they were gone.”

“A shepherd who has never lost a sheep has never had a flock worth counting. I will answer to God for the men I ordained. You may answer to Him for the seals you could not keep safe.” He looked tired for the first time. “We are each of us explaining how the fire must have been set by the man’s lamp, General. The village does not care whose lamp it was. The village wants to know the granary will be rebuilt.”

“The Church knew its clergy were leaving. I told these people as much, at the Vassilou house, weeks ago, and I asked them to look into it quietly, otherwise I risked destroying what little institutional trust this coast has left.”

“And your instinct was to hide it.”

“My instinct was to solve it before it became the only thing anyone talked about instead of the walls, the water and the food.” Nikephoros looked at Mavros steadily. “Your instinct, General, would have been the same.”

“I have asked this room for eyes on the coast since the spring. I was told there would be a survey.”

“You were told there would be a survey,” Antoniou said, “because the last time this Council moved fast on a rumour, it burned a warehouse full of grain for no good reason. Fast is not free, Dimitri.”

“Neither is slow. Slow has a longer bill. You just do not get it all at once.”

Antoniou lifted the pen from the table and then slammed it back down. “We are not here to decide whose house leaks worse. We are here to decide what happens next.”

“What happens next is obvious,” Mavros said. “I will get the details of this recruitment house and I will pull it apart. You find the clerk who sold the papers and you make an example of him, publicly, so that every other man in this building with a seal understands the cost.”

“On that we agree,” Antoniou said.

Nikephoros inclined his head without hesitating. “The parishes will cooperate. Whatever cult cells remain in the city, I will help you find them.”

Arthur felt the room settle. The three men arrived at the same conclusion from three directions and found it tolerable. It was a decision between men who had all been wounded in the same exchange and had no clean ground to throw stones from.

The cult was a security threat and security threats required action, not votes. The politics would continue long after the party left this building.

“Four mercenaries,” Mavros said, leaning back. He was not finished. “I want to be clear about what I have just heard. A cult fed people to a monster for months, inside a perimeter I am responsible for defending, and the first I hear about any of it is today, after the Civil Council made a unilateral decision to offload Kastella to four mercenaries.”

“We had a deal,” Arthur said. “And we are not mercenaries.”

“Don’t think I’m not grateful. If you had not done it, the cult would still be going strong because nobody in this room had the information or the will to act on it.” Mavros looked at Antoniou. “That is the problem I have raised at every Council session. The Council reacts. It does not look ahead. It does not send scouts. It does not invest in information. It waits for the blood to arrive at the door and then it argues about which mop to use.”

“I do not like Council business being handed off to foreigners who do not hold allegiance to the city, and I do not like my jurisdiction being invaded. Regardless of the motive.”

Antoniou took his glasses off and rubbed the bridge of his nose. “Noted, Dimitri. And I am sure the minutes will reflect your position, as they have reflected it every Thursday since the change.”

“The minutes can reflect whatever they like,” Mavros said. “The dead at Kastella did not read them.”

Arthur registered the sudden pivot. Mavros had turned a clean security consensus into a broader indictment without breaking the agreement. The cult cells would be found. The clerk would be caught. The three houses would cooperate on that much. But Mavros would stand in the next session and tell the full Council that the machinery of government had failed, and he would have the party’s report as evidence.

The door opened again and the clerk leaned through.

“Petros Vassilou is here,” the clerk said. “He is asking after his sister.”

“Tell him to wait,” Antoniou said.

Through the half-open door Arthur could see Petros standing in the corridor with his hands in his jacket pockets, rocking on his heels. Petros had found the recruitment house, walked into the compound twice, sat through the communion and brought back the intelligence that made everything after it possible. And nobody but the party knew about that.

The clerk pulled the door shut.

“I also wanted to add that it was Petros who made everything possible. Without his reconnaissance none of this would have been possible,” Arthur said.

Antoniou nodded and made a note in a small diary. Arthur saw Mavros making a note as well.

“We should let you work,” Arthur said, and stood. “You know where to find us.”

Antoniou rose and came round the table with his hand out. “I will have the paper we discussed delivered at the house tomorrow. You have done this coast a service, again.”

“And my ledger?” Steve asked, looking at Antoniou.

Antoniou stared at him for a moment and then laughed. “Po po po. We just talked about a communion with human flesh, and he wants to know about his notebook.”

“Priorities,” Donna said.

Nikephoros touched Sarah’s arm as she passed. “The followers,” he said. “I will see that they are fed and housed. That is the least of what is owed.”

“Thank you, Despóta,” Sarah said.

Mavros did not stand or offer his hand. He watched them go with the appraisal of a man filing four names in a place he kept for dangerous things.

“Home,” Donna said. “We have a boat trip to plan.”

THE ETHER ATLAS: FIELD NOTES OF STEVEN PARR / Piraeus / THE EMPTY SANCTUARY

I have written “cult abandoned, c. —” on more reports than I can count. It is an archaeologist’s shorthand, a line drawn under a site where the material record has thinned to nothing and the people who built it are presumed to have walked away. I filed it as an ending. The sanctuary emptied, the belief died, the world moved on. I built a career on reading the residue of things that stopped.

This morning a congregation failed to disperse when its god was proven to be a dead wolf, and in a room full of men who should have known better I learned that the belief has been spreading inside the city for months.

The precedent is not obscure. William Miller told his followers Christ would return on the 22nd of October, 1844. Christ as we know did not return. But the movement did not collapse. It grew, adjusted the date, adjusted the doctrine and became the Seventh-day Adventists.

Sabbatai Zevi was proclaimed the Jewish Messiah, apostasised to Islam under Ottoman pressure in 1666, and his followers concluded that the apostasy was itself divine.

Leon Festinger watched a doomsday cult survive its failed prophecy in 1956 and published the mechanism: when a belief is held deeply enough and shared publicly enough, the failure of the evidence does not kill it. The believer rationalises, not because he is stupid but because the cost of being wrong is larger than the cost of being irrational.

I should note the honest counter-case. The Xhosa cattle-killing of 1856–57 in South Africa, when the prophet Nongqawuse told her people to slaughter their herds and destroy their grain stores and the ancestors would drive the British into the sea. They did. The ancestors did not. Forty thousand people starved. The belief ended, in famine and colonial dispossession, and it ended because the cost of being irrational finally exceeded the cost of being wrong. Falsification can kill a belief. It is simply not where the odds point.

✎ I have written “cult abandoned” on a hundred reports and never once asked where the congregation went. The empty sanctuary is not an ending. It is the last place the belief let me see it.
