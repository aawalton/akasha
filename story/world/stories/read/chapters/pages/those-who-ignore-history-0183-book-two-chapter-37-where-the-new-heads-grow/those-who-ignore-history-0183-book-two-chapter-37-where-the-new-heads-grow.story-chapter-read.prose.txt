I awoke to the soft rustle of curtains and the faint hum of mana from the lamps. The air in the room smelled faintly of lavender and smoke—burned paper, I realized, as I flexed my fingers. My right hand was wrapped in white bandages. The gauze was clean, but the ache beneath it pulsed like a heartbeat.

Cordelia sat in a wooden chair beside my bed, her posture straight, her expression drawn tight between exhaustion and relief. Beside her was Fallias. She was leaning forward, elbows on her knees, her fingers laced together as if in prayer. Her hair was unkempt, one braid half undone, and there were dark circles beneath her eyes. She must’ve stayed the whole night.

By the window, Fractal rested in her bird form, feathers dimmed to a muted violet-blue. She was silent, though her head tilted toward me as if sensing the shift in my breathing. Basoriel’s little canvas satchel lay on the desk, the drawstring loosened. His beady eyes blinked up from inside the bag, and when he saw me stir, he let out a single bright chirp—sharp and cheerful, utterly out of place in the quiet.

I groaned softly and turned toward them. “I… went off the deep end, didn’t I.” It wasn’t a question. The words came out flat, drained of protest. I knew what I had done.

Cordelia’s sigh was slow, measured. “Alex,” she said quietly, her voice carrying the kind of tiredness that came from worry, not judgment. “Barbatos told me what you went through.”

I met her eyes briefly, then looked away. I could still feel the phantom echo of the mirror shattering beneath my hand.

She stood and placed a hand over her heart. “Your mother hasn’t manipulated your memories,” she said solemnly. “I swear upon my powers that be, to the Maiden of Law who governs this land, and the Archons above her. What I say is true.”

The oath took hold immediately. The air around her shimmered with faint golden motes, like fireflies in water. They drifted outward, weightless, before dissolving into nothing. The room felt cleaner for a heartbeat, and then emptier.

I exhaled through my nose, long and shaky. “Thanks…” I murmured. The word felt too small, too polite for what churned in me. I pressed my bandaged hand against my chest, as if I could still the pounding there.

Fallias shifted closer. She didn’t speak yet—just reached out, her fingers brushing mine. The touch was gentle, hesitant, as though she was afraid I might pull away. I didn’t. Her hand was warm, grounding.

“But that isn’t what worries me,” I said quietly, staring at the space between our hands. “She’s strong. Powerful. Incredible. Everyone in Marr knows her name. Juliet Duarte—the Septuplicator, the woman who built entire armies from scratch, the one who taught the nation how to win wars without ever drawing a sword.” I laughed weakly. “She’s practically a saint.”

Fallias’ thumb traced the edge of my knuckles, wordless comfort.

“I’m scared,” I continued, my voice breaking at the edge. “I’m scared to be… her shadow. To be forgotten. Alone. To spend my life trying to match her and never quite touching the same light.”

Cordelia said nothing. She knew when to speak and when to let silence do the work.

“I’m just…” I swallowed. My throat felt raw. “I’m a scared child, terrified that I’ll never live up to my mother and father’s expectations.” The confession came out softly, almost lost to the hum of the room. But saying it out loud—hearing it—made something inside me crumble and breathe all at once.

Fallias squeezed my hand gently. “Alex,” she said, her tone low and steady, “you don’t have to be them.”

I looked up, meeting her eyes. She wasn’t pitying me. Her gaze was fierce, unwavering, the kind of gaze that refused to let me drown in myself.

“I know,” I whispered. “But I was raised in their shadow. Every time I think I’ve found my own path, I realize it’s one they paved long before I got there. My mother was the mind. My father was the luck. Together they shaped everything I grew up in. And me? I just…” I paused. “I just learned to follow orders and keep quiet.”

Cordelia crossed her arms and leaned against the wall. “You think following orders is all you’ve ever done?” she asked. “You’re sitting here now because you refused to accept what the mirror showed you. That’s not obedience, Alex.”

Her words lingered.

Fallias’ hand found my cheek, soft and trembling. “You’re not her shadow,” she said. “You’re her reflection—the part of her that learned mercy, not control.”

I closed my eyes at that. It hurt, the way truth always does when it brushes too close to the bone.

Fractal gave a low, melodic chirp, her feathers brightening faintly. The sound reminded me that I wasn’t alone, even if loneliness was what I had been trained to wear like armor.

I let out a breath that felt like it had been waiting years to escape. “Maybe,” I said finally, “I’ll never know who I would’ve been without her hand shaping me.”

Fallias smiled faintly, though her eyes shimmered with tears she refused to let fall. “Then start now. Shape yourself.”

“Fally.” I said her name softly, the nickname from the dream clinging to my tongue like something sacred and half-forbidden. “Do you think I don’t know that?” My voice cracked halfway through. “The problem is… how do I shape myself? How do you shape something that was already carved before it ever learned to breathe?”

The question hung in the air like smoke—fragile, heavy, impossible.

That was when the door opened with a faint click. Barbatos stepped in, the scent of iron and spice following her. She didn’t look at me immediately. Instead, she tossed something onto the bed—a slab of raw meat, wrapped in a strip of waxed paper. It hit the blanket with a dull, wet sound.

“Start now,” she said simply, her feline tail swishing once behind her. “You asked me once if you should change. If you should become the Blade Rabbit instead of the Almiraj you are now.” Her sharp teeth glinted faintly as she smiled. “This is step one.”

I stared at the meat, then at her. Her golden eyes met mine, steady, unreadable, almost challenging.

I shook my head. “No.”

Barbatos raised an eyebrow, the faintest curl of amusement tugging at her lips. “No?”

“I’m not going to become a rabbit,” I said, voice firm now, the fatigue gone. I sat up, hand tightening on the blanket. “I’ve been prey long enough.”

Fallias tilted her head, watching me carefully, her expression torn between worry and curiosity.

Barbatos folded her arms. “Then what will you become, little Duarte? What creature do you think carries your soul?”

I met her gaze fully then. “Something much more feared.”

The words came slowly, as if I were tasting them, as if saying them made them real. My heart hammered in my chest, but it wasn’t fear—it was clarity.

I turned to Fallias. “Fally,” I said again, quieter this time. “You’re a dragon. Tell me, what do dragons fear?”

Her brow furrowed. For a moment, she seemed taken aback by the question. Then her gaze softened, contemplative. She leaned closer, her breath brushing my cheek. “We fear only what cannot die,” she whispered.

Her lips ghosted against my ear, the words sinking into me like a spark into oil.

I nodded slowly, the idea igniting. “Yeah,” I murmured. “I’m scared of that too.”

The silence that followed was long, pulsing with meaning. Even Barbatos seemed to pause, her tail stilling mid-sway.

“But I think you’re right,” I continued. “While it’s a path my father set up for me, I can make it mine. My way.” I turned back to Barbatos, the exhaustion in me transmuting—becoming something sharper, alive, something almost dangerous.

Her eyes narrowed in recognition. “And what path is that, Alexander?”

I stood, despite the bandages on my hand, despite the tremor still in my knees. The mana lamp behind me flickered, throwing my shadow tall against the wall, its edges split and fracturing like the heads of something with too many faces.

“I’m going to become a hydra.”

Barbatos blinked once, slowly, but she didn’t laugh. She didn’t mock. Her ears twitched back slightly—a sign of respect, or maybe wariness. “A hydra,” she repeated, almost tasting the word. “The beast that devours its own death.”

I nodded. “Every time someone cuts off its head, it grows two more. Every defeat is a lesson. Every loss becomes strength. That’s what I want. To stop running. To stop being prey. To stop being scared.”

Fallias reached for me, her fingers brushing mine. Her voice trembled when she spoke. “Alex… do you understand what you’re saying? The hydra’s gift isn’t survival—it’s rebirth through pain. It never truly heals. It just endures.”

“I know.” I looked down at my bandaged hand, at the thin stain of red that had seeped through the gauze. “I’ve been enduring my whole life. At least now, I’ll choose it. I’ll choose what I become.”

Fractal stirred at the window, her feathers brightening with the pulse of a thousand colors, like oil over firelight. She let out a soft hum, as if recognizing the shift in me, as if giving silent approval.

Barbatos stepped closer, her tone low. “Then understand this, Alexander Duarte: the hydra is not a creature of peace. It is not a symbol of mercy. It thrives in chaos. Its strength lies in its refusal to die, no matter the agony. That is the path you claim.”

Her words were both warning and blessing.

“I understand,” I said. “I don’t want peace anymore. I want power enough to never need it handed to me again.”

Barbatos smiled—thin, feral, proud. “Then you’ve finally chosen something worth bleeding for.”

Fallias looked at me, her eyes searching mine for the boy she’d known before. Maybe she saw traces of him still, but also something else rising beneath. Something harder, stranger. Something that burned from the inside out.

She exhaled shakily and whispered, “Then I’ll walk beside you. Even if it means walking through fire.”

I reached up and brushed her cheek with my thumb. “You already are.”

The room fell quiet again. Even the hum of mana seemed to recede. For a brief moment, everything—the ache in my hand, the echoes of the shattered mirror, the memory of my mother’s shadow—faded into stillness.

And in that stillness, I could hear it. The faint, rhythmic pulse of something ancient stirring beneath my ribs. A heartbeat with too many echoes.

I looked down at the bandages again, then past them, toward the reflection in the cracked glass across the room. The broken shards caught my image in fragments—eyes here, mouth there, pieces that didn’t quite align.

A smile tugged at the corner of my mouth.

“Good,” I whispered to the reflection. “Let me stay broken. That’s where the new heads grow.”
