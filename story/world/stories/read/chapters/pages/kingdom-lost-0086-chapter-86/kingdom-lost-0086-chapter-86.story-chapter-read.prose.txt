CHAPTER 86

The road back was long enough and the horses set their own pace.

Riley did not push them, a quiet moment was called for. The loaded animals behind her had enough to carry and she had enough to think about. The trees pressed close as the horses weaved on no defined path back, the afternoon light coming through in pale strips adding to the ambiance. Somewhere ahead the settlement was waiting with its timers and its decisions and its people who needed face time she wasn't yet ready to give.

She let the distance stay between her and all of it for a little longer.

Thorne rode beside her without speaking. The locked chest was secured to the saddle of one of the horses behind them, wrapped in canvas, another item recovered from the raiders compound, unremarkable among the other loaded supplies. The history book was tucked in one saddlebag. The Corvessa scroll sat in another. The whip rested on her hip.

She was not thinking about Valrik.

She was thinking about the soldiers she had sent into that basement one at a time.

The hospital near the base had saved soldiers before. She knew that. After the raid on the settlement the wounded or dead had been carried there within the hour, some of them within minutes. The system's healing time was proved through having the injured and dead in the hospital and the timer running in short order. But this raid was different. The dead had been loaded onto horses, transported for hours, carried through the gate and through the settlement to the hospital beds that waited for them. Hours between the moment they fell and the moment the healing timer started.

Would that matter?

She turned it over the way she turned over everything she didn't have an answer to, looking at it from different angles, mulling it over. In the game the soldiers had materialized instantly from the field to the hospital bed. That had never happened here. Nothing had ever worked exactly the way the game said it should. So the hospital timer running right now might be doing exactly what it was supposed to do, or it might be running against a clock she didn't know existed, measuring something in a HUD that didn't always provide answers.

What if it was days? What if the Clawborn met them in open ground and the nearest bed was a three day ride away? What if there wasn't much of a body to bring back?

She did not have an answer for that either. She filed it next to everything else she didn't have answers for and looked ahead.

The existential thread pulled tighter. She had sent soldiers into that basement, one at a time. To trigger traps that might kill them because it was more efficient with a preserved limited downside, than finding another way. She had made that calculation without flinching and she had been right to make it, tactically, and she knew that, and it still sat in her chest like something that had not finished with her yet.

She looked at Thorne.

He was watching the trees in the distance, one hand loose on the reins, the other resting on his thigh. His posture was easy, unhurried, the posture of someone who had ridden long roads before and knew how to carry himself through them. He had not been spawned from her building. He had not materialized from a timer or a training queue, that she knew of. He had woken up human on the floor of her tower and looked at her with eyes that already knew her name.

She remembered traveling to Hoshin. The way he had started to say something and then the wolf had come out of the brush and the conversation never finished.

"Thorne."

He turned his head.

"On the way to Hoshin," she said. "You started to tell me something. About the tower. About the kingdom before mine. We got interrupted."

He was quiet for a moment. Not evasive. Just considering.

"I remember," he said.

"Tell me about your family," she said. "Before all of this."

He looked forward again.

"My father was strong," he said. "Not in the way soldiers are strong. In the way men are strong when they have decided what they stand for and will not be moved from it." He paused. "My mother was loving. She made a home out of whatever we had, which was not always much. We worked hard. I remember being tired as a child more often than not." The corner of his mouth moved. "But I remember happy moments too. Family together. Meals that were simple but felt like important moments, of sharing more than just the food."

Riley listened. She did not fill the space after he stopped speaking.

"They are gone," he said. Not sadly, just stated factually.

She nodded once.

They rode for a while in silence. The loaded horse behind them shifted its weight on a dip in the road and the canvas over the chest rustled and settled.

"What was your biggest disappointment?" she asked.

He did not hesitate.

"The fall of the last kingdom," he said. "I was there and I served as its hero, its protector." He exhaled through his nose. "But it fell, regardless."

He straightened slightly in the saddle.

"That will not happen again."

Not a vow spoken to her. Just a statement of fact, the same way he had said his parents were gone. The moment had shifted and fell to a silence between them.

Riley looked ahead and let his answers sit in her mind and just felt the weight of them. He had a father who stood for something. A mother who made a home. A childhood with tired legs and simple meals with happy moments. He had loved a kingdom and watched it fall and carried that with him.

She thought about her soldiers in the bunkhouse when they rested. She had built rest periods into their schedule without thinking about what rest meant to them. She had never once thought about what they did with it.

She kept thinking about it.

Thorne shifted in his saddle. She felt his attention move before he spoke.

"What do you think is in the chest?" he asked.

She glanced back at the canvas-wrapped shape behind them.

"Not a clue," she said.

"I got a strange feeling from it," he said. "When I picked it up."

She looked at him. His expression was even, not troubled exactly, but attentive in the way it got when something had just registered as a feeling and nothing to back that feeling up.

"What kind of feeling?" she asked.

He thought about it for a moment.

"Familiar," he said.

Then they rode in silence.

The barracks door opened and the soldiers came out in a line.

Nikola counted them as they emerged, the way he counted everything, automatically, without needing to decide to do it. Newly trained, newly armed, the particular straightness of men who had not yet learned to carry their weapons as though they were part of them. They would learn. They always did.

He moved along the line, checking equipment, adjusting a shield strap here, a sword belt there, asking brief questions that the soldiers answered with the clipped certainty of men who wanted to prove they were ready.

They were going to Hoshin. Reinforcement for the escort. The Hoshin people needed soldiers they could see, soldiers standing between them and the tree line, soldiers who showed up every morning and did not disappear.

Nikola believed in visible things. Numbers on a ledger and soldiers in a line. Walls that stood where not that long ago there had been open ground. These were the things that projected facts to frightened people more than the weak claims of a speech did.

He was halfway down the line when he became aware of someone waiting at the edge of the yard.

Nami stood with hands clasped in front of her, watching the soldiers with an expression that was not fear exactly but a close cousin. She was not looking at the soldiers the way the Hoshin children looked at them, with wide eyes and half-hidden fascination. She was looking at them the way a person looks at something they are trying to figure out.

Nikola finished with the soldier in front of him and walked toward her.

"Nami," he said.

She looked at him directly, which was how she handled things. She did not look away when she was uncertain, she leaned in.

"The hospital," she said. "How many?"

"Enough that we are glad we built it," Nikola said.

She absorbed that. Her jaw tightened once.

"Will they recover?"

"The system works," he said. "Riley started the timer herself."

Nami looked toward the hospital building across the yard. Its door was closed. Two soldiers stood outside it, not guarding, more just present, the way people stood near things that mattered.

"She was still out there and she was already managing this," Nami said. It was not quite a question.

"That is how it needed to be done," Nikola said.

Nami was quiet for a moment. The line of soldiers behind Nikola shifted slightly as equipment was redistributed, the sound of buckles and boot leather carrying across the yard.

"When the Clawborn come to these gates," Nami said, "what happens to my daughter?"

Nikola did not answer immediately. He had learned that immediate answers to questions like this one cost more than they helped.

"The army grows every day," he said. "The walls are stronger than they were before and provide safety. Our infrastructure is growing as our community changes."

"The Clawborn are probably growing stronger too," Nami said.

"Maybe," Nikola agreed. "Hard to say."

They both looked at each other.

"I have done the work you assigned me," she said. "The mending. The small things." She paused. "That is not enough. Not for me."

Nikola studied her face. She was not complaining or looking for recognition.

"You are right," he said. "It isn't."

She blinked, expecting more resistance.

"The Hoshin who came with you," he said, "they are frightened. They see fewer soldiers in the yard, they know the hospital is full." He held her gaze. "Your people need your strength, not mending, today."

Nami was still.

"Riley is coming back today," he said. "With news. People will feel better knowing what is going on, and news from Riley will help. But until she is back, give your people the faith you have in this place. Give them hope. When she is back, you will have your conversation with her directly. I will make sure of it."

Nami looked at him for a long moment. Then she unclasped her hands.

"I can do that," she said.

She turned and walked back across the yard toward the bunkhouses where the Hoshin families moved like they were walking on eggshells.

Nikola watched her go. Then he turned back to his line of soldiers.

He had barely resumed his count when the sound reached the yard before the sight did. The low rhythm of hooves on packed earth, steady and unhurried, coming from the direction of the gate road.

The gate opened.

Riley rode through first, then Thorne, the loaded horses following in a line behind them. She scanned the yard, taking inventory before she had dismounted, already assessing, already thinking three steps ahead of this moment.

Nikola straightened.

Across the yard, near the bunkhouse door, Nami had stopped walking. She stood watching Riley ride in, and whatever she saw in that moment seemed to settle something in her, because her shoulders came down a fraction and she stayed where she was, watching, until Riley's horse slowed to a stop in the center of the yard.
