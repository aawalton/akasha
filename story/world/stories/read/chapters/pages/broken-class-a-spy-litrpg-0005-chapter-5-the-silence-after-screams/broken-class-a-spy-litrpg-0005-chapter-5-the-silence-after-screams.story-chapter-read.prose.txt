Arthur quickly learned that seeing a Guardian up close was harder than infiltrating a military base. They didn’t walk among the slaves. Their pristine armor and unnatural powers made them allergic to mud, sweat, and suffering. From a distance, they moved like gods. Detached. Disdainful.

So he asked his father during one of their shifts.

“They’ve got strength beyond imagining,” Paul said. “Faster than the eye can follow. Some use magic. Lifting tons of rock like it’s air, healing cuts, hurling fire or lightning. They don’t hide it. They show off. Just not around us.”

It fit with what Arthur suspected. Power in plain sight, cruelty just out of reach.

Still, he hadn’t yet seen one in action. And part of him was glad.

In the meantime, Arthur and Paul kept their heads down and their production up - six carts of ore each day. Arthur refined his approach: pacing breaks to avoid collapse, rotating mining spots to avoid depleted veins, watching others and adjusting. No heroics. No flash. Just quiet, brutal efficiency.

They survived.

On the fifth day, that changed.

Arthur saw Rajak.

He didn’t need an introduction. The man strolled across the ridge above the quarry like a bored king, his chest bare, white hair cropped close to the scalp. Seven and a half feet tall, muscles carved like marble, eyes gleaming with cruelty. He looked like a Viking warlord who’d eaten his own army. He sat on a boulder like it belonged to him - one knee up, elbows resting, relaxed as a man watching sheep graze.

The slaves below were pushing carts uphill. Arthur among them. Heads down. Breathing dust. No one needed to look up. Everyone already knew.

Rajak reached beside him and picked up a stone. Not a boulder, just a rounded, jagged hunk of quarry rock, the size of a fist.

He weighed it. Tossed it once in the air. Then snapped it toward the slope with a flick of his arm.

The rock screamed through the air and struck a man who had paused to rest, slumping briefly against his cart. Bone shattered. The body dropped. A twitch, then stillness.

Rajak picked up another.

This time it hit a woman who had paused for just a second too long to catch her breath, her shoulders slumping under the weight of exhaustion. The stone cracked against her arm with a sound like breaking planks. She crumpled, crying out, clutching the shattered limb but still breathing, using her other arm to crawl forward through the dust.

Arthur flinched but didn’t stop. His father whispered, “Eyes down. Keep moving.”

A third stone shattered a man’s kneecap as he leaned too long on his cart, sending him screaming to the ground.

Rajak didn’t throw again.

The line of slaves surged forward with sudden energy, everyone moving faster, backs straighter, carts grinding louder.

Rajak watched them for a moment, then exhaled a long, bored sigh.

He stood, brushed dust from his hands, and walked off without a word.

The carts kept rolling.

But the memory of that day clung to them like dust.

The next few days passed in a fog of tension. They kept to their work. Met the quota. No praise. No smiles. Just dust and aching limbs and the ticking clock of the moon’s cycle.

Finally, the day came. The quota period ended. Paul was hopeful. Mary clung to that hope like it could hold her up.

They’d done it.

At dawn, every slave was ordered to the outer yard near the gates, where monsters had once clawed at stone.

The Guardians lined the edges like statues. Silent. Polished. Watching.

Rajak stood in the center, arms folded, towering over everyone.

One by one, he called out the names of those who would be cast out, exiled from the safety of stone into open death.

No explanation. No mercy. Just names.

Each one dragged forward. Some begged. Some cried. Some went silent.

Then he said: “Arthur. Paul.”

Arthur froze. His name rang like a bell in his skull.

Paul’s breath hitched beside him. He took a step forward, stunned. “We met quota,” he muttered, more to himself than anyone else.

Rajak nodded slightly. "I remember. You were the one who looked up."

Paul stepped in before Arthur could speak. "It was my fault," he said quickly. "I was too slow with the cart. He only looked up to help me. That’s all."

Rajak tilted his head, smiling faintly. "So now you’re giving me context?"

Paul didn’t flinch. "If someone had to pay, it should’ve been me."

Rajak’s smile widened.

Without warning, he brought his boot down hard on Paul’s knee. The crack echoed. Paul collapsed with a grunt of pain, clutching his leg.

Arthur didn’t move. Didn’t fight. Resistance meant death, and he needed to live.

He dropped beside his father and helped him upright.

A shadow moved beside them. Mary.

She slid under Paul’s other arm without a word.

Arthur stared. "Mom... you don’t have to."

She looked at him, eyes steady. "This isn’t my place anymore," she said softly. "Where you go, I go."

Together, they walked toward the open gate.

No one stopped them.

No one looked away.

As they trudged toward the horizon, Arthur scanned the broken landscape with a soldier’s eye. Even half-starved, his instincts worked overtime. Mapping terrain, judging angles, calculating cover.

Then he saw it.

A narrow split in the foothills. Deep. Jagged. The kind of place a cart couldn’t reach and a monster might overlook. A crevice carved by erosion or mercy - either would do.

“There,” he said, pointing. “That gap in the rock. If we can make it before sunset, we might stand a chance.”

They moved as fast as Paul’s shattered leg allowed. Every step was agony. Every breath harder.

They were fifty yards from safety when the predator found them.

It was massive. Tiger-like with matted fur, too many eyes, and a second jaw protruding from its neck. A chimera, maybe. But worse. A hunter built to enjoy the kill.

It didn’t charge.

It watched.

Waiting.

Tails twitching.

Playing.

Arthur realized the truth too late. But Mary and Paul already knew.

“Run,” Paul said. “Get to the crevice.”

“We’ll distract it,” Mary whispered. “You have to live.”

He didn’t argue.

Not because he didn’t love them. But because he did.

Because they were right.

He ran.

And from the shadows of the crevice, he listened.

He heard them scream.

He heard them fight.

He heard them die.

And for the first time in two lifetimes, Arthur cried.

Not from pain.

Not from fear.

From loss.From knowing he couldn’t go back.

When silence fell again, he whispered a promise into the dark:

Rajak dies. So does the beast.
