Chapter 57

"Every negotiation has a moment where both parties realise the terms have changed. The good ones adapt. The others reach for weapons." - Codex of the Long Watch, Field Observation

For all his faults, the Baron did set a good table.

Real plates and metal cutlery. Grilled fish laid across a bed of capers and tomatoes, bread baked that morning. The Baron opened a bottle of wine with a corkscrew he produced from his shirt pocket.

The courtyard was lit with oil lamps. The queue along the eastern wall was empty. The door to Sarah's treatment room was closed. Four of the Baron's fighters stood at the compound's corners, visible but not looming. The Baron understood staging.

"Eat," the Baron said with a smile "Then we talk. That is the civilised order, my friends."

The fish was good. Arthur hadn't tasted anything that wasn't dried meat or camp rations in two weeks, and the first bite of grilled grouper with capers took his memories back to a world that still made sense.

Archie's geometry hovered behind Arthur's shoulder, dim, observing.

The Baron poured wine and drank first. The host drinks before the guest, an old smuggler's protocol to prove the bottle isn't poisoned.

They ate in appreciative silence for a while. Fresh food in a world coming apart at its seams was a luxury.

"So," the Baron said finally, setting his glass down. "Tell me about this architecture."

Arthur told him about the Grid, the nodes, the transit chambers, how the monuments connected to infrastructure beneath the surface that predated every civilisation humans could name. He left out the Curators and the Seeders, the Fence, the endgame.

He told the Baron what a man who controlled territory around a Sanctum Zone needed to know. The monuments worked as amplifiers. Activation spiked the Ether yield, made absorption faster and more stable, and only they, as far as they knew, held the key to the sequence.

The Baron listened with the attention of a man calculating return on investment in real time.

"The activation at Ħaġar Qim," he said. "Everyone on this island felt the change. The creatures are denser. The Ether is thicker. My fighters are levelling faster." He leaned back. "That was you?"

"That was the node responding to the activation sequence, yes."

"Which you performed." The Baron smiled. "And you can do this at other sites?"

"Mnajdra is next. There may be others."

"You know more than 'may be,' my friend. How? You arrived on this island two weeks into the end of the world. You are not a government. You are not a military. You are three people from London with weapons that glow and a floating shape that watches me while I eat. How do you know where to go and what to do when you get there?"

Arthur looked at Archie's geometry. "Show him."

Archie brightened. The facets snapped from dim observation to full operational luminescence, the rotation accelerating. The blue-white light cast geometric shadows across the tablecloth.

The Baron's wine glass stopped halfway to his mouth.

"Autonomous Relay, Cognitive Interface Hub," Archie said. "Designation AR-C1H1-E. Planetary Guidance Subsystem, Earth Assignment."

The Baron set his glass down. His eyes hadn't left Archie's geometry. The charm was gone. His fingers were still on the stem of his glass and he hadn't blinked.

"An artificial intelligence," the Baron said. "How did you get it?"

"He attached himself to us," Arthur said with a smile. "We didn't get a choice."

"How can I get one?"

"You seem to be under the impression that Arthur installed me," Archie said. "Even worse, you appear to believe I could be downloaded off a website. I predate your species' oldest surviving institution by approximately thirty-three thousand years. I was assigned to the candidate based on genetic and behavioural criteria you do not meet."

The Baron's jaw tightened. The first crack in the charm.

"Additionally, based on my observation of your forearm, you have made decisions regarding biological modification that suggest a relationship with risk assessment I would characterise as suboptimal."

Steve coughed into his wine.

The Baron recovered. The charm came back, the professional warmth reassembling itself across his features with the speed of a man who'd heard worse from people holding guns.

"Your companion is direct," the Baron said to Arthur.

"He's not my companion. He has his own agenda. It overlaps with mine."

"A pragmatic arrangement. I understand those." The Baron looked at Archie once more, then back to Arthur. "The nodes. You activate them and the yield would increase permanently?"

"As far as we know. Yes. Permanently."

"Then we have common interests, my friend. You activate these nodes, as you call them. My territory benefits. Everybody gains."

Donna leaned forward in her chair. "I’m sorry but my shoulder's been stiff since the fight. Is the doctor available?"

The Baron glanced at her. The transition was seamless, from negotiation to hospitality. "Of course. Enzo, mela, get the Doctor."

Enzo left and returned two minutes later with Sarah. She came through the courtyard door. Her eyes swept the table briefly as she took in the remains of the dinner, the party and the Baron. They settled on Donna.

"The shoulder," Donna said. "Left side. It's not rotating properly."

Sarah motioned Donna to sit on a lower leatherback chair and knelt beside her. Her hands went to Donna's shoulder, palpating the joint through the fabric. Enzo stood five feet behind, close enough to hear them breathe.

Donna felt Sarah's fingers press against her arm. Then, beneath the palpation, against the inside of her forearm where the skin was thin, a rhythmic pressure. Short. Short. Long. Short long short. Short short short.

Y-E-S.

Donna didn't react. Sarah's hands continued working the shoulder in a clinical and unhurried manner.

"Some inflammation," Sarah said aloud. "I'll need a follow-up session tomorrow."

"Thank you, Doctor," Donna said.

Sarah stood and looked at Enzo. He motioned her to follow with a too big smile on his face.

Donna stood up, cleared her throat and said

"We're taking Sarah with us."

The compound went silent. Every fighter at the courtyard corners straightened, hands finding weapons. Arthur looked up at Donna she nodded once.

Arthur stood. Steve was already on his feet.

The Baron's charm dropped like a mask hitting a stone floor. "Explain."

"She's here under duress," Donna said. "Her door bolts from the outside. She looks at your guards for permission before she treats a patient. She is coming with us."

"I have extended my hospitality to you." The Baron's voice was level but the temperature in it had changed. "Do not confuse that with weakness."

"Nobody is confused," Arthur said.

The Baron looked at his fighters. Four at the corners and Enzo. More in the buildings. The calculation he ran was visible in his eyes.

"You are three. I have thirty in this compound alone."

"Three unknowns," Arthur said. "You don't know what we can do. You know we have killed a guardian that was made from your temple. You've heard about Donna throwing an axe thirty metres from a crutch and hitting a moving target between the eyes. You haven't seen what Steve does."

The Baron looked at Steve.

"Have you ever seen a fireball?" Arthur said. "Like in the games. Because Steve can put one through that gate and take everyone standing near it."

Steve's hands were at his sides. The light between his palms was building. Faint, but visible. The Baron's fighters at the courtyard corners took a step back.

"I'm willing to bet each of us can take four of yours," Arthur said. "Maybe more if Steve gets an area effect right. So yes, you'll eventually kill us. But we'll take ten, maybe fifteen of your fighters with us. What does that do to your arrangement with the military? What's the guarantee that you personally walk out of this courtyard alive?"

The Baron was still. His eyes moved from Arthur to Donna to Steve. To the light in Steve's hands. To the Ironhold leaning against Arthur's chair. To Archie's geometry, which had brightened to full combat luminescence, the facets rotating at a speed the Baron had not seen before.

"When you leave a man no choice but to fight," the Baron said, "cost is of no consequence."

"Either Sarah walks out tonight, or we find out how many of yours we take with us." Arthur said

“But,” Arthur picked up his shield and continued after a pause “We are willing to offer an exchange which will benefit you more than one healer”

"What exchange?" The Baron's voice had gone flat, settling into the smuggler's negotiation register.

"Sarah walks out with us tonight. In return, we finish our business at the temples. The node activations are permanent. Your territory benefits regardless of whether we're here. And before we leave the island, Steve and Sarah train anyone in your camp who has an inclination toward healing. Your people learn. You lose one healer and gain three, maybe four."

"Incompetent healers," the Baron said.

"You don’t know that and these will be healers who stay because they choose to, not because you bolt their door."

Sarah was standing by the courtyard wall. She hadn't moved since Donna had spoken. Enzo was beside her, his hand near his weapon but not on it. He was looking at the Baron for instruction and the Baron was busy staring down Arthur.

The Baron finally picked up his wine glass and drank.

"The Doctor is a valuable asset," he said. "I have invested significantly in her welfare."

"You've invested in a prison with good food," Donna said.

"Negotiation at knifepoint is always tenuous, my friend." the Baron said to Donna while still looking at Arthur.

"Which is why we leave the island once the temples are done," Arthur said. "You won't want us here after this. And we don't plan on staying."

The Baron set his glass down and looked at Sarah. The expression was the one Arthur was waiting for. A man writing off a depreciating asset, calculating whether the write-off improved the balance sheet.

"Your choice, Doctor," the Baron said. "It always was."

Sarah heard the lie. The Baron heard himself say it.

Archie's geometry slowed to a drift. He said nothing about the lie, or about choice, or about the millennia of data he carried on the subject of cages.

Sarah stepped forward.

"I'm going with them."

They walked out through the gate. Now, four of them. Sarah's footsteps on the stone were unsteady for the first ten metres, as though her mind hadn't been informed that the door was open. By the twentieth metre, they had steadied and by the time they reached the goat track, she was keeping pace.

At the ridge, Sarah turned and looked south, towards the compound, towards the single lit window on the second floor of the central building where she'd slept for three weeks behind a bolt she couldn't reach.

She breathed in salt and limestone and rosemary and the faint sweetness Ether left on everything. Then she turned north, toward the temple, toward the camp, toward three strangers who'd sat at a smuggler's table and bet their lives on a doctor they'd known for four days.

She kept walking.
