The way back went faster.

Sam slept through most of it, but he'd earned it twice over. The worst day of his year, a kraken, a leviathan, and a dunk in the open ocean, all packed into one stretch of dark. He went down in the bottom of the boat somewhere past the second hour and didn't stir until Arkhos was a low smudge of harbor lights on the horizon.

Adom didn't sleep. He simply sat at the tiller with the wind in one hand and the book closed on his knee, watching the city rise slowly out of the dark, turning the rune over and over in his mind.

They came in tired. Bone-tired in Sam's case, and even Adom felt the day sitting heavy behind his eyes by the time they tied off and hauled themselves up onto the dock. The sky was just beginning to think about going gray at its edges.

"All right." Adom rolled the stiffness out of his shoulders. "We'll need somewhere private for the inscription. I've got everything we need at the—"

He stopped.

"...The lab."

Sam looked over. "What about the lab?"

"My tools. The fine set, for inscription work. They're at the lab." He pressed the heel of his hand into one eye. He was more tired than he'd realized. "I cleaned them after the last session and never brought them back home. And the lab's across the whole city, and it's the last place I want to be doing this with people able to walk in on us."

"So come to mine."

Adom looked at him.

"My place." Sam was already tugging his coat tighter against the dawn chill. "I've got tools. Not as nice as yours, but I do fine work, I've got what we'd need. Adom didn't argue.

"Lead the way."

Sam's place turned out to be a manor.

Adom always forgot that, somehow, between visits. The Harbinskys had come to Arkhos a few years back, not long after Sam finished at Xerkes; moved the whole household, and moved his mother with them, for the healers.

Arkhos had some of the finest in the world — Adom's own mother among them, and a good many besides. If there was a chance for the woman, it was as good here as anywhere.

Adom had been to the house only a handful of times, and gotten lost on every one of them. It was that kind of house: wings and back stairs and corridors that folded in on themselves, staff appearing and vanishing through doors he hadn't known were there. He'd even once gone looking for a bathroom and wound up in what he was fairly sure had been a small ballroom.

A man met them at the door before Sam's hand even reached it. Silver-haired, immaculate, entirely unbothered by the hour or by the state of the pair of them, which was considerable.

"Master Sammenel." A small bow. "Master Adom. Welcome home."

"Morning, Albrecht." Sam dragged a hand through his salt-stiff hair. "We'll be in my practice room. Don't let anyone bother us, please."

"Of course." If the butler had questions about why the young master had come home at dawn soaked to the skin with the Ghost of Arkhos in tow, not one of them reached his face. "Will you be wanting anything? Tea, perhaps. I could bring something up; a little of the sweet, a little of the savory."

"That'd be—" Sam glanced at Adom, who realized he hadn't eaten in longer than he could readily calculate. "Yeah. That'd be great. Thank you, Albrecht."

"Very good." Another small bow, and the man was already gone.

Sam's practice room was up two flights and down a hall Adom would have hardly found on his own.

It looked nothing like a battle mage's training space. There wasn't a scorch mark or a practice dummy in sight. It was a workshop: long clean benches, good lamps, drawers labeled by hand, and along one wall, in careful rows, every tool a working runicologist could want. Fine styluses. Etching needles. Loupes and ruled glass and little stoppered pots of inks and binding agents. A proper inscription set, better kept than Adom's own.

Adom took it in and wasn't surprised in the least.

Sam had the wrong title. The fire, the combat spells, the whole battle-mage track the young man had walked was all because Adom walked it first, and Sam had followed.

Left to his own shape, with no one to keep pace with, he'd have ended up here. At a bench like this one, a loupe to his eye and a needle in his hand. He'd have made a fine runicologist. Maybe a great one.

Which was, Adom thought, rather convenient tonight.

A rune on stone was a forgiving thing. A rune on paper, more forgiving still. A stone rune could be drawn wrong, scraped off, started over, and the worst anyone lost was an afternoon and a little explosion if they were unlucky.

Unfortunately, flesh was not as forgiving.

Almost nobody put runes on flesh anymore. Ancient mages had done it routinely; a rune carved into the skin became permanent, passive, always ready, no need to shape a fresh spell or hold it in the mind. But the practice was archaic and dangerous, and the world had long since traded it for the daily headache of weaving the ordinary way after optimizing spells.

Drawn wrong, a flesh-rune didn't just fail; the mana had nowhere to go but the body. Burns. Rot. Worse, sometimes.

Adom had never done one. Neither had Sam. The only one he'd ever seen up close was Eren's, and that was natural, born into the skin, not cut there.

So this was new ground, for both of them, on Adom's arm. And he was a little worried. But the rune wanted flesh, and there was a woman across the city running out of month.

So. Carefully.

There was no real question about whose arm it went on.

"It has to be me." Adom was already pushing his sleeve up past the elbow. "I've got more mana than you, by a wide margin. Whatever this thing ends up enhancing, the bearer's the one feeding it, and the more I can pour in, the stronger the effect. If your mother's as far gone as the healers think, we'll want every drop we can get."

He turned the arm over, baring the pale underside. "And I understand the rune. If it starts to go wrong while it's being laid, I'll feel it before you'd ever see it."

Sam looked at the offered arm. "...Right. Yeah. That all tracks."

"You're drawing it, though." Adom said it like it was obvious, because it was. "I can't do clean work on my own arm at this angle, and you've got better hands than I do. Always have."

He sat and laid the forearm flat on the bench, under the good lamp. "I'll talk you through every line. You just have to understand what you're drawing while you draw it. The rune doesn't care how steady your hand is if your head's somewhere else. Intent's half the work."

So Adom walked him through it. But not the way he'd teach a student, as there was no time for that. He gave Sam what he needed and nothing he didn't.

This line carried the working outward. This one held it shut until it was called on. This cluster, here, was where the mana entered, too shallow and it would never fill, too deep and it would flood.

He went slowly and made Sam say each piece back to him. And by the end, watching his friend's face, he could see it had taken: Sam wasn't just looking at the rune anymore. He understood it, the way Adom finally had across a leviathan's stretch of black water.

"Got it?"

Sam let out a slow breath. Picked up the finest of the needles. Set the tip to the skin of Adom's forearm. Didn't quite press.

"...This is going to hurt you."

"Probably. Go slow."

"Sorry." Sam pressed. The needle bit, and a thin line of red welled up behind it as the first stroke went down. "Sorry. Sorry—"

"Sam. You don't have to apologize every time you—"

"Sorry." He was already onto the second line, brow furrowed, tongue caught between his teeth, eyes huge behind the loupe. "I know. I know. I just— sorry—"

Adom shut his mouth, let his friend work, and held the arm very, very still.

"Master Sammenel." Albrecht's voice, from the hall. Not coming in. "May I bring this through?"

"Yeah, come in."

The door opened just wide enough. He did his task and went.

"Thanks, Albrecht," Sam said.

"Thank you," Adom added.

A small bow, and he was gone.

After that, they worked for hours. Adom guiding every line, Sam's hands doing the work, the rune growing across his forearm a careful stroke at a time. It was slow, exacting, draining work, and they paced it the only sensible way: stopping every couple of hours to rest their eyes and hands before either of them made the sort of mistake there'd be no scraping off.
