The terrain had changed as we moved north. The tall grass that once brushed against our legs had thinned out, replaced by clearer ground broken only by patches of dry soil and scattered stone. It made travel a lot easier, but it also left us more exposed since there were fewer places for anything to hide here, including us.

Shingo was leading the group this time, his large frame moving steadily at the front. Katar had taken rear guard duty instead of Raik, walking a short distance behind the rest of us with his usual relaxed posture, silver sword resting over his shoulder while his two other blades remained sheathed around his hips.

Raik, for once, was not paying attention to our surroundings. He walked beside me, completely absorbed in his magical practice. He was currently trying and failing to freeze the water inside his canteen.

He shook his head in frustration and turned to look at me.

“You said that heat is just energy, right?” he asked, frowning slightly as he tried to use his mana on the water again.

“Not just heat,” I replied. “Everything is energy in some form. Heat is only one way it shows itself.”

He nodded slowly, but I could tell he was not fully convinced.

“Then explain this to me,” he said. “I can use mana to create heat, that makes sense. I am putting energy into something. But when I try to remove heat, what exactly am I doing?”

“That is where it gets tricky,” I said. “There are rules to how energy works. We call them the laws of thermodynamics.”

Raik had better be thankful, I spent a couple of hours last night brushing up on my physics knowledge using my computer. I honestly was never a physics girl; I was always better in biology and the human sciences. I haven’t touched the subject of physics since high school, and high school was 8 years ago.

The fireboy raised an eyebrow.

“Laws?” he repeated. “As in something that cannot be broken?”

“In theory, yes,” I said. “The first law says that energy cannot be created or destroyed. It can only change form.”

Raik glanced at his hand, and I could hear the water boiling inside the metal container.

“So when I use mana to heat something, I am converting mana into heat, right?”

“That is the assumption,” I said. “If mana is just one form of energy, then yes.”

“And what is the second law?” he asked.

“That one is more annoying,” I said. “It says that energy naturally spreads out. Heat flows from hot things to cold things. You cannot just take heat from somewhere and make it disappear. You have to move it somewhere else.”

Raik frowned more deeply.

“So if I want to make something cold...” he began.

“You would need to take that heat and send it somewhere else,” I finished.

He was quiet for a moment.

“Then why do cryomancers exist?” he asked. “They use mana to create ice. I have seen it myself.”

I shrugged.

“Good question.”

“That is not an answer,” he said flatly.

“I know,” I replied. “At this point, I can only speculate.”

He looked mildly annoyed, so I continued.

“Maybe they are not creating cold,” I said. “Maybe they are summoning ice or moving heat somewhere else, somewhere we cannot see.”

“Like where?” he asked.

“Another dimension, maybe,” I said. “Or they could be dumping it into the environment in a way that is not obvious.”

“That does not feel correct,” Raik frowned.

“It is just guesswork,” I admitted. “We are trying to apply my world’s rules to a system that clearly has magic.”

He crossed his arms slightly as we walked.

“So your laws might not apply here at all.”

“Exactly,” I said. “Mana might not be energy in the way I understand it. It might only look similar on the surface.”

He glanced at me.

“That is not helpful.”

“I told you,” I said. “I cannot draw conclusions without extensive observation and experimentation.”

He let out a small breath, somewhere between a sigh and a laugh.

“So either cryomancers are moving heat somewhere else, summoning ice from another place, or breaking the fundamental laws of reality as you know them.”

“Those are the options,” I said. “Pick the one you like.”

“I do not like any of them,” he muttered.

“That sounds like a you problem,” I chuckled.

He gave me a look.

I shrugged.

“If it helps,” I added, “even in my world, we still argue about this kind of thing. We just do not have magic to complicate it further.”

Raik shook his head.

“I was hoping understanding this would make my power easier to control,” he said. “Now I am more confused.”

“It might still help,” I said. “Even if the rules are not exact, the way you think about it still matters.”

He nodded slowly.

“Yeah. Bloodline powers are all about hereditary definition, intent, and visualization.”

Our conversation faded after that, both of us drifting back into our own thoughts as we walked.

It was Ja’a who broke the silence.

“So where exactly are we going?” she asked, looking ahead at the empty stretch of land.

Raik glanced up, as if remembering that he was supposed to be leading.

“There should be an iron mine somewhere around here,” he said. “Old records mention it. It was abandoned after the dead zone expanded toward it.”

“Do you mean it was not part of the dead zone at some point?” I asked.

“The dead zone is not a perfect circle,” Raik explained. “This mine is technically farther from the center of the dead zone than Ectamal.”

“So the dead zone spread in an irregular shape just to claim that mine?” asked Vena, who was walking behind me next to Ja’a.

Raik nodded.

“That is why only Ectamal is allowed to stand at the western border. Otherwise, the dead zone could spread in unpredictable directions.”

“A mine being more recent means the loot is more likely to have survived,” Ja’a said, visibly perking up at the idea.

“Or it could already be picked clean,” Katar added from behind us. “Do not get your hopes too high.”

Raik nodded.

“If we had been coming from the northeast, this spot would not have been as deep into the dead zone as the other places we visited. It is more than a day’s travel from Ectamal, but it is barely past the edge.”

“Boo. Spoilsport,” grumbled Ja’a.

Kan, walking just ahead of us, glanced back.

“I am more interested in the metal,” she said. “If we are lucky, we might find something with mana in it.”

She tapped her bag lightly.

“I already ate all the pieces from the golem core I had.”

I raised an eyebrow.

“How is that working out for you?” I asked.

Kan shrugged.

“I did not feel sick,” she said. “So no backlash at least.”

Ja’a nodded.

“Her soul growth did accelerate after she ate the core,” she added. “But I am not sure whether that was from the metal itself or from the fight against Donas’ daughters.”

Kan crossed her arms.

“It is probably both,” she said. “But I would not mind having more metal to test.”

“Of course you would not,” Calr muttered with a shudder. “You were eating that core like candy.”

“Maybe it was the mana, but it did taste good,” Kan said with a smile.

I glanced at her again, still not entirely used to the idea of someone casually eating magical metal as part of their progression.

“And you are sure that is safe?” I asked.

Kan gave me a look.

“If it was not, I would have stopped.”

“She is fine,” Vena said. “I checked her regularly. She digested it perfectly.”

Well then, if the healer says she is fine, then she must be fine. Still, kindred metabolites are insane. I wonder if I, too, can eat magic metal… Probably not without an evolution first.

Ahead of us, Shingo slowed slightly, raising a hand to signal something.

The group tensed almost immediately.

“I sense it,” Ja’a said. “Around fifteen SB, about a hundred meters forward... slightly below us.”

“Below?”

I focused my eyes ahead, trying to pierce through the fog. At first, I saw nothing but gray haze and uneven ground.

Then I noticed a break in the earth, a jagged fissure that cut through the terrain like an open wound.

A few seconds later, I heard metallic clanking, sharp and irregular, like iron grinding against stone.

Then I saw it.

Something climbed out of the fissure.

At first, I thought it was a spiked metal ball. On closer inspection, it looked more like some layered structure... a gyroscope of sorts. Eight spiked metal rings connected at two poles, spinning and shifting as one, and at its center was a floating skull.

“That is the most evil-looking thing I have ever seen,” I said, genuinely taken aback.

Shingo stepped forward without hesitation, planting his feet and placing his tower shield between us and the creature.

The thing accelerated.

When it hit the shield, it did not stop. Instead, it climbed. The spikes dug into the metal like climbing picks, scraping loudly as it rolled upward, using Shingo himself as a ramp. In one fluid motion, it reached the top and launched into the air above him without losing momentum.

It was coming straight for us. For Raik and me.

Before I could react, chains snapped into place.

Kan’s chains shot upward and formed a curved railing in midair. The spinning mass struck them and continued rolling along their length, redirected rather than stopped. It passed over us by mere centimeters, the sound of grinding metal loud enough to make my ears ring.

It landed behind us with a heavy thud, right past Katar.

Then, without pause, it adjusted its trajectory and came back.

“Again?” I muttered.

A fireball slammed into it mid-roll. Raik’s spell exploded against the metal rings, flames licking across its surface, but it did nothing to the creature. It did not even slow down. With no hesitation, it simply rolled through the fire as if it did not exist.

Katar stepped in.

He moved at the last possible moment, swinging his large messer blade in a precise arc.

CLANG.

The impact rang out like a hammer striking an anvil. The sound was sharp, metallic, and painful.

The blow was strong enough to halt the creature’s momentum, but the force pushed Katar back a full step, his boots digging into the ground to keep his balance.

For a brief moment, the spinning slowed.

That was all Calr needed.

An arrow flew from his crossbow and struck the skull at the center with perfect accuracy.

I heard a crack, then a second later, the skull inside the monster shattered.

The entire construct stuttered as the spinning lost cohesion, making the rings wobble unevenly before completely stopping. Finally, the spiky gyroscope stabilized, no longer moving under its own power.

Silence returned, broken only by the faint hiss of cooling metal.

“What the hell was that?” I asked.

No one answered immediately. So we all turned to Calr, expecting him to somehow come up with a name for this monster. His eyes were wide, almost wild, and his crossbow still raised in his hand as if he had only just realized what he had done.

“What?” he asked when he noticed us staring.

“Firstly, nice shot,” said Raik.

“Thanks.”

“So, do you recognize that creature?” Raik prompted. “Is the skull its only weakness?”

“I have no idea,” Calr said. “I have never heard of anything like this.”

“Then why did you target the skull?” asked Ja’a.

He shrugged.

“Lucky guess. It is not like my arrow would have done anything to the metal parts.”

Kan stepped forward cautiously, circling the remains without getting too close.

“That was not a normal monster,” she said. “It was almost a combination of a golem and an undead.”

She crouched near it, one of her chains slipping between the outer rings to retrieve a ping-pong-sized monster core sticking out from the bone shards.

“This looks more like a metal core than a death core,” she said, passing it to Raik.

Raik frowned as he examined it.

“This core is similar to the ones found inside tormentors of doom,” he said.

“Makes sense,” said Calr. “Both are living metal objects.”

Katar stepped closer to inspect the wreck, tapping one of the rings with his blade.

“Good steel,” he said. “Very good steel.”

Kan glanced toward the fissure.

“It came from below,” she said.

We all looked back toward the crack in the earth.

The fog seemed thicker there now, making it barely visible. I felt a chill run down my spine, like something inside was watching me. If that thing had climbed out of the mine… Then there were probably more of them.

“Let’s discuss strategy before dealing with those things,” said Raik.

“Before that, should we take the spiky ball?” I asked, glancing at the inert construct.

“It is too big to take as it is,” said Ja’a. “Bags of holding are restricted by volume, and those things take up too much space for their weight.”

“Couldn’t we cut it apart?” I asked.

Kan shook her head.

“Cutting it apart would take too much time.”

“Not if I do this,” said Raik.

He stepped forward and placed a finger against one of the poles holding the rings together. With no soul contesting his powers, heat shimmered in the air as he focused. The metal glowed faintly, softened, then melted just enough to lose its structural integrity.

He repeated the process on the opposite side. With both connection points gone, the construct collapsed into separate pieces. The eight spiked rings fell apart into loose arcs of metal.

Raik crouched and picked one up, testing the weight.

“Good material,” he muttered.

He slipped the pieces one by one into his bag of holding with practiced ease. Then he stood and looked toward the fissure again.

“Now,” he said, his tone sharpening, “how do we deal with a group of them?”

After a short brainstorming session, we approached the fissure.

It looked like the remains of the old mine entrance had collapsed into a hole in the ground, creating a jagged descent into a narrow tunnel, with broken stone forming an uneven path.

We stood in formation. Shingo in the front, Ja’a, Vena, and I in the back.

“I sense them,” Ja’a said quietly. “Three. No... five.”

Metal scraped against stone as they came out all at once. Spinning, clanking, and moving faster than before.

“Positions,” Raik ordered.

Shingo moved first.

This time, he did not brace for impact. As the first construct rolled toward him, the giant boy stepped into it and slammed his shield forward with a brutal bash. The impact rang out as the force redirected the spinning mass sideways instead of letting it climb.

“Good,” Raik said. “Do not let them go past you.”

Kan followed up immediately.

Chains shot out, partially wrapping around the lead construct and interfering with its spin. She did not stop it completely, but the added resistance slowed its rotation enough to create an opening.

“Now,” she said.

Katar was next.

He stepped in and thrust his blade through the gaps in the rings. The timing had to be precise, otherwise the rotation might have snapped his weapon aside.

He hit the skull inside, and it shattered into pieces.

Calr fired.

The bolt threaded through the opening of the second construct, even without Kan slowing it, and struck dead center.

Crack.

Two down. Three more to go.

“Keep them from bypassing you,” Raik ordered.

He moved differently this time. Instead of staying back, he closed the distance.

One of the constructs rolled toward him, spikes grinding against stone. Raik sidestepped at the last possible moment, twisting his body just enough to avoid the direct path. Heat flared around him, distorting the air as his aura intensified. He reached out and hovered his hand near the spinning rings. He kept close, keeping the monster within his aura, dodging each time the construction tried to roll him over.

Slowly, the metal began to glow. Raik's soul being stronger, he was able to overwhelm its defences. The rotation slowed as the heat built rapidly. The inner structure could not keep up. The bone at the center cracked under the stress before the metal itself could fail. The skull shattered from within, and the construct collapsed mid-motion.

Raik stepped back, breathing slightly heavier from the rapid movement.

“That works,” he muttered.

Katar and Kan engaged the fourth one while Shingo and Calr handled the fifth.

Then Ja’a suddenly called out.

“More are coming.”

Another construct slipped past the front line, rolling wildly to the left before veering back.

Calr tried to shoot it, but the arrow struck the outer shell this time.

It rolled straight toward the backline.

Toward us.

From behind me, Ja’a immediately took to the air, turning invisible a second later.

I stepped forward instinctively, spear already in hand. My heart started thumping in excitement, a smile slowly creeping on my face.

I did not use lightning. My perception Soulbook made it painfully obvious what a stupid idea that would be. Metal was conductive, and if I used it now, my lightning could arc unpredictably. Vena was too close for that to be safe.

The construct lunged.

I planted my feet and thrust the bident spear forward.

The two prongs caught one of the outer rings.

The impact nearly ripped the weapon from my hands, but I held on, forcing the angle downward. The spinning resisted as one of the spikes caught hard against the Y-shaped spear.

I gritted my teeth and drove the shaft into the ground, using it as leverage.

“Vena, now!” I shouted.

The rotation slowed just enough.

Vena stepped in without hesitation. Her claymore came forward in a clean, decisive thrust, and the skull shattered.

The force of the blow traveled through the structure, and the entire construct went limp, collapsing against my spear before sliding off.

I exhaled, tension leaving my arms.

“I guess that worked,” I muttered.

Vena gave me a quick glance.

“You held it well.”

“Thanks,” I said with a smile still on my face.

Around us, the fight continued.

Shingo bashed another construct, I think the seventh one, away from the group, preventing it from gaining height. Kan’s chains kept interfering with their movement, not stopping them outright but constantly creating openings. Katar and Calr delivered finishing blows, alternating between stabs and arrows.

Raik moved between them, no longer hesitant, his heat flaring at close range to disrupt and break the constructs from within, always one step ahead, dodging their attacks.

One by one, the spinning monsters fell.

Eventually, the clanking stopped, and the Doom Balls stopped coming.

The final tally was twelve spiked abominations.

The team stood there, catching their breath while watching the hole in the ground, while Vena and I watched our rear.

This mine seems to be more challenging than the sights we visited the last couple of days.

If we had to face more of this, then whatever was inside had better be worth it.
