As Eola finished the last sentence of Components, her stomach growled painfully. She’d curled up on her bed, Atta in the crook between thigh and knee under her blanket, and worked there for the last hour. It was just too cold at her desk.

She had no idea how Patrice did it, growing up in the far north, against the Lake of Shards. It sounded miserable—though Eola’s home of Greenarbor wasn’t exactly the warm south, either. She closed the book and gathered her notes, steeled herself, and pulled the blankets off.

Atta yowled, rolled, and arched her back. Then, when the hissing was finished, the blue cat stared Eola down. “You finished your translation, then? About time. I expect something from the kitchen when you go.”

“How do you know I’m going?” Eola asked.

The cat raised her eyebrow in an uncannily human-like gesture. “Your stomach’s been roaring like a dragon for the last half-hour, silly girl. You need to take care of yourself better.”

Eola sighed and put her notes and book in their place. “Fine. I’m already going.”

“I like fish! Preferably trout!” Atta said just before she vanished and a familiar weight dropped into Eola’s pocket. She ignored the cat and opened the door.

To her surprise, both Colin and Patrice were waiting. In fact, Colin was more than waiting. His knuckles were midway to knocking on Eola’s door, and she missed getting hit by inches. “Sorry. I was thinking about getting something—“

“We’ve been waiting for about thirty minutes,” Patrice said, “but Colin only got the courage to knock just now.”

Colin’s yellow ferret glared at her from between his feet, and Colin shrugged, staring at the familiar. “We figured if we went, you’d stay here all night, working, but Patrice said something about you getting mad the last time she interrupted you.”

Eola’s face heated up, and she nodded. “Sorry. I’m going anyway, so let’s go together.”

It was late, but the students at Varin’s Academy kept strange hours—especially the older ones, who often had independent courses and large blocks of study time, or who vanished into the upper floors of the library for hours at a time. Or who, like Eola, alternated between extreme focus on their research and only listening to their stomachs. The cafeteria was very much aware of that, and dinner was often served almost until breakfast. So, when Eola, Colin, and Patrice arrived, they weren’t alone.

Which was unfortunate.

The meal of the night was a noodle dish with thick red sauce that smelled a little spicy and a little floral. Chunks of the same vegetables the kitchen so often roasted floated in it, and a blob of thick, melted goat cheese covered the top. Eola ignored everyone else, securing her plate and making a break for the closest empty table and bench. She was three bites in when someone sat across from her.

“Eola.”

“Catrine,” Eola said between bites. Patrice snorted, and Eola tried not to smile.

“Lady Andrese to you.” To Eola’s surprise, there were no taunts. No teasing, either—just a smug smirk. “You don’t deserve to be here. It’s an affront to Y’aer’s gift that you haven’t cast a single First Order spell.”

“What do you mean?” Eola asked. The noodles were getting cold, and while that wouldn’t stop her from eating, the steaming plate was meant to be eaten piping hot.

The smirk didn’t go away. “Something’s obviously wrong with you—and not just the magic. At first, I thought it was that you were simply unattuned. A toddler in an adult’s robes. But you’ve never once used my proper title or shown me the deference I deserve.”

As if to punctuate that point, Patrice gave a short, quick bow before sitting, and when Colin arrived a moment later, he wouldn’t look Catrine in the eyes. Eola flushed slightly, face going pink. “That’s what this is all about, Catrine? Half of the professors don’t use your title, either. Reyanna’s the only one who does.”

“You’ve gotten a lot braver, haven’t you?”

“Why do you have it out for me, specifically?”

“Because I’ve known you don’t belong since you got here. You’re no spellsword, even if you have a smallsword and wand, and I’m going to prove it to you.” For a moment, Catrine glared at Eola, and Eola stared back.

Then, Eola’s fork dipped into the noodles and sauce, and she raised a bite to her lips. It wasn’t a conscious decision. The sauce just smelled so good, and she was so hungry. But it happened.

Catrine’s eyes narrowed as Eola chewed, swallowed, and sighed in pleasure. “How can you enjoy that? No, ignore that.” She pulled her wool mitten off and dropped it onto the table. “I, Lady Catrine Andrese, Heir to Highstone and daughter of Y’aer’s Chosen Calliene Andrese, have had my honor besmirched. I challenge Miss Eola Lemiene to a duel of honor.”

Patrice’s own fork clattered to her plate. Colin only looked confused. But Eola stared Catrine down, not blinking. “When and where?”

Catrine only laughed, smirk growing wider by the second. “You have no idea what you’re getting into, do you?”

“Lady Andrese, excuse us for a moment.” Patrice grabbed Eola’s hand, pulling her away from the table, loaded fork and all.

Eola popped the next bite into her mouth as they walked, enjoying the spicy-sweet taste. It was almost a paste, really, but not quite—and it was cooling down rapidly.

“This is a bad idea, Eola. You’ve never taken part in a duel of honor, or even watched one, have you?” Patrice asked.

“I’ve seen a lot of battle mages fighting. They had a tournament arena in Greenarbor.”

“A duel of honor is different. Much, much different. You’re a good fighter—you survived the third floor.” Patrice sighed, rubbing her eyes. “You should decline. You really, really should decline. House Andrese’s attunement’s not one to mess with, and you have no idea what you’re doing. Being embarrassed isn’t so bad for you—you’re just a commoner, so it’s expected, even.”

Eola turned back toward the table. Then she looked at Patrice, eyes hardening. “I can’t decline. She’d win.”

For a few seconds, Patrice was silent. Then she nodded slowly. “Listen, if you really want to do this—“

“I think I do.”

“If you really want to do this, you decide which weapons, and she decides when and where. It won’t be for a day or three unless she’s really offended, so we’ll have some time to figure out a strategy and to run you through some practice bouts. I’ll help you as much as I can, but you’re going to be at a disadvantage the whole time.” Patrice looked over her shoulder at the still-smirking Catrine, whose lackeys had shown up with their plates and sat down across from Colin. “House Andrese’s known for their excellent duelists—I’ll explain why in private—and Latrine Catrine and I go way back.”

Eola groaned. It was so frustrating, not knowing the rules for something. First, it was her Ordered Magic workaround, the spell-chains. She couldn’t get those figured out at all. Then, before she could make progress, she got thrown into this mess with Catrine. There were whole sets of rules she didn’t understand—and worse, hadn’t ever seen.

And her food was getting cold.

She walked back to the table, trying to keep herself calm and avoid glaring too hard at Catrine. Then she sat back down, took another bite of the now-lukewarm noodles, and swallowed.

“I accept. Wands and swords?”

“That’s fine with me,” Catrine said, smiling widely. She took a bite of her own pasta, swallowed, and grimaced. “Next First-Day, in the hedge maze, then?”

“Acceptable.”

“Who’s your second?” Catrine asked.

Before Eola could say anything, a hand tightened on her shoulder. She looked up. Patrice stood over her, at Catrine. “I’ll be her second.”

“Wonderful. I choose Lord Garreth Skaget.”

The rest of the meal was eaten in silence, as Catrine ignored Eola and her friends, and Eola did her best to do the same. And, about halfway through, a dark-haired boy stood up from three tables down, dropped off his plate, and disappeared out the door.

Eola finished her meal, then strolled back to her room, feeling a little smug—and a little nervous. Catrine had been trying to get her to back down, and she hadn’t given her what she wanted. And even better, she’d done it without relying on anyone else’s power. Instructor Tarik hadn’t had to get involved, and it had been, to her mind, a clear victory.

Patrice and Colin followed her. Colin looked nervous, but Patrice was…Eola glanced over her shoulder, then did a double-take. Patrice’s face was unreadable. She was usually so expressive, so self-assured in who she was, that for a moment, Eola second-guessed herself.

But no. No, she’d done the right thing. Catrine Andrese had been targeting her for a long time, and it was time to make a real stand and make it stop. She narrowed her eyes, new determination filling her.

That lasted until the three students stepped into their suite. Then Patrice threw an arm around Eola’s shoulder and steered her toward her narrow, windowed room. Colin followed behind.

The moment Eola’s bedroom door closed, Patrice and Colin both started talking.

“What were you thinking?” Colin asked. “Do you even know anything about her attunement?”

“That’s a very good point. Eola, you don’t have any idea what you just signed up for, do you?”

Eola tried to answer both questions at the same time, stuttered, and went silent.

“That should have been your first reaction, Eola,” Patrice said. “Shutting up and keeping your head down.”

“And she’s powerful. Not just as a mage, but her family’s name is—“

“I told you to back down. Now I’m stuck as your second.”

Colin held up a hand. “I could have been her second. What’s a second?”

For a few seconds, the room went quiet. Patrice sat on the edge of the narrow desk, cheeks flushing with anger under the folds of her scarf. Atta’s eyes locked onto the redhead, and after a few moments of awkward, stretched-out silence, Patrice sighed loudly. “What’s done is done, I guess. You accepted a challenge from the daughter of one of the best dueling houses in Alemia. A duel of honor. You’re committed now, and so am I.”

“We,” Colin said.

“Yes, we. But mostly Eola and me,” Patrice corrected. “So, if you’re going to fight Lady Catrine Andrese next First Day, I need to teach you the rules of a duel of honor before then. Listen carefully.”

Eola opened her notebook and readied a pen.

“The first thing you need to understand is that you can back down at any point. Duels of honor are fought to either first blood, spell defense failure, surrender, or, in rare cases, satisfaction. That usually means death. Since neither you nor Catrine clarified that last point, it’ll be first blood, defense failure, or surrender.”

As Eola wrote, she opened her mouth. “That sounds like—“

“No, it doesn’t.” Patrice sighed again, fingers on her closed eyes. “It’s nothing like an exhibition duel. You’re looking at three phases of duel, and as the challenged party, you’re going to have to know how to handle each of them, as well as what Catrine’s doing. Once you’ve arrived at the dueling ground, which is a mistake on her part, you’ll have to declare your weapons. It’s usually sword, wand or staff, and spellbook, but expect her to have a few other tools and trinkets as well. I’ll reject anything too outrageous, but she’ll still have an advantage.

“Then you go to your respective spots. Twenty or so feet apart to start.”

“I fail to see the difference between this and an exhibition duel,” Eola said.

“The next part. As the challenged party, you get to choose whether to attack or defend first during spell-trading. I recommend defense for you, because your attack won’t end the duel anyway. No offense, but your offensive magic is…”

“Lackluster?” Colin finished.

“I was going to say ‘atrocious,’ but ‘lackluster’ works,” Patrice said. “So, she gets a chance to break your magical defenses, and then you get a chance to break hers. If either of you succeeds, the duel’s over. That’s a defense failure. After that, the duel is mostly traditional in nature. The spell-trading phase is there to manage gross imbalances in power and to allow participants to surrender early. Personally, I’d surrender right away if I were up against Latrine Catrine, or any mages from that family.”

Eola started to ask, but Colin beat her to the punch. “Why?”

“Because House Andrese’s family attunement is scent.”

Scent Mana.

Eola had seen a scent mage at work, in one of the exhibition circuit duels that occasionally arrived at Greenarbor. She’d been eleven—old enough to know about her broken attunement, but young enough to still be wildly hopeful that a real one would manifest. The duelist’s opponent was a water mage, and she’d bet her dad a whole copper that he’d mop the floor with the scent mage. Maybe literally, but even if not, he’d definitely win.

That much was obvious to Eola. Water could make weapons and shields, and there were dozens of spells for each of them. It was one of the standard duelist attunements, and it even had a few Worldshaper spells, though she’d never even seen their runes. Water attunement was just…better…than scent.

What was the scent-attuned mage going to do to her opponent, anyway? Make him smell awful?

The look in her dad’s eye when they shook on their bet should have been all the warning Eola needed. But she was young, and she hadn’t seen any scent magic before.

Greenarbor was pretty much all port, and pretty much no city—and the dueling arena reflected that. It was a field of sand, about a hundred yards long and half as wide, with wooden benches stacked up against its sides and a rough fence around it. The two duelists took their spots twenty feet from each other. The flag waved, and the fight started. Then it ended. Just like that.

One moment, the water mage was drawing a First Order spell in the air and sprinting to the left while the scent mage stood perfectly still, almost leaning against nothing. Her sword wasn’t even out. Eola was ready to cheer as the rune neared completion.

Then the woman’s wand ripped through the air, and a piece of Child’s Magic erupted around the water mage. He doubled over, gagging and coughing. His spell persisted for a few seconds, until the scent mage’s blade tapped his back.

Eola was retching, too. So was the rest of the audience. It smelled like rotten eggs. Like the outhouse near the docks, next to where the fishing boats cleaned out. Like a trog that had gone months without bathing. It was, without a doubt, the worst thing she’d ever smelled in her life. Only a few people in the stands were unaffected, and one of those was her dad. He laughed, held out a finger, and sketched a symbol in the air over Eola’s head. “Breathe through this,” he said.

Then, as Eola slowly regained her composure—but not her lunch—he held out a hand. She glared at him, face sweating and skin clammy, and slammed her copper coin into his palm.

She’d copied the spell into her book that night—both the scent mage’s spell and the one her dad had used to counter it. But even Air Mesh wouldn’t be enough. He’d been very clear about that. “It’s only working because we’re near the edge of the spell’s effect, and because it’s a windy day.”

That wouldn’t work. Not against a scent mage in a real fight.

Not against Latrine Catrine.
