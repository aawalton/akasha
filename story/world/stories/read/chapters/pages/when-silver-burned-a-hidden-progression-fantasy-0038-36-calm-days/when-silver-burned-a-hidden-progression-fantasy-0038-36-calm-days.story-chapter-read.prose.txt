Time, by virtue of it being time, spins on the same axis for everyone and everywhere under the sky. From the magnificent southern towers of Két Raining, to the isolated half-island of Shepard's Field in the north, and to the undiscovered beyonds of The Corner, no material thing remains untouched by the cure—or poison—that is the tick of the clock. Warmlight turned, turned over and again with Blindlight chasing on its tail, and the dual suns eventually nudged Nagy of Summer to give way to Kis of Autumn in the tumultuous year of 785.

Days around the Eastern Expanse have gotten wetter and just a touch colder, though soldiers stationed in the Outer Line of Defense would've hardly noticed as they were soaked enough, often enough, courtesy of their rigorous physical labor. There was always something to find and help out doing around the Old; gathering shell fragments with sieves from thin fractures and grinding it to paste, maintaining the buildings and filling the cracks with wattle and daub, hoarding provisions from one place to another, and so on. It was easy to fall into a routine and forget about the ever looming danger lurking beyond the earthwork.

The newest batch of recruits that had arrived more than a week ago still hadn't seen combat, but with experienced older boys coming and going around them—sometimes poking, sometimes teaching—they soaked up a great deal of knowledge. Get stronger, get smarter, they'd been instructed. Rest easy and don't hold your breath. Expect long and boring days, but always be prepared for the worst, they'd been told.

Sleeping on the fringes of what was essentially an active frontline and wearing partial armor from morning to evening was difficult in theory. In practice, it was even more so. And it was thrice as difficult for a certain silver-eyed individual who kept looking over his shoulder, shuddering at every unexpected movement at the edge of his vision.

Kalsin had not come across a single hint of his would-be killer since the evening with the poisoned thorns, and it wasn't for the lack of looking. He swept his sleeping quarters each night and kept his sword close to his chest during his shallow slumbers. He braced his back against solid walls when alone so nobody would sneak up on him. He took his meals from the communal cauldron, never put his bowl down or left it alone, and even then he waited several minutes after the first tentative bite to see if poison would twist his guts.

It was exhausting. The first day, Shutter woke during the middle of the night and Kalsin startled, barely stopping himself from thrusting his concealed blade beneath his blanket through the thin sheet. But Shutter didn't come to him. He walked out of the building, then came back to bed a minute later after having relieved himself, and Kalsin was left with a thudding, guilty heart as his sweat-soaked hand gripped his sword hilt. He felt like scum, but maybe it was for the better, because from that point forward Kalsin realized he simply didn't have the mental fortitude to suspect his closest companions. Shutter, Fals, Littleguy, Crawl, and Pike... they had plenty of opportunities to take him out. It made no sense to suspect them. He also ruled out Cyanfish, who had given him countless servings of food and Kalsin never got as much as a stomach ache. Eventually he came to the conclusion that the poisoner probably wasn't Dweller, Ringrail, Joe, or any of the others who'd been training with him from the start.

No, it would've taken time for the Civil House to receive the news that Kalsin would be given safe harbor in the army. It would’ve taken time to hire a spy, give them information about their mission, supply them with the necessary tools, and dispatch them. The mole would have arrived days, maybe weeks after Kalsin started his training under Brownweather's command. Which still left the potential pool of spies at nearly a dozen—and none of whom Kalsin was familiar with.

Much as he kept his eyes open, he couldn't keep the regularity of the place from lulling him in a vestige of peace, the tension of his shoulders easing up day after busy day.

The recruits spent much of their time under the roof of their long row of huts. The buildings were solid and warm from the inside, but they'd been neglected and so it was their job to get them fixed. And the huts needed fixing if the residents wanted to avoid a midnight shower dumped in their neck, a likely scenario with how common storms were. It surprised Kalsin to see how far the winds tended to blow the rain inland from the distant sea. He didn't mind, though. He'd never been partial to the heat of sunlight. Rainy, grim and overcast was infinitely more to his taste.

Kalsin had taken up arms training again when his fingers finally healed enough to be useful. The thin, fresh layer of skin at his fingertips tingled when having to deflect an especially rough pommeling by some of his larger mates, but it was an inconvenience buried under the weight of the adrenaline-stirring rush of the fight. He avoided curling his fingers into a fist, though, as the digits digging inside the middle of his palm still responded with a spike of pain—which he found out too late, right after having delivered a solid blow to Fals' side. She'd shrugged the hit off in twenty seconds flat. He'd cradled his hand for the rest of that day.

Regardless, Kalsin was glad to be able to practice again.

He stood in the training boundary gouged in the rustic earth with Shutter by his side, shield held high as he faced off against Wardoll and Yesfellow. The blonde was breathing hard and kept adjusting her grip as if her sword was a piece of chicken-thigh to roast from all sides over the flames. Yesfellow had a vein throb near his narrow forehead every time he shifted his weight. Like Kalsin and Shutter, they kept the edges of their shields overlapping in the standard formation that had been drilled into them. A stationary scenario—no detachment, circling, or otherwise any sideways movement allowed. They even had a knee-high plank jutting out at their feet between the two groups, slanted and slightly closer to the defenders, a miniature representation of the earth wall.

"Out," Shutter said, and Kalsin moved his shield just enough to let his companion's sword sneak out from between the two metal barriers to deliver a ringing strike against their opposition. Shutter quickly drew his arm back, and the gap disappeared as they shifted back in position.

The small opening in the shield wall was necessary for two reasons; they'd have to be striking downward during defense and reaching over their shields would greatly hamper their range against the Veiled advancing from below. And the vast majority of recruits had the same dominant hand—right—so if the person on the left wanted to attack, the one on the right would have to make some room first.

Hold the barrier. Brace and wait for an opportunity. Open, poke out, close. That would be the dance of the older boy. And that last part of closing the shield gap, it needed to be quick, quick as a scorpion's tail because for every moment your body is exposed in a battle is a moment you're inviting sparks on an oil keg.

Yesfellow gave his partner a small nod and they pushed forward, but by the time it took for the two of them to communicate their plan, Kalsin had already suspected it. He moved a foot forward on top of the plank, then planted his right forearm horizontally against the inside of his shield, sword sticking up, and leaned forward with most of his body weight. Shutter followed his example almost instantly, bracing himself just in time.

Four sheets of metal collided with a thunderous crash, and they held against their opponents who bounced slightly back but didn't relent. Kalsin saw Yesfellow's eyes squint in exertion above the rim of their shields. Kalsin pulled his shield up and his head down, metal grinding, to avoid a strike from overhead. He saw that Shutter had positioned his sword to protect his ribs instead of upwards, which was a good thing because it stopped the jab from Wardoll as she struck from the side.

They had to end it quick. "Up," Kalsin said, and they rose in tandem to stand on the plank. Hits below the belt line were not allowed—during defense, only their upper bodies would be exposed against the Veiled—and Kalsin had every intention to make use of their height advantage.

His muscles aided by the pull of the earth while the attackers were being hampered by it, Kalsin could afford to free his right hand and swing his blade around the shield in front of him from the side, much like how Wardoll had done.

Yesfellow let out a strangled yelp as Kalsin's dulled practice sword scored a hit. Feeling the pressure against his shield lessen, Kalsin used the opportunity to wrench forward and down with his shield. A second later Yesfellow was sprawled on his back, and Wardoll hastily stepped back and out of the training ground to avoid the same fate.

She backed up a few more steps for good measure, fallen sword kicking up a plume of dirt by her feet. "Good! Whew, that was good. You got us, alright? Fair is fair, and— Shutter, for Gods' sake, take that hunk of metal out of my nostrils."

Shutter, for his part, stopped at the edge of the gouged line and scowled down at Wardoll's discarded sword. He hesitated a moment as if the drifting cloud above the weapon was a string of insults quickly evaporating in the air, then turned on his heel to help Yesfellow stand among the standing.

Kalsin dabbed at his dirty cheek and noticed the group that had been playing dice a few minutes ago now stood nearby, watching their sparring session. Cyanfish gave Kalsin a grin, flanked by Leather and Dweller, the latter thoughtfully chewing on a blue pear that grew in abundance toward the southern shores of the gulf. Ringtail rounded out the ensemble of onlookers, running a finger along her belted sword with a cocky smirk on her face that Kalsin already dreaded.

But thankfully before she could butt in, Shoreline strolled up to the whole lot of them. "Ey, Radolf! Brownweather wants a word with you."

"What for?" Yesfellow asked.

Shoreline shrugged. "Plowed if I know. He didn't have a nagged look about him, but then again, when does he ever." Her half-lidded eyes naturally gave her a lazy visage as she surveyed her fellow recruits. A proper bedhead would have completed the look of someone who just crawled out of dreamlands not a moment ago.

"Well, I'm beat anyway," Wardoll said as Yesfellow walked off. She discarded her shield and massaged the red lines the straps left on her arm.

Kalsin couldn't blame her. Heavy, unwieldy things, shields were. He and Shutter followed her example, though Kalsin wasn't as careless with his sword, even if it was just for training. He wiped the blunted blade against the cloth of his forearm, then turned it over and repeated the motion.

"One-on-one, Ghost?" Shutter asked.

"Don't think so. Not for today." Kalsin said after a short pause. He would've enjoyed a good spar if it was only him and Shutter, but the present company seeped some of the color out of his mood. Maybe a sketch or two would bring it back. "But I’m sure 'Tail here is more than ready to go a round in my stead." Kalsin nodded toward the sword-brandishing Ringtail.

Her smirk wilted a touch. "I could, I could. Buuut it's been a long time since I had the opportunity to pummel a certain hauntling into the ground."

"Not false," Kalsin said. He was still rusty after his long recovery. He could've said so, but then Ringtail could've taken that as an offhand insult, which then could've deteriorated into a shouting match that Kalsin really didn't have the energy to weather. "Not today, though, I’m afraid. How about we count it as your win for now?"

"Naw, you can have it. The scoreboard is already tilted in my favor."

"Right. Are you just saying that, or are you actually keeping score?"

Ringtail ignored his question. "And now that I've granted you a free win, selfless as I am, there's a Ghost caught in my debt. I'm collecting immediately."

Kalsin was not amused. He took a deep breath, but before something unseemly had a chance to leap from his tongue, Ringtail spoke.

"Heeey. Easy. I just need help setting up some fishing lines. Needed it done yesterday, actually, but... yeah." She looked to the side while speaking, rubbing one arm, as if asking for his help took a monumental amount of effort.

Kalsin went to speak again, but she continued with a frantic pace. "And it’s not, like, I was delaying it or anything. I just can't fucking stand the monotony and there's no way I'll be sitting there, fiddling with a pile of strings and hooks under the sun all day."

"You done?"

"Yes. Not with the work, but, uh. Yes." She paused. "So?"

"Fine." Kalsin said. Maybe some monotony was what he needed right then. "But this will erase any and all imaginary debt I have, and will have in the future." He took a glance toward Shutter, who immediately turned and pretended to find the time of his life rearranging a basket full of training swords. "Anyone else feeling helpful? I'm willing to offer twenty-five percent of my weekly salary."

"We don't get a weekly salary," Dweller said.

"No, but I also wouldn't give you twenty-five percent of it, so there's no reason not to offer."

Shoreline, who's been watching Dweller's pear slowly disappear in her gullet, glanced toward Ringtail and Kalsin. "Any of you happen to know how to set those fishing lines up?"

Ringtail scowled at her, folding her arms but otherwise not debating. Kalsin stared with a blank face.

"You got extra help, then, because I, for one, like my underwater delicacies on a plate, rather than swimming merrily and uncaught in the deep."

And that was the beginning of a long row of events that Kalsin would replay many times in his head, and wonder if it would've mattered at all.
