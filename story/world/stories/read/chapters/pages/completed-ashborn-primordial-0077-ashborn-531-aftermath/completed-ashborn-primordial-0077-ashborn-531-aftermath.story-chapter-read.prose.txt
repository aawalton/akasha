The moment of his death had moved.

Vir saw it clearly on the tapestry. Saw the thread of his life extend past the machine that had for long plagued his nightmares. Saw it extend out into a new future that, only moments prior, hadn’t existed.

It now ended in a far more mundane way. In an open field to a seric blade thrust into his abdomen, wielded by a warrior of pure gold. Her face was somehow obscured from his sight and all he knew was that she was crying.

As the last of Janak’s soul burned away and the all-seeing clarity of thought the machine granted began to fade away, Vir knew.

That for all this knowledge, he was powerless to alter that moment. That it would come as surely as the encroaching Ash, and not a thing he did would matter against it.

Vir would be lying if he were to say he wasn’t bothered. No mortal should know the time and place of their death, and Vir had been forced to confront it not once, but twice now.

But that was a matter for another time, for the machine was still active, still yet to finish its cosmic process.

Whatever miracle had allowed the process to complete despite everything the Fateweavers had seeded against it, the prana of the Ash was still roiling through the machine's systems, unchecked and accumulating well past the threshold of what the new realm could absorb—or rather, what its freshly transferred denizens could handle.

The realm itself was more than capable of receiving the prana of the Ash. In fact, such a realm would benefit Vir and the Asuras to a great degree.

Just that they would be the only ones left alive.

If Vir allowed it all through, the prana density in the new realm would climb well past the point where all but the strongest could survive it. He would have transferred every soul faithfully across only to kill them all on arrival.

Vir couldn't let that happen.

And yet, how? How did one redirect half the prana of the Ash? And to where?

The tapestry was already fading from his mind, but Vir fought it. He held onto what threads remained and searched, pushing his awareness through the infinity of universes, past and present, looking for anyone in any time who had ever faced something similar.

He found only one.

A Parai the Ancient in some other universe long, long ago, who had stumbled upon a compressed font of Imperium magic and devised a mechanism for capturing it rather than allowing it to disperse it. This Parai had gone on to wield a near-infinite source of prana for years until a war was fought across the realm that resulted in his death, the destruction of the device, and the annihilation of clan Baira in the explosion’s aftermath.

The solution wasn't perfect. It was an experiment aborted halfway.

Unstable, it leaked prana continuously, and moreover, it hadn't been designed for anything close to prana of this magnitude.

And yet, a child’s half-finished school project was the only hope he had. The only solution the tapestry showed him, and the tapestry was nearly gone.

Vir had no choice.

Leveraging everything Vir had learned of prana through his entire life, he dove into the contraption. Studied its impossibly complicated inscription, and, aided by the machine, understood them.

He co-opted the process, refining it as best he could, fixing its flaws and redirecting the flow, funneling the excess prana back into the machine itself, using the contraption as a compressor.

In tandem, he siphoned off a fraction of that torrent, ripping a hole in the fabric of the new realm, not to create a Gate, but to inject as much prana as he could.

Strengthening it against the colossal weight of the compressed prana.

The concepts required for this divine feat—concepts Vir had never learned—came to him naturally, as though he’d performed this very task a thousand times. Janak's machine fed them to him in its death throes, the way it had fed him the knowledge of life weeks ago in his mindscape.

Vir absorbed and applied them, letting his instincts and the machine guide him.

He became the machine, his only purpose in life to compress prana. To strengthen the fabric of the realm that supported it. To somehow stave off mass extinction.

And after what felt like an eternity, but could only have been less than the blink of an eye, he managed it.

The remainder of the Ash's prana compressed, smaller and smaller, until it occupied a space so minute Vir almost couldn't locate it.

A core so dense it warped the space around it. Time bent along its edges, until Vir siphoned a portion of the energy back out and used it to stabilize the area around it.

Around the core.

Around him.

For the only place strong enough to house such an absurd amount of prana, the only place where Vir could be sure it would never be used or abused… was within him. Nestled within his soul.

The machine shut down, and the deafening din with it.

Vir finally became aware of his own body again, of the floor beneath his knees, of the weight of his own limbs, and for the first time in his life, he felt trapped.

Having experienced that near-omniscience, having expanded himself beyond what any mortal could hope to dream, his body now felt like a prison, restricting him in every way. And not only because of its limitations.

Somewhere deep within his chest now resided a metaphysical weight. A compressed remnant of half a realm's worth of prana, small enough to be invisible to the eye and yet dense enough to unmake all life if ever destabilized.

A metaphysical weight so heavy it nearly brought Vir to his knees as his soul strained to contain the prana of an entire realm.

Strained… and slowly failed.

Vir forced aside the feeling. There would be time to deal with the prana core another time, and Vir was nothing if not a veteran to pain.

Checking himself over, he realized that aside from the core, his body felt no different. He checking his prana and the state of his Chakras. He found nothing amiss, which struck him as deeply strange given what his body had just been through, channeling all that prana.

He pushed a button and the lift hummed down, to where Maiya waited.

She appeared no different either, save for the tears in her eyes and the expression of alarm that she wore when she watched him do something dangerous. Shan sat at her feet, watching Vir with his keen eyes, as did the rest of his brethren.

For a moment, Vir wondered if he had failed. If the machine hadn’t worked.

But something was different. Something that had been present every moment of every day since Vir had first entered the Ashen Realm, so constant and pervasive that he had long since stopped noticing it, was gone.

The overwhelming prana here, at the core of Mahādi, that had defined the Realm since its inception and plagued and poisoned the world, was gone.

Not entirely, but what remained was a level slightly above what was normal for the human realm, and Vir knew that it was this way everywhere from here to the farthest tip of the Voidlands.

It was the exact level he had set in the illusory realm.

Vir Blinked to the nearest lift, startling Maiya and Shan, who, to their credit, reacted swiftly, piling into the lift before the doors swiveled shut. As did all of Shan’s half-dozen brothers and sisters, packing the small space.

“I’d ask where you’re going,” Maiya said with a small smile. “But I think I know.”

“Sorry,” Vir said, shaking his head. “I need to know.”

Maiya nodded, and it was only now that Vir noticed the tears staining her face.

The lift traveled to the very top of the tower, to a level they had never been, for it led to the roof. A place that would have spelled the death and destruction of them both.

Trembling, and despite Prana Vision insisting there was no longer a deadly Prana Swarm outside, Vir trembled as he injected his Ash prana into the door’s lock, which despite having gone unused for five thousand years. Slid open as if not a day had passed.

And Vir was forced to avert his eyes.

Not because of a mythical beast… but because of the brightness. Brightness of the sun, so bright in the cloudless blue sky that it blinded him.

Vir said nothing for a long moment, trying to take it all in. The sound of birds perching atop the long-dead spires. The warm sunlight that bathed a land perpetually scoured by lightning and thunder… the lack of eternally falling Ash.

Only then did the truth finally sink in. The three realms were no more. There was just one now, as it was supposed to be. With a sun that would rise and set, and air that living things could breathe without prana slowly killing them, and every soul he had managed to transfer living their lives inside it without knowing anything had changed.

Janak's mistake, all those thousands of years ago, had been resolved.

He was alive. They were alive. And while Janak’s plan may not have worked as originally intended, they now had a chance.

He looked back at Maiya, who dropped to her knees in front of him, sobbing. Vir held her face in both his hands and broke down in tears right with her.

Right until a small girl with long black hair cleared her throat. “I prithee, forgive mine intrusion, yet wouldst someone deign to explain what passeth haer?”
