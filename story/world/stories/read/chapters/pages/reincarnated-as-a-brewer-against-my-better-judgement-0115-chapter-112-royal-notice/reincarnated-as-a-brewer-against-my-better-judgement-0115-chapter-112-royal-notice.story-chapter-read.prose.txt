The king was being buttoned into authority when the report arrived. There were, at that moment, three men around him whose entire profession seemed to involve making sure fabric submitted to the crown in the correct order. One adjusted the collar. Another fastened the side clasps. A third stood back with the grave expression of a man prepared to defend the honour of a sleeve if necessary.

The king endured it with the patience of someone who had long ago accepted that ruling a kingdom involved being dressed by committee.

His outer robe had just been lifted from the stand when the chamberlain entered, stopped at the sight of the half-finished monarch, and performed a bow that managed to apologise for both his presence and the continued existence of bad news.

Behind him stood Lord Calven.

Calven did not apologise. That was one of the reasons the king kept him close. He was narrow, grey at the temples, and clever enough to understand that a report became more dangerous when no one had exaggerated it yet.

The king looked at him through the mirror. “What is it?”

Calven’s eyes flicked once toward the servants.

The king raised one hand. The man with the sleeve froze in place, still holding it as if it might attempt escape.

“Speak,” the king said. “If it is important enough to interrupt dressing, then just be vague.”

The chamberlain went very still.

Calven stepped forward and held out a sealed bundle. “The reports on the brewer have been gathered, Your Majesty.”

The king did not take it immediately.

“The brewer,” he said. “The one summoned for spring?”

Calven nodded with a grave expression.

The king took the bundle. It was thick. Too thick for a man who made beer, unless the beer had committed treason.

He broke the seal, unfolded the first page, and began to read.

The dressing room became quiet around him. Fabric waited. Servants waited. The chamberlain stared very carefully at a point somewhere above everyone's heads, which was a skill palace staff developed early if they intended to survive court.

The king turned one page. Then another. By the fourth, the robe was still hanging open around his shoulders, and no one had dared to suggest finishing the clasp.

Calven said, “It is more serious than the early reports implied.”

The king looked up.

“That is an understatement.” he said.

Calven inclined his head.

The king folded the report once, not neatly. “You’ve done well to get all this so quickly Calvern. Gather the senior advisors. Also the queen. Private office.”

“Yes, Your Majesty.” Calven said bowing and left.

The king handed the pages back to the chamberlain, then held out his arms. The servants moved again with the relief of men returning to an ordinary crisis.

“Finish,” the king said.

The sleeve man obeyed so quickly he nearly dressed the king’s hand twice.

By the time the king entered the private office, the reports had already been copied, sorted, and placed in front of the people who mattered.

The room was not large by palace standards. It had no throne, no audience carpet, and no carefully placed statues of dead ancestors looking disappointed at everyone. It had a table, chairs, maps and locked cabinets.

The king preferred it. Men lied less in rooms without velvet.

The queen sat to his right, already reading. Her expression was calm, but the king knew her well enough to see the first line of concern forming between her brows.

Calven stood opposite with his own notes arranged in a precise stack.

Lord Fenril had chosen the chair near the window, which allowed him to look thoughtful while also avoiding the most difficult papers. Fenril had been placed on the council because his family mattered, and because the king found it useful to know what a typical noble might say before the typical nobles arrived in groups and said it louder.

Two other advisors sat along the table. Neither spoke. For a while, there was only the turning of pages.

The queen tapped one finger lightly against the margin of her page. “He entered the city last year. With a merchant train for an item box?”

The king looked down at the first report again. “It was not inspected I should add. His item box can hold twelve wagonloads of goods, apparently.”

“Measured informally,” Calven said. “The magery estimate says more than twelve wagonloads of grain.”

Fenril frowned. “Why grain?”

The queen did not look up. “Because he is a brewer.”

Fenril absorbed this as if it had been unfairly hidden in plain language.

The king tapped the report with a finger before looking to the queen. “He walked through our front gate with twelve wagons inside a spell.”

“If those twelve wagons had carried weapons,” Calven said.

The king nodded turning to Calven. “We discussed this, it could have held siege equipment. If he wanted to attack, he would have already and we would not have been ready.”

Calven nodded, avoiding eye contact.

"Normally an item box can hold merely a few tools, I know Atrel uses his to sneak snacks to the princess for her lessons." The queen said with a frown.

The queen turned another page. “He is an elder mage. At twenty?”

“We don’t know his age your majesty, we also do not know where he came from.” Calven added.

The king picked up the magery report. He read the line once. Then again.

“He has more mana than Atrel. Much much more.” The king added letting go of a deep breath.

Atrel was not in the room. This was fortunate. The leader of the royal atelier was a proud man, and proud men handled being surpassed by unknown commoners with the grace of cats placed in bathwater.

The queen sat back. “How does a commoner reach elder mage without being known?”

“That,” Calven said, “is one of the questions, we cannot answer.”

Fenril leaned forward, perhaps sensing a place where the world had become simple enough for him to enter it. “Could he be a spy?”

The king looked at him.

Fenril continued carefully with a smug expression. “A planted citizen. A mage disguised as a brewer. It explains the irregularity.”

“If he is a foreign spy,” the king said, “find out who sent him. I would like to ask whether they have another. As much as he is irregular, he’s useful. He’s also awful to call this much attention to himself.” He gestured to the stacks of documents when saying the last.

No one laughed. The king turned the next page.

The reports had a way of becoming worse without raising their voice. Merchant registration. Tavern supply notes. Guild complaints. Magery assessment. Temple correspondence. Festival accounts. Dock fragments. Each separate report had been annoying, interesting, or strange. Placed together, they stopped being reports and became a shape.

“He started a business,” the queen said, “And this business caused the trouble at the previous harvest festival?”

Calven glanced down. “Contributed, I’d say..”

The king read the relevant page. “By selling cider to adventurers?” The queen looked at Calven who was nodding, “Single-handedly?”

“Sort of.”

The king lifted his eyes. “That is not a phrase I enjoy hearing in this office.”

Calven accepted this with a small nod and shrug. “The business was his. The sales were done through his people. The issue was caused by the entire festival shifting entirely to the adventurers.”

Fenril frowned. “So he sold cider well?”

“He sold enough cider to make every tavernkeeper jealous,” Calven said.

“That is not illegal. Merely rude, in a profitable way.” Fenril said.

The king placed that page aside. The next was worse.

“He brewed beer in winter,” the queen said slowly, “During the blizzard.”

The king stared at the line.

There were certain things men did during blizzards. They stayed inside. They ate through stores. They complained about roads, roofs, fuel, and the price of anything that could be made warm. They did not usually begin solving winter production.

The king turned to Calven. “Explain.”

“Honestly your majesty, we cannot. We think he used heating stones to keep the beer warm but apparently his winter beer is… different. No one could explain how exactly.”

The room paused.

Fenril blinked. “He used the blizzard?”

“As part of the process,” Calven said.

The king looked down again. “The man used weather that shut roads and kills livestock as an ingredient?”

“That is one interpretation.”

“It is the only interpretation I like.”

The queen made a quiet sound. It was not amusement. It was the sound of a woman placing one more weight on a scale and watching the table beneath it begin to bend.

The next page involved the taverns. Then the guilds. By then, concern was creeping into everyone’s expressions.

“He bullied two minor guilds out of their monopolies,” the king said.

“Reduced their market power,” Calven corrected.

The king looked at him.

Calven lowered his gaze. “Severely.”

“How severely?”

“They have redirected their attention toward the arena project.”

Fenril seemed relieved. “That may be useful. Arenas produce coin.”

“They also produce corpses,” the queen said.

Fenril hesitated. “Yes, Your Majesty, but taxable corpses.”

The king looked at him for several seconds.

Fenril shifted in his chair. “That came out poorly.”

“No,” the king said. “That came out like several nobles I know. Continue doing that. It is why you are here.”

Calven’s mouth did not move, which the king counted as discipline.

The queen returned to the page. “The guilds lost tavern influence because of his beer and rather than recover their old markets, they turned to public violence?”

“That appears to be the generous reading,” Calven said.

The king leaned back. “And the ungenerous?”

“They were beaten in drink and chose blood, seating, and gambling.”

Fenril made a small sound and hid it behind a cough.

The queen turned another page. This one had the temple seal. Her expression changed.

“He embarrassed the temple.”

Calven took a careful breath. “There was a dispute regarding blessed ale and his beer.”

“I remember blessed ale,” the king said. “Several clerics explained it to me at length. I aged during the conversation.”

Calven continued. “The temple were investigating how his beer doesn’t seem to spoil. The result was unfavourable to them.”

“Unfavourable,” the queen said.

“They blessed his brewery.”

The king looked up.

Calven did not blink. “Publicly.”

The queen looked down again. “How significant a blessing?”

Calven turned one page. “The largest blessing ever recorded for, well anything, according to the temple.”

The room became quiet again.

Fenril, drawn toward error by instinct, said, “That sounds beneficial.”

The king rubbed two fingers against his temple.

The queen answered before he could. “It means the temple cannot easily condemn him without condemning its own blessing. Also it sounds to me like the temple were covering for things they couldn’t explain.”

Fenril’s expression cleared. “Ah.”

“Yes,” the king said. “Ah.”

“Also, the temple wants us to know they want absolutely nothing to do with him in anyway shape or form.” Calvern added with a complicated expression.

“The temple is afraid of him, do you know anyone else the temple fears?” The king asked wide eyed. This got a small shaking of heads.

He took the temple report and placed it beside the magery report. Then he placed the merchant registration beside both.

A commoner merchant. An elder mage. A winter brewer. A man with twelve wagons in his pocket. A public temple blessing. Guilds displaced. Taverns dependent.

And still, somehow, the reports had more pages. Calven moved one sheet forward. The king did not like the way he did it.

“What now?”

“He has put in the paperwork to register his own mage atelier.”

Fenril laughed before he could stop himself. No one joined him.

The queen said, “A commoner can form an atelier?”

“Yes.”

“Registered?”

“In progress. The magery has been made aware.”

The king turned to the magery report again. “With whom?”

“Margaret of the magery is involved. There are references to experimental work, heating stones, and strangely no requests for materials.”

The king read the line on experimental tools and decided, with some irritation, that it was too early in the morning to learn new ways a brewer might become an engineering problem.

Fenril gathered himself. “Can commoners form ateliers?”

Calven said, “Legally, yes, research atliers are not subject to rulers typically.”

Fenril frowned. “Should they be able to?”

Calvern cleared his throat, “We get some say to what the mage’s guild does but normally an atlier is only formed with noble backing, in exchange for oaths from the mages themselves. We don’t know what oaths he requested.”

Fenril sat back.

The queen’s eyes moved across another line. “He bought Baron Isewald’s vineyard stock?”

“All of it,” Calven said.

The king stilled.

“How much is all of it?”

“All of the available stock from the baron’s vineyard.”

“That was not what I asked.”

Calven looked down at his notes. “The exact figure remains unconfirmed. Enough to strain transport. Enough that Baron Isewald’s household appears to consider the matter both profitable and socially awkward. Our estimates are upward of thirty wagonloads.”

The queen’s brow tightened. “Why would a brewer buy that much grape stock?”

“Wine,” Fenril said, as if pleased to have found a safe answer.

The king waited.

Fenril added, less confidently, “Probably.”

Calven said, “Wine is the one assumption. Distillation is another possibility. Trade leverage is another. Control of future tavern supply is another. A social tie to Baron Isewald is also possible.”

The king looked at the page. There it was again. The shape.

Not one act or one mistake. Not even one business doing one business thing. A man had arrived, and wherever pressure had touched him, something had changed direction.

The queen set her report down. “All of this,” she said, “happened in a year?”

Calven nodded. “Less, depending on which starting point we use.”

The king looked at the original merchant registration.

“And he remains under tax exemption.”

No one answered. That was answer enough.

Fenril cleared his throat. “Technically, if he registered properly, then the exemption would still apply.”

The king turned to him.

Fenril looked proud for half a heartbeat, then realised he had said something true in the least helpful possible direction.

“Yes,” the king said. “Technically.”

The word entered the room and made itself unwelcome.

The king stood. He moved over to the window, glancing over the city.

“He did not break the gate,” he said. “He made it irrelevant.”

The queen said nothing.

“He did not overthrow the guilds. He made their old power less profitable.”

Calven waited.

“He did not fight the temple. He made them bless him.”

The king turned back to the table.

“He did not evade the crown. He entered correctly, registered politely, and then became too useful to move easily.”

Fenril shifted. “Could we not arrest him? He must have done dozens of illegal things by now.”

The queen closed her eyes.

The king looked at Fenril with something almost like gratitude. There it was. The noble answer. The clean answer. The answer that turned a knot into a sword stroke and then looked surprised when the rope became five ropes.

“How many riots would you like?” the king asked.

Fenril blinked. “Your Majesty?”

“How many? One large riot? Three smaller ones? A week of tavern unrest? Shall we stop a large portion of the city’s beer supply and see whether labourers become angry or merely annoyed?”

Fenril opened his mouth.

The king continued before sound could become policy. “If we arrest him, production falters. If production falters, taverns complain. If taverns complain, workers complain. If workers complain, employers complain. If employers complain, guilds arrive here and explain loyalty at great length while asking for compensation. Then the temple gets involved, because the temple has already blessed the thing we would be seizing. Baron Isewald may also have a say, because apparently the brewer has just purchased enough grapes to make a vineyard feel understood for the first time in its life.”

Fenril looked down at his hands.

“I only meant that the law may permit it.”

“Yes,” the king said. “That is why the law is not allowed to sit on the throne unattended.”

Calven lowered his eyes to his notes.

The queen reached for the summons copy lying near the edge of the table. “The first summons says spring.”

“It did,” the king said.

Calven looked up. “Your Majesty?”

“Spring is too far away.”

The queen did not argue.

Calven drew a fresh sheet toward him. “When would you prefer?”

“Next week.”

Fenril looked startled. “So soon?”

“A man who became infrastructure in under a year does not get another season to surprise me.”

Calven wrote that down.

The king returned to his chair but did not sit. “Private office. No public audience. No ceremony beyond what has already happened. No temple presence. No guild presence and no noble spectators.”

“Yes, Your Majesty.”

“And he is not to be threatened at the door.”

Fenril looked up again. “Not threatened?”

The king turned slowly.

Fenril chose to continue, because the gods were not always merciful. “If he is dangerous, surely a strong posture is wise.”

“Strong posture,” the king said.

Fenril nodded once, encouraged by the repetition.

The king looked at Calven. “Write down that Lord Fenril recommends frightening the unregistered elder mage who can carry twelve wagons of anything he likes and who has already defeated two guilds, embarrassed the temple, and made half the taverns dependent on his continued goodwill.”

Calven’s pen hovered.

Fenril went pale. “That is not precisely what I meant.”

“No,” the king said. “It is merely what it becomes when placed beside the facts.”

The queen’s mouth twitched once, then settled.

Calven did not write it down. The king noticed and approved.

The king rested both hands on the table. “Men like this do not become safer because frightened officials have been allowed to feel useful.” He said.

Calven nodded. “Then how would you like him handled?”

“Carefully.”

The answer did not satisfy anyone, which was often how the correct answer began.

The king looked at the reports again. Merchant. Brewer. Mage. Supplier. Problem. Asset. Threat. Commoner. Atelier founder. Tax-exempt absurdity in boots.

“First, we find out what he wants.”

Calven wrote.

“Then we find out what he needs.”

The pen moved again.

“Then we find out who thinks they own either.”

The queen nodded slowly. “And if someone already has a hold on him?”

“Then we learn whether that hold is useful, dangerous, or imaginary.”

Fenril, now proceeding with the caution of a man crossing thin ice in expensive shoes, said, “And if he has no obligations?”

The king looked down at the reports.

“All men have obligations,” the king said.

The queen watched him. The king picked up the summons.

“If he has none, then that is the most alarming thing we have learnt.”

Fresh paper was brought. The second summons was drafted with language polite enough not to startle a dangerous man and firm enough not to sound like a request. Calven reviewed the phrasing. The queen adjusted one line that made the crown sound nervous. The king removed another that made it sound pleased.

Fenril suggested adding a phrase about loyal service to the realm. Everyone ignored him, which was efficient.

The first summons had gone out with trumpets, carriage lacquer, and a man dressed as though fabric itself had received a minor title.

This one would go quietly.

Somewhere beyond the palace district, a brewer already held a royal summons that was no longer current, in a city that had still not understood how much of itself had begun to pass through his hands.

“Also,” he said, without turning, “Find out how much beer he actually makes. I am tired of governing words like sort of.”

Calven bowed.

“Yes, Your Majesty.”
