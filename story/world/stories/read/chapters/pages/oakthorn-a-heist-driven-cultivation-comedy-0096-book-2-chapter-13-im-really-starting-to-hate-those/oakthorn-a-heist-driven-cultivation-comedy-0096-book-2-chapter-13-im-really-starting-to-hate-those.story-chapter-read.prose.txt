The forest swallowed Nora and the others whole, a merciful darkness consuming their flight from the mill. The paladin cradled Chubbs against her chest, the child’s weight a familiar anchor as they navigated the treacherous terrain.

Moonlight painted silver edges along both leaf and stone, transforming the mundane woodland into something otherworldly—a fitting backdrop for their company of outcasts and their napping goddess.

Behind her, Rayka supported a limping Orro, while Elena scouted ahead, her nimble form disappearing and reappearing between shadows every few minutes. Cade was behind them, using tiny bursts of his magic to disrupt any evidence of their passage.

Nora’s breaths came steady and fast, her armor—combined with the weight of the dozing toddler—were welcome distractions to focus on. It bested her mind’s traitorous need to hyper-analyze the past day’s events.

“Evsies,” Chubbs mispronounced in the most adorable way possible. Nora watched her rub her eyes and stretched where she was perched in the paladin’s arms.

“Will you do the sings for me, please?” the goddess requested.

Evie’s staff tapped a playful rhythm against the forest floor as she skipped alongside Nora, her natural buoyancy seemingly untouched by the night’s horrors.

“Little Chubbs with golden eyes,” she sang softly, waving her fingers before the toddler’s face, “makes big traps for Arbiter spies!”

Chubbs giggled, clapping her tiny hands. “More!” she demanded, her golden eyes sparkling with delight.

Nora let a smile gather across her lips. She easily ducked underneath an overturned log, her vision more than up for the task of spotting such hindrances, even in the infrequent light of the moon through the foliage.

“Sneaky Cade with core so bright,” Evie continued, casting a mischievous glance at their leader, “burns their asses through the night!”

“Evie!” Nora hissed, though she couldn’t entirely suppress her smile. “Language around Chubbs.”

“What’s an ass?” Chubbs asked immediately.

“It’s a donkey, little one,” Evie answered smoothly. “A very stubborn animal.”

“Like Orro!” the child declared, pointing at the scowling assassin.

The unexpected comparison startled a laugh from Rayka, who quickly covered her mouth when Orro shot her a dark look.

Behind her, Nora heard Cade chuckling evilly, though he didn’t interrupt the fun.

“Birdie,” Nora chided gently, using her pet name for Evie, “perhaps your next verse could be less inflammatory?”

“The paladin fears my wicked rhymes,” Evie whispered dramatically to Chubbs, “but boring words make boring times! Shall we sing of pretty flowers? Or daring thieves with mighty powers?”

“Thieves!” Chubbs chose eagerly.

“Elena picks locks with fingers so quick,” Evie began, but faltered mid-verse.

The transformation was instantaneous and chilling. One moment the siren was smiling, eyes dancing with mirth—the next, all color drained from her face, her pupils dilating until they nearly swallowed the sea-green of her irises.

“Evie?” Nora’s hand instinctively moved to her sword. Her pulse quickened. “Birdie, what’s wrong?”

The siren’s body went rigid, her staff slipping from suddenly nerveless fingers. She swayed, lips parting to draw in a sharp, broken breath. Then her eyes snapped fully open, seeing something far beyond the dark forest surrounding them.

“Blood and fire,” she whispered, her voice hollow. “Burning, burning bright.”

Before anyone could react, she snatched up her staff and bolted into the darkness, moving with unnatural speed that belied her slight frame. The faun head atop her staff flared with sudden, brilliant light, illuminating her passage for a heartbeat before she vanished among the trees and underbrush.

“Evie!” Nora thrust Chubbs into Cade’s arms without hesitation. “Take her. I’ll bring Evie back.”

She didn’t wait for a response.

Nora plunged after the siren with silver-ranked speed that transformed the forest into a blur of shadow and moonlight. Every instinct screamed danger—not just from their pursuers, but from whatever had seized Evie with such terrifying intensity.

The forest closed around Nora like a labyrinth of living shadows, each branch a grasping hand, each root a trap laid for unwary feet. Her enhanced senses stretched to their limit, catching fragments of Evie’s passage—the lingering scent of fear that hung like metallic frost in the air, the whisper of disturbed leaves, a single golden strand of hair caught on thorns.

Panic threatened to rise in her throat—not for herself, but for Evie. The siren’s visions came from the stars themselves, ancient and terrible truths that mortal minds were never meant to bear. Whatever Evie had seen had broken through her practiced serenity, leaving only terror and desperate purpose in its wake.

But damn it all, she was going to get there in time.

Huffing against the weight of her steel armor, she tore through the underbrush.

She entered a small clearing illuminated by moonlight. Nora’s stormy eyes roved over the entire area.

“C’mon, birdie. Where’d you go?” she whispered between sharp breaths.

A flash of radiance ahead cut through her thoughts—Evie’s staff, its power briefly unveiled before darkness reclaimed the forest. Nora altered course, lungs burning as she pushed her body toward its limits. Another flash, more distant. Then another, each one pulling her deeper into the wilderness.

The trail wasn’t random. Evie moved with the terrible precision of one guided by forces beyond her control, drawn inexorably toward something even as her consciousness recoiled from the knowledge.

Nora hurtled through the undergrowth, sword drawn but useless against the true enemy—time itself slipping away with each labored breath. A wall of thorns rose before her, dense enough to halt even her desperate pursuit—until she saw the path carved through its heart, edges still glowing with fading magic from Evie’s staff.

Beyond the severed thicket lay a small clearing bathed in the cold light of stars and a waning moon. The scene that greeted her stripped away years of training and discipline, leaving only the raw humanity that no amount of Fateweaver conditioning could erase.

Blood. The stench of burnt flesh and hair. Soot stains across charred bark.

At the center of the moonlit space knelt Evie, her body curved protectively over another form like a living shield. Nearby crouched Jer, his face transformed by exhaustion and something darker—rage, bare and unfiltered, burning in eyes usually bright with mischief.

Between them lay Gav, motionless, his elegant clothing reduced to tatters, skin blistered with burns so severe they barely resembled human flesh.

“Help him,” Evie pleaded, her voice cracked and raw. Not the musical lilt of a siren, but the broken plea of a woman watching someone she loved slip away. “Please, Nora. Help him.”

Nora crossed the space in three heartbeats, training overriding emotion as she dropped beside Gav’s body. Her fingers found his throat, counting the weak, erratic rhythm of a pulse fighting a losing battle.

His breathing came in shallow gasps, each one a victory against encroaching silence. Where burns hadn’t claimed his skin, fever burned beneath the surface—infection already establishing its deadly foothold.

“What happened?” The question emerged more sharply than she intended, the soldier in her taking command when the woman wanted only to gather them all close and shield them from further harm.

Jer’s laugh was a terrible thing—it was brittle and edged with hysteria. “Rufus happened. That fire-wielding bastard was waiting for us.” His hands clenched into fists, knuckles white with strain. “We were just returning from the outpost—doing exactly what Cade told us to do. No risks, no engagement.”

“Then the flames came.” His voice dropped, eyes unfocused as he relived the moment. “No warning. Just fire everywhere, trees exploding, air burning. Gav—” His voice broke. “Gav shifted. Put himself between me and the worst of it. He was magnificent, Nora. Half-shifted, claws out, fighting fire with fury.”

Evie made a small, wounded sound, her fingers hovering above Gav’s face without quite touching the burned skin.

“We almost had him,” Jer whispered. His eyes were rimmed with red, though from tears or the smoke, Nora couldn’t tell.

“Until Bernard showed up with the rest of his crew,” Jer continued, hatred hardening his features into something almost unrecognizable. “They were celebrating, Nora. Laughing about some fire they set in town, about framing Cade. About how perfectly their plan had worked.”

Nora tore strips from her cloak with practiced efficiency, preparing fresh bandages. “Their plan?”

“The tavern fire was a distraction.” Jer’s voice was flat, emotionless now—a dangerous sign in someone usually so expressive. “They wanted to draw the Arbiters away from the outpost. It worked like a charm, too. Once the garrison responded to the fire, Bernard’s crew hit the outpost and took everything—every single gods-damned mask.”

“Almost everything,” he amended, reaching behind himself to produce a bundle wrapped in bloodstained cloth. “Augustus dropped his pack when the Arbiters caught up to them. I managed to grab this in the chaos.”

A single mask—intricate metalwork and crystal filters designed to protect against whatever poisoned the Blightmarshes.

One mask, when they needed so many.

She couldn’t fixate on the loss now. Not with Gav’s breaths arriving farther and farther apart.

Nora’s hands moved with mechanical precision, cleaning wounds, and applying what meager medicines remained in her pack, all the while knowing the terrible inadequacy of her efforts. Gav needed real healing, not wilderness first aid and desperate prayers.

“He’s so cold,” Evie murmured, finally allowing her fingertips to brush against Gav’s cheek where it remained unburned. “Why is he cold when these burns are so hot?”

The clinical response rose to Nora’s lips—shock, the body’s systems shutting down, blood retreating from extremities—but she swallowed it back.

“His body is fighting,” she said instead, gentler than she would have been moments before. “Conserving strength where it can.”

“He can’t die.” Evie’s voice hardened with sudden certainty. “The stars showed me. He was alive, Nora. In my vision—he was alive.”

The faith in her voice struck at something deep in Nora’s chest. Evie’s gift was real; the visions granted by her connection to Fate were glimpses of possible futures, threads in a tapestry still being woven. But they were rarely simple, rarely literal, and never guaranteed.

Sometimes what the stars revealed was symbolic, sometimes conditional.

Sometimes merely cruel.

“Birdie,” Nora said softly, using the name that had always bridged the gap between them, “we’ll do everything possible.”

The snap of branches at the clearing’s edge sent Nora whirling to her feet, sword drawn in a fluid arc that would have severed any threat that breached their sanctuary. Instead, familiar faces emerged from the darkness—Cade with Chubbs nestled against his chest, Elena a shadow at his side, Rayka supporting Orro’s limping form.

“Holy gods.” The oath slipped from Rayka’s lips as she took in the scene, her practiced composure faltering.

Something transformed in Cade’s expression as he assessed the tableau before him—Gav’s burned body, Jer’s rage-ravaged face, Evie’s desperate vigilance, Nora’s blood-slicked hands. The change was subtle but profound, like watching a master locksmith shift from casual observation to deadly focus. He passed Chubbs to Elena without a word, crossing to kneel beside Gav’s prone form.

“Tell me,” he commanded, the words simple but weighted with authority that brooked no evasion.

Jer’s composure fractured, raw emotion bleeding through the cracks. “It was a setup, Cade. All of it. The tavern fire, the accusations against you—just a distraction to pull the Arbiters away from the outpost.”

“Bernard planned it all,” he continued, words tumbling over each other now that the dam had broken. “We tracked him and his crew back to the outpost, quiet just like you asked. We were going to get a count, map their positions, then report back. But Rufus was not with them. When he headed back, he hit us with fire—so much fire.”

Evie’s hand found Gav’s, fingers lacing through his where burns hadn’t claimed him. “Gav shifted,” she said, pride mingling with despair in her voice. “Protected Jer. Fought back. They almost won.”

“Until Bernard and the others returned from the outpost,” Jer continued, fists clenching with renewed fury. “They’d taken everything, Cade. Every mask, every map, everything that could help navigate the Blightmarshes. They were laughing about it—how they’d framed you for the tavern fire, how the Arbiters would be too busy hunting us to notice what was happening in the marshes.”

“I managed to grab this—one mask. That’s all I could save” he added, indicating the bundle beside him.

“That’s all you needed to save,” Orro countered, his gravelly voice cutting through Jer’s self-recrimination. “One is better than none.”

“And Gav?” Cade asked, his eyes never leaving the telepath’s ravaged form.

Nora met his gaze across Gav’s body, allowing the full weight of her assessment to show. “Critical. The burns are severe, and infection has set in. Without proper healing...”

The unfinished sentence hung between them, heavier than any spoken declaration could have been. They all understood what went unsaid—that wilderness first aid and makeshift bandages wouldn’t be enough. That without intervention they didn’t possess, time was measured in hours, not days.

“He saved my life,” Jer said, guilt threading through his anger now. “Shifted and took the flames meant for me. If I’d been faster, if I’d spotted Rufus sooner—”

“Don’t,” Cade cut him off, the single word sharp enough to sever the spiral of recrimination. “You did exactly what was asked of you. Both of you did. This is on Bernard and his crew. No one else.”

A heavy silence settled over the clearing as the full implications sank in. They had been outmaneuvered at every turn, their advantages stripped away one by one until only desperation remained.

“He can’t die.” Evie’s voice cut through the silence, steel underlying the tremor in her words. “The stars showed me. He lives. He has to live. He’s going to—”

Nora’ heart broke to see her best friend like this. Gently, she placed a single hand on Evelyn’s shoulder. It went unnoticed as the siren continued to rock back and forth.

The certainty in her voice struck at something deep in Nora’s chest—not just fear for Gav, but for Evie herself. The siren’s connection to Fate was both gift and burden; when reality collided with vision, something had to give. Sometimes that something was sanity itself.

Gav’s breathing had grown more labored, each inhale a battle, each exhale a potential surrender. Nora worked mechanically, cleaning burns, applying what meager salves they carried, knowing with crushing certainty that it wouldn’t be enough.

“What do we do now?” Rayka asked, the question directed at Cade but encompassing all of them.

Cade didn’t answer immediately.

His silver-ranked senses would be cataloging every detail—Gav’s deteriorating condition, their dwindling supplies, the hostiles that surrounded them on all sides, the impossible odds that faced them at every turn. His face revealed nothing, but Nora had fought alongside him long enough now to guess at the calculations taking place behind those eyes.

When he finally spoke, his voice carried a control that must have cost him dearly.

“We’ve lost this round,” he admitted, the words falling into the clearing like stones into still water. “Bernard outplayed us completely. He has the masks, the head start, and the Arbiters doing his work for him.” His jaw tightened. “But the game isn’t over.”

It should have been inspiring—the legendary thief refusing to concede defeat.

Instead, the words hung hollow in the night air, contradicted by the evidence surrounding them: Gav’s dying body, Orro’s burns, their dwindling supplies, their lone mask against an uncertain number of enemies who had clearly prepared for this conflict far more thoroughly than they had.

Nora met Cade’s gaze across Gav’s broken form, seeing in his eyes what he couldn’t bring himself to voice aloud—that for perhaps the first time since she’d known him, the legendary thief had no brilliant plan to turn disaster into triumph, no impossible heist to pull them back from the edge of catastrophe.

They were trapped between enemies and a quarantined zone of certain death, with a dying friend and a goddess-child whose powers were as unpredictable as they were terrifying.

Whatever lay ahead in the Blightmarshes was now Bernard’s for the taking, and they had been reduced to nothing more than desperate fugitives trying to survive the night.

The hollow realization settled over the clearing like the mist that crept ever closer from the marshes beyond—cold, insidious, and inescapable.

They had lost.
