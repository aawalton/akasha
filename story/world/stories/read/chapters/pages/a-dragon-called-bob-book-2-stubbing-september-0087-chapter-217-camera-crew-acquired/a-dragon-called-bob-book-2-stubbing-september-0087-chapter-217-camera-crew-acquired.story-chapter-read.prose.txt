“Grahidin!”  I called out as I entered the lodge at the edge of the Mages Guild's grounds.  The evershifting towers and rainbow bridges threw strange multicoloured shadows across the squat building, which were thankfully cut off as the door swung shut behind me.

The array of teas was the same as the last time I had visited, but the room was empty.  I poked at the array of magazines on the coffee table and picked up a copy of Hat’s For the Stylish Wizard and began flicking through.

Luckdire had worn a silly hat, which he mostly used to store fishing tackle, from what I remembered.  The use of the hat as a status symbol wasn’t new to me, but the pointy-and-floppy-brimmed aesthetic that the city mages preferred was far too strange for me.  How were you supposed to take someone seriously when they looked like they were wearing a teepee?

I blew out a breath and tossed the magazine back on the table, then moved over to the array of hot drinks.  Klippergast, Jushire, Fabled Longlenorn, the selection was as varied as I remembered it.  I picked up a sachet and waved it in front of my face, tongue darting out to taste the scent.  Blueberries and… sauerkraut?  Not my cup of tea.

A scream rang out from behind the door that led to the room of nightmares.  I had hoped to avoid having to revisit the leech farm, but the frantic yells suggested Grahidin might need to help.  The door swung open at my touch, and the hall of clear vessels with their swimming lumps of grey and brown flesh greeted me.

“Gerty!  No!  Not like that!”

I hurried forward, keeping as far away from the toothy suckers that plastered themselves against the sides of the jars as I passed.  Angry chicken noises accompanied the ongoing bellows of pain.  Rounding a large container filled with murky water, I stumbled to a stop.

Grahidin was sprawled on the ground, his step ladder lying on its side next to him.  One hand was reaching for his umbrella, which had half-closed, and the rubber ducks that dangled from its edges were all tangled up.

“Baronet!  Help!  Get Gerty off me!”  His other hand was wrapped around the leech, just below the sawtoothed mouth, and he struggled to push it away.

“I’m not touching that thing.”  I shoved his umbrella into his outstretched hand, and it snapped open at his touch.  He shoved it into the mouth, the teeth scraping against the material like nails on a chalkboard.  The flesh began to ooze around, spreading across the parasol, but then it got close to the rubber ducks and recoiled.

Grahidin was still screwed, as far as I could tell.  On his back, he couldn’t get the leverage needed to shove it away.  “If I try and help, Gerty probably won’t like it,” I offered.

“Really?  At this point, I’m not too worried about her emotional well-being, Baronet,” he grunted, trying to keep the umbrella between the maw and his face.

“Mutatio Magnitudine!”  The leech shrank by about five percent.  It went from being overwhelmingly large to merely disturbingly large.

“Don’t use magic on her!”  Grahidin yelled as he managed to heave the leech upwards slightly.

“Why the hell not?”  I carefully avoided actually moving too close and helping.

“It will make her—”

A rumble issued from the leech, and a cloud of grey gas vented out of previously undetectable orifices along the thing’s sides.  I carefully avoided moving backwards as the cloud spread and began to dissipate.  Grahidin gagged and coughed, the umbrella getting pushed back closer towards his face.  His body was mostly wrapped in the grey flesh of the leech already.

“She lives in an academy for wizards.”

“This—erg—place is shielded.  Bloody help me, you fucking idiot!”

That was enough of that.  I stepped forward and punched a fist into the blob of protoplasm, expecting it to burst apart.  I was wearing a relatively cheap tunic, and Gledna was excellent at removing stains from clothes.  Instead of my punch scattering the creature into lumps, my arm disappeared into the mass up to the elbow, and Gerty clamped down.

I backed away and reached out with my unswallowed hand to shove the thing off me.  This resulted in a reprieve for Grahidin and the consumption of my other hand.  The umbrella fell to the ground, and the giant tried to get up, but that toothy maw swung in my direction instead.

“Bad Gerty!”  The maw enveloped my head as I tried to back away, and the rest of the bastard’s body wrapped around my legs.  “I will do unto you as I did unto Umbraxis!” I mumbled into the inside of the leech.

I swung my arms, but Gerty was horrifically flexible.  Angry shouts, muffled by the leech that was now completely surrounding me, sounded as Grahidin attempted to remove my passenger.  The rasping teeth grated around my shoulders, ruining my clothes, but not even scratching my skin.

I closed my eyes, opened my mouth and instantly regretted it.  Gerty did not smell good from the inside.   A small blast of orange-green fire turned my eyelids translucent for a moment, and the pressure suddenly went away.

“You bastard!”  A fist hit the side of my head at the same moment I blinked my eyes open, and I went tumbling across the room.  I rolled to a stop at the foot of a large tank filled with dozens of purple leeches.  I leapt up and grabbed it, holding it in place to still the wobble.

Wails of soul-wrenching misery rang out as I turned, satisfied we weren’t going to spill the contents of my backstop all over the place.  Grahidin was kneeling, hugging what looked like half a large grey blanket to his chest and weeping like a six-year-old with a skinned knee.

Wrath and Vanity, who had been tag-teaming the wall of my sanity, suddenly stopped as a new member of my mental meagerie manifested.  Pity was small and weak in comparison to the big guys that lived in my head, but the fragile pink lizard-thing put a hand on each of his cousin's shoulders, and they both began to calm down.

“I’ll let you off that punch,” I grumbled as I approached the weeping titan.  His massive shoulders heaved as great sobs shook his body.

“She’s dead.  My sweet Gerty.”

“It was a massive fucking blob with teeth.  I don’t want to be unsympathetic… but I will.  Man—giant up.  And wipe your damn nose, you’ve got snot in your moustache.”  While Pity was now a thing in my mind, it was definitely on the lower end of impulses I was subject to.

“Eighteen years.  So long.”  But he did run a sleeve beneath his nose.  Then he looked up at me with murder in his eyes.

“I’d recommend against that as well.  My ARM rating is over nine thousand.  Luckdire checked.  I’m sorry about your pet flesh balloon, and I’d like to point out that if I hadn’t happened to pop by, you’d be being digested right about now.  I’ve got a meeting, the Imperial—”

“Narble and Longlens are awaiting your arrival in the main hall.  Take a right at the door to my lodge and follow the path.  It’s the door with the Unitgryphs on either side.  I—I need to bury my friend.”

The sanity of a man who befriends blood-sucking parasites, especially ones the size of a small car, is understandably questionable.  However, I still felt some semblance of sympathy.  It was like a kid whose puppy had died, but gross and ugly.  And in that metaphor, I’d have been the one who hit said puppy with my car.  Ouch.

“I’m sorry, Grahidin.”

There was nothing else I could say.  His hands shook as he patted at the folds of rubbery skin he had pulled up over his knees.  I walked away.  I followed the directions, doing my best to ignore the acid-trip building and the ever-shifting shadows.

The doors were guarded by a pair of statues.  Chimaeric monsters covered in numbers and letters, the symbols carved into their stone skin.  The statues towered to either side of double doors.  As I stepped between them, a barrier of light sprang up, and I paused.

“Are you worthy to pass?” the pair intoned at the same time.  Their eyes glowed red as their heads swung in my direction.

“Yep”

“No self-doubt detected.  You may pass, traveller.”

“Piss off.”

I shoved the doors aside and stopped into what definitely wasn’t a popular magic academy from Earth literature.  Candelabras floated in the air above me, long ranks of them running down both sides of the impossibly long hall.  Shadowy figures passed in and out of walls, and the images in the paintings on the walls moved, the human figures turning to look enquiringly at me.

“I will shove any talking hats down Daniel Radcliffe's throat, then eat him,” I declared loudly.

The people in the hall stopped for a moment and then went back to discussing house points and an upcoming bout of the squidditch games.  Broomhandles were mentioned, but not in a way I felt comfortable with.

“Baron Bob?” The timid voice came from my right.  A pair of wizards, their hats clearly identifying them as such, rose from a bench.  One of them waved hesitantly at me, then winced and stepped back, bumping his legs against the bench and making enough noise that half a dozen heads turned to glare at him.

“Narble and Longlens?”  I asked.  One was tall, thin and bespectacled.  His robes were a dull green, mottled like military camouflage.  The other was short and rotund in bright red robes.  He looked like a dumpling wrapped in red velvet.  He tipped his head back and winced.

“I can still hear some of your thoughts,” The short one grumbled.

“You’re Narble then.”

“Yes, and I’m not fat, I’m big boned. And you just thought, ‘sure buddy’.”

“You b-bought our c-contracts, we’re yours to c-command,” said the lanky one.  Good lord…

“He thinks you’re ugly,” Narble added helpfully.

“First off all, ugly is a polite way of saying it looks like someone set his face on fire and put it out with an axe.  Secondly, get out of my mind, or…”  I left it hanging, but thought some pretty terrible things, which made the short wizard blanch.

“I know you didn’t mean that.  You’ll kill me if I steal from you, try to sleep with your wife, and a couple of other… your mind is so opaque, it’s hard to see for sure.  And no, neither of us is a kinky weirdo or degenerate.”

“You sure?  Almost everyone I’ve met in this world has been a creepy weirdo.”

“I collect postage stamps, and Longlens has an interest in painting.  Not nudes.  Why does your mind always go to such dark places?”

“I didn’t buy your contracts.”  I decided not to argue the point about the average person I’d encountered in this world.

“A Lady Artington procured our services, but d-didn’t tell us what we would be doing,” Longlens said.

“Making… films?  It is an interesting idea.  Using projection crystals to create entertainment,” Narble said thoughtfully. “We are already contracted to you, Baron!  There’s no need to think such things!”

“Keep it to yourselves.”  I glanced around, glaring at the handful of floppy-hatted idiots still looking in our direction.  “You’re both worryingly normal.  This isn’t going to end well.”
