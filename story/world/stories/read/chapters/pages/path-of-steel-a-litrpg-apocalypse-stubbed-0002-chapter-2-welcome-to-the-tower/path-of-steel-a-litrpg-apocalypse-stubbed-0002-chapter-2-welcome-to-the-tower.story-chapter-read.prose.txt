Gary appeared in a white room which contained only a black, messy desk with a person behind it.

This person wore glasses so slim they were almost non-existent, as well as an old fedora. Their clothes were what a futuristic detective might wear—white shirt covered by a brown coat, with a mismatched tie hanging diagonally from the person’s neck. The desk hid the person’s waist and under.

This person was a robot. A white, humanoid, gear-whirring robot.

Gary’s addled mind could make no sense of this. He stood there like an idiot for less than a second before the robot gestured him closer.

“Hey there, bud, buddy. Gary Steel, right? Can I call you bud? Come in, we don’t have all day.”

Gary inched closer. The robot snapped its slender fingers, and suddenly Gary was standing right in front of the office. “Take a seat,” it said, “but not literally because then the rest of the climbers would have no chair, am I right?”

Gary stared.

“Woah, somebody call the humor police, we got a vandal over here,” the robot said.

“What’s happening?” Gary finally managed to ask. His eyes finally moved on from ogling the robot to find its desk completely covered in documents. Some were gathered in stacks, while others lay there like they’d been blown by the wind, the ones closest to the edge almost tipping over. The robot was rapidly going through them with two of its hands, while another two moved through empty space as if typing furiously. Once the robot was done with a document, it disappeared. Yet, no matter how many the robot cleared, more popped out of thin air to take their place, floating gently to the desk from a foot over it.

Gary looked down at himself, realizing he was naked and half-covered in blood—not his. Vomit rose up his throat but got blocked by something, nearly choking him.

“Sorry, hygiene standards. Those inspectors crawl up your ass faster than Tulumian mites in heat,” the robot said. It snapped its fingers again, and the blood covering Gary disappeared.

“What— What’s going on? Who are you? Where am I? And why am I naked?”

“Nobody wears clothes in the Tower.”

“What!?”

“Just kidding. And here I was hoping you’d stay shocked for the entire thing. Listen bud, you’re like the seventy-thousandth climber I see today, so make this easy for me, please. Relax.”

“Relax? You murdered almost my entire planet, including Clara, and teleported me who knows where to do who knows what, and you are telling me to relax?”

“Hey, don’t make it sound like it was my idea. I’m just an office worker.”

“Well, take your office and ram it up your—”

The rest of the words refused to leave his mouth. The robot pointed up and shrugged. “Profanity guidelines. Can’t beat ‘em. Anyway, yeah, it’s pretty much what you read in that System screen. 99.9% mortality rate, which sucks I guess, but hey, you’re alive.”

“Why did they have to die!?” Gary roared. He tried to rush for the desk, but his legs wouldn’t move.

“Aura resistivity. Not much to be done, sorry. Moving on.” The robot fished a piece of paper from the stack and pretended to read it, though it was visibly blank. “Gary Steel, 23 Earth years old. Achievements, none. Connections, none. Skills, none. Profession, irrelevant. Oh wow, you are making it easy for me. Thanks bud.”

“I’ll jump over there and—”

“Profanity, bud. How many times do I need to say it?”

Gary steamed in his spot. “I do have a profession. I’m a back-end developer.”

“Yeah, says it right here. Irrelevant.”

“It says nothing right there. You’re literally holding a blank piece of paper.”

“Blank? Paper?” The robot looked up. “Sounds like you need some glasses, bud.”

The paper blurred in Gary’s vision. For a second, he saw in its place a tuning fork vibrating without sound, then it was paper again.

“What was that?” Gary asked, stumbling back. “Oh, God. Everyone is dead. They’re all gone.”

“Yeah, it’s terrible. Anyway. You’re about to enter the Tower’s first floor, the Floor of Trials. It’s usually out of order, but they open it up for the drafters.”

Gary locked in. “Trials? What kind of trials?”

“I could tell you, but then I’d need to tell everyone else too, and who’s got the time for that?”

“Tell me!”

“I’m busy, bud. You’ll figure it out. I believe in you.”

All of its fingers rose from the stacks of paper to dart across the air as if typing in invisible screens.

“Wha— Wait, I need something better than that!” Gary shouted.

“Oh come on, where’s your sense of adventure? There’ll be some monsters, a few field bosses, but stick around and you’ll be fine.”

“What!?”

“Oh yeah. Try not to be too spectacular or we’ll talk again, and I don’t think either of us wants that.”

“What?”

“And try not to die, obviously.”

All at once, Gary realized the shock wasn’t gone. He dropped to his knees. “What about my family? Are they alive?” he asked. “That woman I hit. Is she okay?”

“Are any of us okay?” the robot replied. “Alright! All details registered, profile created. Given your rather unremarkable background, your starting equipment will be a random household item, courtesy of the Royal Family.”

“What? Wait! How do I leave this place?”

“I’ll show you out the moment I’m done, trust me.”

“I mean this tower. The Tower. How do I leave?”

“Make it up to the 25th floor. They have exit portals. Shouldn’t take you more than a few years.”

“YEARS? Are you saying I’m a prisoner?”

“Aren’t we all?” the robot replied with a sigh. It snapped its fingers, and the piece of paper it’d been holding dissipated into motes of blue light. “There, all done. I wish you luck. And hey, bud—welcome to the Tower!”

“No wait, I have more questions,” Gary said as a hole appeared under his feet. The last thing he saw before he fell was the robot giving him a tired wave.

As Gary fell out of the white room, it disappeared, leaving him alone somewhere in the void of space. For a moment, the sheer vast emptiness around him filled him with awe and dread. There was nothing for light years around, only distant stars. He was alone. Forever.

He realized he was moving.

Gary was now flying through the galaxy so rapidly stars and constellations zoomed past him, dots turning into lines and blurs. He was trapped in a kaleidoscope. He remained naked.

“They’re all dead,” he muttered, curling up in a fetus position.

Sometime later—maybe seconds, maybe hours—the world slowed down around him. Gary raised his head, and through his tears, found himself dragged towards a massive tower. It hung there, in the middle of all the stars, perfectly unmoving. Endless spaceships of various shapes flocked around it like tiny dots, while tremendous amounts of astral energy flowed into its bottom and out of its top. Those streams of energy were so massive they dwarfed even the tower itself. Gary had no idea what he was seeing. His mind felt on the verge of breaking.

He kept approaching, and the tower kept growing, until it became so large it didn’t fit into his field of vision. It was vibrating, too. A tune that shaped the world. Gary’s eyes were wide as he accelerated towards the black stone that made up the tower’s bottom. He was about to hit it. He screamed.

***

Gary’s eyelids fluttered heavily. He opened his eyes to find a blue sky dotted with little clouds blowing to the right. His back felt soft—curling his fingers, he realized they held grass. He lay in the middle of a forest clearing, surrounded by short, sparse trees.

“Where am I?” Gary wondered, rising to a sitting position and rubbing his head. It all came back at once. The slaughter, the tower, the busy robot. Gary shot to his feet as his heartbeat accelerated. He looked around, finding no danger, and only then realized he was holding something.

“A pencil?” he asked himself, staring at the little thing in his hand. It was a standard-issue model, black and yellow like the ones they used at school. Sharpened, too.

“What’s that doing here?” he muttered.

The robot’s words echoed in Gary’s mind. “You’ll be given a random household item, courtesy of the Royal Family.”

“Well, aren’t you generous?” he asked the sky. “What am I supposed to do with a freaking pencil, write my will?”

No answer came. Instead, it was exhaustion, pulling Gary to the ground again. He let the pencil drop, brought his hands to his face, and wept.

It was only a while later that he felt ready. He stood up again, not forgetting to grab his pencil, and looked at the world around him. The trees were unfamiliar, though similar to the ones from Earth. A few bore white berries. A gentle breeze blew through the forest, rustling Gary’s—

“Oh! I got clothes!”

He quickly inspected himself. Form-fitting white cloth covered his body down to his calves and forearms. He wore shoes too, not quite sneakers but easily as comfortable. A quick check confirmed he had underwear.

That last thing somehow grounded Gary to reality. He was crushed between gargantuan amounts of loss and guilt. His planet had been massacred. Almost everyone he knew was dead. Clara had been twisted into a bloodstain right in front of his eyes. She’d been about to whisper something.

He almost believed it was all a dream, but something inside him knew with grave certainty that it was not.

Opposite that feeling of loss stood something entirely different. Joy. Longing. Those were mixed with guilt, given the circumstances, but just for a moment, Gary felt so glad he’d entered the tower. To his great shame, he felt alive. That missing piece he’d been talking about with Clara—it was right here.

Am I crazy? he wondered, but the feeling was impossible to suppress.

He was excited.

Goddamnit, he thought, sensing his rising grin. God fucking damnit.

“Eva, Mom… Nick will keep you safe for now. I promise I’ll come back for you—all of you who remain. Floor 25, huh?” he promised aloud, then tightened his grip on the pencil. A nagging feeling pressed the back of his mind. The robot had mentioned monsters. He feared for an ambush before realizing this tingling sense wasn’t ominous—it wasn’t instinct, but a calling. He accepted it.

A blue screen popped up, half-transparent so as not to obstruct his vision.

You have been registered with the System. Happy growing!

Gary Steel #00301, Level 0

Floor: 1

Strength: 2

Speed: 3

Vitality: 2

Fortitude: 4

Wisdom: 4

Advance your cultivation to access more System functions.

“Perfect. This makes all the sense in the world,” he complained to the air. When no response came, he grumbled and inspected the screen.

Cultivation was an unknown word. Gary wondered what it could mean before turning to what seemed like videogame stats.

Strength, Vitality, Fortitude… Does it mean mental fortitude? Are these values considered good? My mental stats are better than my physical ones, which checks out unfortunately.

The videogame similarities gave him a sense of familiarity. He fell into a known headspace. Gary basked in the feeling, recovering his mind and sense of safety.

The average has to be a proper number. It can’t be four. If it’s five, that would make me below average in everything… Is it three?

Wait. This is a new world. Could they be using four as the average here? I know they ruined my planet, but this is a whole new level of perversion. They couldn’t possibly be this sick.

He clenched his fist around the pencil.

Can I raise these stats by leveling up? Why am I level 0 instead of 1?

Mid-pondering, Gary realized there was a blinking exclamation mark on the top right side of his vision, so faint it was barely there. When he focused, it solidified, letting him see a rapidly growing notification number on its corner.

“What the heck?” he whispered, willing the notifications open. Whatever this was—the System? The Network? The Tower?—it was so intuitive it barely estranged him.

The exclamation mark unraveled into a massive blue screen that occupied his entire field of vision. It contained rows of text that scrolled endlessly past as new ones appeared faster than the screen could hold. With a mental tug, Gary arrived at the start of this screen.

Marla Jackson #87390: test

Marla Jackson #87390: How does this work?

Jeffrey Higgs #13833: Hey Marla. I believe we can communicate here.

Kristen Decante #56124: BE CAREFUL THERE ARE MONSTERS!

Jeffrey Higgs #13833: I’m in a swamp. Anyone nearby?

Marla Jackson #87390: Hi Jeffrey. I’m in a desert. It’s scorching here.

Phillip Under #08612: What is this?

Marla Jackson #87390: Phillip, I believe this is a chatroom-like service anyone on the floor can use. It will soon become useless. If anyone is in the desert, please come find me. I’ll head towards the stone spiral.

Jake Anchorville #98001: EVERYONE IS DEAD!

Jeffrey Higgs #13833: I see the desert. I’m coming.

The top of the screen said Floor Chat. What Gary had read was the beginning of the conversation—soon after those messages, a hundred thousand people discovered it at once, and the chat became impossible to read.

Gary Steel #00301: Hey, I’m in a forest.

Gary watched his message disappear the instant he sent it. The chat was flooded by people warning about monsters, copy-pasting their location endlessly, or screaming grief into the void. Of those who broadcasted their location, nobody mentioned a forest.

“Perfect,” he said, already tired. He closed off the chat and wished for the notifications to stop arriving, which did happen. “Nice UI… The typing was fluid, too. I did it with my thoughts like some sort of weird VR. Is this a virtual world? Some government experiment?” he muttered, hoping with all his heart that it was true, yet knowing with certainty that it was not.

Suddenly, he froze. The hairs on his neck were standing. Though the notifications were gone and the system screen closed, that nagging feeling from before remained. He turned to find a set of red eyes peering at him from inside a bush. Something jumped out at him, alongside a blue screen that didn’t obstruct his vision. If anything, the blue screen shot directly into his head, so he absorbed the information in an instant.

Machete Squirrel, Level 1

What do you get when you take a serial killer, shrink them, and make them cute? That’s right. Ma-che-te Squi-rrel!

They use tiny sharp instruments, so they probably want to trim your toe nails. You should lie down and let them. Unless you’re carrying a bagpipe, in which case they’ll leave you alone.

I don’t have a bagpipe, was Gary’s first thought.

The squirrel launched itself out of the bush and into the air, its little hands holding an honest-to-god, squirrel-sized machete. Its eyes were bloodshot, and its mouth had managed to warp into a sadistic grin that completely ruined any cuteness the fluffy tail created.

As the squirrel sailed through the fifteen feet of air separating them, Gary first froze, then tried to step back and tripped. He fell to the ground as the squirrel fell on him. Its machete—the size and sharpness of a steak knife, way less tiny than the description wanted him to believe—grazed his forearm, drawing a thick line of blood. Gary had barely managed to roll away in time.

As he crawled away, the squirrel locked eyes with him and licked the bloody edge of its machete. Its eyes lit up even more.

“I’ll fooking carve ya,” the squirrel said.

“Why are you Scottish!?”

It charged.

Gary glanced at the blood dripping from his forearm, then the charging squirrel. He growled. His hand slapped for the machete, but the squirrel was faster, ducking under it and slashing again. A sting came from Gary’s ribs. He turned—faster than he expected—and fell on the squirrel, which wasn’t quick enough to escape. “Oi,” it said. He pinned it to the ground with one hand and punched it with the other. Repeatedly. It went limp quickly.

The little machete stuck out of Gary’s arm.

“Crap!” he said, pulling it out. Blood flowed thick. His eyes widened. “Shit!”

Grabbing onto the wound, he noticed light in the corner of his vision. Both squirrel and machete went up in blue smoke. He groaned. “I wanted that!”

Over the squirrel’s body, however, something was off. A strange humming sound reached Gary’s ears, and the air shook a little. A stream of power entered his body, healing his latest and most critical wound, as well as the blood lines from where the woman in the cafe had raked him, but the healing was just a byproduct. Most of the power remained inside him. He could sense it, spreading around his body as a warm, tingling sensation. Gary had the odd sense that he was an empty container filling up.

Is this like a game? he wondered. Do I get stronger from beating monsters?

Forgetting all about wounds, Gary clenched his hand. He felt…marginally stronger. The change was so subtle he wasn’t sure whether he was imagining it.

“Status!” he cried out, opening his status screen.

Gary Steel #00301, Level 0

Floor: 1

Strength: 2

Speed: 3

Vitality: 2

Fortitude: 4

Wisdom: 4

Advance your cultivation to access more System functions.

“Well, that’s just unfair.”

His level and stats hadn’t changed, but that wasn’t right. He clenched his hand a few more times. He was slightly stronger—maybe not enough to tick up the stats? Maybe one monster wasn’t enough to level up?

He could still sense the strange energy in his body. It was definitely present, but it just sat there being useless.

At least it had turned his wounds into little more than scratches.

“Did I do something wrong?” he wondered aloud, but there was no one to answer. Then it struck him.

Gary Steel #00301: Guys, I beat a Scottish monster and I think something like energy entered me, but I didn’t level up. It just healed me. Does anyone know what’s going on?
