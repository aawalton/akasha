[Time]: Twenty-three years, concurrent[Location]: The court that was never convened

The gavel was gone. The ring of dry marble had not moved.

[Exhibit E]

Ovelia had gone missing. She had fallen asleep in flight over the Sky Sea and gone down through it, and out of the sky, and into the ocean, and had continued down until a trench received her, where she slept for seventy-nine days. The Council had run without her, cleanly, the way it always did. She walked back into the chamber and went straight at the Grand Witch who held the Treasury.

"Cat was gone that long and you didn't come looking for Cat?"

Almost every seat in the chamber spoke for her. Famia Schüder went first, which carried weight, because Famia Schüder had never once said a word about Ovelia that was not devout. The Fifth Seat had carried the administration through the entire absence without a lapse. This was not the reception she had earned. Marianne Horton took the edges off it. Paddy did not.

The First Seat rejected all of it at volume, lost interest within a few minutes, and moved on to the agenda. Nobody thought anything of it. That temper went as fast as it came, and everyone in the room had been under it at some point. Irene said nothing at all.

Some weeks later Irene sat down opposite five Time Dragon Witches and smiled at them.

"I am told you forge the finest contractual instruments in the world. Funding is not a consideration. I would like your best work."

One of them asked what the terms were, and Irene took a quill and wrote them out in full. The ink ran smooth. Her hand did not stop anywhere.

They took the parchment and read it, and something moved across five faces in sequence, the way it does when professionals discover what they have just been asked to build.

One of them went back through it a second time and asked, with enormous care, whether the latitude left to the servant in the fourth clause and the ninth was an oversight.

"No," Irene said.

The rest of the meeting stayed on materials and delivery dates. What it was for, who it was for, why an instrument of that severity left the servant so much room: five of the finest contract-forgers alive held all of it behind their teeth and quoted her a price. Irene saw all of it and did not care.

In time they delivered the masterpiece of five careers. Irene listened to the operating notes, thanked them, and settled the invoice.

Ovelia had taught her the craft herself, in a warded sanctum, with a dark flame and a winter cloak and a pebble she had doodled on while she did it. A finished instrument at the top of an art is a lesson with the answer written into it.

Irene took it into a sealed and empty room, put it on the table, and was still looking at it when the phantom sat down across from her. "What a cute little collectible."

"It is a prototype."

The phantom folded her arms and shivered, elaborately. "You are such a pervert."

"Mm," Irene said. "However did you know I want you crying and begging me?"

"Your possessiveness is like a monster. It's revolting."

Irene tipped her head and smiled. "Say it again. You give me so little." Her gaze travelled down the phantom and came back up.

The phantom recoiled half an inch. Her tail puffed to twice its width, her ears went flat into her hair, and then she came forward again to put more venom into it. "There cannot be anyone in this world more perverse than you. To actually want to use a slave contract on your own master."

The smile went off Irene's face. What was left was flat, and very calm. "I looked for you. Seventy-nine days, every place I knew and every place I could reach, with an Aspect in my chair and my whole network running a single query. Your luck held the water quiet over that trench the entire time, because a long sleep was what you wanted.

"And then you came back and asked the chamber why nobody looked. It is entirely at your discretion, and there is nothing in this world I can put in the way of it."

She turned the parchment a quarter turn on the table, squaring it to the edge. "Yet."

"You truly believe you can cage Cat, stupid girl."

"The real one will be written in root code, and nobody but the two of us will ever be able to use it. You will keep your claws, and all the room you could want for them. The one thing you will not have is the door."

Irene's hand went out across the table toward the shape in the chair. The chair was empty before she got there, and her palm came down flat on the parchment, over the blank signature line. I have been much too good to you.

The blood in the courtroom rose to her waist and parted around her in a perfect ring.

[Exhibit F]

Emilia Lucas was an exit: holder of the Axiom of the Water Dragon, head of the Lucas dynasty, a few years older and no more than that. Below the High Council there was no better match anywhere in the Inner Sea, and perhaps two that could be argued to equal her.

Emilia brought the masterpiece of her life and set it down, and her face carried the raw, unprotected nerves of a first love. She had already spoken to her family, all of them, and she said Irene could have everything.

Irene listened, and the phantom arrived and stood between them. "She's confessing to you," she said, reproachful. "And you're distracted."

You are the one who insisted on coming. The phantom's mouth went sulky. "Then say yes to her. Say yes and I won't come anymore."

Irene did not blink. "I am very sorry. Emilia."

"May I ask why?"

"There is nothing lacking in you," Irene said. "I cannot pay for it."

Emilia's lips trembled once. She got them under control, and she nodded. "My oath does not change. Then at least, please, accept this as a friend."

Irene accepted it. She opened her own vault and returned an artifact of exactly equivalent value.

When Emilia was gone, the phantom drifted down off the desk. "Stupid girl. You are in a great deal of trouble now."

The starry black eyes had come alive with it. "A curious, intelligent woman is the most troublesome object in this world, because they can always turn up a secret the way a dog turns up a buried body."

"And what does that have to do with me?" Irene said. "Isn't the handling of secrets your work? You were always so enthusiastic about it.

"Let her dig. Let the continent take sides and the purges start early. I would come out of that fire higher than I went into it."

Her voice went down, level and thin. "You struck your name off every line of my record, so that the world looks at me and sees a woman who made herself. What it bought was time.

"She has just put a date on the end of it. I am not finished. I need a world where every way out you have ends in my hand."

The phantom drifted to the window and draped herself along the frame. "And once you have it. What is it you mean to do with Cat then?"

Irene could not say it. "You are a hallucination."

The phantom sighed. "Coward. You cannot put it into words in front of a thing that is not even here, and you believe you are going to do it."

"You are my hallucination," Irene said. "Say something I want to hear."

The phantom came off the glass. Everything she held rooms with had gone out of her face, and what was under it was pale and plain and grieving.

Her mouth came to rest a breath from Irene's ear, and the scent arrived with her: green grapes, and something burning underneath them.

"I love you, Irene." Irene's heart missed.

"I take you." The voice went in through her ear and into the bone. "In the dark you were given, and in the dark I have kept you. I take every drop of blood you poured and every silence I asked you to carry. Under no law but the one between us, I take you as mine and give you what is left of me, from the first time you called until the sky goes out."

"And your cup will run over. Through all the lives you will ever live, and every death you will ever die."

A hand came up toward Irene's cheek, and Irene took her face out from under it before it could arrive.

Irene was shaking. "She would never say that to me," she said. "She would never let herself. You are not her. Get out."

The phantom's hand stayed in the air. Her lips parted, and nothing came, and then she was gone, exactly as she had gone all those years ago.

Irene sat down and went back to the draft in front of her. It was the nineteenth she had written, and it would not hold her either.

[Exhibit G]

A desk. A stack. The card had been printed: [The Witch of Eternal Slumber · Ovelia], Starlight Foil.

A likeness of that woman carried an echo of her in it, the way a cloud leaves the shape of itself on the water for a while after it has passed. Witches are a shameless species, and an image at that fidelity works in both directions: a witch could stand a rite on it and draw down whatever answered, and the woman in the ink could look back out whenever the whim took her. The face in the picture hears its own name spoken. Ovelia had spent a century keeping herself out of circulation and had explained it to nobody.

Irene had raised it once, years earlier, over a card game. Some time after that, the woman who would not be photographed let the universe print her face. There was one obvious way to join those two facts together, and it was the single conclusion Irene had no way to hold.

She pulled it out of the first pack she opened, on the day of release, and three days later the window in the middle of the card began to close. The stars went first. Then the shape reclining among them. Then the script along the top. What was left was a rectangle of solidified void that took the light off her fingers and gave nothing back. What was in it could not hold still under her attention. Irene entered it as a refusal, and filed it with the others.

If it would not stay with her, it had no business staying anywhere else, and she had the wealth of a continent to see to that. She bought the copy off the nearest counter, and then the copies in the next shop, and then every copy that surfaced on the continent for twenty-three years, and she bought sealed product by the case and opened it herself to take them before they ever reached a shelf. A few days after each one came into her hands the window would begin to close. She opened the next case.

The first run went out within a year of the doors closing. Twelve years later, in the season Irene took her seat, the publisher announced a reprint on grounds that were commercial, documented, and would survive an audit. The likeness was Ovelia's to license, and she licensed it again.

The ledger was current to the hour, because she kept it herself, and it ran in two columns. Two thousand two hundred and seventy-four out of packs she had opened with her own hands. Six hundred and eleven bought back off the market.

The publisher's printed rate was one card in five thousand packs. Irene's was one in one, and she had never been able to establish whether the first figure was a consequence of the second.

The first purchase had cost four thousand two hundred Solars, which was a figure a schoolgirl could reach by going without lunch for a season, and the curve out of it ran smooth and steep and had never once corrected: not through the reprint, which should have halved it, and not through the two market contractions that took the rest of the collectible index down with them.

The market price of the Ovelia Starlight Foil had reached six hundred and twenty-five thousand Solars, because the market had worked out that somewhere there was a buyer who would absorb the entire supply forever, at any price, without ever explaining why.

Every trader pricing the foil was pricing a durable asset, two thousand eight hundred and eighty-five counterexamples lay in a vault under the house in Selvantara, and the only witness to what they did was the woman underwriting the market. Sixty of them sat squared into a neat stack on a gold desk: that month's.

The blood in the courtroom stood at her ribs. The dry cylinder held.

[Exhibit H]

The banquet.

Two rounded nails, drawn slowly across the center of Irene's open palm, and she knelt for it. A duchess greeting her monarch was a position with walls, and it was the only distance in that room that had a name.

Then Akkukataya took the opening.

Intimate friend and comrade of the Great Ovelia.

Its whole value was that it could be said out loud in a room. Everything Irene owned, she owned in silence.

Her thumb had been pressed into the center of her own palm for some time before she noticed doing it, and the phantom arrived without being asked. "Akkukataya is still so good at pushing her luck."

"You like it when she does that."

The phantom's face became that of a cat suffering an enormous and public injustice, and her voice went up. "She's retribution! Cat told you that from the beginning!"

"And you keep her beside you. One day she will call you darling, and you will not correct her."

The tail lashed. "The mouth is on her face. Cat cannot sew it shut." And then, muttered, at the ceiling: "She wouldn't dare."

"When you first met her," Irene said, "you also did not expect her to ask you to kick her in the face."

The phantom's ears went flat. She examined the ceiling, and then the floor, and then several other things, none of which were Irene, and Irene tracked every degree of it out of the corner of her eye.

Irene waited. The phantom stayed where she was.

"When I come for that chair, she will stand with you to the bitter end."

"And then you will kill her, my dear stupid girl."

"No." Irene kept her eyes on the room. "She will live, and she will keep everything she has, and she will hold all of it from my hand. One more reason for you to sit still in the cage."

"You know what is left of a songbird when you close your hand that hard," the phantom said, gently.

"So suppose there is no cage. Suppose the chair stands empty and Cat is simply gone. What becomes of them then?"

Irene's fingers moved once at her side. "Then I will take this continent apart until—"

"And I come home to that." The voice stayed exactly where it was. "My cities burnt. My cats starving in the alleys. Every name in this room face down in the dirt. I would look at you one time, and you would never see my face again."

Across the hall Josephine Durant held the archway with half the room listening to her, and Allison stood where the First Seat was meant to stand, and Famia Schüder carried the continent's honour on her back the way she had carried it for six decades.

Irene went along the row of them and priced the alternative.

"Then they will thrive." It came out flat. "Ailuronia entire. Famia. Josephine. Akkukataya. Every Lantern Cat in this world, down to the ones who still bully you for fish. Endowed and garrisoned and defended at my expense." Her nails worked into her palm, and the skin knitted under them as fast as she opened it. "Everything you ever spoke well of, kept.

"And when you come back to look at it, you will have to look at me."

The phantom stood very still.

"A golden age, the like of which this world has never once been given."

A hand came up and closed around the shape of Irene's fist, a hair above the skin.

"You are holding the whole sky hostage to a homecoming you invented, darling. You hate me because a hallucination cannot hold your hand."

Irene stood where she was with her hand open and unmarked at her side, and the banquet went on around her, and every name she had just underwritten laughed at something out of earshot.

The blood in the courtroom reached her collarbone. The dry cylinder around her narrowed.
