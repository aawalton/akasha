Gilbert closed his eyes, but did not sleep. After the short clash, the cell had gone quiet. His new neighbor sat by the door, pressing his injured arm to his chest and breathing angrily, but he was clearly behaving more carefully now.

That suited Gilbert. He lay on the narrow bunk, feeling the cold stone against his back, and slowly steadied his breathing. His body hurt, his nose stung, and the foreign core beat unevenly under his collarbone. Sometimes its rhythm differed so sharply from his own that it felt as if an argument had started inside him, and neither side intended to give in.

But there was no time to rest. Lir was below. The infirmary connected the lower level with the intake block. Runic doors were opened by local mages. All of it already had a shape, but it still gave him no way out. Even if Gilbert managed to reach Lir and get him out of his cell, even if they managed to take a runic mage hostage and force him to open every passage for them, the Castle would still be surrounded by mists. The guards crossed through them only with the proper protection. Choosing the wrong type of protective suit on a specific day meant death.

He and Sakura had agreed on the most important thing in advance: the third prison day would be the day of the escape, if everything went at least half according to plan. Gilbert had to find out from inside what protection would be needed by that day and send a sign outside. There was a workshop in Greymost where the prison’s protective sets and materials were repaired. Sakura only had to reach it. But without knowing the mist, even the most successful theft could prove useless. Sakura could not steal every color at once. There was not even a guarantee that all of them would be there, which was another risk in their plan, though an unlikely one, and one for which Gilbert had found a “Plan B” even before getting into the prison.

But right now, what they needed specifically was the forecast. The mist forecast. Gilbert knew the Castle had one, otherwise the guards would not be able to prepare suits in advance. But he still did not know where exactly that forecast appeared, who received it, when it was updated, or by what route the order went to the suit service.

Closer to evening, footsteps and bowls rattled in the corridor. The hatch in the door opened with a dry metallic click, and two plain bowls of thick gray stew, two pieces of dark bread, and wooden spoons with edges so dull it would have been hard to harm even one’s own pride with them were pushed into the cell. The door itself did not open.

Gilbert noted this without interest. The food was being delivered by an ordinary young servant who had come without a runic mage. That meant there were probably no rune locks on his route from the kitchen to the cells. This was useful information, because where there was a kitchen, there was usually a storeroom with supplies. And a storeroom with supplies often had a specific service entrance where wagons from the city delivered food. One of the escape routes out of the prison. For now, Gilbert could not be sure that door had no runic protection. Still, he marked that retreat route in his mind.

His neighbor looked at Gilbert’s bowl with a hungry stare, but then he looked at his own hand. The desire was immediately visible, and so was the calculation. A big man, a small cell, two portions of food, and one sick, exhausted mage on the bunk. Simple arithmetic.

"Try it," Gilbert said quietly, without opening his eyes.

The neighbor froze, thought for a little while, and then slowly pulled only his own bowl toward himself. That was the most correct choice he could possibly have made.

Gilbert ate a little, so his body would not become completely useless, and set the rest aside. The stew was tasteless, but it was warm, and right now that mattered.

Night in the intake block turned out louder than he had expected. The prison did not sleep. There was always some coughing, someone arguing, or doors and hatches opening and closing. One guard complained that he had gotten a bad shift, while another laughed at a prisoner from the neighboring corridor who had somehow managed to ask for a second portion. Then Gilbert heard a conversation about the already familiar healer, and his reports were mentioned with such anger that the old man seemed to be not a healer, but a separate kind of punishment.

Most of what he heard was worth nothing, but Gilbert listened anyway.

Closer to deep night, two men passed through the corridor. One of them spoke irritably and dully, as if he were trying not to wake the entire block, but doing a poor job of it.

"The storeroom will be stalled until noon again tomorrow."

"It’s always like that after the measurer," the second answered. "Not the first time."

"What do I care? I’ll be standing in the outer yard. Until the chief signs the main sheet, the suit service won’t issue anything, and then everyone runs around like they’ve been scalded."

"They’ll tell us what to take before the shift anyway."

"Before the shift, sure. Very convenient when the filters haven’t been checked yet."

"Quiet. If you want to argue with the chief, go argue with him."

The footsteps moved away, and Gilbert sharply opened his eyes. The echo of that dialogue was still sounding in his head.

There it was. From the context, the chain of events could be assembled well enough.

The mist forecast was not handed out immediately. First, the head of the guard received it. Then he put his seal on it, and only after that did the orders go farther, to the storeroom, the suit service, and the shifts. Ordinary guards received exactly as much as they needed before going out, while the full sheet stayed higher up.

But the head’s office was an almost useless target, because it was certainly too well protected. A simple prisoner had almost no way to get there. The path between the seal and execution looked much weaker. Simple logistics. Any system became more vulnerable when it started moving.

Toward morning, the dead mage’s core under Gilbert’s skin fell further out of rhythm.

At first, the mage thought he had simply dozed off badly, but then he realized he could not breathe normally. Something jerked under his collarbone, his chest grew tight, and the fingers of his right hand went numb. He slowly sat up on the bunk and pressed his palm to the stone so he would not fall.

The neighbor by the door lifted his head. The cell was dark, but Gilbert still felt his gaze. Cautious at first, then hungry. Big men often believed too strongly in simple moments of weakness.

"Stay seated," Gilbert said.

His voice came out quiet and hoarse, but level and metallic enough. And it worked. Hearing the strength in that quiet, slightly broken voice, the neighbor did not move.

Gilbert waited out the attack, counting the beats of his own pulse and the foreign, uneven pulse of the core beneath his skin. He already knew how this could be used. From the outside, it looked like the consequences of interrogation: weakness, pain, nausea, bad breathing. No one in the Castle should see anything else.

In the morning, they came for him before breakfast was brought.

"Interrogation," the guard said.

Gilbert did not rise at once, demonstrating the weakness in his body. That was also useful for the overall picture.

The interrogator waited for him in the same room. The folder on the table was thicker than before. That meant the soapworks had already been checked.

"Northern soapworks," he said without greeting. "Old basement under the drying hall."

Gilbert remained silent.

"Who managed to leave?"

Silence.

"What is the lower group?"

Gilbert looked at the edge of the table, where the wood had darkened from old stains.

"How many points remain in Greymost?"

He stayed silent again.

The interrogation moved quickly. Questions returned from different angles and changed order. The interrogator clung to words Gilbert had already said. The pain returned too, but now they did not want to break him too badly. Now he was even more useful, because he had already given them a piece of information they needed.

When the guard struck him under the ribs, Gilbert allowed himself to bend a little more than necessary. He entered the next question already short of breath.

"Next point," the interrogator said. "Where?"

Gilbert stayed silent for a long time. Then he rasped, "There is a sign among your finds."

The interrogator froze for only a second.

"What sign?"

"I don’t know how your clerks recorded it. To them, it could have been trash. A scrap of cloth, a candle stub..."

"Describe it."

"I don’t even know what exactly it is. I wasn’t the one who chose it. And if it could be described, I would describe it."

The interrogator leaned closer.

"Do not play with me."

Gilbert raised his eyes to him.

"I told you the truth. The second point can be found by the sign. No other way."

It did not work immediately. First, the interrogator demanded the inventory and ordered several lines read aloud. Gilbert listened and rejected all of them. Eventually, the interrogator stood.

"If you are wasting time, I will know."

They led him to the evidence room through corridors where the air became drier. The dampness of the prison block gave way to the smell of wax, paper, and ink. Different people walked here, not dressed in the same rough uniform as in Gilbert’s block. They more often carried folders and wore glasses. Doors opened faster, but not more simply. Some had no runes, but clerks with keys and lists stood nearby. After the measurer, the administrative wing was nervous and alive.

The evidence room was narrow, but tall. Closed cabinets and boxes stood against the walls. The keeper, a dry man with a long face, greeted them with open displeasure.

"Hands behind your back," he said to Gilbert at once. "Touch nothing. Look from here."

Gilbert had no intention of arguing. One by one, they opened boxes in front of him, containing candle stubs, cheap reagents, scroll scraps, and many other useful or useless things. All of it was kept at a distance from the mage and sometimes lifted with tongs.

So far, nothing had happened that could help Gilbert in his plan, so he simply dragged out the time, studying each object before his eyes for a very long while.

"Not that."

The next box.

"No."

The keeper exhaled irritably.

"Do you at least know whether it is large or small?"

"If I knew, I would say."

The interrogator stood nearby and grew darker with every minute, risking turning into a thundercloud.

Gilbert supposedly almost recognized one fragment, asked them to turn it, then shook his head. He asked them to open another bundle. Then another. He dragged it out, but did not overdo it.

At some point, a door in the neighboring corridor opened. Gilbert did not turn his head, but caught movement from the corner of his eye. A young servant entered the head of the guard’s office with a thick folder in his hand. That was already much more interesting. But Gilbert could not show his interest openly, so for now he asked to see another box and examined its contents for a long time.

When the office door opened again, he only managed to see the edge of a desk, the shoulder of some clerk, and a gray wall. No clues about the mist forecast. And the door closed too quickly for him to see anything else.

The plan was catastrophically ruined. At that moment, Gilbert allowed himself real irritation for the first time. If all of this turned out to be for nothing, if he could not find what he needed and send a sign to Sakura before the end of today...

But then Gilbert saw the same servant coming out of the door, now with another folder pressed to his chest. A seal darkened on the top cover. A service tag with the embroidered sign of the suit service hung on the side. The servant walked with nervous speed.

From those signs, Gilbert guessed that the servant was most likely carrying a copy of the forecast for other departments. That was what the mage needed.

"I didn’t find it," Gilbert rasped.

The interrogator slowly turned to him.

"What?"

"The thing I need is not among these. Or your people did not take everything."

The evidence keeper straightened, offended.

"Everything seized was entered into the list."

"Then the sign is not here," Gilbert said, and let his shoulders sag slightly. "Take me back. I feel sick."

"He always feels sick when it becomes inconvenient," the interrogator threw out.

But the guards still took Gilbert under the arms. One too firmly, the other irritably and without caution. They led him into the corridor at the exact moment when the servant with the folder had almost come level with them.

Gilbert drew a breath. Then he stumbled. His breathing broke, his knees buckled, and his body sharply lurched to the side. He grabbed the nearest guard’s sleeve as if trying to hold on and put his full weight into the fall. The guard had not expected a sideways pull. He jerked, stepped across the narrow passage, and slammed his shoulder straight into the servant.

The servant cried out and dropped the folder, falling back with it. The cover sprang open, a sheet slid halfway out and skidded across the floor with the colored side up.

Red. Green. Blue. Three marks for three days.

The servant rushed to the sheet almost on all fours and shoved it back into the folder.

"Close it!" the interrogator barked.

"I... he..."

"Pick him up!" one of the guards shouted about Gilbert. "He’s going to die right here."

They hauled the mage up. His vision darkened, and this time almost all of it was real. A strong burn flared under his collarbone. Gilbert did not look at the folder again. He simply hung in the guards’ arms like a man who no longer cared where he was being dragged.

"Take him to the healer?" one asked.

"To the healer. We still need him alive," the interrogator snorted with contempt.

Gilbert lowered his head, but in his mind, debates and rearrangements of the escape plan were already underway. Red was first. That meant the current day. Green was tomorrow. Blue was the day after tomorrow.

The day of the escape meant green mist. He had half a day left to send this to Sakura, and one night for her to use the sign. If everything fell apart, they would have to move it to the next day and hope the core lasted until blue.

Now he needed only one thing.

Get this information outside.
