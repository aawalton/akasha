Gorvak Ashhide didn’t expect the slap that sent him flying barely an instant after he attacked the newbie.

At the thirty-second tier, he stood squarely in the middle of the outer sect’s power hierarchy. An uninitiated greenhorn shouldn’t have been able to defeat him at all—let alone so effortlessly. Newbies were supposed to be weak, wide-eyed, and stupid. That was why he picked on them. That was why he’d singled the boy out.

So someone must have intervened.That was the only conclusion that made sense.

His ears rang as he dragged himself out of the clutter he’d crashed into, the amused gazes of dozens of disciples pressing down on him like physical weight. Heat flooded his face. But the moment his eyes found the cloaked boy again, that humiliation sparked into righteous indignation.

“Who hit me?” he snarled, sweeping a glare across everyone standing near the boy, certain that it must have been one of them. “Which one of you dares?”

“I did,” the newbie replied, his face hidden behind a rippling veil of storm. “But in all fairness, you attacked me first.”

Gorvak froze. Then he looked around—and this time, the silence told him everything. It was true. The shame burned deeper, sharper, ripping through his ego like a flaming blade. Gorvak did the only thing he could do to save face.

He responded with rage.

“How dare you!” White lightning burst from his core, sheathing his arms in jagged spikes as bloodlust rolled off him in suffocating waves. His fingers clamped around the hilt of his sword—and he lunged at Aodhán once more.

He had taken barely three steps when a voice thundered from deep within the shop.

“This is not a dueling ring, Gorvak. Take your fighting outside.”

Gorvak skidded to a halt. For a heartbeat, fear widened his eyes—then his expression hardened into petulant fury as a massive woman strode from her office, soot streaking her face, auburn hair still crackling faintly with lightning.

She stood nearly seven feet tall, with a face as hard as stone and a core blazing like a living pyre. At the sixty-fourth tier, she was—without question—one of the strongest cultivators Aodhán had yet seen in the outer sect, and that power was not hidden.

It pressed down on the room like invisible heat, and the moment she arrived, disciples rushed out of the shop in fear, while staff members who had been slouching or sitting lazily immediately jumped to their feet.

Aodhán himself straightened instinctively, his eyes narrowing as he observed the woman with open curiosity. He had no idea how the sect categorized its members, but from what Rhetta had told him, he assumed it was based on power and potential—both of which this woman clearly possessed.

Her core wasn’t nearly as bright as his own, but it was bright enough that Aodhán would have pegged her as an inner sect—or even a core sect disciple. The fact that she hadn’t made it into either made Aodhán wonder just what sort of trial a disciple had to pass to make it out of the outer sect.

But he didn’t have a chance to think much about it before the woman boomed again. “I won’t repeat myself, Gorvak. Pay for the blade and get out before I throw you out myself!”

Gorvak fumed, his entire body shaking with humiliation and rage. Every eye in the shop was on him. Laughing at him. Mocking him.

He couldn’t take it. He was losing too much face.

He glanced back at Aodhán, fury burning in his gaze, and in that moment, his humiliation fermented into something poisonous. He jabbed a shaking finger toward Aodhán, lightning crackling. “You think this is over, greenhorn? I challenge you to a duel!”

Gasps echoed around the shop, but Aodhán wasn’t too surprised. As much as he would have loved to avoid it, he’d seen this coming from a mile away. He could feel dozens of gazes settle on him, waiting, watching, expecting him to decline.

He would lose face if he did—but Aodhán didn’t care about face. Whatever he lost now, he would regain a thousandfold when he was eventually proclaimed a transmigrant. He had nothing to lose from declining the challenge and absolutely nothing to—

His eyes drifted, unbidden, to the crimson sword clenched in Gorvak’s fist, and in that moment, he realized he did have something to gain.

A slow smile spread across his face, and he gave a small, deferential nod. “I accept.”

Gorvak snarled and began storming toward the exit. But just as he reached the door, Aodhán called after him. “What do I gain if I win?”

Surprised gasps and murmurs swept through the shop—followed by Gorvak’s furious growl. “Don’t bother. You won’t win!”

“But what if I do?” Aodhán pressed calmly. “Do I get your blade?”

Gorvak’s grip tightened around the weapon with a surge of killing intent, hatred roiling off him in dense waves. He glanced at the other disciples, feeling the weight of their gazes descend upon him once more.

He had just bought the weapon and wasn’t ready to lose it. But backing down now would only brand him a coward, and he couldn’t afford that. His gaze hardened.

“Fine!” He spat at last, his expression venomous. “But if I win, you forfeit every promotional trial past the outer sect for the next ten years.”

“Gorvak!” the shop owner exclaimed loudly, echoing the shocked expressions on the faces of everyone present. “That’s too much!”

“That’s what I want from this smug brat, Algrim!” Gorvak nearly roared. “Either that—or he bows to me right now, a hundred times!”

All eyes turned to Aodhán, silently urging him to accept the escape Gorvak had offered him. He would be foolish not to.

And if he were anyone else, that would have been true.

But Aodhán wasn’t anyone else.

He was a transmigrant. Defeating Gorvak would be as easy as breathing. He could practically do it with his arms folded and eyes shut. And so, to the shock of every disciple present, Aodhán said, “I’ll meet you at the arena by noon. And Gorvak… I’m sorry for the loss of your blade.”

Gorvak’s snarl deepened, but he said nothing else as he stormed out of the shop, lightning snapping in his wake.

Whispers immediately rippled through the shop—some shaking their heads in sympathy, others snickering about arrogant newbies and early graves.

Then a rough snort cut through the noise behind him. “You sure have invited yourself a great deal of trouble, kid.”

Aodhán turned to face Algrim—the shop owner—and smiled. “I’m sure I’ll be alright.”

She huffed, obviously not believing him. “Well then, you’d better find yourself a weapon. Unless you plan to last less than a second in that ring. You did just bet your promotional trial on it.”

Aodhán opened his mouth—then hesitated. “…What’s a promotional trial?”

Algrim’s eyes widened, and she hissed. “You don’t know? And you bet on it? Are you insane?”

Aodhán grimaced but didn’t respond.

Algrim shook her head. “You stupid fool. The promotional trials are your ticket out of the outer sect. How could you—” she closed her eyes and forced herself to breathe through her nose, muttering something about stupid newbies. “You know what, I want to help you. I can offer you a sword for five hundred Gordars since you have no sect points yet. Can you afford it?”

Aodhán shook his head and grimaced when Algrim’s eyes widened again. Before she could speak, though, he cleared his throat loudly and pointed to the exit. “I think I’ll just take my leave now.”

Algrim didn’t stop him, despite her obvious desire to, and a few seconds later Aodhán finally escaped her sympathetic gaze. He activated his Stealth perk the moment he cleared the doorway and finally exhaled, feeling a little stupid.

This wasn’t the low profile he’d intended to keep today.

Varéc growled comfortingly in his mind, and Aodhán sighed. “Why can’t I ever avoid trouble, Varéc? Why does it always follow me?”

Varéc growled again, and Aodhán shook his head in resignation. “I need to speak to Zarxan again. Whatever trial the High-Elders are going to throw at me, it would be foolish to go into it completely clueless.”

Unfortunately, he realized after a few steps that he had no means of contacting Zarxan. If the High Elder didn’t visit him, then…

Sighing heavily, Aodhán abandoned the crafters section of the market and began walking toward the alchemist section. He reached the section a few minutes later, scrunching his nose at the sour scent of potions that filled the air.

Just like in the previous sections, disciples filled the stalls, buying willpower potions, pills, and healing potions. Apparently, their natural regeneration wasn’t enough for the frequency of their duels.

There wasn’t much for Aodhán to look at in this section. However, just as he thought to move on, his gaze fell on a wooden board hanging from a large shop.

HERB GATHERING MISSION AVAILABLE!

Payment in sect points only.

Apply within!

Aodhán’s eyes lit up at the sign, and he immediately moved toward it. Sect points were the currency of the sect, and it seemed he had just found the fastest way to earn them.

He arrived at the shop’s entrance an instant later, a chime bell clinking as he pushed the door open and stepped into what he could only describe as a forest of awakened plants. A thick haze of verdant essence hung in the air, and at the center of the roof, an artificial sun blazed, casting every plant in liquid gold.

Shelves lined the walls from floor to ceiling, each crowded with jars of verdant plants labelled in tight, neat scripts, and despite the obvious lack of windows, air streamed into the shop in a steady wave, curling around leaves and petals delicately.

It was absolutely beautiful.

Hundreds of awakened plants gathered in one place. Aodhán could barely imagine the sheer amount of wealth all of this was in Lutia. However, before he could even begin imagining, a man stepped out from behind a shelf, a small watering can dangling from his hand.

He smiled when he saw Aodhán. “Storm bless you, dear customer. What plants are you looking to buy today?” His gaze flicked to Aodhán’s robes. “Or sell us?”

“None,” Aodhán said, shaking his head. “I saw the sign outside about the herb-gathering mission and wanted to ask about it.”

The man glanced at his robes again and gave an awkward chuckle. “I fear you’re mistaken. You must be new to the sect.”

Aodhán frowned. “And?”

“And clearly not yet initiated,” the man replied flatly. “Missions are for registered sect members. Once you are, feel free to return. I assure you, the work won’t disappear—especially with the promotional trial approaching. Demand will only rise.”

Aodhán nodded. “I understand. I didn’t come to take a mission—only to look around. I was an herb gatherer before I arrived.”

“Truly?” The man’s expression softened then. “An herb gatherer?”

Aodhán nodded proudly. “I am serious. I was quite good at it.”

“Well then, you must come back after your initiation if you’re assigned to the outer sect. It’s very rare to see anyone with an interest in herb gathering these days. Amazing.” The man gestured to the shelves. “Feel free to look around. It’s always an honor to meet a fellow plant lover.”

Aodhán smiled and, with a nod of gratitude, turned to browse the shelves, the familiar peace he’d always felt around plants settling warmly over him. For the first time since arriving in Gordon, his muscles truly relaxed.

He exhaled deeply, as though an immense weight had slipped from his shoulders. He couldn’t explain it—and didn’t try to. Instead, he walked deeper into the shop, eyes scanning the shelves for a plant he recognized.

He didn’t find any, but after nearly ten minutes of searching, Aodhán noticed something significant, and he turned to ask the shopkeeper. “There are only nine affinities of plants here. Why is that?”

The man looked up from a plant he was watering, frowning in broken concentration. “What did you say?”

“The plants,” Aodhán repeated himself. “There are only nine affinities here—water, wind, lightning, sound, shadow, light, ice, force, and storm. Where are the other affinities?”

“Ah!” the man smiled. “Those are kept separately, as we rarely have any need for them. These nine affinities you see are the core affinities that make up a storm, and so, for storm cultivators, every pill, potion, or whatever is made from there. Any plant we could ever need would fall into any one of the nine categories.”

Aodhán blinked, having never thought of it that way. A thought came to mind, and he asked again. “Does doing it this way help to maintain core purity?”

“Absolutely.” The man replied as if this were knowledge he should possess. “The purer your core, the higher your chances of evading a system-enforced limit.”

“Right,” Aodhán whispered, realizing just how ignorant the people of Lutia truly were about cultivation. Why wasn’t something like this common knowledge?

Grimacing, he glanced back at the shelf, his gaze landing on a pure black plant surrounded by a thin haze of roiling clouds. It was a rare-ranked plant—the first storm-affinity specimen he had ever seen. But before he could ask its name or function, the entrance bell chimed, and two disciples walked in—a tall, blonde boy and a girl with metallic gray hair, both dressed in outer sect robes, each carrying translucent bags filled with plants.

Loud laughter trailed their entrance, and an instant later, the bell chimed again, and a familiar figure stepped in, shouting. “You guys are so mean. Why didn’t you wait for me?”

Aodhán pulled down his cowl to reveal his face, and when her gaze strayed in his direction, her eyes widened in recognition.

“Aodhán!” she exclaimed. “Storm bless you. What are you doing here?”

Aodhán mimicked her greeting and smiled. “I wanted to take a look around before heading to the arena.”

“Why—” she began, curiosity coloring her tone until understanding dawned and her eyes widened in concern. “You got challenged to a duel?”

Aodhán nodded. “At a crafting shop not too far from here.”

Rhetta froze, and even her friends did the same. Then she spoke. “Please tell me you’re not the one Gorvak’s trying to beat to a pulp? This is the fifth newbie in the last month alone.”

Aodhán grimaced. “I fear I am.”

“By the storm,” Rhetta cursed. “Why didn’t you decline the challenge?”

Aodhán shrugged, “I’m not scared of Gorvak. Besides, I hear declining a duel is about the worst thing a person could do in the sect.”

“That is true.” Rhetta grimaced and then glanced back at her friends. “Come on, I’ll introduce you to my friends, and we can go to the arena together.”

Rhetta’s friends, as Aodhán soon discovered, were very nice—an apparent rarity within the outer sect. Zarion, the boy, was at the 27th tier, while Yerxa, the gray-haired girl, was at the 29th tier. But what they lacked in terms of power and magical strength, they made up for by being humane, giving him tips on how to deal with his first duel.

“Don’t be nervous,” the blond boy advised as soon as they stepped out of the shop, several Sect points richer. “I cannot emphasize it enough. Nerves are your worst enemy within that ring.”

“I doubt it can be helped, Zarion.” Rhetta shook her head, her expression marred faintly with concern. “Your first duel practically sets your status and reputation for the entire year, and for a newbie like Aodhán, it would be a permanent stain on his record.” She glanced at Aodhán then and grimaced. “However, this time, I wish you’d be more nervous.”

“Leave the boy be, Rhetta.” Yerxa spoke, eyeing Aodhán curiously. “If he’s confident of victory, I see no reason to hamper that.”

The girl met his gaze and smiled. “I believe in you, Aodhán. It’s high time a newbie knocked one of those pompous asses on their side.”

Aodhán smiled, putting his cowl back on. He wasn’t sure if she was just being enthusiastic or working with information Rhetta had no doubt given her about him.

But he appreciated it nonetheless.

“You’ll be fine.” Zarion spoke, smiling down at Aodhán like a proud elder brother. “Win or lose, you’ve saved face by accepting the duel. Whatever happens after that doesn’t really matter.”

Aodhán nodded gratefully. “Thank you.”

Their walk to the arena was filled with idle chatter, and somewhere along the way Aodhán finally learned what the promotional trial truly entailed. It was a spirit-mountain climbing trial held in a place Rhetta called the Steps of Tempest. But from the way they spoke about it, Aodhán doubted it would be anything resembling simple mountain climbing.

“Don’t worry, newbie,” Zarion chuckled. “The initiation trial is usually very simple.” He glanced at Yerxa with a grin. “Ours was just a combat and spirit test.”

Yerxa nodded. “Rhetta, however, only had to do a spirit test. Damned lucky, I keep telling her.”

Rhetta snorted. “Let it go, Yerxa. That was seven months ago.”

“Not long enough,” Yerxa turned her nose up in mock anger. “I can’t wait for the next promotional trial to get myself out of these damned robes.”

“Me too,” Zarion chuckled. “I’ve been training my spirit with intense meditation for the past few months. I think I can make it into the inner sect this time.”

Yerxa and Rhetta nodded in agreement, stating reasons why they thought they would make it. But Aodhán sincerely doubted any of them would. If Angrid was still in the outer sect, then what chance did they have?

He kept his doubts to himself though, and before long, they arrived at the arena—a towering bowl of shimmering steel covered in storm runes. The arena was huge, filled with thousands of seats, and nearly half those seats were filled with people, their excitement thrumming in the air like the build-up to a lightning strike.

It was loud.

Laughter, arguments, chanting, booing—it all converged into a chaotic storm of sound that caused the earth to vibrate. Above, arcs of lightning crawled along the arched ceiling, jumping in tandem with the frenzy below.

At the arena’s heart lay a wide circular ring—the dueling platform—and floating above it were a dozen screens displaying the ongoing match with crystal clarity.

Two girls fought on the screen, one cloaked in gold lightning and the other wielding a rune-sword very similar to the ones he’d seen in Algrim’s shop. A roiling storm cloud rumbled above them both, and every so often lightning struck down, its intensity shocking both summoner and target.

They were both twentieth-tier cultivators, yet so unskilled it was like watching a pair of newly awakened flail at one another.

The sword wielder stomped forward, lightning bursting from her palms in a streak so slow Aodhán was certain he could have dodged it at the fifteenth tier. Yet somehow—he couldn’t even understand how—the other girl failed to evade it. The strike slammed into her shoulder and blasted her clean out of the ring.

Boos and riotous laughter erupted from the stands, and Aodhán shook his head as the anchor boomed,

“AND WE HAVE A WINNER! LERRISA VOLTAGE TAKES HER FOURTH VICTORY OF THE DAY!”

Aodhán couldn’t believe it—not just that she had won, but that she had already won three matches before this. It was absurd.

Yerxa apparently shared his disgust because she scowled. “Ugh. I hate people like this. Growing their fame and reputation by dueling weaker opponents. It’s disgusting.”

Aodhán frowned. “Are there no rules to these duels?”

“Rules?” Zarion scoffed. “You’re in the outer sect, newbie. The only law here is natural selection. Survival of the fittest.” His gaze darkened slightly. “And even if you don’t survive… you don’t get to die in peace.”

He laughed at his words as if they were merely a joke, but Aodhán had sensed an undercurrent of emotion in his tone that said otherwise. Before he could think too much about it, though, the anchor called out.

“AND FOR THE NINETIETH DUEL OF THE DAY, GIVE IT UP FOR POPULAR DUELLER, GUMP IRONHILDE, AND HIS OPPONENT, CRIXIS MARCELLAN.

Aodhán perked up at the announcement, his gaze flashing to the screen to see Crixis calmly walking down the dueling ring. Another screen showed Gump and his friends, cocky smiles on their faces as they walked down their aisle to the dueling ring, violent intent wafting off them like smoke.

They reached the platform together, locking gazes. But just as the anchor drew breath to speak, Crixis beat him to it.

“Where are your friends, Gump?”

Gump sneered. “Oh, you’ll meet them soon enough. After I bash that pretty face of yours in, they’ll be happy to finish the job.”

But Crixis only smiled. “Why don’t they join in now? Make it three against one. That’s the only way you can stand a chance against me.”

His legs settled into a battle stance. Thunder rumbled, and a single bolt of red lightning struck down, becoming a crackling spear in his hands. The crackling weapon painted his face in a crimson glow, and when he smiled, it almost seemed bloody.

His smile widened into something feral, and in that superior tone he knew grated so much on Gump, Crixis continued. “But then again, it doesn’t matter. One. A thousand. Trash is still trash.”

No graver insult could have been hurled in that moment.

With a guttural snarl, Gump charged. Lightning exploded beneath his feet as he launched skyward, reappearing above Crixis in a flash of fury and unleashing a condensed beam of lightning straight down.

But Crixis only grinned wider.

He hoped his neighbor had bought those bandages because Gump and his friends would most certainly be needing a lot of them very soon.
