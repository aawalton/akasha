Kalsin never really pictured what the heavens could look like, but if the gods allowed him a peek, this certainly wouldn’t be it.

He could just make out Jack's group ahead of them as she marched with her éj-lantern held high no more than a dozen feet away. The world was awash in shades of gray in the rocking lantern lights, dark glass sucking in lucent rays and leaving an aura of relative darkness that shielded their sight, weakening toward the edges. When Kalsin accidentally jerked his arm and the lantern swayed, it almost borrowed the radiance a fog-like quality. Except the air was cold, dry, and his eyes started watering if he stared into the white wall for too long.

The boy, perhaps a decade younger than himself, kept close to Kalsin with his teeth picking at his lower lip, shooting glances at the lantern as if to reassure himself the protective dark remained in place. On Kalsin's other side, a little farther walked the old man, and even half-leaning on his daughter he seemed to have trouble keeping up. His skin was pale, sockets gaunt, graying beard painted white entirely under the light and putting another couple of years on his already ancient visage. The girl with features similar to that of the boy, carried not only the old man, but also a weight of responsibility that made her look the senior of the two.

Treyant dictated a quick pace ahead, her hive’s morale-boosting surge spiking in their minds every few minutes like a mental banner to rally around. When the old man stumbled a step, she must've sensed it because their pace slowed a notch and remained from there onward.

Kalsin lowered his arm holding the lantern for a moment before raising it back up. "Sorry," he said when the boy flinched.

The boy muttered a reply and Kalsin smiled. It wasn't even on purpose. His arm felt like a lead lever just begging to be pulled.

"First time out during Blindlight?" he asked, stepping over a hole in the stone paving.

Again mutter was the answer, though somewhat louder. Kalsin looked toward the older man with a concerned frown, asking if they needed help without asking, and received a mute stare from the girl. Negative.

Kalsin shrugged at the boy. "Here's a few pointers that helped me in my earlier days. Your head, better if you keep it angled downward. Peering into the light will only give you a headache, and that's the best case scenario. Sounds boring, hm?"

Kalsin pointed at the road. "See the cracks in the paving? There's something I want you to notice. Watch it closely."

They walked for a good five minutes before the boy broke his self-inflicted trance and asked. "What is it?" When Kalsin let out a laugh, the boy was confused only for a moment before understanding dawned. He smiled. "Oh."

"Distracting yourself usually works for a while, but I have an overeager imagination. When I grow tired of numbing my mind with one thing, I move on to the next. The guessing game is an old favorite of mine." Kalsin closed his eyes for a second, as if thinking hard. "I make a prediction. Like, how long until we need to turn a corner? Or, in exactly sixty seconds, is the ground going to be a darker or lighter shade of gray?"

"You travel during Blindlight a lot?"

"Have to," Kalsin said. "In the delivery business, clients are rarely reasonable. Late package; partial pay. But I've found the best method is talking—provided you've a companion at your side. Out during Blindlight is a good time to settle differences. Words might get heated, but people aren't suicidal enough to follow up with fists. If the lantern’s glass breaks, it's over."

The mention brought the boy's attention back to the éj-lantern. He studied the darkly pulsing sphere for a while, and Kalsin studied him in turn. Fourteen at least, seventeen at most. The brittle light of curiosity about the world hadn't been snuffed from his eyes yet, and Kalsin envied him. Keep it, treasure it for as short as it lasts.

"Not everyone is out to get you, you know," Kalsin said quietly.

The boy stared at him for a moment. He had an uncertain look to him. "I don't—"

"You do," Kalsin interrupted, but smiled to lessen the strain. "Is it your father? Sister? They protect you, so you feel it's your responsibility to protect them in turn. But hiding things from everyone will only make them more prone to dig in, even if they have nothing to do with it. You don't want that kind of attention. You don't want the weight that comes with it."

"I'm not hiding anything."

"Of course you do. I do as well. Everyone who takes the services of a darkfinder does."

Surprise colored the boy's voice. He looked askance at the girl, whose wary eyes kept on the ground despondently. "Really?" he asked.

"Mhm," Kalsin said. "But the thing you will learn is that people are selfish creatures. Some good, but ultimately selfish. So when you talk about your troubles, they all listen, but really, they don't care much. Not unless they get something out of it. Even if that thing is a mere sensation.

"Now, don't look at me like that. The point I'm trying to make is not a bad one. The opposite, really. Our main concern is ourselves, but we crave companionship. It's a truce. It's a bargain. You and I, we have no connection whatsoever, no involvement between our lives other than this short trip, and yet we utter words from our mouths to give an outlet to feelings that want to break free."

"Don't trust you." The girl said. She had closed the distance and was walking with the old man leaning on her.

"I was to deliver a package to one of the overworld. Spark," Kalsin said. Making up stories came easy to him from the years and years of practice. "He didn't like it much, so he tried to take out his frustrations on the nearest soul, which was me. Happens once in a while, but I've no clue how my employer thought sending a Scarrd into a Clenn residence was a good idea. Wealthy, too. He has resources that I cannot evade easily. So here I am, several month's wage gone in the 'finder's pocket and will probably receive no compensation from my boss."

Though the story was a complete lie, Kalsin found it strange that it felt good to get the fabrication off his chest.

"Julien would've liked him," the old man suddenly replied. His speech stopped for an erratic breath. "Are we getting ready for the weekend festivities, Olu? We can't afford much else. Vault's near empty. No. It's so... it's so bright in here." He squinted, then faltered a step as the girl grunted, righting them both. She shushed him not ungently, then noticed the boy's questioning look.

Eventually the girl addressed Kalsin. "Ours isn't a big secret. Father is sick. Broke his surge when he tried advancing to hive."

Kalsin held back a wince at that. Advancing wasn't a simple task. It was a risky, days-long affair to attempt, and it was rare. Maybe one in a thousand ever tried to advance, and even less succeeded. He took a closer look at the old man, and he could see it. The way his eyes rolled, he focused on one thing that appeared as if he completely forgot about it, then a minute later his pupils widened as if seeing it for the first time. He'd been a rot, but he wasn't in control of his mind-enchanting surge anymore. He had become a victim to it.

"I'd imagine it hasn't been easy," Kalsin said.

"No," the girl said. "But the real problem came from a loan our family had taken a year back. Father, he..." She trailed off.

"Rot business can lead to dark places. Nothing new." Kalsin nodded. "Information brokers? Mob bosses? Spies?"

"The second. Long story short, we couldn't pay it back in time, so father tried breaking through to hive. We would've had a better ground to stand on, we thought. Financially, too, with a better paying job maybe."

"But it didn't work out," Kalsin finished her story. The old man failed his advancement, he went insane, and they gathered what little they had to pay a darkfinder and flee the city under Blindlight. "You have no family to help? Distant cousins, anyone?"

"No. Not in Grenstel, at least."

Kalsin lowered his lantern for a second to reach for his belt.

"No need for that. We have enough to start again, and then, I guess we'll see where things go," the boy spoke up. "You were right, though. It does feel nice to share."

The weather had gotten colder, and Kalsin felt his fingers tingle with reluctant numbness. They went on the side of the road, nothing but whiteness on the left, a sturdy iron fence running with no end on the right. A cemetery, if Kalsin had to guess. While he was unfamiliar with the city, Treyant led them as if she could've found her way drunk and with her eyes shut. Every minute or so her hive surge tapped their consciousness without expending energy to bolster or undermine morale, which was the main strength of hives and abysses. Her technique was so subtle that she probably could keep up without tiring for days. It gave them no comfort, but accomplished the primary purpose of pinpointing the darkfinder's exact position ahead of them.

Though Kalsin had already made up his mind, he still wanted to make sure. "You've lived in Remallon for long?" he asked the boy.

He nodded. "Since I was born."

"I've had another message to deliver, but I never got around to it. It's for the azTél family."

He observed their reactions closely.

But no such luck.

"Never heard of them," the boy said with a shrug.

"Well, I'm sure it's not that important," Kalsin said.

With agonizing slowness, Blindlight continued its ascent somewhere unseen above the city.

Three targets out of the picture. Too much of their time—gone.
