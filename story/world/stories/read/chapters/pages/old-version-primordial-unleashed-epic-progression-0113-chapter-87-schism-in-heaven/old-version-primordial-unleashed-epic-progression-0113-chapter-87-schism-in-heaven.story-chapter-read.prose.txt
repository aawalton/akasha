The chasm swallowed the sky. The wind howled in his ears as he plummeted. The pit swallowed all light, cold and terrifying. Skippii screamed. Fires burst upon his flesh, but did nothing to slow his fall.

A powerful gust battered his face. Shielding his eyes, he twisted on the updraft, splaying his limbs for a handhold. The air dragged at him, slowing his fall. In the dark, he caught only glimpses of the chasm’s walls, and the fire on his flesh. All was chaos in panic.

Another gust of wind thrust him in the chest. He gasped, winded by its force, but found himself suspended. The chasm no longer rushed past him; the darkness no longer grew. A tremendous updraft lifted him. Frost glistened in its current, singed by his flames’ touch.

“Skippii!” Kylinissa’s voice rode on the winds. “Climb.”

“How?” he cried. The chasm walls trembled and closed on him. Flames erupted from his flesh in a jolt of fear. The outburst turned him in the air, twisting and grasping in the wind.

“What’s happening?” he shouted.

Rise. The very wind hissed, and on its current he rose. Fly, fool.

It was the voice of a God, but not Seismorix. It was Kylin herself.

Skippii did all he could think to do, and with a breath, brought the full depth of his fires to bear. The cavern erupted with light. But he did not rise higher.

An image came to his mind–a memory long burried–of a blazing bail catapulting through the air, fired by legion’s catapults. The bail flew like a comet with a blazing trail. Now, so too, did he direct his fires beneath him, drawing them from his face and shoulders, pressing them down through his chest. The wind rose in pitch as the chasm closed on him, swallowing him, but a crack of sky still remained above. With his fires beneath him, and the power of Kylin’s winds, Skippii rose from its maw. Debris showered him, and at the last moment, he burst through the enclosing crust and into the light of day.

With a tremendous shudder, the chasm closed beneath him. The wind fled him, and the fires of his comet were not enough to suspend him. Skippii fell to the earth and landed in the grass. Panting, he tried to rise, but his head spun and he loped dizzily, and fell to the ground. Sucking in the thaugia of the earth, he forced himself to rise. Then a hand grasped the collar of his tunic and pulled him to his feet. Kylinissa grasped his wrist and pulled him towards her horse. He climbed, still dizzy, and clung to her back as she yipped, and they bolted.

Skippii’s vision swam. All around, the wind howled. The earth tremoured. The sky was black with clouds, and frost stung his cheeks. He dared a glance behind him. A mound rose from the earth; three terrible eyes. Thunder crackled in the air above it, then a long golden spear struck the mound. The central eye exploded in a shower of dirt. A dreadful screech pierced his ears. The earth itself was screaming.

“You…” Skippii shouted over the rushing wind. “The winds. You-”

“Kylin chose,” Kylinissa replied.

“The Gods are here?”

She did not respond. It was evident. Seismorix had come to claim his life. Kylin had saved him.

They were here… the elusive Gods, for whom he had many questions, and one demand.

“Stop,” he said.

“I can’t.”

Pressing himself against her back, he reached around her waist and pulled on the reigns. Her stallion braced and slowed to a stop. Leaping down, he planted his feet in the earth and turned to face the Gods.

“I am not your enemy,” he cried. “I will not flee in my own land. I will not be denied my power.”

Reaching underground, he sought the source of his thaugia. A tomb encased Cor, but no tomb was powerful enough to completely submit the Primordial being. And merely a sliver of Cor’s power could fill Skippii to the brink. Summoning his strength, he shaped the world beneath his feet; he evoked a Rockfang with a blunted tip and stood upon its precipice. His tower rose above Kylinissa atop her horse, and he raised his voice over the meadow.

“I will not be ignored,” he shouted. “I will not be threatened. I have my duty, to face the incursor gods. Your enemies, are they not?”

Thunder receded, and the quakes ebbed. The Gods were listening.

“Let old grudges die. I, the Primordial heres, propose an alliance.”

“Skippii, come here,” Kylinissa shouted. “Kylin demands it. We must go. We must ride.”

“I shan’t be threatened.” He gritted his teeth. “I shan’t be caged.”

“Come!” Kylinissa’s voice pitched with fear, and thunder crackled about them.

A hum rose about them as the ground tremoured, and Seismorix turned his attention from the skies to Skippii. He planted his feet and sought the binding thaugia of his tower, and sought to hold it in his grasp–to withstand the tremors of Seismorix.

“I wield the same power,” Skippii roared. “It is mine to claim. Mine–older than yours. Older than the Gods. You are a thief, Seismorix. At least do not dishonour yourself by allying with the corrupt gods.”

With a growl, a dozen fissures split the earth. Kylinissa’s horse screamed, but she tore at his reins and brought the stallion to the base of Skippii’s tower. “Get on, now!”

Suddenly, the fear in her voice reminded him of his companions. He searched the meadow for Arius, and his company of cavalry. “Are the others safe?”

“They ran,” she yelled. “Now come. It is folly.”

Fissures–like many fingers–clawed through the earth and came to the base of his tower. The ground crumbled, and Skippii’s power wavered. With Seismorix’s coming, he felt his thaugia dwindle, as though the God’s enormity eclipsed his flames.

“Fool,” he snarled, and slid from his tower, and climbed int the saddle. Kylinissa spurred her horse into a canter. They bolted over the meadow as a storm raged at their backs. Rain showered them, soaking him to his core. The world grew dark. Skippii could not see ahead through the rains, but somehow Kylinissa knew the way. They rode through a grey void until finally, the gleam of bronze shone through the haze.

“Skippii!” Tenoris strode forward, shielding his eyes from the rain. “You are safe. What power is this?”

“It is him,” Kylinissa said quickly. “It was Skippii who shook the earth. And I, and Kylin, rage a storm. The heretics are dead. We must run now.”

Tenoris looked shocked. His companeight all bore expressions of doubt; but the rest of their company looked on in wonderment and admiration.

“Tremendous,” Lord Cesturos beamed. “What fine company we have. Onwards now, to the mountains and out of this storm.”

With a cheer, the Kronaians set out. Skippii’s auxiliaries followed them, heads bowed in the rains. But his companeight lingered. He dismounted and looked them each in the eye.

“That was not me,” he said, quietly enough that the winds would hide his admission from the others. “It was the Gods.”

“Seismorix came to our aid?” Tenoris’ jaw dropped, and his blue eyes were bright with hope.

“No,” Skippii said, turning away. “He sought to kill me, or capture me. He is not our ally.”

“But Kylin is,” Kylinissa said. “Now go. We must leave swiftly. She and Oyaltun keep Seismorix at bay. But this is no land to linger in. We must leave before our lives are forfeit."

***

It was not until the sun had set that the storm overhead cleared, and the trembling earth relented. Their company made camp in a grove nestled before the shoulders of the northern mountains. Silence hung about them like the chill damp of the rains, and it was not until they had lit a fire and dried their feet that Orsin broke it.

“Has Oyaltun spoken with you again?” he asked.

“No,” Skippii said. “Not for weeks. I am beginning to think she has abandoned me.”

“She has not,” Kylinissa said sharply. The priestess sat cross legged by their fire, draped in her dark blue gown. It may have been a trick of the firelight, but her dark eyes seemed to possess a newfound golden glint. “Her Sentiescence was there today, or did you not see the icy mists?”

Skippii blew derisively. “Tremendous. Mists. I should divine a lot from that, I suppose? I should be comforted?”

“You should be honoured,” Kylinissa said.

“Oh , I am,” he said cynically.

“You know this would come.” Thales sat atop a fallen tree, to which his donkey was tethered. At his side was the mute woman whom Skippii had rescued from Thylon. She was wrapped in a blue sash and sandals which the Brenti had gifted from one of their dead. Her scabs and soars had diminished, but a redness still clung to her flesh, and her skin sagged darkly beneath her eyes.

“You could not evade them forever,” Thales said. “For this is their realm.”

“I don’t wish to evade them,” Skippii said. “I wish to entreat with them.”

Kylinissa scoffed. “And what would you say that they do not already know? Be grateful that Oyaltun speaks in your place, and now Kylin.”

“The Stormstress is our ally?” Tenoris said. “Then… not all the pantheon oppose our quest.”

“They oppose Skippii,” Kylinissa said, though her tone diminished. “They oppose what he is.”

“They’re ignorant,” Skippii grimaced. “Fools… I thought they were wise, benevolent beings. This pantheon is as foolish as any man or senate.”

Kylinissa faltered, but shook her head. “We were created in their image, afterall. It bears to reason that they are as fallible as we.”

“But Kylin came to your aid,” Tenoris pressed. “This is excellent news. Just like upon the steps of the temple. She is with you, Skip. She is with us all.”

“Kylin’s power is divided,” Kylinissa said. “Now, more so, that she must contend with the might of Seismorix, and what will come of the pantheon. However, I…” The breath escaped her, and she blinked, looking past them all at the shadows of the grove. “I now wield her power in full. Me, alone. Perhaps not as potent without her presence being nearby. But… it is mine. More than before, much more.” Her voice trailed to a whisper as her eyes drifted to the skies.

“Then this is a schism?” Cliae said. “I read about it in Eirene’s tomes. I told you a week or so earlier, Skippii. The Gods disagree about you, about the enemy… They will argue. They might fight. They may war.”

Cur heaved and groaned. “Chrysat. We’re doomed.”

“Some will take our side,” Arius said calmly. “Some may not.”

“Some may ally with the heretic,” Skippii said.

“There is no evidence of that,” Kylinissa snapped. “You may not have seen it, Skippii, but the heretic magi fell into Seismorix’s maw before you. They were destroyed.”

“But I was his target,” Skippii said. “They were merely collateral.”

“That is not true,” Kylinissa said. “Seismorix is not as feeble minded as you. He may do one thing to serve many ends. He split the earth to claim the heretic, and to ensnare you.”

“Oh, how reassuring.”

“Wait,” Kaesii said. “Hold on. You mean, they might…” He rattled his head. “The Gods themselves might…”

“Oppose us,” Skippii said. “Today, Seismorix spoke to me. He claimed he was a thief. He was going to drag me down into his dungeons. He has entombed Cor down there. That is where his power comes from. It is the same with all the Gods.”

“Not all,” Kylinissa corrected. “Some, like Oyaltun, created their own magia in this realm.”

“But most of them stole it from the Primordials,” Skippii said. “And Seismorix stole mine… Or my acheron’s, if you would use that term. His power and mine are in contention. It’s no wonder that he sees me as his enemy.”

The weight of his words cast a silence over his companions. But Thales broke it.

“For long, I have been wary of the pantheon. I have travelled with you all long enough now to tell that you are good men. Honest men, and true to your oaths. I am sure you are all astral, but I do not begrudge you it. This world is conquered by the Gods, but they do not love mankind. And they do not hate the heretic, as we would hope.”

“And with what authority do you make such presumptions?” Kylinissa sneered.

“With the authority of one who has lived nearly three times your years, priestess, and sought questions where you would only seek answers.”

“I am guided by Kylin herself,” she said. “And her years dwarf any mortal’s.”

“I do not claim to be older or wiser than the Gods,” Thales said. “I only state that they hide much of their nature from you. And you are too devoted to see far into the fog.”

“Kylin saved Skippii’s life today.” She drew her breath like a blade. “But she may not look too kindly on yours. What have you kept hidden, old man? And what of this magia you possess? I chose not to pry, for you are Skippii’s ally. But now I wonder, have you had us all fooled? Are you a blight in our midst?”

Thales faced her scrutiny bold-faced, but the woman beside him cowered.

“He is a thaugic practitioner," Skippii said. “Similar to myself, only he has no master. He has no God, not pantheonic or heretic. And you’re right, he is my ally. And friend.”

Kylinissa’s eyes flickered to him, then she settled Thales with a scowl.

“What do we do now?” Kaesii said uneasily.

“We go on,” Skippii said. “Nothing has changed.”

“Into the mountains?” Cur said. “To see what Summitor makes of you?”

“That’s right,” Skippii said.

“Any way to find out now?” Cur begged the priestess. “Can you talk to him beforehand? I wouldn’t want to knock on the mountain’s door when we’re not welcome.”

“I can only commune with Kylin,” she said dryly.

“Can anyone?” he groaned, and turned to Thales. “Hey, old man. You can put in a good word for us, right?”

“Weren’t you listening?” Orsin said. “He’s astray.”

“What about your tomes?” Cur glanced at Cliae. “Is there anything in there about… You said there was a schism, so how do you know? How do we…?”

His question went unfinished, but his unease was palpable, and reflected on all their faces.

“The schism just means that they are in disagreement,” Cliae said. “I do not know how we would join them in debate, other than to die and rise to the heavens themselves.”

In spite of their mood, Tenoris chuckled to himself. “Then let us not entreat with haste. The Gods have their decisions, and we have ours. We need not stray from our path.”

“Tenoris…” Skippii muttered, but could not bear to poison his optimism with the truth: the time had come to face the wrath of the Gods themselves, and he was woefully unprepared. But Tenoris’ deep blue eyes warmed him with an assuring gaze.

“Summitor will admit us.” Drusilla’s voice rumbled low and wearily. He sat upright in the cart that was his resting place each day and night. Kaesii jolted to his side with a waterskin, but Drusilla ignored him.

“He’s my acheron,” he said. “He won’t do anything. He loves power. He respects strength. Skippii is strength. He will respect that. He will not stop us.”

“I am inclined to agree,” Kylinissa said.

“And if he does oppose us,” Thales said. “If he is persuaded by his brother, Seismorix, then will Kylin come to our aid? Will you stand before his magnitude, priestess, and lay your life down to defend the heres?”

Kylinissa’s lips parted, but she paused uncertainly. “The Gods will make the right decisions in the end.”

“They better,” Skippii said.

The next day, Skippii took the lead of their column and led his company into the foothills beyond the domain of Seismorix and into the hands of his brother, God of the Mountains, Summitor.
