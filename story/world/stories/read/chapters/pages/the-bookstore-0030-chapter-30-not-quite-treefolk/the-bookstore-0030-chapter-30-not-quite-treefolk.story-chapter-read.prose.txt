The room beside the old stables no longer looked anything like storage. Anne had taken the back chamber and remade it into a kitchen piece by piece, until the place finally resembled something a sensible person could cook in. A stone stove stood against the far wall beneath a smoke tube that carried fumes upward. To one side, a long wooden table held bowls, tools, and the newly placed stone grinder. On the other stood another table beside a water tank built against the wall. Shelves filled the remaining space nearer the door, already stocked with grain, dried plants, roots, and whatever other food Anne had managed to secure before winter tightened its grip.

She stepped back from the grinder and studied the last array she had just completed. Faint symbols still shone across the stone, matching the ones she had already inscribed into the stove and the wall above the water tank. Technically, auromancy was not within a bookmancer’s natural range of abilities. Anne knew that perfectly well.

Technically, however, had never been a particularly strong word when placed opposite necessity. Or curiosity.

Her grandfather would certainly have disapproved. In his view, every page and every chapter of a grimoire should work together in proper harmony, all of them narrowing toward the clean refinement of one’s craft. Anne respected the principle in the abstract. Unfortunately for that principle, she had never been particularly inclined toward harmony. Defiance suited her better, and so did stretching a craft until it revealed something new.

Auromancy had become one of her most satisfying stretches. It was the art of arrays—of using ancient symbolic structures to guide arcana through matter and produce controlled magical effects. Most enchanted objects relied on it in one form or another. Spatial bags, magic locks, reinforced weapons, wards, moving constructs—auromancy touched all of them.

Anne, however, was not truly using auromancy. That was the clever part.

The method she had devised came from adapting bookmancy techniques rather than stepping outside her craft entirely. The idea had first appeared while she studied the principles behind training books and enthons, both of which remained beyond her current ability to create from scratch. She could restore, analyze, and understand them. But full creation still sat several steps above her.

Naturally, that had not stopped her from trying to steal something useful from the theory.

The result was a technique she privately called craft-mirroring. It came with limits, as all stolen brilliance did. The mirrored craft could only be used where her understanding ran deep enough, and complexity quickly made the technique impractical. Still, within those limits, it worked.

The grinder’s arrays would rotate the upper stone and spare her the dull labor of turning it by hand. That was not revolutionary, but it was useful. The stove arrays, once properly supplied with fire essence materials, would be far better. She still lacked part of what she needed for that, which irritated her profoundely.

So she avoided dwelling on it, and admired the room, instead. The building was becoming more hers every week.

Then she stopped standing around admiring herself and moved on to the next task. Pride was all very well, but it did not put food on the table. Today she intended to prepare something warm, deserved, and, if the gods continued to favor her, better than the meals she had been tolerating lately.

Anne brought over a basket of wheat and poured the grains into the central opening of the grinder. Then she activated the arrays.

The symbols carved into the upper stone glowed softly. A moment later the piece began to turn, steady and obedient. Grain slid between the fixed and moving surfaces, cracked, crushed, and became rough flour that fell from the edges into the waiting bowl below.

The motion was strangely satisfying. As was the sound.

Stone against grain, quiet and rhythmic. A simple household task made just magical enough to feel civilized. Anne found herself relaxing despite the cold outside and the long list of things still waiting to be done.

That lasted until Moruh arrived already offended.

“Mistress,” he said, with the tone of one presenting a matter of serious institutional concern, “please tell this insufferable tree to stop placing plants all around the building. The bookstore has environmental requirements, and she does not understand the concept of humidity.”

He glared toward Spring, who stood near the doorway looking unrepentant and faintly curious about the grinder.

Anne had already begun assembling the speech she would use to drive both of them out before they turned the kitchen into another territorial dispute. Then she paused.

Moruh, for once, might actually have a point. Books and excess humidity were natural enemies. Spring’s enthusiasm for surrounding the building with greenery had charm, but charm did not dry damp pages or prevent mold. Anne opened her mouth to address the matter properly.

Spring stepped toward the grinder instead.

“Spring do it?” she asked brightly.

Her speech was improving. Not quickly enough to stop Anne from finding it adorable, but noticeably.

“Yes...” Anne said, surprised for only a moment before a smile caught up with her. Spring loved usefulness with a sincerity that made refusal difficult. “You only need to pour the grain from the basket, Spring. That is all.”

She did not get to finish the explanation. Both Anne and Moruh stared as Spring raised one arm and calmly let golden grain spill from new-formed stalks growing along it.

For a heartbeat Anne simply watched. The grain fell into the grinder as though this were the most ordinary thing in the world. Spring, meanwhile, looked entirely pleased with herself and saw no reason at all to stop. If she could produce soul tea leaves from her altered body, then perhaps other plants were not impossible. Still, this was not soul tea. This was wheat. Golden wheat, at that, and not any strain Anne had planted herself.

A dozen theories rushed through her mind and none were satisfying.

“I am not an expert on treefolk,” Anne said slowly, almost to herself, “but they should not be able to do that.”

Spring froze. Then she turned and stared at Anne with widening confusion.

“Spring... not treefolk?”

The question came out with a thin, whistling sadness that made Anne regret speaking aloud immediately. Spring was still young enough, in mind if not in form, to cling to whatever few concepts helped define her. The idea of being treefolk had clearly become one of those.

Anne moved quickly to repair the damage.

“Not an ordinary treefolk,” she said. “You are... a special treefolk.”

That was vague enough to remain mostly honest and kind enough not to wound her.

Spring brightened at once.

“Special,” she repeated, and her fibrous mouth stretched into something very like a smile.

Good. Crisis avoided.

Anne stepped closer to the bowl and lifted a little of the newly ground flour between her fingers. The texture was wrong—in the best possible way. Too soft and fresh. It carried a faint scent of flowers and greenery rather than the usual simple dryness of grain.

Then she felt it properly. Nature essence.

Anne’s eyes sharpened.

“It’s nature essence grain,” she said, more to confirm the thought than from any need to say it aloud. Then she looked up at Spring, whose happiness had returned so fully that she was once again feeding the grinder without concern for metaphysical questions. “Spring, how much of this can you make?”

She tried to keep the question light. Pressuring Spring out of greed would be despicable.

Spring tilted her head as if considering the answer. Rather than speak, she simply willed the rest of what she could produce into the grinder.

Anne understood immediately. Not much.

Better than nothing, certainly, but not enough to transform her kitchen into a miracle of endless grain. She hid the first flicker of disappointment before it could reach her face.

Then a better idea appeared.

“Spring,” she said carefully, “can you plant a sapling of this golden wheat in the backyard?”

Spring nodded with instant enthusiasm.

They left the kitchen together.

The garden behind the bookstore had changed almost beyond recognition in recent weeks. What had once been a practical little plot fighting for survival now stretched all the way toward the well. The original wheat had already been harvested, leaving only the soul tea tree, the growing pink apple tree, sunflowers, and a few sturdy greens from Anne’s first plantings. But after Spring’s transformation, flowers had begun appearing everywhere. The little not-quite-treefolk had somehow recovered bloom after bloom from weeds and half-dead growth around the property, restoring them until the whole place looked far more alive than autumn had any right to allow.

Even now, with winter approaching, the garden resisted the cold far better than it should have.

Spring moved through it as though she were following music only she could hear. She stepped between flowers and young trees, drifting rather than walking, clearly searching for the correct place. Anne knew that herbmancers and anthomancers sometimes spoke of feeling proper arrangements in soil and growth, but that remained theoretical knowledge to her.

At last Spring chose an empty patch past the well. She sat down cross-legged—cross-vined, perhaps—and held one arm toward the earth. Anne waited, expecting some variation of what she had already seen. A seed. A cutting. Perhaps a root cluster.

Instead, after several quiet minutes, a pale yellow sprout emerged from Spring’s palm.

Anne leaned forward slightly. She expected Spring to pluck it free and bury it.

But Spring insisted in going against the norm. The little being kept her arm outstretched and bent close, whispering to it in soft wind-made sounds while her other hand stroked the stem with ridiculous gentleness. It looked less like planting and more like soothing a nervous child.

Then the impossible improved itself. The sapling detached from Spring’s palm on its own.

It walked. Not far, only a few tottering little movements down her hand and onto the soil, but that was enough. Once there, the tiny roots pushed into the earth as though it had chosen the place for itself.

Anne stared upward and drew in a long breath. There would be no end to this, would there? Spring would simply continue producing one astonishment after another until Anne either understood everything or died of academic frustration.

It was, she had to admit, an excellent way to live.

The life of a bookmancer was not boring at all.
