“You know I’ve never liked three days without a fourth day?” the master of the Order asked.

“Any news on Tarot?” the master asked.

Job had no idea who Tarot was. “Tarot, sir?” he asked, confused.

“My apologies, master,” he said. “I will strive to be better.”

The master tsked. “Ass kisser,” he muttered under his breath.

Job heard it.

“Uhm…” Job didn’t really have an answer for it. Since the master had just treated ass kissing with disdain, he decided to go for honesty. “I have no idea, master. You were the one who woke me up and told me to follow you.”

The master’s jaw dropped in shock. “And you just obeyed?”

“Yes?” Job blinked, confused.

“By the gods, boy, why would you do such a thing?” The master ran a hand down his face. “Didn’t your parents ever tell you not to talk to strangers?”

Job was already mentally exhausted dealing with the head of the Order. “They did, master,” he answered.

“I see. You had good parents.”

If this was the kind of person the master was when he was with instructor Torat, Job was beginning to see why the instructor had chosen to go on a very daunting mission rather than stay here.

The master turned suddenly to the wall on the right. “Ah,” he said. “Here we are.”

He was looking at a wall. Job fought the urge to point out that it was just a wall while considering the possibility that the Order might just be led by an overpowered madman. Then the master walked forward and disappeared into the wall.

I stand corrected.

Job followed only to bump into the wall. He paused, looked up at it. Holding out his hand, he touched it. It was a solid wall.

The master’s head popped out of it. “Sorry about that, you can come in.”

Job hesitated for a moment while the master withdrew his head. This time, when he walked in, he did so very carefully.

And just like that, he found himself in an empty room. They were surrounded by nothing but brick walls. The master retrieved the key from his pocket and put it up against the wall. It was too high up to be a keyhole, but Job was done questioning things.

The master brought the key back down. He turned with a frown on his face. It was as if something wasn’t right.

“Hand me that mallet over there will you?” he requested.

The room was empty with only the both of them occupying any space in it. Still, unwilling to question a potentially overpowered madman, Job turned and picked up a mallet.

He paused, staring down at the mallet.

How?

He’d simply turned to bend and pick up what he knew was not there. How the hell was he holding onto a mallet?

“Thank you kindly,” the master said.

Job held the mallet out to him, flabbergasted. The master took it. With the mallet, he turned, placed the key to the wall again in a position that was taller than him, and hammered the key into the wall. All Job could do was watch in confusion as the master hung up a picture that he had pulled out of nowhere. Just how powerful was the master? Just how confusing was the building that housed the Order?

Job had always known that the Order was powerful and mysterious, but this? This was beyond anything he had ever thought possible.

Done hanging the picture, the master stepped back to admire his work.

“Who would’ve thought a child not even twenty will have a place in the abyss of the Order,” the master mused.

Job froze. He’d heard of the abyss of the Order. But it was nothing but a rumor among the recruits. Rumor had it that it was where the master kept secrets of the Order that he alone knew and was allowed to know. The abyss was nothing but a myth.

Yet, here I am.

It made him feel important as he looked upon the picture. It was a vague description of two people. They stood in a forest clearing each a painting of blue with no distinct feature. One of the stood tall, looking down at the other. The other man was on his knees, looking up at the one that stood before him.

Above the picture, scribbled on the wall, was a title. It read:

The death of the Immortal.

Job could not believe the implication of what the master had said. He took a step towards the painting and the master did not stop him. When he was close enough, he looked up at it.

“A boy brought the Immortal to his knees?” he asked.

“Once,” the master said, “but the Immortal’s the one standing.”

Job peered at the picture and frowned. Both men were a pale blue, the general color of base mana, except one thing.

“What’s wrong with his arm?” he asked. The forearm of the boy on his knees was a sharp black. “Why’s it black?”

“Corruption,” the master answered. “Something that should not be is there.”

Job looked at the master and found the man looking upon the picture with a touch of solemnity. The master was looking at the Immortal.

“He died on his feet?” Job noted.

The master nodded. “As most powerful men pray to do when death finds them.”

There was a moment of reality there, a moment when Job realized that the master was not mad, simply tired. It probably had something to do with the death of someone that was not supposed to be able to die.

“Everyone dies, master,” he said kindly.

“Yes,” the master agreed. “Even the Immortal.”

“Do you…” Job hesitated, then pulled his confidence together. “Do you wish to die on your feet too, master?”

“I wish to die.” The master smiled sadly. “I just wish to die.”

In all honesty, the mistake was his fault. Years on Nastild and he still often thought along the lines of someone from Earth every now and again. Just because she was his woman, it did not mean that she was supposed to be weaker than him. On Nastild, it was not women and children first, it was children first. Yes, men still did their best to protect their wives and girlfriends, but it was more from a place of love and not the strong protecting the weak.

The thoughts flowed in his mind as Aiden rode his jepat down an empty road, leading the others as they rode behind him. It was a few days since they left the forest where they had slain Nosrath. They’d run into a few monsters once or twice. The team had been more than happy to silently stand by while Tanor played watch over Zen and Fjord as they fought against the monsters.

While Aiden watched the fight, his mind had continued to look for new ideas. He was at a level where monsters that would be sufficient threat for Fjord and Zen to learn and level up with were practically a waste of time for him. Killing monsters that weak would be a waste of his time—they would add no level to him, not unless he kills a very sufficient size.

They rode past merchants moving from one point to another, most likely going to sell wares that they had bought from one town to another town. They ignored Aiden as easily as Aiden ignored them, his mind lost in thought. The others behind him were more than happy to exchange greetings and information.

Aiden was only half listening to their conversation. Apparently, the news of the death of Nosrath had traveled further than he had expected in such a short time. It was barely three weeks since they’d left the forest. Then there was the news of the Immortal. The death of the Immortal seemed to be a subject. There were already rumors going on about it.

The king was trying to broker deals and make allies without his usual underlying power of being able to send an unkillable monster after them. Sadly, and unsurprisingly, no kingdom was willing to make any deals.

King Terlernor can’t seem to keep anyone in business with Talnort. They say that now that the Immortal is gone, the citizens are rising into a rebellion once again.”

“Because he is not the system sanctioned king,” Valdan answered calmly. “And he has been ruling with an iron fist. The citizens still continue to seek out the true king, but there has been no luck so far.”

Aiden knew the truth, though. The system-sanctioned king was locked away in Terlernor’s dungeon, barely staying alive. He could not remember the man’s name and could not care to. By this time in the timeline, the man would be nothing but a shell of himself, obeying Terlernor out of nothing but the fear of pain. It was sad to think about, but Aiden had no use for the emotion.

The group exchanged some more information with the merchant, before topping it all up with a few purchases, buying food that Zen, Fjord and Feira would eat. Not yet at level fifty, food was still a requirement for them, even if not as urgently needed as it was for those not yet at level ten.

Melvet stroked her cheek, the hero relic of a spear resting casually on her back. “Being jumped. I think I like it.”

He could not lie, he was impressed.
