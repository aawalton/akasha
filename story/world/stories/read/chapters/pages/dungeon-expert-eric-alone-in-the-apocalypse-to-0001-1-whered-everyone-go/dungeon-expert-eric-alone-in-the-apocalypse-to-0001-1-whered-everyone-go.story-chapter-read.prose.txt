Eric was on his way back to the office, coffees dangling from a little cardboard holder, when everyone vanished. Specifically, he was double-checking the orders when it happened, and was so distracted by checking to see if they’d properly put seven sugars in the double-shot mochaccino double whip double chocolate no flake no drizzle coconut milk extra hot and not in the no sugar nonfat single-shot latte that it took him a solid ten seconds to realize that the entire world had gone quiet.

He looked up to find himself alone on the street. The hustle and bustle of the city was gone. No longer were women in powersuits pushing past him or joggers jogging by with their jogging dogs. He was alone. Completely, utterly alone. No shoes left comically behind or empty clothes: everyone had vanished completely, dogs, leashes, powersuits and all.

Metal crunched with a bang. Silver blazed toward him, and Eric threw himself to the side, barely dodging as a silver sedan crashed into the building where he’d been standing moments ago. He peered into the car, but it was empty too. All of them were. The bustling city traffic crunched, bumped, and rolled to a halt all around him, leaving just him and his five coffees and his one insane mochaccino.

It’s not even mine. It’s Ada’s. He glanced up at his office, then back at the coffees. A sinking sensation set in. If everyone was gone from out here, what were the chances anyone was left in the office?

There was only one way to find out. Keeping his head on a swivel for more errant cars, Eric picked his way around one fresh wreck after another and made his way back to the office.

“Did everyone really disappear? Or am I just having some kind of grand delusion right now?” he wondered aloud. Like everyone, his first thought was to pinch himself, and naturally, it hurt. He looked around him, stopping for a moment. As loud as he could, he announced, “Hello, everyone! If I am having a delusion and there are still people on this street, could someone please call 911? I’m having a massive delusion and I probably need immediate medical attention! I have healthcare, I will pay for it!”

He waited a few seconds to see if invisible hands caught him and took him to the looney bin, but when nothing happened, he shrugged and pressed on. A second later, he stopped again.

“Is this a prank?”

A big black SUV rolling down the hill off the highway smashed into the back of a nice sedan and accordioned it. Fire burst out of the sedan’s hood. Eric stood on his tiptoes to be sure—he wasn’t going to run into the dangerous street unless he needed to—but the sedan and the SUV were both completely empty.

No one’s destroying two cars that expensive to prank a first-year engineer like me, he thought, twisting his lips. Whatever was happening, this was real. It wasn’t a delusion… or if it was, he could feel pain. It wasn’t a prank… or if it was, it was the most expensive prank he’d ever heard of, too big-budget even for the highest-rated prank television show.

I need some coffee. He pulled his own cup out of the cardboard holder and took a sip. The hot, bitter liquid didn’t make people reappear, but it did settle his nerves a little. Okay. So everyone had been Raptured, or whatever the latest doomsday scenario was; he was a little out of the loop on that. He was the last guy around. What did he do?

What don’t I do? he thought a moment later, a smile spreading across his lips. He didn’t have to go to work. He didn’t have to—

Wait. Hold on. Let’s not go crazy yet. Eric took a deep breath and looked at the building beside him. He was already here. He might as well make sure everyone inside had vanished, too, before he took the best day off ever. Maybe whatever had happened—cosmic radiation, mass alien abduction, hell, magic disappearing spell—had only happened to people outside (excluding him, for some reason). It was only five minutes to check.

Five minutes later, he sat on the executive table at the top of his office, legs dangling off the expensive marble, cardboard carton of drinks sitting beside him. He clutched his phone loosely in his hand and stared down at the empty streets. Black smoke gushed up from the occasional burning car, but aside from that, it was relatively peaceful.

Peaceful. Yes, that was the word for it. Not uncanny, or terrifying, or hollow. Peaceful.

I need something stronger. He reached out blindly to the carton and picked a drink, took a sip—then almost spat it across the room. His tongue tingled and his teeth ached. He yanked it away from him and stared.

How the hell does Ada drink this?

Setting her mochaccino aside, he took a more careful pick this time and chose another: Nate’s triple-espresso black coffee, insane in its own special way. He only needed one sip, but that one sip was powerful. It jolted him back to reality: the reality where he was alone, the only person in the city. There was no one in the building. No one in the streets. No one responding to his calls, messages, or texts. No matter how many times he tried, no matter who he tried to contact, no one answered. He was alone in the city.

In the city? Or in the world?

There was a television in the executive suite. Eric hopped off the table and fumbled around for the remote control in the fancy cabinets, then turned on the television. Instantly, someone beamed at the camera. “Now that’s good eating!”

I’m not alone. Relief flooded through Eric—and then he rolled his eyes. Right. A cooking show was proof he wasn’t alone. The pre-recorded cooking show—in fact, he recognized this episode. The re-run of the pre-recorded cooking show proved nothing. Why the hell were the execs watching cooking shows? A mystery he’d never solve now. He flipped through the channels. Some were playing endless ads. Others were prerecorded. At last, he found a live news channel, and his heart sank. A camera pointed at an empty chair. Nothing moved. No one cut to commercial. The camera just stared at the chair forever.

He checked a few more channels. Every live channel was abandoned. No one present. Not anywhere in the world. No one was talking about the disappearances, but, Eric was starting to realize, there was no one left to talk about them. It was just him. Just him, all alone in the whole wide world.

Eric sat back, staring at an empty newscaster chair, sitting all alone in the executive suite. He ran a hand through his hair and let out a long breath, not sure what to do or say. He was alone. It was just him. The last man alive in the world.

“Holy shit,” he said at last.

The empty chair was depressing, so he turned off the television. Abandoning the coffees at last, he moved to the floor-to-ceiling window instead, staring down on the city. Nothing. Nothing at all. Stillness.

And then: motion.

In the far distance, a building trembled. It cracked and shook, then came apart, dissolving into a pile of dust and rubble. In its place, a massive tree hurtled up, chased by other trees.

What the hell.

Things rushed out from that forest, clear motion. Eric leaned forward, excited for a moment, but they weren’t people. They were… he didn’t know what they were. Monsters, was the only word for it. Deformed horses with fangs and eyes that glowed like embers. Massive ape-like creatures with elephant tusks. A swarm of wasps big enough he could see them from all the way up here. A huge snake that moved cars aside as it weaved through the road.

What the hell!

Another building shook, then collapsed as more trees spread out from the initial spot of forestation. Then another, and another, until an entire city block was shaking. The trembling spread, pushing across the street, coming closer and closer. Eric stood there, watching it spread.

And then the floor began to shake under him.

Holy shit! What was he doing? Standing at the top of a building watching, as some kind of… alien terraforming or bio-weapon destroyed building after building… am I stupid? Abandoning the remote, abandoning the coffees, he ran for the stairs, paused, then ran to the elevator instead. He wasn’t going to make it down twenty floors of stairs before that shaking reached him. Contrary to all fire and natural disaster advice, his only hope was the elevator. He sprinted to the elevator and threw himself in, jamming the shut door and ground floor buttons at the same time. The elevator jerked into motion. As it dropped, it slowly began to sway more and more, until the lights began to flicker and the elevator moved in fits and starts rather than in one smooth descent. Eric held his breath, praying to whoever was listening that he’d make it to the ground.

Ding! The doors slid open. Eric threw himself out and sprinted across the lobby. The floor trembled beneath him, making him feel shaky on his feet, like he’d had a few too many to drink instead of a half-dozen sips of a half-dozen peoples’ coffees. The automatic doors swung slowly open, but he had no time to wait. He grabbed the edges and threw them wide, yanking himself outside.

Constant rumbling filled the air. Dark gray dust billowed toward him, rushing down the road as building after building tumbled. Eric’s stomach pulled to his spine. That’s not good. That stuff was cancer city, and no question. Some of these buildings were old enough to have asbestos in them, and honestly, that was probably the least of his problems, given how little anyone knew about what modern materials did to the lung when aerosolized and how little medical attention he foresaw in his near future, as the only human left on the planet.

Forget about the cancer twenty years later, I might not even survive the acute pneumonia. He looked left and right, then ran for the big black SUV. Despite crunching the little sedan, it was still in great shape. The door swung open when he tried it, and the engine was still rumbling along. The keys sat in the cupholder, button already pressed. He shifted the car into reverse, backed up, then swung around. The SUV maneuvered like a boat, and like a boat that really wanted to pull left; it still moved, but its front axel was clearly not in great shape.

Doesn’t matter. Eric backed up again, turned again, and slammed the gas. The SUV lurched forward. Ignoring all the DO NOT ENTER signs, Eric drove up the off ramp and back onto the highway.

He found himself on a divided highway. This side of the highway flowed into the city, with a high wall between this side and the side that went the right way, but into the city was a bad way to go right now, so Eric spun the wheel, pointed the nose of the SUV into traffic, and floored it. Cars piled up here and there, some crashed off to the side, others stalled across the road. He dodged left and right, putting the sport utility part of SUV to the test as he drove it off-road around clump after clump of cars. The axel screeched and the engine roared, but not loud enough to drown out the roar of crumbling buildings behind him.

Eric drove. Pointed the nose of the car down the road, fought the left drift, and drove. He drove until he couldn’t hear the grumble of the crumbling buildings behind him, then started looking for a place to stop. He couldn’t go home. Home was an apartment back in the city, and the city was currently rubble. All his best day ever plans were also kaput; he couldn’t go make whatever he wanted in a fancy restaurant kitchen if the kitchen was buried under seven hundred tons of twisted steel and chunks of concrete, or take all the video games and the nicest gaming PC if the shops were… well, destroyed.

The road shuddered under the car. Eric sat up, leaning forward to see the road. The road itself was… he didn’t know the proper word for it. Drying out? Rapidly aging? The black, freshly tarred asphalt turned gray, then almost white. Cracks appeared in the road, spidering through it. In rapid speed, weeds pushed up through the cracks, while the edges of the road started to fall into the wild grasses.

I need to find a place to stop. The road was currently up a hill from the town, and from where he sat, Eric could see down into the town. Most of the town was flattened, just like the city, but for some reason the gas station still stood.

Good enough for me. He took the exit toward the town and drove up to the gas station. As he parked the SUV, it let out a groan and a crack, and the front axel snapped.

“Damn,” he said, surprised, and then the rest of the car started falling apart around him. Startled, he clambered out the door seconds before the entire chassis gave out, dumping the body of the car on the ground. The wheels rolled comically away. He stepped back, watching as the plastic cracked and crumbled and the frame oxidized to rust. Whatever was killing modern… everything had finally gotten to his trusty steed as well.

Eric turned, eyeing the gas station with trepidation, but it remained shiny and new as the day it’d been built. The road leading up to it crumbled to pebbles, the concrete curbs dissolved into dust, but the gas station stood strong, a shining paragon amidst all the destruction.

He hesitated one last second, then shrugged. There was food in there. Water. Shelter. A toilet. Plus, it was only one story tall, and the front door was right there. If it started to go, he could hit the panic button and get out long before the roof fell in.

Eric pushed the door open. A friendly chime rang out, and an automated voice said, “Welcome to the safe zone.”

“What the—” Eric stared at the ceiling, looking for the speaker. He saw nothing. Frowning, he backed out, then stepped into the shop again, watching the ceiling the whole time so he could find it with his eyes the next time it sounded. Once again, the friendly chime sounded, and the automated voice repeated, “Welcome to the safe zone.”

The ceiling was bare. He grimaced, confused, then shrugged. Maybe this gas station chain was doing some kind of… safe zone promotion? Anti-human-trafficking or something? He had no one to ask.

It was eerily functional. Questionable hot dogs and taquitos still rolled on the rollers, fresh donuts sat on the racks, a slushy machine churned frozen sugar water in the back, and the refrigerators hummed quietly in the background. A pack of cigarettes sat on the counter, and the checkout machine occasionally dinged as it scanned them yet again. Eric walked over and gently nudged it out of the scan range, keeping it from dinging nonstop at an annoying rhythm.

He looked around the shop. Food. Water. Shelter. Everything he’d been looking for, all in one neat package. Immense relief flooded through him that this, at least, was normal, but the next second, he tensed once more. Who knew when this new world would yank this, too, out of his hands? He couldn’t let his guard down yet. Just because he had food and water right now didn’t mean he’d have food and water forever. “Alright. Who knows what’s happening, so let’s pack up.”

This gas station was a relatively large one, with a small apparel-slash-trucking-goods section. He found a black duffel bag with yellow straps (and a huge yellow truck and I LOVE TRUCKIN’ emblazoned on it in yellow in a font that looked like tire tracks), then started stuffing it full with all the long-lasting, high-calorie, high-nutrient snacks he could find: protein bars, shelf-stable protein drinks, nut mixes, crackers, chips, cookies, peanut butter, whole apples, canned beans, and so on and so forth. He grabbed a single-serve bottle or two of milk, and orange juice, too, and got started on chugging them, preloading the nutrients and calories he’d have a hard time finding in the near future. He chased those with some only-slightly-questionable gas station sandwiches, then a few fresh bananas and oranges. He only stopped eating when he was stuffed. Civilization was dead. All modern infrastructure was crumbling. He didn’t know what was happening, but he could put two and two together, and it made a grim picture of his near future. This gas station might crumble in minutes, and when it went, if it went, he didn’t know when he’d find food again.

I’m going to have to hunt. Gather. I’m all alone on the earth, and I’m instantly back to caveman mode. He grimaced, running his hair back. He didn’t know how to hunt. His idea of gathering was going to the local grocery store. He was a city boy through and through, whose closest brush with survival was tent camping with the scouts as a child.

He leaned against the counter, overwhelmed by his new reality. All alone. Just him. No food, no water, no nothing. The world as he’d known it was rubble, and there were apparently monsters out there—

Iron clattered to the ground. He looked up in time to watch a horde of forearm-length cockroaches dismantle what was left of the car, eating it down to the nuts and bolts. Their mouthparts snapped shut, cleaving through steel. One turned and looked at him. Its eyes appraised him, and its antennae wiggled menacingly.

He backed up, suddenly aware that there was only glass between him and the killer cockroach, but it just turned and went back to chewing on the bones of his SUV.

Eric swallowed. There were definitely monsters out there, who could definitely kill him. What was he supposed to do? How was he supposed to survive?

If this gas station crumbles, am I dead? He paused, looking out at the cockroaches again, then pressed his lips together. I need a weapon.

He went back through the gas station, looking for something, anything. In the trucking gear section, tucked away in the corner mostly out of sight, he stumbled across a selection of short but sturdy wooden bats. He picked one up, turning it over in his hands. The Breaker, screamed the packaging. Swing for the fences and hit a home run every time! Below that was an image of a man swinging the bat really hard right into the face of the pitcher, due to “accidental” alignment of the images. The “pitcher” dressed all in black and seemed to be wearing a balaclava for some reason. Somehow, the accidental alignment of the hit sfx on the “pitcher’s” face made it look like blood was flying out of his face. It had “barely legal” vibes, but it also had ““secret” self defense weapon” vibes.

Works for me. He wasn’t sure who was going to enforce the laws on him when he was the only person on Earth, so it no longer mattered if this club was technically sports gear or just a weapon anymore. He stripped it out of its packaging and gave it a few test swings. It was heavy; it felt like it was weighted at the tip. Just to test it, he swung the club at a rack of shirts as hard as he could. There was a muffled thump, and he felt the rickety metal bend as the rack fell over.

He nodded, looking it over. Better than nothing. He didn’t like the odds of a wooden bat against metal-eating cockroaches, but at least he had something now.

Now what?

For a while, he just sat there, holding his bat, watching the cockroaches outside the gas station’s enormous windows. In the distance, an enormous forest pushed toward the sky, and somewhere beyond that, a huge crystal loomed, growing ever onward toward the sun like some mad scientist’s middle school science fair rock candy. The world was being transformed before his eyes, but why? For what? Into what? He didn’t know, and there were no answers forthcoming.

A little closer to his new home, the cockroaches dismantled the car one bite at a time, chewing through the seats, the frame, the tires, everything. The proud, enormous SUV slowly whittled down to a skeleton, to scraps, to the barest tidbits. And then the cockroaches started leaving. One at a time, they scurried off, until there was only one cockroach left chewing on the scraps of the SUV’s headlights.

Eric sat forward, gripping his bat tight. I can take it. It was just one bug. And if the gas station crumbled, he’d need to know if he could take it or not. No—he had to try it. The gas station might fall apart at any moment. While he had the safety of the gas station to retreat to, he needed to find out if he could fight these things or if he should simply flee on sight.

Cockroaches have protein, a horrified little voice whispered at the back of his head, as if even the little voice was terrified at the thought. But it did have a point in this brave new world he found himself in. He might be sustenance hunting very soon. He didn’t fancy his odds against the mutant horses or the giant apes, but a cockroach he could handle. Probably.

Gripping the wooden bat tight, he stood and approached the door. The cockroach sat there, merrily munching on headlights and totally ignoring Eric. He put a hand to the door handle and took a deep breath. One last time, he checked the perimeter. No other monsters. He was safe. As safe as he could be with steel munching cockroaches on his porch.

Here goes nothing. He slammed the door open and beelined for the cockroach. His vision narrowed to a tunnel as he totally locked in on that dun-colored back. The cockroach turned and hissed at him, but he’d already made up his mind. He slammed the bat down on the cockroach’s head, hammering it home with all his strength.

Chitin cracked as the cockroach’s headplate dented, but the cockroach just hissed louder and flared its wings. It lifted off and flew at his face, mouthparts wide.

Holy shit, it can fly? Trying not to panic and failing, Eric screeched like a little girl and swung for the fences, just like the packaging told him to do. He felt its wings beat against his wrist, beating so hard they felt like light slaps, and then he struck home with a sickening crunch.

The cockroach went flying. It struck the gas station and dropped out of the air, then folded its wings back in and fled at speed, scurrying a zigzag pattern toward the trees.

“No you don’t!” Eric chased after it, already raising his bat for another strike. His soccer skills kicked in, and he stomped down on it, pinning it against the floor. It wriggled, trying to break free, but Eric wouldn’t let it. He smashed its head with the bat over and over and over again, until the chitin shattered, until its brains splattered across the floor in a mush of yellow ichor, until he destroyed enough of its residual nervous system that its legs stopped twitching. Only then did he stand, panting heavily, and wipe his face.

Sweat dripped down his forehead. His leg—the one that had pinned the bug—was splattered with yellow ichor, staining his khakis from the knee down. He looked up and caught sight of himself in the reflection of the gas station’s windows: hair plastered to his sweaty head, eyes wild, his shirt untucked, stomping a giant cockroach into the remaining concrete with his work shoes. He took a deep breath, forcing himself to look a little less crazy. It’s okay. The world hasn’t gone nuts. I’m fine.

The woods rustled. Not just from the left or the right, but from all directions, the woods came alive with a dry rustling, like leaves in the wind. Eric looked up. The trees were still. There was no wind.

Terror came over him. He turned and fled, darting back into the gas station and leaving the cockroach behind. The door clanged shut. The chime and the automated voice played. And a second later, a tide of cockroaches poured out of the trees.

Shiny brown bodies clambered over one another. Prickly legs tore through the leaves. The ravenous ocean poured out of the undergrowth and bore down on the cockroach he’d killed. The roaches all fell on their fallen comrade and devoured it, chewing it into tiny pieces. They fought, ripping pieces off of it, battling over bits of its leg and fleeing with a bite of its chitin. Every now and again, one of the cockroaches bit another roach rather than their fallen comrade, and in that moment, all the roaches turned upon the now-injured roach and tore it, too, to shreds.

Eric gagged, pressing a hand against his mouth, his stomach churning. He had to turn away, or risk spilling all his hard-earned calories over the tile floor. It was beyond disgusting. Absolutely hideous.

Note to self: get out of dodge if you kill one of those nasties.

He sat down again, this time a little further into the store, and just stared at the stupid trucker shirts for a while. The world is over, and I’m prey. I’m the last human alive as far as I can tell, and I have to be legitimately afraid of cockroaches. I’m stuck in a gas station, and if this thing falls too, I think that might be it for me.

He took a deep breath, then laughed aloud. At least I’m not the coffee gofer anymore. I can die with dignity, in a truck stop, using a trucker’s sap to kill cockroaches.

He thought for a moment, then snorted. Is that more or less dignity than the coffee gofer?

Eric leaned back against the wall, settling in to rest. Speaking of coffee and food in general, right now, he had access to a finite number of calories. He needed to preserve as many as he could, and that meant not moving a whole lot when he didn’t have to. Holding his bag tight in case the gas station tipped over and he had to bail, he closed his eyes and waited for the cockroaches to leave.
