After a brief trip to the Sunless Reach to recharge my star mana, I jumped back to the hill where the rest of my team was still observing the siege. The moment I reappeared, everyone turned toward me.

“Well?” Raik asked.

Instead of answering immediately, I held up the small pouch and tossed it gently toward Ja’a.

The cyan-haired girl caught it with practiced ease, loosened the string, and peeked inside. Her eyes widened almost instantly.

“Oh,” she breathed, sounding genuinely happy. “You sold all of it?”

“All of it,” I said with a smug grin.

Ja’a made a noise that was frankly inappropriate.

“That is beautiful.”

Meanwhile, Katar looked visibly disappointed.

“What...” I gave him a flat look.

“I was hoping you would cause enough problems to force us into participating,” he shrugged.

Ja’a immediately smacked the back of his head. I ignored the two of them and focused on Raik.

“So, good news,” I began. “The village is doing fine.”

“That is reassuring,” he said.

“Bad news,” I continued, crossing my arms. “Loumpa seems completely unconcerned by the six hundred mercenaries surrounding him.”

That got everyone’s full attention.

“He was not worried at all?” Raik frowned.

“Not even slightly,” I gestured dramatically toward the siege camp below, “He just said that he called for help.”

“But do you not need an army to repel an army?” Vena blinked.

I pointed at her immediately.

“Exactly,” I said, happy that finally someone seemed to understand my frustration.

“That is precisely what has been bothering me,” I continued. “Even if he sent word the moment the mercenaries started marching, it would still take more than a week for a proper force to mobilize from Hano and get here.”

“Assuming anyone was willing to send one,” Kan added.

“Right.”

Loumpa’s confidence made absolutely no logistical sense. Either I was missing something obvious, or he knew something I did not.

“Oh, he called an army all right,” Calr said suddenly.

We all turned toward him. He still had the spyglass pressed against one eye.

“What do you mean?” Raik asked.

Calr adjusted the lens slightly.

“It appears they have arrived.”

That got everyone moving. I stepped closer and immediately noticed what he meant. A growing cloud of dust was rising in the distance along the road from Hano.

Before I could even lift my own spyglass, a trumpet blast echoed across the fields. It was a bizarre sound. It was not especially loud, yet somehow carried absurdly far. The note rolled across the battlefield with no visible source, almost like cinema speakers.

I quickly raised my spyglass. I watched a group of mounted riders charging down the road at full speed. There were only twenty of them. Leading the formation was a man I recognized immediately.

“No way,” I gasped.

“What?” Ja’a asked.

“That is Edmund.”

My spear instructor was currently charging toward an active siege with unwavering confidence.

He carried a blue banner in one hand. Emblazoned on it was a large silver twenty.

Behind him rode the rest of the group in a tightly packed formation. Four of them wore matching plate armor similar to Edmund’s, though none seemed to favor spears. Nine others wore leather armor layered like scales, flexible and lightweight. The remaining riders had a much less uniform appearance, wearing an assortment of travel clothes, robes, and stranger personal styles.

I narrowed my eyes suspiciously.

Was that woman in the back wearing a maid's outfit? Not the ridiculous anime version, either. An actual proper Victorian maid dress.

I lowered the spyglass for a second.

“Am I hallucinating?”

“No,” Calr said with a grin. “That is just them.”

I raised the glass again and spotted another familiar face.

“Wait. Is that Sergeant Lanka?”

The muscular kindred woman was unmistakable. That was when everything clicked.

“Oh,” I gasped. “The Twenty Men Army.”

That name now sounding far less metaphorical. I stared at the approaching riders, dumbfounded.

“They are not seriously planning to fight six hundred mercenaries by themselves, right?”

No one answered immediately.

Finally, Vena let out a long, deeply resigned sigh.

“Alice.”

“Yes?”

“Do you still have sand in your bag?”

I blinked. “What?”

She gave me the exhausted expression of someone already anticipating a day of hard work.

“I need to perform spirit ablutions,” she said. “There is probably going to be a significant amount of healing required very soon.”

I stared at her.

“Are you seriously telling me you think twenty people can defeat an army?”

“Not an army,” Raik corrected. “A large gathering of poorly disciplined cowards.”

“I know most of them are weak,” I admitted, “but surely they have some elites.”

“Probably a few,” Calr muttered.

“Not enough to matter,” Katar said, shaking his head.

Still, I handed Vena the small bag of sand along with the large wooden bowl she had used before. If she felt the need to prepare for healing in advance, I was not about to stop her.

She immediately knelt on the grass and began purifying the sand with water from her canteen and cleansing miracles.

Meanwhile, the riders continued their approach. The trumpet still had not stopped.

Now that they were closer, I could finally make out the musician. A blond man with long flowing hair and small seagull-like wings was riding near the center of the formation while somehow continuing to blast the trumpet without pause.

“Is that musician a Dreamer?” I asked.

“No,” Calr said. “Half-dreamer, probably.”

As the riders closed the distance, the mercenary camp finally reacted.

Movement exploded through the siege lines. Men scrambled to reposition. Forces previously facing the slime village began hastily rotating backward to face the incoming threat.

From the central command tent, a man in pristine armor stepped outside. Even from this distance, he radiated importance. Two officers rushed toward him, and the three began speaking rapidly. Their body language looked tense enough to read from halfway across a battlefield. A few minutes later, one of the officers broke away and began shouting orders.

I looked back toward Edmund’s group, expecting them to continue their suicidal cavalry charge. Instead, to my complete surprise, they slowed to a stop, then dismounted.

The Twenty Men Army began calmly preparing themselves as if this were a scheduled afternoon routine.

Lanka pulled a massive axe from her back and rested it over one shoulder. Another man unpacked a basket full of javelins from his bag of holding and arranged them neatly in front of him.

Meanwhile, the woman in the maid outfit stepped forward.

She raised a feather duster.

“What is happening?” I blinked.

Light flared around her dusting tool. Her power looked oddly similar to Vena’s miracles, except it was blue instead of gold. Her magic dusted off armor, wiped sweat from faces, polished breastplates until they shone, and somehow even made their hair look freshly styled.

Finally, she pulled out a brush and began caring for the horses.

I lowered my spyglass.

“What even is she?” I said, dumbfounded.

“Priestess of Kana,” Calr explained. “The Goddess of service.”

“Not one of those,” grumbled Vena, who was having Raik evaporate the water from her now-clean sand with his heat powers.

The trumpet player had finally stopped. Instead, he reached into a bag of holding and pulled out a set of large drums. A moment later, rhythmic war beats began echoing across the valley.

On the other side, the mercenaries had finally reorganized into something vaguely resembling a proper military formation.

Then, to my surprise, one of the mercenary officers raised a yellow flag. About half a minute later, Edmund raised a yellow flag in response.

I frowned.

“What does yellow mean?”

“Parley,” Raik answered.

I blinked. “They are negotiating surrender?” I asked in a hopeful voice.

Calr adjusted his spyglass slightly.

“I do not think so.”

“Of course not. It is customary to discuss terms before battle,” Katar explained, as though this were the most obvious thing in the world.

“That is stupid,” Ja’a said immediately. “If you are going to talk, why fight at all?”

“How would you know who is the strongest without fighting?” Katar glanced at her.

“That sounds like something only someone obsessed with fighting would say.”

“It is also true.”

Before they could continue bickering, the rhythm of the drums shifted.

The steady war beat became slower, more deliberate, almost ceremonial.

Everyone instinctively turned their attention back toward the field.

From the mercenary side, the man in pristine armor began walking forward beneath the yellow flag. Five armed soldiers followed him in a neat honor guard formation.

On the other side, Edmund stepped out carrying his own flag. Beside him walked another man wearing similar polished armor, though unlike Edmund, he carried a sword and shield instead of a spear. Judging by the way everyone subtly deferred to him, I guessed he was the actual leader of the Twenty Men Army.

Following behind them came the bard and the maid.

Yes. The battlefield maid. I am still not emotionally prepared for her existence.

Both delegations met roughly halfway between the two forces.

Then the maid reached into her bag of holding and pulled out a folding table.

I blinked.

“No.”

Next came two chairs.

“No.”

Then a tea set.

“Absolutely not.”

She calmly arranged everything with the efficiency of someone setting up afternoon refreshments in a garden rather than the middle of an active siege.

The two leaders sat down across from each other, and the maid poured hot tea.

I lowered my spyglass and stared blankly into the distance.

“This cannot be real.”

I raised the spyglass again just in time to hear the mercenary captain speak.

“Who is to say this is not poisoned?” he asked suspiciously, pointing at the tea.

That made me pause.

“Wait,” I said. “How can we hear them?”

“The bard is also a sound mage,” Calr explained.

That was a really good combo.

“He is probably broadcasting for the sake of the people in the slime village.” Calr pointed toward the wall.

I turned my spyglass there and spotted Loumpa and his wife watching.

“I offer you hospitality in the name of Kana,” I heard the maid say.

Her voice was terrifying. Well, terrifying was the wrong word. Her tone reminded me of the strict schoolteacher everyone feared because she assigned lots of homework and gave terrible grades. Even from this distance, I felt compelled to stand straighter.

The armored swordsman seated beside Edmund inclined his head politely.

“Allow me to introduce myself,” he said. “I am Lieutenant Widmore of the Twenty Men Army, freelancer of Hano, and friend of Chief Loumpa, whom you are currently besieging.”

Across from him, the mercenary captain leaned back slightly.

“Captain Walfang,” he replied. “Commander of this battalion from the Endless March Mercenary Company.”

So this was the idiot causing all of this.

Widmore smiled pleasantly.

“Well then, Captain Walfang, allow me to be blunt.”

He folded his hands together over the table.

“You do not stand a chance.”

Widmore continued in the same polite tone.

“So, how about you pack up and leave before too many of your men get hurt? If you do so now, I will generously refrain from requesting compensation for the inconvenience you have caused.”

I nearly choked at the disrespect.

“You are too cocky, freelancer,” Captain Walfang scoffed. “My men are a match for any of your freelancers.”

“Are they?” Widmore said slowly. “Then perhaps you would be amenable to a different arrangement.”

Walfang frowned at that.

“A duel,” Widmore proposed. “Your strongest fighter against one of my own.”

“Oh?” I sat up slightly. That at least sounded practical. It would minimize bloodshed. A lot of tribal and clan warfare had been settled with duels back on Earth.

Walfang stared at him for a moment, then scowled.

“Why,” he asked, sounding genuinely curious, “would I agree to that?”

Widmore’s expression shifted to a frown for the first time.

Walfang spread one hand toward the battlefield behind him.

“I outnumber you thirty to one. Accepting a duel would mean voluntarily discarding my overwhelming tactical advantage.”

Widmore sighed dramatically.

“I see there is no reasoning with you.”

He lifted his teacup and took a delicate sip.

“Very well. Let us discuss terms.” He set the cup down. “Once we win, you will pay twenty gold from your company coffers to Loumpa’s village as compensation for interrupted trade and unlawful blockade.”

Captain Walfang’s expression darkened.

Widmore continued without pause.

“The dead will be burned on the spot. We are too close to both Ectamel and the dead zone to risk transporting corpses or leaving them buried nearby.”

That was... very practical.

“Any soldier who throws down his weapon and walks to either side of the battlefield will be spared.”

He glanced toward Edmund.

“Was that everything?”

Edmund paused for a second before speaking.

“All camp followers are advised to retreat immediately. Any soldier using civilians as cover will be executed.”

That line was delivered with such casual certainty that I felt a little bad for anyone dumb enough to test it.

Captain Walfang frowned deeply.

“My terms...” he replied coldly, “are that after I crush your force, the village surrenders and opens its gates.”

Widmore nodded thoughtfully.

“That is acceptable.”

Before any of us could process that, the bard, who had been standing nearby with his instrument tucked under one arm and wings flared, let out a soft snort.

“Terms,” he said lazily, “are for people with the strength to enforce them.”

That was such an unbelievably disrespectful line that I could almost feel the burn.

Captain Walfang’s jaw visibly tightened. Widmore gave a small shrug.

“I am sure Loumpa would surrender if, by some miracle, we were defeated.”

The way he said "miracle" made it abundantly clear he considered losing to be impossible.

“So yes,” he finished calmly. “We agree to those terms.”

The tea meeting concluded with an amount of civility wildly disproportionate to the fact that both sides were about to attempt large-scale murder.

The two leaders stood. The maid collected the cups, and the table disappeared back into her bag of holding. Just like that, both delegations turned and walked back toward their respective armies.

“I have so many questions.” I lowered my spyglass.

“You and me both,” Vena muttered while using the sand to wash her feet, scrubbing the coarse substance between her toes.

“So many questions...” I gave her a look.

Katar grinned.

“This is going to be entertaining.”

After both sides returned to their lines, the mood almost immediately shifted back into something grimly serious. The mercenary company reorganized with renewed urgency. Orders were shouted, ranks tightened, shields locked into loose formations, and spearmen shifted forward while archers repositioned behind them. Across from them, the Twenty Men Army somehow looked relaxed, which was more unsettling than if they had shown even the slightest sign of nerves.

I lifted my spyglass again and studied their formation carefully. They had arranged themselves into a loose wedge pointed directly at the center of the mercenary line. Edmund was in front, still somehow carrying that ridiculous banner as though charging into battle while holding a flag was perfectly normal. The blue cloth snapped in the wind, the silver twenty written in the common script emblazoned proudly at its center.

On the opposing side, another standard-bearer was carrying the Endless March banner, though he at least had the decency to remain in the back near the leadership tent where Captain Walfang had retreated. From a distance, their banner looked exactly how a mercenary company trying too hard to appear intimidating should look: dark green cloth bordered with silver embroidery, clearly expensive enough to suggest success, while the bottom half was stained with old dried blood that had been very intentionally left unwashed.

“That banner is needlessly dramatic,” Kan muttered.

“Mercenaries love posturing,” Raik replied.

“Posturing?” Ja’a scoffed. “It looks like they wipe corpses with it.”

“That was probably what they did,” Katar shrugged.

While both armies settled into formation, one of the Twenty Men stepped forward. He looked like a mage, broad-shouldered and wearing practical robes reinforced with stone plates around the joints. Without ceremony, he slammed both hands into the ground.

A low rumble rolled beneath our feet as the earth shifted.

Stone surged upward in a controlled column, shaping itself into a narrow tower perhaps three stories tall. It was not especially impressive architecturally, more practical than elegant, but it rose quickly enough to make me blink in surprise.

The mage climbed the tower alongside the javelin thrower and another freelancer carrying far too many hatchets on his belt. From there, they now had a perfect vantage point over the battlefield. The maid started lighting a bonfire concealed from the opposing army by the safety of the tower, while the bard slowly moved the horses behind cover.

“That seems useful,” Kan observed.

Then my attention shifted to something even stranger.

A witch-looking woman, pointy hat and all, stepped away from the formation and pulled a black cauldron from her bag of holding. She placed it over the campfire the maid had already lit and immediately began dropping ingredients into it with practiced efficiency.

I squinted harder through the spyglass and recognized some of the ingredients she was using: rowan leaves, horseradish root, and monster cores; Sky affinity, judging by the color.

Then, for reasons beyond mortal understanding, she coated an arrow in honey and dropped it into the now-bubbling mixture.

I stared at her in disbelief as the cauldron hissed ominously.

“I have absolutely no idea what she is doing.”

“Witchcraft,” Vena replied with a glare toward the woman. “There were some of her kind left in the Veil Holy Kingdom. They are often more trouble than they are worth, especially when clerics offer more reliable healing.”

Of course, that is not biased at all, considering she is a cleric. I decided not to point that out.

Still, this was fascinating. This was not elemental bloodline mana shaping, divine miracles, or soulbook powers. This was actual symbolic magic, the folkloric kind most similar to what people on Earth used to believe in. Apparently, some mythic people still retained fragments of those older traditions, and one of them was currently brewing incomprehensible nonsense soup on the battlefield.

Meanwhile, the maid and bard remained near the horses, both looking entirely unconcerned by the fact that large-scale violence was about to begin. The maid calmly brushed one of the horses while the bard resumed rhythmically playing the drums.

The rest of the Twenty Men checked weapons and armor, calmly preparing like people who clearly expected this to go well.

The mercenaries, to their credit, seemed to recognize the danger of allowing elite freelancers unlimited preparation time. Their lines began advancing before the Twenty Men could finish whatever bizarre ritualistic nonsense they were doing.

It was not a full charge, more a disciplined forward push. Shields up, spears angled, archers already preparing their first volley.

The bard raised his trumpet again, and a sharp note split the air, and another trumpet answered from the mercenary side. With that, the battle had begun.

The sound alone sent an unpleasant chill through me. It was strange how ceremonial it all felt, almost rehearsed, despite the fact that people were about to start dying in large numbers.

Edmund, Widmore, and the four remaining plate-armored fighters advanced directly at the center of the enemy line. Behind them, Lanka and the eight other freelancers in scale armor advanced at a slower pace. With the three on top of the tower and the three support members behind it, everyone was in position.

Golden holy light burst from Edmund and the five plate-armored fighters. They accelerated even more, almost mimicking a cavalry charge without the horses.

“Why are they not charging on horseback?” I asked.

“Horses durable enough for elite combat are rare and expensive,” Katar explained without looking at me, his attention fixed on the battlefield.

“And those animals just sprinted here from Hano,” added Calr. “They are tired.”

That was practical, I guess.

Then I noticed something even stranger. Edmund entered battle still carrying the banner.

“Wait,” I said. “He is actually fighting with it?”

“Most armies carry a banner into battle,” Raik replied. “That is how a victor is decided.”

“What do you mean?”

“When a banner falls, either by killing the bannerman or disabling him, the enemy side must either reclaim the banner or raise a new one. That makes the new banner bearer the next target, and so on until no one is brave enough to raise a new banner,” Calr explained.

“Bannermen are usually the bravest people in an army, even when they are sitting safely in the backline,” Katar added, pointing toward a stern-faced mercenary near the rear.

“In Edmund’s case, he carries his banner to the front,” said Vena. “As a Holy Knight, he cannot fight like a coward, or he would lose his miracles.”

I was at a loss for words…

The mercenary shield bearers braced for impact while archers released a volley. Dozens of arrows darkened the sky above the battlefield. My body tensed instinctively, expecting impact.

Instead, the arrows slowed mid-flight. Their momentum bled away until they were crawling through the air slowly enough that the Twenty Men simply stepped aside to avoid them.

I blinked. It was as if the projectiles suddenly moved through invisible molasses... or maybe honey.

The witch cackled at the sight. She actually cackled.

I stared at her. Then, at the arrows that are flying in slow motion.

“Oh, my god.”

Her cauldron was glowing faintly. That had to be her doing. Real symbolic witchcraft, proper old-school magic operating on sympathetic principles.

“That is incredible.”

From atop the stone tower, the javelin thrower moved next. He drew back and hurled a spear with terrifying force. Unlike the arrows, whatever slowing effect was distorting the battlefield did not seem to affect his weapon at all. The javelin flew like a launched missile and punched cleanly through an officer standing behind the forward formation.

The man collapsed instantly, and I winced at the sight of blood fountaining from a hole in his torso.

Chaos rippled through the mercenary line just as the formations finally collided.

The Twenty Men slammed into the mercenary front like a wedge of sharpened steel. The center of the battlefield exploded into motion. With ranged attacks largely neutralized, the elite freelancers plunged directly into melee. They did not fight like individual heroes, nor did they scatter themselves into dramatic duels. They moved like a coordinated unit. Shields created openings, spears exploited them, and swords followed through immediately afterward.

Edmund and the other plate-armored fighters kept advancing. They barely seemed interested in the men surrounding them. They cut directly through the formation, carving a bloody path toward Captain Walfang and the opposing banner.

The nine scale-armored freelancers, including Lanka, slowed instead and began systematically thinning enemy numbers.

That was when the real ugliness of battle became impossible to ignore.

I winced as I watched. Blood was everywhere, far too much of it. Men screamed in genuine pain. Someone dropped, clutching his stomach. Another collapsed after taking a strike to the leg. One man was hit across the shoulder hard enough to spin sideways before crumpling. And others simply stopped moving entirely. Head wounds, torso wounds, the kind of injuries people did not recover from.

The sounds were somehow worse than the visuals.

Metal clashing against metal.

Wood splintering.

Wet impacts I tried very hard not to think about.

People screaming in panic, crying out, and begging.

My stomach twisted unpleasantly.

I watched as Lanka casually beheaded a mercenary with her massive axe.

That was enough. I lowered the spyglass. I could not keep watching.

Without saying anything, Katar gently took the spyglass from my hand. He raised it to his eye and watched with genuine appreciation.

It was not bloodlust, not really. More like honest admiration, as if he were watching an exceptionally skilled performance.

That unsettled me even more.

For a moment, I just stood there staring at him, then back toward the battlefield below, where twenty freelancers were methodically dismantling an army while the rest of us watched from a hill like spectators.

It hit me then with uncomfortable clarity.

This was not my world.

While I had adapted here surprisingly well, all things considered, I had made friends, built routines, learned about religion and magic, started businesses somehow, and become alarmingly comfortable with teleportation and combat.

But in moments like this, I was reminded how alien everything still was.

Back on Earth, war had always felt distant, something happening somewhere else. A headline in the news, a tragedy I could donate money toward, read about briefly, and then continue with my day while carefully avoiding booking flights through unstable regions.

Here, it was immediate. It was close enough that if the wind shifted, I could probably smell it.

And everyone treated it with alarming normalcy.

Even Vena was still half-watching while continuing her spiritual ablutions, cleansing her hands, face, and feet with sanctified sand while occasionally glancing up at the massacre.

When Ectamel turned nightly undead slaughter into entertainment, I could at least rationalize it. The victims were monsters.

But to someone like Katar, the distinction between monster and person did not seem all that important.

Here I was, standing on a hill watching twenty freelancers butcher hundreds of mercenaries while a bard provided background music and a colorful banner highlighted the advance of the assault formation.

Beside me, even Ja’a, who was technically a civilian, watched the slaughter with open admiration.

“Well,” she said, sounding thoroughly impressed, “they are actually ridiculously strong.”

It was the understatement of the century. The mercenaries stood no chance.
