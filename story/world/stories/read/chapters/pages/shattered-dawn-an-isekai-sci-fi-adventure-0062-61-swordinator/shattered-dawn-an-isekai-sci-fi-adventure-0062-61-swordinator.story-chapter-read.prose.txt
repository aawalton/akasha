They walked with Yaret over to the work location. Open barrels already lined the space, carted in by a small arachnatronic that carried four at a time attached to its undercarriage.

Elion started hammering lids, watching the pump running. Across the rocky path, mounted at the side of the acid pool, the pump churned, sucking green liquid through its orifices. A pair of workers shoved barrels beneath spigots and pulled levers to open the spouts. Acid filled the barrels, which were placed in the staging zone for the Arachnatronic to cart them away.

Noting several structures around the rim of the pit, Elion decided Keyla was right. These other workers probably stayed in those buildings. Every single person he could see might be from Aterfel. There was no way to tell, thanks to the protective suits everyone wore.

Elion and Keyla started working together, in a rhythm. Keyla would place a lid on a barrel, Elion would pound it into place, then Keyla would carry it over to the clamper. Every 5 barrels they would switch jobs. The tactic worked to temporarily ease aching muscles, but as the day wore on, everything hurt.

Stopping to take a breather, Elion stood beside Keyla and watched Yaret work.

“We’d be here all night if not for him,” Elion said.

“He’s had a lot of practice,” Keyla agreed. “I hope I’m never as good at this as he is.”

“Should we get back to it?” Elion asked, starting to full the pull of his bond.

“I’m worried about that pump,” Keyla said, turning and pointing to the machine filling the barrels with acid. “The tone is changing. This morning it was a smooth whir, but now it’s more of a whum.”

“Boff told us to lid barrels, not listen to pumps,” Elion said. “Come on.”

“He didn’t say I couldn’t warn them,” Keyla said. “Maybe that’s just a normal sound for the pump. A consequence of running acid through it all day. It just doesn’t sound good.”

They returned to lidding barrels.

“Hey, why doesn’t the acid burn through the barrels? Or the pumps?” Elion asked. “How do they contain it?”

Keyla tried wiping her brow, but forgot she was wearing a suit and just ended up smudging sweat across the face plate.

“See that box over there?” Keyla pointed to a crate, about three feet tall sitting on the ground beside the pump. “That’s hydrophobic grease. They coat things with it that come in contact with the acid. I watched them rubbing it all over the hose that they dropped into the pool. I assume they use something similar inside the barrels.”

Elion examined one of the barrel lids. A thin, shiny substance lined the inside part of the lid, smudging on his glove as he touched it.

“If I coated myself in that grease, I could get splashed by acid and be fine?” Elion asked. “Why don’t they coat these protective suits in it?”

“I wouldn’t recommend it,” Keyla said. “What if you missed a spot? Besides, it would make us all greasy and sticky, and get rubbed off throughout the day.”

They returned to their work.

A few minutes later Keyla stopped again, leaving a barrel in the clamping machine and pointing. A pair of warlocks escorted a man and a woman in uniform hauling a heavy looking device between them down the mountain. They were heading straight toward the lidding area.

“Do they know I’m here?” Elion asked, carrying a barrel over to Keyla.

“I doubt it. Besides, it’s impossible to recognize anyone. We’re all wearing the same, grey rubber suits.”

The warlocks stopped and consulted the machine being hauled along with them.

“That looks like the device they used to steal my knife,” Elion said.

“Could they be tracking you?”

“I don’t—maybe,” Elion said, looking back at the warlocks. One of them was pointing in their direction. A pang of fear clutched Elion’s stomach.

The warlocks continued along the path, winding down the side of the ravine, past other acid pumping stations. When they were out of sight behind a large machine, Keyla grabbed Elion’s arm.

“You need to hide,” she said. “Here.” She pulled him to an empty barrel.

“How will this help, if they have a way of tracking me?” Elion asked. “Won’t they—”

“Maybe,” Keyla said. “But it’s worth a try. If there’s a fight better to have the element of surprise.”

Elion groaned, but climbed into the barrel. The sides were slick, probably recently coated with acidproof grease. Keyla set a lid on top of it, blocking out all the light. A moment later the sound of a barrel mallet reverberated through the drum, deafening Elion. Then the drum tipped, and he felt himself being wheeled across the ground.

“This wasn’t part of the plan,” he grumbled quietly.

Keyla set the drum down with a clang. Then he heard the clunk of the clamper’s arms gripping his barrel. “No,” he hissed, but he began spinning. The rotation speed increased, pressing his body outward against the side of the drum.

It took several moments of deep breathing before he realized that the drum had stopped spinning and he was being carted away from the clamper.

He heard voices. Keyla set his barrel down. Sweat trickled down his spine.

“…around here somewhere,” a man said, voice thin and reedy. “The dial is going crazy.”

"Hey, get over here,” said a different man with a deeper, more commanding voice. Elion sensed his blade drawing nearer.

“It’s just pointing over at those barrels,” said a woman. “I think your machine is busted.”

“Um, er… It is sorta locking on over there,” said the reedy voiced man. “Are there tunnels?”

“Beneath the acid pits?” the woman asked. “Maybe. But I’m pretty sure it’s all full of acid down there.”

“Well, uh—” The reedy voiced man was cut off.

“Is there anyone else working around here?” This from the deep voiced man.

“No,” Keyla said. “Just the two of us.”

“Are you sure?” the woman asked. “I’m ordering you to tell the truth.”

“I’m telling the truth,” Keyla replied.

“I don’t think it works like that,” the reedy voiced man said. “I think it has to be—”

“This is a waste of time, Dave,” said the commanding man. “Fix your machine. We need to find that boy!”

“I—I think it’s working,” the reedy voiced man—Dave—said. “Um, sir, lord, um Elfron. I’m working on adding in the other element which should give us a vertical indication—”

“Well he isn’t here,” Elfron said, voice hard. “So lug your device back up that hill and don’t bother me again until it works.”

Grumbling from the reedy voiced man preceded the crunch of footsteps, moving away from Elion.

“What do you think?” Elfron asked.

“We’d be fools to not check for tunnels. If it’s possible he’s below us somewhere, we’d better find out,” the woman said.

More footsteps sounded as they moved away.

Elion was struck by a sudden sensation of claustrophobia. He felt a desperate need to get out of the barrel, and start capping off the open vats of acid out there waiting for him.

He couldn’t wait any longer. He started pushing up against the lid of the barrel, thrusting his shoulders into it. The lid flexed but did not open. Squatting as best he could in the tight confines, he thrust his body up, slamming into the lid.

The barrel swayed, moving and shifting as it was lifted into the air. Elion had a mental image of being placed onto the cargo rack of an arachnatronic.

He redoubled his efforts, repeating the maneuver, to no avail. He wasn’t strong enough to break free.
