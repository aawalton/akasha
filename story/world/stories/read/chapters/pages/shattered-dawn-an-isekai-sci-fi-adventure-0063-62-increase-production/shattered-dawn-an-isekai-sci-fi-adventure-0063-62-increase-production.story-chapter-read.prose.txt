Prator leaned back in his chair, drumming his fingers on the arm rest. He sat in his office, an elegant space patterned after pre-cataclysm buildings. Dark wood accents crossed pale stone walls. Deep blue curtains hung over the windows.

Venya had given him a hard time about the blue curtains. “Starholder blue? Where do your sympathies lie?“

“Dorian is a Starholder,” Prator had said, brushing the comment aside. “What’s wrong with blue?”

Then again, he was hiding his true sympathies.

Bortim lounged in a plush leather chair, swirling a drink of brown liquid in his hand.

“It’s getting more dangerous,” Bortim said. “With the increased warlock presence here. I think we should stop the dig entirely.”

“We can’t do that,” Prator said. “It would look suspicious.”

“Not after the order they just placed. We need all the labor we can get down in the acid pit right now.”

Prator groaned. “You’re right, you’re right. But I can’t help but feel that we’re so close. Every time I go down there, I have this sense—”

“Do you really want to unearth it while Venya is here? In New Kairn Tol?”

“I’m worried, Bortim. I’m worried about the Tephalian.”

Bortim chuckled. He stood and walked across the room, pouring himself another drink from a crystal ewer.

“Outages haunting you again?”

“Haunting us, Bortim.”

“I don’t know,” Bortim said. “I’ve been gaining experience at a faster rate than I’ve ever been able to do before. It’s almost as good as Ascendency stones. And it doesn’t cost anything.”

“So it’s not worrying you at all?”

“I’m sure there’s a good explanation for it,” Bortim said. “I know there are outages on the outer planets occasionally, and they don’t complain about it.”

“The good explanation is a Tephalian warrior,” Prator said. “Do you have a better way to explain the outages? Doesn’t it worry you that something out there can shut down Praxis like that?”

"Dorian says the Tephalians aren’t real,” Bortim said with a wave of his hand. “I’ve never seen one.” He took a sip of his drink. “Besides, why would a Tephalian be giving us experience points? Not a good strategy to level up your enemy.”

“You believe Dorian? Then what do you think is causing the outages?”

Bortim shrugged, his long red cloak rippling as he returned to his seat. “Could be related to that giant crystal up by the Ater river. False sentinel power.”

“Yeah,” Prator groaned, resting his head in his hands. “I’m worried about that too.”

“Scouts confirm that it’s gone. They think the explosion a few days ago had something to do with it. But they haven’t been able to get much closer, because of the army camped out there.”

“You’re really making me feel better, you know. Listing off all of our problems, one by one. Do you have any new information for me? What are we going to do about it?”

“I think it’s a good thing that Venya is stuck here,” Bortim said. “I see all these things as opportunities for us. If that army moves against us, then Venya will be here to see it happen. They’re clearly interested in preserving the flow of Thrandicite right now, so the warlocks will likely step in to defend New Kairn Tol.”

“Hmm…” Prator rubbed his chin, the short whiskers of his neatly trimmed beard prickling his fingers. “I suppose that’s true.”

“All we really need to do is pump up production,” Bortim said. “And collect the double payment that they’re offering for each shipment. As far as Venya’s missing Knight, well, we have no reason to think he’d be coming after us. He’s another opportunity. Find him, turn him over to Venya, and collect the reward.”

“You’re thinking short term, my friend,” Prator said. “What do you want to bet that Knight knows something about the army that’s gathering out there?”

"True, true,” Bortim said. “So we interrogate him, then hand him over to Venya. You’re worrying too much.”

“There’s an army out there, only a few days away from New Kairn Tol,” Prator said. “I don’t know what they want, or what they’re planning. They’ve killed every scout or messenger that we’ve sent over there, so it can’t be good.”

“Not every scout,” Bortim said. “And the ones who come back say it’s hardly an army. It’s more of a large mob.”

“I’ve made a lot of enemies on my recruiting trips, Bortim.”

“We are stronger than they are! If they come charging in here, we’ll kill a few, capture the rest, and just increase the labor pool. And the warlocks will help us. Besides, Arnolfo is due back soon with the arachnatronics you ordered. You won’t even have to use them, just show ‘em off to that mob of scavengers and they’ll go running.

“When you put things that way, they don’t seem as bad,” Prator said. “There’s still the issue of the outages…”

“Bah, what’s one little glitch with Praxis every couple of weeks. It’s been almost a month since that happened. I wouldn’t be surprised if they stopped all together, what with the destruction of that crystal shard.”

“Maybe,” Prator said. “Maybe you’re right.” But Prator couldn’t shake the feeling that everything he had worked for was slipping away from him; falling apart as he strove desperately to hold things together.

He needed Selna back. He needed her expertise. He cursed himself for being a total idiot.

“Any progress with Selna?” he asked. “It would be good to have her running the refinery again, right about now.”

Bortim shrugged. “You know what I know.”

Prator scratched the back of his neck.

“Shall I tell Jamis to stop the digging? Transfer the owned over to Boff’s pit, or start them training with the guard?”

“Don’t stop the digging yet. We can move people over to the acid pit or the refinery if Boff needs them. I want to keep on the dig for a bit longer.” He rose from his chair. “I’ll talk to Boff about where he could use more labor, and how soon. If we can just keep digging for a few more days, I feel certain we’ll find it.”

Bortim rolled his eyes, but didn’t protest. Why would he? Prator had conceded, agreeing with nearly everything the man had suggested.

Walking down the street toward the refinery, Prator wondered where he had gone wrong with Selna. He had tried to care for her, to treat her well. Maybe he waited too long to try creating the Bondhold, like Bortim said. But he’d been excited about the relationship they were building. Proud of the fact that she freely chose to be with him.

He still couldn’t believe that she’d tried to kill him.

An arachnatronic carried a shipment of Thrandicite out of the refinery, heading toward the staging area near the front gate.

Prator pushed open the door into the waiting room outside Boff’s office.

“Boff!” he called. “Are you around?”

The short, wiry man pulled his office door open. “Come on in,” he said. Prator followed him, eyeing the numerous bondholds that Boff wore.

Prator took a seat in Boff’s chair, behind his desk. Boff pulled up a wobbly plastic seat. The door to the corridor leading to the owned quarters was ajar.

“Got a payment for you,” Prator said. He dumped a handful of Zelian Splinters on to the desk.

“Ascendency Stones again?” Boff said, scoffing. “I need currency, coinage. These are hard to move out here. Hard to split up and divvy out. You can’t keep trying to pay me with these.”

“This is your pay for just one shipment,” Prator said.

“One?”

“Yes. One.”

Boff’s consternation evaporated, and he grinned greedily. “Oh? What’s going on?”

“The warlocks are voracious,” Prator said. “They’re doubling their prices on shipped Thrandicite. Everything we can produce, and they’re anxious for more. We need to up production as much as possible. There was even mention of buying acid barrels and shipping them to a processing facility in Erod.”

“Oh, no, that would be bad,” Boff said. “Better for us to refine it here. And make more money that way.”

“Agreed,” Prator said. “How much can you increase production? And how quickly? How many more laborers can you use?”

“Ah, well, a few more would be good. Depends on some of the machines we’ve got running, and how far we can push them before needing to do repairs…”

“Boff,” Prator said. “I know that you’ve been doing your best, but refinery production has been falling off over the last several months. Is this something that you can do?”

“That’s not my fault,” Boff protested. “There was that glitch in the heating tub, and then that big storm forced us to shut down and clear out the pumps for a few days and we ran through all our stored capacity. And stupid owned keep breaking machinery…”

“Do whatever you have to to pump up production. Check your heating tubs, install more pumps, whatever. We’re going to earn a lot of cends and I don’t want to mess this up.”

“Yes, of course,” Boff bobbed. “And we’re still working on the direct pumping project. We’ve got a dozen feet of pipe produced so far, and our modified pump was able to push liquid nearly half way up to the refinery. When we get that working—”

“Stop working on that for now,” Prator said. “Anything that isn’t maximizing production today is a waste of time.”

“Okay,” Boff said, wringing his hands. Bondholds jingled on his arms. “You got it.”

Prator nodded and turned toward the door that lead outside the building.

“Oh, one more thing,” he said. “Venya is looking for an Aurelian.”

“White hair, gold eyes?” Boff asked.

“A Knight,” Prator said. “But yes, Aurelian features too.”

“Ah, a knight. Not someone that Kendry is likely to catch out on patrol.”

Prator scoffed. “Not without significant casualties.”

“Okay then,” Boff said. “I’ll keep my eyes open.”

Prator grunted, then left the room.
