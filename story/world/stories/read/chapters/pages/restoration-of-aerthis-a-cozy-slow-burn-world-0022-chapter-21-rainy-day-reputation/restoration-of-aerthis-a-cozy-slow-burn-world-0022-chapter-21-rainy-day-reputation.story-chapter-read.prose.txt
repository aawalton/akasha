The next three days were marred by a lot of heavy rain. At first she thought it would pass quickly.

But no.

The rain refused to stop. Not after the first night. Not the second. By the third, the whole town felt like it was wrapped in a damp blanket of grey.

Elynara stood by the window with her forehead lightly pressed to the glass, watching droplets race each other down the pane like tiny wandering spirits. Her breath left a foggy patch.

She didn’t bother wiping it away.

Behind her, Starpuff zoomed in slow, lazy spirals while occasionally bumping into furniture just to hear the soft thunk. It had discovered that bouncing off the hanging kitchen ladle made the most satisfying noise. And was not above doing so repeatedly.

Elynara sighed.

She had expected the weather to turn at some point. But not this much.

At least her new waterproof robe got proper use. A dull silver-gray thing, with a slight sheen and practical hood that stayed up even when the wind gusted. She wore it now like a blanket, draped loosely over her regular clothes, arms tucked underneath. Practical. Reliable. A little sad.

The courtyard was empty save for puddles and a few determined weeds.

“Three days,” she muttered.

Three days of wet skies, chilled air, and muddy boots. Not that it stopped her from going to the guild, even if Alicia raised an eyebrow at the likely effort it took her to swim there.

Much as she liked having a little time off, she could only stay cooped up inside the church halls for so long.

And frankly, she just enjoyed helping people too!

But she hadn’t wasted the time spent inside either.

Each morning, she rose early despite the rain and sat cross-legged on her bed with her staff across her knees, the slow draw of mana becoming her ritual. It was steady work. Capacity training had grown into a kind of rhythm. A breath. A beat. A familiar push at the edge of her limit until the ache set in and she knew it was time to stop.

Then came the new experimental “mana breathing” practice.

No straining her limits. Just gentle motion.

She did it while helping chop vegetables, while walking halls, while pretending to listen to arguments over board games. Even while Mina braided a squirming kid’s hair beside her, Elynara would breathe slow, light threads of mana passing through her like drifting mist.

But… Despite how much and how often she did it, the effects were subtle. Very subtle.

Three full days passed before she even noticed something was different. Her cycling came a little easier now. She could extend a few cycles longer. Her practice felt just a little bit smoother.

No new point to her mana pool, though. Not yet.

She didn’t know how to feel about that.

“A whole new training method,” she mumbled, “and I still don’t get the satisfaction of seeing a number go up.”

Starpuff thunked into the back of her head in what was likely meant to be sympathy.

She waved it off with one hand.

Still, the test had value. It proved something. Just not something flashy. Or fast.

Maybe that was the lesson. That everything took work and effort.

Even tiny, subtle breaths of magic.

But if she kept going, kept practicing, and just stayed a little patient, maybe she would find something better at the end of the slow path as well.

And hey, at least the robe was warm!

She pulled the hood tighter, turned away from the window, and stepped lightly toward the common hall.

Another rainy day to make hers.

At least it didn’t stop her from visiting the library!

In fact, it made the place feel cozier than usual. A steady patter against the windows, the faint scent of old pages and warm dust, and the ever-present shuffle of robes as the helpful nun worked her way through endless piles of books.

Elynara returned daily. Sometimes to read, sometimes to finish what she started earlier, and more often than not just to ask a lot of questions.

The nun's responses were always patient, and with a kind of soft-spoken precision that made Elynara feel strangely secure even as she listened.

And maybe also a bit guilty.

So by the second day of this impromptu research spree, she brought snacks.

She wasn’t even subtle about it. Just shuffled into the library, set a little pouch of treats on the desk, and tried to act like it was completely normal to bribe nuns with sweets.

The nun looked at the pouch, then at her. Smiled gently.

“Thank you, dear. That’s very kind of you.”

Then patted her head.

Elynara blinked. And blushed. Hard.

Starpuff, who up until then had been behaving, immediately let out a high-pitched snort of laughter and spun around her head in triumph.

“Traitor,” she muttered under her breath.

The puff spun smugly and perched on a shelf like it had earned a reward.

Despite everything, the visits were pleasant. Even though the books weren’t particularly magical, containing mostly local histories, old travel journals, or treatises on farming tools, she still learned things.

Some things.

She confirmed much of what she already suspected: magic was rare. Especially around here. And what little there was tended to come in stories or half-remembered tales. One log mentioned a woman who could calm animals with her voice; in another, a soldier who claimed to have once cut through stone. Nothing clear. Nothing teachable.

But during one of their quiet conversations, the nun mentioned something else. Something that caught her attention.

“It’s not often discussed,” the woman had said, sorting scrolls into a cubby, “but many of the more advanced skills used by craftsmen and adventurers alike draw on mana.”

Elynara straightened.

“Wait–like passively?”

“Not always. It’s not something most of them are even aware of. But the more advanced a skill is, the more effort it takes. Some of that effort is mana, whether they notice it or not. Then again, many likely just require mana regardless.”

It made sense.

Maybe they didn’t think of it like magic, but if mana was involved...

She tapped her pen slowly against her notes later, flipping back to her observations on skill growth and core strain.

If they were using mana unconsciously, then perhaps it wasn’t just mages who could ‘train’ it. Maybe smiths and scouts and hunters, all of them could slowly refine their skills by channeling mana without realizing it.

It explained a lot.

Especially how some people had such uncanny precision. Or stamina that didn’t make sense. Or how Alicia always seemed to know what she was up to, in her own professionally scary way!

But what stood out the most wasn’t the mystery.

It was the conclusion.

She might actually be the only proper mage in Oltenar.

Not just someone with a skill or two. Not just someone who sparked fire in an emergency or shaped wind once as a child. But someone who actually trained it. Lived it. Built spells, practiced shaping, meditated daily, and bent magic to her will like a muscle.

The thought hit harder than she expected.

She was… the only one?

Or at least, that’s how it seemed. In this town. Maybe the nearby villages too.

She stared out the library window for a moment, watching the rain race down the glass in tiny rivers.

Alone in it.

But not lonely.

She had Starpuff. Mina. Father Alaric. The guild. The kids. And she had her path.

It was strange.

But it was hers.

And most of all, she just loved to learn about magic, even if practice was sometimes hard. Or dull. Or it made her the ‘weird’ one in the room.

Not that the kids minded, echoes of them calling her ‘Fire Queen’ still living in her head rent-free. Those little brats!

But it wasn’t until a little later, during one of her get-togethers with Mina, that Elynara learned the other piece of the puzzle.

It really just started as a casual comment.

They had been sitting in the courtyard, half-tucked beneath the wide awning while the rain made puddles of the stones beyond. Elynara had conjured a faint, flickering wisp of light for no real reason—just a gentle spiral of warm flame to keep the children occupied.

Mina sat beside her, puffing air into Pufflet’s fur while trying to braid a leaf into its fluff.

Elynara let the flame flicker once more between her fingers before murmuring.

“You know, I might actually be the only mage in town.”

Mina blinked.

“You think?”

“Feels like it,” she said, and added more softly, “or at least I have yet to see anybody else practicing any spells.”

“Well... most people can’t,” Mina replied, leaning back against the stone step. “It’s hard. And slow. And doesn’t really give much back unless you really push for it.”

That was fair, Elynara thought. But still...

“But I’ve seen people train. Fighters. Scouts. Even hunters. They get better with effort.”

“Sure,” Mina shrugged. “But I don’t think they use mana. Not the way you do. Not really.”

That’s when it clicked.

Mana Attuned.

Her gift from Celestia.

It hadn’t just helped her learn and manipulate magic—it had quietly tilted the entire playing field.

“Wait,” she said slowly, “how long does it take you to recover mana?”

Mina tilted her head.

“I don’t really... know? I can sort of tell when it’s full, if I focus. But I never even really used all of it up.”

“Um, you can still check with your Status, right?”

“Oh, yes! It says I’m getting, uh, some 6 mana per day? So if I needed to recharge all of it… Maybe... three? Four days? Not whole though!”

As cute as Mina looked trying to figure all of that out, Elynara still nearly choked on her breath at the news.

“Four days?!”

Mina shrugged again, casual as ever.

And suddenly it all made sense.

Her regeneration wasn’t just decent—it was incredible. What she recovered in a few hours, most people would take days to regain, at least until they reached higher levels. And that was even before counting her meditation techniques, her careful shaping, and the fact that she could pull mana from the world directly.

Even here. Even in this small town where everything felt relatively peaceful, she had been sprinting through progress others could barely crawl through.

No wonder magic was rare.

No wonder most people didn’t bother.

If mana recovery took that long, even the most curious person would eventually give up. Without the right body, and the right mindset, a teacher and outside help, and without the time—it simply wasn’t worth it.

Although… that would likely change again if ambient mana increased. By a lot. She didn’t know by how much yet, but if it could be done… then perhaps things could change.

Elynara let her flame vanish and leaned back against the post with a soft groan.

“Well, that’s depressing.”

“Yep,” Mina said cheerfully, now pinning a second leaf to Pufflet’s head.

The small animal blinked slowly, looking extremely betrayed.

Starpuff, who had been napping overhead like a lazy ceiling ornament, apparently found it very funny and shot down to tease the poor Pufflet plenty.

“Here we go,” Elynara muttered.

The next minute became an all-out fur war, with the two trying to chase each other. Or at least Pufflet was the one trying to catch the invisible sprite bothering it, while Starpuff circled and poked it unbothered.

Mina giggled and Elynara leaned back, hand over her face.

“Somehow, this is the bit I understand best,” she said.

Mina leaned against her shoulder.

“You're still the only one that makes butterflies out of fire.”

“Should have never done that show,” she groaned.

Because of course, the children hadn’t let her live it down.

Every spare moment since then had been full of poking and pleading and dramatically loud declarations of:

“FIRE QUEEN ELYNARA, PLEASE GRANT US FLAMES!”

She tried to set boundaries. She really did.

But they kept finding her.

So she improvised.

A simple trick: conjure a few flame butterflies, make them loop and flutter so the kids turn away chasing them, and then—run.

The first time she pulled it off, she was proud. Disappeared behind the hall in record time. Smug satisfaction followed her all the way to the pantry.

The second time, she hid in the laundry closet.

They found her anyway.

By the third time, the kids had made it a game.

“Chase the Flame Queen!” they cried, little arms stretched out like wings, one of them wielding a mop as a makeshift sword.

“Defend her honor!”

“Capture her fire wisdom!”

“There’s no wisdom!” she shouted, sprinting past a confused nun. “Only pain!”

Even Starpuff had betrayed her—twice. It had cheered for them. The traitor.

Now, whenever she saw more than two of them huddled together, whispering, she started backing toward the nearest door.

There really was no winning.

Kids really were energy incarnate. And apparently? She had become their main quest objective.

But... even as she muttered her complaints and plotted her next butterfly escape route, she never really meant it.

Their laughter still warmed her chest in the best way.

Even if her cheeks were still a little sore from too many smiling fits.

Yet it also warmed her during the times she had to go outside.

Rain hadn’t stopped for more than a handful of hours in the past three days.

It wasn’t the dramatic sort, but persistent. Endless mist one moment, heavy drumming the next. It soaked roofs and courtyards, washed the cobblestones smooth, and turned gutter paths into lazy streams that trailed toward the edge of town like they had nowhere better to be.

Elynara didn’t mind it too much.

Her robe kept the worst of it off, the wide hood hanging just low enough to keep her lashes dry. Starpuff had decided it hated the rain and mostly took refuge under the cowl, though it occasionally peeked out to glare at puddles as if they personally offended it.

Town requests didn’t stop just because the sky was sulking.

They just... slowed down.

“Amazing how few adventurers want to get their boots wet,” Alicia had muttered on the first day, half-burying her face in a stack of incomplete postings.

Elynara had smiled then, taken two of the easier ones, and gone off to help with the repair of a broken fence line before the wind finished the job.

That trend continued. Small, simple jobs.

Mostly just deliveries that needed to be done despite the weather, helping one older woman cart her wares from her flooded porch to the covered stand by the market corner. Nothing that needed spells. Just hands, effort, and a little attention.

She came in soaked more than once, her scarf damp and satchel edges dripping. But she didn’t complain. Not once.

Because no matter how bad it got, it felt good to help.

Alicia PoV

There she was again.

The door creaked open with a familiar squeak, and the scent of wet wool followed. That oversized hood first, then a spark of green from under the fold, and finally the girl herself. Rain-dappled, cheeks a little flushed from the cold, and holding one—no, two—slips of parchment carefully in one hand.

Alicia resisted the urge to sigh. Or smile. She wasn’t sure which one was more appropriate.

“I told you to take it easy,” she said, not even looking up from her ledger yet.

“I did,” came the sheepish reply. “This is easy.”

Alicia set the quill down.

The girl had said that before.

And sure, it was just rain. Just small jobs. Just errands no one else wanted to do. But still.

She leaned back in her chair slightly and studied her.

Elynara had changed.

Not in the obvious way, maybe.

She was still soft around the edges, still a bit awkward when praised too much, still carrying herself like she didn’t quite believe she belonged. But there was strength and confidence now where previously there was only awkward shyness. She changed not just in her steps, or her work, but also in the way she looked people in the eye. The way she handled requests. The way she acted, like someone who knew she could help.

Alicia remembered when the girl had first walked through the door. Half-whispered voice, flinching at raised tones, and clutching her satchel like a safety net. Back then, she figured that without her guidance and encouragement, the girl might last a week. Two, tops.

Then she killed that monster.

Alone.

Alicia still wasn’t sure how she felt about that. Somewhere between impressed and very annoyed.

She flipped through the latest local summaries. Her fingers tapped once, twice, then stopped at the notes she had scrawled the other day. A mention of spirit traces shared with the shrinefolk. Another of fieldwork done near the farmstead. And here—a report of someone requesting specifically for Elynara. Word had started to spread.

Not from her directly. Alicia doubted the girl had the ego for it. But others noticed. Farmers, traders, even that one old traveler who swore she saw a butterfly made of flame zip through the alleyway at night.

Right, Alicia thought, eyes narrowing faintly. The butterflies.

She had had to rewrite the guild board description three times to keep it from sounding like a fairytale.

But the girl wasn’t chasing praise. That was the strange part. She just... wanted to do better. Learn more. Push forward.

Alicia respected that.

Even if it meant watching a barely-rested apprentice sprint headfirst into danger with an invisible spirit in tow.

She glanced up just in time to catch Elynara straightening by the desk, the request slips placed neatly into the tray.

“All done?”

Elynara nodded, and then fumbled as apparently the invisible spirit hiding under her hood chose that exact moment to bother her with a prideful prrrt - if the girl’s murmurs were to be believed.

She probably wasn’t even aware how often she would just say things into the air, like nobody was listening. Yet was too shy to talk to strangers sometimes!

Alicia raised an eyebrow at both of them, but let the moment pass.

“You’ve been getting some requests now. By name.”

“I noticed,” Elynara said quietly, almost bashfully.

Alicia hummed.

“Good work.”

The girl blinked. Then blinked again.

“What?”

“I said, good work,” Alicia repeated. “Don’t make me say it a third time.”

Elynara gave a small, startled laugh.

Alicia looked back to her notes, lips twitching just slightly.

The girl didn’t ask for much. Didn’t complain. And did her work well. Which meant Alicia would keep her safe. Would give her the right assignments. Would clear the path forward.

And if anyone tried to take advantage of her?

Well, Alicia knew how to break fingers with a ledger spine and make it look like bookkeeping.

Let them try.

She glanced up once more as Elynara turned to leave, the door creaking faintly in the rain-washed quiet.

Yeah.

She really did look forward to seeing just how far that girl would go.

But for now?

“Oi,” she called, just before the door closed.

Elynara peeked back in.

“Yes?”

“Dry off properly this time. And if I see you catching a cold, you’re banned from the board.”

“Yes ma’am.”

Then she was gone.

Alicia exhaled, leaned back, and scribbled one more line in the notes for tomorrow’s log.

“Promising.”

Her first mistake was agreeing to it.

Elynara remembered nodding. She remembered Marcus grinning. She remembered thinking, how bad could it be? After all, she had survived a horned bear.

So surely taking Marcus up on his offer of training wouldn’t go too badly, right?

Oh, how wrong she was, her confidence evaporating quicker than she could run.

And there was a lot of running.

So much running.

“I thought we were supposed to practice staffwork!” she gasped, trying to keep pace as Marcus jogged backwards with infuriating ease.

“We are,” he called back. “Can’t dodge well if you’re winded, can you?”

“Then maybe stop winding me!”

He only laughed.

The rain had slowed to a drizzle by then—enough to keep the streets slick, but not enough to stop Marcus from dragging her to the back of the training yard behind the guild. He had started her with drills. Easy ones, at first. Step and pivot. Weight and balance. The basic things she thought she would have down quickly.

She did not, apparently.

Her second mistake was thinking he would go easy on her.

Marcus didn’t yell. He didn’t scold. But he had a way of pointing out every flaw with relentless cheer.

“Too wide.”

“Don’t swing your elbow like that, you’ll knock yourself out.”

“Imagine I’m three times your size. Try again.”

And every correction came with another round of running. Or circling. Or dodging the flat end of his staff as he poked her ankles mid-step to test her balance.

He called it encouragement.

She called it pain.

Still, she didn’t stop.

When the footing clicked, when her legs moved without stumbling, when her grip caught the arc just right—Marcus nodded. Just once. But it was enough. That nod meant progress. Real, tangible progress.

And her third mistake?

Pushing too hard.

By the time they wrapped up, her arms were trembling, her knees wobbly, and she had used mana once or twice just to keep her grip from failing.

Marcus handed her a waterskin as they cooled down.

“Not bad,” he said.

She stared at him.

“I’m dying.”

He snorted.

“You’ll live. And your footwork’s better. You're lighter on your heels now. Less like a duck.”

“I feel like a duck that got thrown down a cliff.”

“Then it’s working.”

She wasn’t sure whether to be proud or offended. Though soon enough the only feeling that remained was pain. And soreness.

…Her walk back towards the church sure felt quite long, all of a sudden.

Her legs dragged, her shoulders slouched, and she was fairly certain Starpuff had gone completely silent out of respect—or perhaps fear that nudging her now would result in spontaneous combustion.

She didn’t talk much. Just focused on walking without collapsing into a bench.

But somewhere under the ache, she felt... good.

It had hurt. It was still hurting. But she had learned. She had grown.

And when she collapsed onto her bed later, limbs sore and soul wrung out like a cloth, she did so with a quiet, stubborn grin.

Progress came one bruise at a time.

By the time Elynara reached the church steps, her legs were a chorus of complaints. The rain had stopped at some point—she wasn’t sure when—and the path up to the main hall glistened faintly with puddled sheen, catching bits of lantern light.

She pushed the door open with her shoulder and stepped inside.

“Elynara!”

The voices hit first. A trio of little feet thundered down the hallway, two stopping short and one launching directly at her hip like a particularly enthusiastic pillow.

“Oof–careful, careful!”

“She’s soaked!”

“She’s back!”

Warmth hit her harder than the rain ever could.

“There you are!”

And before she could answer, Mina nearly tackled her too. Only her own momentum saved her from toppling over. The hug came fast and full, arms wrapping tight around her waist. Then a step back, wide eyes scanning her like she might vanish again.

“You look awful,” Mina said.

“I feel worse,” Elynara answered, barely managing a smile.

Then came the others. One child peeked around the corner with a whispered “Fire Queen?” before vanishing again. Another darted up, handed her a half-wrinkled drawing of a butterfly on fire, then ran off.

And finally, Father Alaric, seemingly summoned by the commotion, peeked out from just behind the bench with his hands folded and a quiet pride softening the corners of his mouth.

“Welcome back, Elynara. You’ve been busy,” he said gently.

She nodded.

“It was just… training.”

“And you’re still standing. That’s more than most can say, after training with Marcus.”

He stepped forward, resting a hand lightly against her shoulder in encouragement.

“Well done.”

Something about those words stuck with her, and soon her cheeks were just a little more rosy.

…She really was too weak to praise!

Though it made the ache in her legs, in her arms, her back matter far less. Like just being around those kind souls was enough to dismiss it.

And soon enough dinner came - with warmth and familiar clatter. A few of the kids snuck more glances than usual. Someone slipped a folded note beside her bowl that just said “thank you for firebird” in messy pencil.

She didn’t say anything.

But she kept it.

Later, as the lanterns dimmed and the halls quieted, Elynara sat curled beneath her blanket, letting the comfort of warmth and exhaustion lull her toward rest.

And yet her thoughts were still awake.

This world—this place she had ended up in—was hurting. She had seen the land stretched thin. Mana fading. Forests empty of spirits. Whispers of slow dying.

But not everything was gone.

Not yet.

The children still laughed. Mina still scolded her gently. The old halls still smelled of warm bread and herbs. And Father Alaric still stood in the chapel doorway with a smile like sunlight filtered through old glass.

So long as those things remained...

She could still do something.

She didn’t know how yet. Didn’t know the shape of the answer, barely an inking of a plan. But she would find it. She had to.

And even if it took a hundred small steps, even if she had to walk through rain and ache and failure—

She would keep going.

For them.

And for herself too.

A quiet trill sounded at her elbow. Starpuff floated closer, nuzzled her sleeve once, then flopped against her chest with exaggerated exhaustion.

She chuckled, barely.

“Fine. You earned your mana.”

A slow, lazy thread passed from her fingers into the puff. Its glow brightened, content. She watched it drift slightly, then settle in with a sleepy hum.

“Tomorrow again, then,” she whispered.

She tucked the blanket in tighter.

And fell asleep with the warmth of their smiles still wrapped around her.
