The door pulsed again, and nobody moved toward it.

Kage noted that, the way he noted everything, and let the silence do its work. A party that had just watched a sky un-write itself into a single doorway was a party that could afford five seconds of standing still. He gave them ten.

Zherdev broke first.

"So," he said, with a slightly hushed cadence. "We go in now? Like, right now? Or do we like—do a thing first. Do we do a thing?"

"Final wing," Vami said, eyes still on the door. "That's the final wing. The whole sky tipped over into one frame."

Jax cleared his throat. He had been cleared-throating for a while now, working up to whatever he was working up to, and Kage had been waiting for it the way you waited for a teakettle.

"Hear me out," Jax shifted his weight. He had, over the last few hours, developed the habit of pre-checking with Kage before he said anything load-bearing, and Kage was choosing, for now, to find this acceptable rather than humiliating. "The XP," Jax said. "Like. The XP in here… the loot. The drops have been insane. We're a few good wings from twenty-five across the board."

"Twenty-five," Zara said, quietly, "is the subclass threshold."

"Twenty-five is the subclass threshold," Jax agreed.

The platform got very quiet.

Kage looked up at the door, and then at the drifting frames in the void around them, the ones nobody had bothered to count. He looked at the marks on Vami's and Ren's and Zherdev's hands, currently all pointing one direction. He looked at the other frames, the ones not currently pointed at by anything, and remembered Zara with her arms crossed and her mouth doing its annoyed line.

We've been doing this wrong.

She had said it once. She was not going to say it again. She was, instead, standing at the edge of the platform with her hands folded and her face set in the careful neutral.

"It's not the worst idea," Kage said. "Walk into one not pointed at by the keys, see what happens. If it wipes us, we don't do it again. If it gives us exp, we do it again."

"Romantic," Zara murmured.

"Pick one." Kage gestured towards Zara.

She picked one.

It was, she explained, on the basis of having no observable distinguishing features whatsoever, the safest frame to try. Medium size. Drifting on a slow lateral trajectory about thirty meters off the platform's edge. The painting inside the frame was so unfinished it was barely a painting; just a wash of pale color that suggested field without committing to it.

A panel opened in the air between her and the frame.

[Entry Denied.]

[This draft is unstable. Access restricted.]

Zara's hand stopped. She tried again.

[Entry Denied.]

[This draft is unstable. Access restricted.]

"Well," Zara said. "There it is. Decoration after all."

Kage did not say anything for a moment, because the part of his brain that the Operator had spent years trying to muzzle was making its small soft huh sound, and the Operator was, for once, listening.

Unstable, he thought.

The dungeon, on entry, had recognized him as Master. The Curator, somewhere in this archive, was waiting in a room that had been built specifically by reassembling the world out of poems because Kage had walked into the threshold. Probably. The mark on the back of his hand was, apparently, the First Maker's ink, and the First Maker had, by every available signal, written this place.

And the system was telling him a draft was unstable.

Unstable, he thought again, and almost smiled. Unstable to whom.

"Wait," he said, out loud, to the party. To Zara especially, who had already started turning back toward the door in the sky with the expression of someone marking down a column. "Give me a second. I'm going to try something stupid."

"Sure," the party collectively replied.

He stepped to the platform's edge and looked at the drifting frame. It was unbothered by his attention. The pale wash inside it continued to be a pale wash. The frame itself drifted on its slow lateral trajectory, indifferent to anything that was not also a frame.

He thought about it.

Truth Factor check, he thought. The dungeon already calls me Master. I don't need to bluff. Make it about access. Make it about stability.

He ran his keywords mentally. [Shape] was open at twenty-two percent. [Bind] sat at twenty-nine. [Shape] meant form, meant give a thing the boundary that lets it be a thing. [Bind] meant fix in place. An unstable draft was a thing that had not yet been shaped, an outline that had not yet been bound to itself. The keywords were not just thematically compatible. They were thematically correct.

He rolled the couplet in his head.

"An entry."

Then he spoke it. Quiet. Even. The voice he used when he wanted the system to register intent over volume.

"Hold this draft in steady shape.

Bind its borders. Grant escape."

Two keywords. They rhymed. They told the system, in plain language, that this unfinished frame was to become enterable; that its instability was now to be considered a closed question.

[-140 Awen]

The frame, drifting on its slow lateral trajectory, stopped.

It hung in the void. The pale wash inside it stuttered, and then, with the slightly embarrassed motion of a thing that had been caught with its pants down, finished itself. A field came into existence inside the frame. Long grass. A horizon. A specific quality of afternoon light. The painting that had been a suggestion of a painting was, now, a painting.

A new panel opened.

Zara, behind him, said one word. Quietly. With feeling. "Hilarious."

Vami started laughing. "You can just do that," he said. "You can just write the lock open."

"Apparently."

"That's… okay. Okay."

What followed was, in the language Kage privately used for these things, a grind.

A good one.

The frames were not, individually, dungeon wings on the scale of the five marked ones. They were drafts in the original and literal sense; sketches, half-finished compositions, scenes abandoned in various stages of incompletion. Some of them were field-and-sky. Some of them were a single half-rendered building standing in fog. One was an entire river with no banks, water hanging in mid-air in long unsupported ribbons because the writer had not gotten around to deciding where it was for. Each of them had monsters, because the system, like every system, abhorred a vacuum, and so it filled the unfinished spaces with creatures pulled from the surrounding poetry; concept-things, half-words with teeth, sentences that had forgotten what they were trying to say.

The party adapted.

They got fast.

The pattern repeated. Kage spoke his couplet, the frame stabilized, they went in, they killed everything, they came out, they picked the next frame. The verse cost adjusted itself down as Truth Factor compounded; by the fourth frame the system was charging him barely above a base couplet's cost, the dungeon's resistance to him eroding the more often he edited it. By the eighth frame, the Entry Denied panel had started to feel less like a wall and more like a hold-music message played at someone who had already proven they were allowed past the front desk.

The XP came in waves.

Jax dinged twenty-five somewhere in the third frame, made a sound like finding money in an old coat, and immediately opened his subclass selection panel and started panic-reading. Lily hit twenty-five in the fourth. Finn in the fifth, with a soft startled noise and the immediate apologetic deflection of someone who did not want to be congratulated for anything. Zara, second. Valdrias, sixth. Each of them, on hitting the threshold, paused in whatever they were doing and stepped back from the fight long enough to read the panel; each of them, when they were done reading, came out the other side with the slightly different posture of a person who had just made a permanent choice.

Kage did not ask what any of them picked. He did, however, watch.

Lily's healing got noticeably more aggressive. Valdrias's shield work began incorporating a small bright pulse on every taunt that Kage suspected was a guardian-line specialization. Zara's frost started leaving structures behind on the ground after her casts; little geometric residues that her own party could walk on for a few seconds. Jax, predictably, picked something that made him hit harder. Finn picked something that gave his hawk a faint blue overlay, and the hawk, when it dove, now came down faster than physics quite permitted.

Julie had hit twenty-five in the second frame, quietly, the way she did most things; she had glanced at her panel, made a small considering face, picked something, and gone back to keeping Valdrias alive without comment. The change showed in the field. Her Tender threads, the spatial tethers she wove off other people's momentum, started catching not just damage now but positioning, tugging Vami a half-step out of bad ground without him noticing she had done it.

Vami hit twenty-six early, twenty-seven on the seventh frame, gave Kage a single tight nod, and went immediately back to vaporizing things in straight lines. Ren hit twenty-six somewhere in the middle of all of it, and finally, finally, selected the subclass. He stood very still while reading it, as if time from twenty-five to twenty-six wasn't enough to choose. When he closed it, his curved longsword had picked up a slow water-line of light along the edge that Kage caught one glimpse of and then deliberately stopped looking at.

Zherdev hit twenty-five in the fourth frame and announced it to the void in a way that suggested the void had been waiting to hear. He read his subclass panel with slow lip-moving. When he closed it, his greatsword grew a second sheath of red haze that pulsed in time with his breathing, and his swings, which had been firewood-chopping before, became louder firewood-chopping. Berserker-line, Kage assumed. Some rage-stacking damage spiral. Zherdev looked extremely pleased.

Kage himself crossed twenty-seven on a Margin Warden that had been built out of an unfinished sentence about wind. The level-up was clean and quiet and arrived with the small realization that he was now one from another Specialization Stone and three from the level required to equip The Lachrymal Warden's cape that was still sitting in his bag like a debt waiting to be called.

The loot was generous.

It was, more importantly, not for him.

Valdrias picked up a plate piece that fit him as if the dungeon had cut it to his measurements. Finn picked up a quiver that doubled the velocity of his first shot per encounter. Zara picked up a robe with frost-conduction so clean she actually said thank you to the corpse it had dropped from. Jax got a sword. He held it the way people held swords in commercials. He looked happy.

Kage got one piece.

He saw it on the corpse of a Bound Stenographer in a frame that had not bothered to finish its sky. A long flat cord-and-vellum sash, dark blue and silver, sized for a Poet's cut of coat. It hummed against his Synesthesia the way correctly-fitted things hummed, the way Mumyo had hummed when he had first set hand to it, just smaller. The other gear that came off the Stenographer he kicked, without ceremony, toward the others. The belt he kept.

[Sash of the Held Margin]

Quality: Rare (Blue)

Type: Cloth Belt

Armor: +10

Artistry (ART): +18

Stamina (STA): +5

Requirement: Level 25, 50 ART

Base Belt Equipment Mechanic:

[Reactive Vial] - When the wearer's HP or primary resource falls below 30%, automatically consumes one potion stored in the belt's reserved slot. Subject to standard 30-second potion cooldown.

Passive Effect:

[Held Margin] - Damage taken while performing poetry reduced by 25%.

Description: A scribe's sash, weighted at both ends with small ink-stained vellum tabs. The Stenographer who wore it kept the margins of an unfinished draft from spilling into one another. It will, if asked, do the same for you.

He read [Reactive Vial]. Considered it. Dismissed it.

Convenient, he thought. Also dumb.

Potions had a thirty-second internal cooldown. A belt that fired a potion on threshold did not, in any practical sense, save a player's life so much as decide, on the player's behalf, when a player's only emergency button was spent. Edge case: he popped a panic potion at twenty-nine percent HP because the belt's threshold tripped, then ate the kind of two-second burst that would have wanted the potion at six percent, ten seconds later. Now the potion was on cooldown. Now he was dead.

He'd pop them himself, thanks.

[Held Margin], on the other hand. 25% damage reduction while performing poetry. The next time he had to write a Haiku into the middle of a boss fight, the dungeon was going to be marginally less successful at making him regret it.

He slotted the belt.

The belt sat against his coat like a footnote in the wrong font.

A panel obliged him almost immediately.

[Visual Cohesion broken.]

[Bonus Lost: +5% to Social Interaction outcomes with NPCs.]

Yeah, he thought, looking down at himself. Fair.

Everything else on him was charcoal and burgundy and dark leather, expertly walked through Mirethiel's vats until the silver filigree on his coat caught light like a vein. The sash was navy blue and unapologetically not. Against the rest, it looked like someone had tied a stranger's bookmark around his waist.

Five percent NPC disposition was not nothing. It was, on a normal week, the difference between a vendor laughing at him and a vendor knocking three silver off a stack of trash loot. But the belt was 23 points of raw stats, which was, in any honest accounting, significantly more than five percent.

He read the description a second time. The Stenographer who wore it kept the margins of an unfinished draft from spilling into one another.

Stenographer. A clerk. The archive had staff once, then. People whose work was keeping the drafts from bleeding into each other, keeping the unfinished world organized.

Interesting.

He'd let Mirethiel dye it when he got back.

Which, helpfully, lined up with a quest he had been carrying in the back of his head. [The Chromatic Thread - Master's Palette]. Petals. Verdant Reach. ART check. Later.

Dungeon first.

The other thing the dungeon kept giving him was ink.

It dropped from every frame. Different colors, different volumes, different qualities. Most of it was middling. Some of it was not. By the time the party had, finally, voluntarily, decided to stay out of frames, Kage's inventory contained a small library of bottles: greens, deep golds, two of something that read as Rare and one bottle marked only with a smear of script he did not recognize and a tag that said Identify at Higher Artistry.

Good enough, he thought, and closed the menu.

The party reassembled on the obsidian platform.

Newly twenty-five, mostly. Newly twenty-six, in Ren's case. Wearing gear that, on most of them, still had the faint shine of something that had not yet been hit hard enough to scuff. Lily's hands moved differently when she cast. Valdrias stood with a small fractional widening of his stance that suggested whatever his subclass had given him, it had given him roots. Zherdev had, somewhere in the grind, acquired a helmet that he had again declined to actually wear.

The door above them had not changed.

It still hung in the sky where the moons had been, fully drawn, breathing its slow patient breath. The rare droplets on Vami and Ren and Zherdev still pointed up at it. The blank white sheets that had been moons drifted around it, empty, waiting.

"Office hours over," Vami murmured, quiet enough that maybe only Kage and Julie caught it.

Kage looked at the door.

He looked at his party. Twenty-five across the board, minimum. Three at twenty-six or above. And himself.

Newly belted. Inks in the bag. Mumyo at the hip, faint warm pulse against his thigh, satisfied with the work.

"Form up," Kage said, mildly.

They formed up.

The door pulsed once, the same patient pulse it had done before the grind, as if it had been waiting and had not minded the wait.

Kage took the first step. The platform generated a long quiet stair of obsidian that rose toward the door's height in a clean inclining line. Except the stairs came from him.

The script orbiting his arm uncoiled, sentence by sentence, and laid itself out ahead of his boot. Each line settled flat, hardened into a tread, and waited. He climbed his own writing toward the door.

The party climbed behind him.

The door, as he reached it, changed. The line of script down its center brightened, and the panel and the lintel went soft at their edges, and the entire surface dissolved into a single still page of waiting white.

He stepped through.
