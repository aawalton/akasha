Zora stood between Kita and Enki, and before them, the three Grafting Bug Afflicted shifted. They sharpened their claws. Extended their stingers. Narrowed their eyes. They were hardly afraid, but they were counting heartbeats until it was time for the next murder.

In response, Zora thumped his staff, and the sound landed like a judge’s gavel.

“From the old tale of The Earthen Princess—when cities of stone and steel rot, let the earth remember its first shape.”

The world answered him.

Stone and timber around them shuddered, and the nearest buildings unmade themselves. Bricks loosened. Beams groaned. Entire walls collapsed inward and then… didn’t fall. They broke into their foundational pieces instead—raw planks, stones, carved pillars, roof tiles—and those pieces lifted into the air, turned, and began to assemble into something else.

A hundred metres of street around them became a living storm of flying pieces. Wood splintered and braided together. Stone stacked into trunks and buttress-roots. Shattered doors became broad leaves, and window frames became branching antlers of architecture, reaching skyward. The grafting bugs took careful steps back and observed as, in a mere minute, an artificial fungi forest made of reformed timber and mortared stone rose where there’d just been a city.

Zora grinned as the last of the floating pieces found their places amongst the artificial fungi trees. He hadn’t spent the last two years learning everything he could learn from the people of the Attini Empire for nothing. He’d learned about breaking down buildings from demolition experts, rebuilding fieldworks overnight from farmers, and reseeding trees in a fungi forest from gardeners and forest harvesters. He’d learned the principles of what made up a fungi forest from the empire—and now he could visualize himself piecing together an entire forest from scratch as long as he had the materials to do so.

If he had to build an entire artificial forest by hand, brick by brick, timber by timber, it would’ve taken him years, if not decades… but as the old War Commanders always told him, with equal admiration and annoyance, his Art was pretty bullshit.

The grafting bugs finally had enough of watching. All three launched forward the moment they felt the terrain was about to stop changing, and Zora—with his staff still lifted to make his spell reverberate and last longer so it could finish affecting the world—glanced over at Enki.

“Bring us up,” he said.

And Enki opened a wormhole beneath the three of them, swallowing their feet out from under them. They fell through as the nearest scorpion tail swept the space they’d occupied a heartbeat prior, and then they landed on a broad plank-made several dozen meters overhead. Zora caught himself by stabbing his staff into the branch. Enki balanced himself without any effort, while Kita stabbed one of her blades into the brick-made bark.

Below, the grafting bugs whirled in confusion, snarling in three different ways. But it only took them a few more seconds to whirl up, sensing Zora’s presence.

Zora lifted his staff and pointed it straight down at them.

“Forests are easy to burn, aren’t they?”

His voice rippled outward, amplified by his staff, and a plume of heat burst from the tip of his staff to turn into a crawling, devouring wave of hellfire. The flames smashed into the ground and washed out, licking up roots and spilling debris, and the entire artificial forest ignited as though it’d been soaked in oil.

The forest immediately became a giant grill with roaring flames for the ground. The streets below vanished beneath the heat, and with it vanished the silent approach of Giant-Class and Mutant-Class bugs that’d been crawling around the former alleys and buildings. Hundreds of bugs shrieked as they burned all at once, chitins popping, wings crisping. The air filled with the sounds of things dying too fast to understand what he’d just done, and even Kita and Enki flinched as fire was all they could see of the ground for a hundred meters around them.

Kita, in particular, let out a nervous laugh as she drilled her blade deeper into the bark for more stability. “You really are like a mage,” she said, voice tight with awe. “I would have had to go down there and chop up every single bug individually, but your spells… cover everything.”

Zora’s smile stayed small as the forest burned beneath them.

“Keep your eyes on the grafting bugs,” he said. “They’re not rabble who die from just a little bit of heat.”

True enough, the three grafting bugs directly beneath them weren’t burning the way the lesser bugs did. They screeched and screamed as portions of their grafted bodies smoked, yes—some of their thin antennae curled and some of their softer tissues blended—but most of their shape and bulk held.

Zora heard their muscles creaking beneath their grafted parts. Their bodies were adapting to the flames.

“Fire resistance,” he murmured. “They have bug parts grafted on them that give them resistance to high heat. They’re learning how to use them as they regenerate.”

The left one’s scorpion tails twitched, soot-black and wet, and the toxin-sacs at their bases tightened as if preparing to spit venom into the flames themselves. The middle one’s wings vibrated, adjusting to the heat currents. The last one’s many eyes blinked in an uneven pattern, different sets taking in different spectra as it adapted to seeing in the hazy air.

So Zora listened to each grafting bug—deeply—before drawing on all of the bug knowledge he’d slowly accumulated over the years.

“The left grafting bug has a total of fifteen grafted parts,” he said, pointing his staff at it. “Wax moth antennae for catching vibration in air, mantis scythes for close-quarter severing, a trapjaw ant mandible grafted beneath its throat to snap and catch, a bombardier beetle gland in its ribcage, leafcutter ant muscle bundles for repetitive cutting, rhinoceros beetle horn plating over the spine, camel spider hairs for sensing pressure, tiger beetle sprint tendons in the calves, velvet ant pain glands under the collarbone, cicada tymbal plates for disruptive vibration, whip scorpion sensory filaments, a wasp stinger sheath embedded in the wrist, a fire ant heat gland in its chest—badly fused—cricket femur springs, and a bark beetle boring spur at the ankle.”

Then he moved onto the second bug.

“The middle one has thirteen grafted parts,” he continued.

“Beetle horn pauldrons, glasswing membranes with ant mandibles lacing the edges, mantis shrimp strike muscle grafted into one forearm, dragonfly compound eyes stitched at the temples, teeth reinforced with carpenter ant cartilage, silverfish scale plating along the ribs, hornet venom sacs in the abdomen, weaver ant grip pads on the palms, lacewing egg-silk glands—likely for binding—praying mantis cervical joints, mole cricket digging claws at the heels, mosquito siphon tubes in the throat for rapid hydration, and termite soldier headplate over the skull.”

Then the last.

“And the right one has sixteen,” he finished. “Eight extra eyes harvested from different species—some nocturnal, some polarized—whip spider raptorial arms fused under the ribcage, stick insect mimic plates, wasp wing joints for midair corrections, beetle elytra shields, cricket stridulation plates, firefly photophores under the jaw, leech suction pads at the knees for wall-cling, and velvet ant pain glands again.”

He was no true insect scholar. He didn’t pretend to be. But he didn’t have to be to come to a conclusion after hearing everything he just said—and neither did Kita nor Enki.

“Now, with all of that in mind, “he said, smiling at the two of them, “what do you think their strengths are, and what do you think their weaknesses are?”

“... They have too many grafted parts,” Kita murmured. “Underneath all of those stolen abilities… they’re still human, aren’t they?”

“All of those grafted parts are fighting for space,” Enki said plainly. “If we can make their grafted parts clash against each other, they will tear themselves apart.”

“Good.” Zora nodded, satisfied. “So here’s what we do.”

And he spoke quickly, laying out his plan in just a handful of breaths as the grafting bugs below were finally adapted to the flames, already shifting their legs for a pounce.

Zora lowered his chin at Kita when he finished. “You’re up first,” he said. “Buy us the time we need to get ready.”

Kita didn’t hesitate. There was no nervous grin, no apology for fear, and no searching for confidence in his face. She simply leaned forward over the branch and stared down at the three regenerating bugs with the ferocity of an empress who’d already decided what the world owed her.

And Zora, hearing the change in her breathing—hearing the way fear had stopped leading some time ago and started following—couldn’t help the light smile that touched his face.

“Got it!” she said

Then she leapt down, twin sawtooth blades at the ready as the three grafting bugs launched up in the same instant.

Zora swung his staff down at her back like he was striking a bell.

“Hasten and strengthen!”

His voice shot down and wrapped around her body. Now, he couldn’t actually make her muscles stronger the way Marcus could with the same spell, but he could imagine himself standing behind her, grabbing her limbs and pushing whenever she pushed or pulling whenever she pulled. He could imagine pulling her out of danger by a fraction of a second or swinging her blade alongside her to give her that little bit of extra strength—and that was how he understood ‘hasten’ and ‘strengthen’.

That little bit was enough as Kita swung her blades as one, hitting the bugs with a crack so violent all three were sent flying to the side. One, two, three bodies slammed into the nearest artificial trees with enough force to shudder stone-trunks and rain wooden leaves down into the flames below.

Kita blinked, kicked off a falling branch mid-air, and stabbed her blades into the nearest tree to cling to it while she looked at the vibrational armor wrapped around her entire body.

She looked up.

“Thanks for the spell!” she shouted.

“Don’t waste it,” he called down. “Spend it.”

Kita’s reply was a feral nod. Then she was gone again, a blur of steel and flowing cape made of muddy red ants, and her speed as she dashed between the trees made the grafting bugs look almost… clumsy.

While their first clashes reverberated through the burning forest, Zora lifted his staff and began twirling it over his head.

“And the princess, weary of watching her village bleed, did not pray for mercy from the sky, but dug her hands into the ground—rise, earth, rise, and become a wall taller than the proudest trees, thicker than the greediest kings, and unbreakable as the vow of the sun.”

The ground beneath the flames trembled, and a giant cylindrical tube of earth began to lift from below, as if Zora were pulling a giant well up by its throat. Soil and stone compacted as it rose. Layers of roots and soils pressed together with a deep, grinding groan. Flames licked its sides and hardened the walls as it slowly emerged, turning the outer surface into baked clay and blackened rock.

As the earth-well climbed higher and higher, growing to five meters, ten meters, then fifteen meters tall, Zora kept twirling his staff and reciting the same passage from The Earthen Princess. Despite his heat-resistance mutations, it was difficult to continuously cast without taking a breath, and the heat certainly didn’t help. Sweat slicked his palms around his staff. His throat was parched. It was now that he found himself sorely missing Ifas’ hot chocolate, but…

Even as he continued casting, he couldn’t help but feel a small amount of pride when he saw Kita matching the three grafting bugs in an even fight.

She clashed with the bugs again and again across the trees and lower branches, the impacts loud enough to make the artificial forest shudder. She dodged the moth-antennae bug’s scythes by moving where its senses didn’t expect her to be, and then she baited the beetle-pauldron one into swinging too wide, letting its own horn plating catch on the bark of a tree for a fraction of a second. It was over two dozen limbs against two blades, but she swung and slashed with ferocity far beyond anything he’d ever seen from her. Ferocity, he thought, was the biggest difference between the her of today and the her he’d first met nine months ago.

This version of her would’ve absolutely kicked my ass back in the academy, huh?

She was always strong like this.

She was simply missing… ‘fire’ in her heart.

Three minutes later, Zora’s recitation finally ended, and the giant earth well stood complete: a twenty meter tall hollow cylinder towering in the middle of the burning forest, though it was still a little bit below his branch. Zora stepped back, chest heaving, and then hopped down onto the edge of the well alongside Enki.

Kita heard them moving and immediately began backing towards the well, luring the three grafting bugs over without even needing to be told. She sprinted across branch-bridges, hopped up several branches, and then leapt, landing on the edge of the giant well beside the two of them before taunting the bugs to follow with a flourish of her blades.

The three bugs closed in, eager to finish what they’d started, but the moment they pounced over the hollow section of the well with their claws and tails brandished, Zora thumped his staff.

“Down.”

An invisible force immediately slammed down on their backs. All three of them jolted, folded in half, and were sent plunging twenty meters down into the bottom of the well.

Zora, Kita, and Enki stared down at the bottom. The flames down there were weak, given the lack of easy access to oxygen, so the three grafting bugs were already snarling and shifting. They were completely fine, and they immediately dug their claws and carved footholds into the walls, trying to climb back up.

“Not happening,” Zora said, pointing his staff down. “This is a Zora Fabre original: divination.”

A giant plume of flame smashed down into the well, slamming the bugs back to the bottom with brutal force the moment they made it ten meters up. These flames, however, weren’t a wide-area inferno like the one he’d used to burn the forest. This was concentrated pressure and pure heat, a furnace blast aimed to punish anything that tried to climb out of the well.

The bugs screamed, limbs splayed and twitching at the bottom of the well. Zora didn’t stop.

“Divination.”

Another plume struck, stronger, hotter, more focused. The well became an oven throat. The air itself howled as heat compressed.

“Divination.”

Again, and again, and again.

Each cast layered onto the last as if he was pumping bellows into a forge, feeding oxygen into a fire that wanted to be cruel. Sweat rolled down his jaw. His arms trembled from the repeated recoil of firing essentially flame cannons out of his staff. His throat, too, burned from forcing the red-hot words out through smoke and strain, but he kept going.

He heard the bugs’ grafted parts beginning to fail, heat making different tissues and parts expand unevenly. He heard chitin plates creaking. He heard softer organs hissing and blistering. He heard the beetle-pauldron one’s wing membranes shrivel, the sound like parchment being held too close to a flame—and once the entire well began swaying from how hot the bottom was getting, he turned to nod at Enki.

Enki, too, had been preparing this entire time. He’d been sucking in a single, long breath for minutes, chest barely moving. It probably helped that he was ‘half-inorganic’, as he claimed himself, which meant he didn’t need to breathe the way everyone else did. He could hold air like a weapon, and now he leaned over the edge of the well to exhale.

Cryogenic mist poured down.

‘Mist’ was underselling it, though. Enki was spitting an intensely frigid pillar of pure cold that slammed down the well and extinguished Zora’s flames in a single instant. The air physically cracked. The well’s interior became a frozen throat, and as the seconds went by—five, ten, fifteen seconds—the bottom of the well began to fracture.

Section by section, meter by meter, the well began to collapse. Zora kept his staff stabbed in the earth for stability. Kita did the same with her blades, while Enki didn’t even sway as he kept spewing cryogenic mist.

“Extinguish,” Zora called out, making the flames across the forest recede and slowly dwindle. Blackened stone-trunks and scorched wooden leaves were the only things that remained, and as the entire well finally crumbled in on itself—allowing the three of them to hop off and back onto the ground—the cryogenic mist that'd been stuck inside the well was also allowed to wash out.

Kita sneezed, and that was enough for Zora to wave his staff through the air.

“Warmth.”

A gentle heat rolled out from the staff in a controlled ring, the nearest fog thinned and recoiled from them, revealing the three grafting bugs on the ground before them.

The bugs weren’t doing very well. Their bodies were completely broken. Their chitin plates were split by jagged cracks, their grafted limbs hung at wrong angles or had buckled inward, and their joints had shattered like mismatched hinges that’d finally refused to carry a single frame. They’d been superheated and then supercooled in a confined area, so with how haphazardly all of their grafted parts had been stuck onto their bodies, thermal shock absolutely demolished them by expanding and contracting different grafted parts at different rates.

Of the three of them, two of them were already dead. Only one tried to flex a claw and managed a weak, spasmodic tremor that shivered up its human arm and died at the shoulder.

Zora looked down at them, and despite everything—despite the multiple eyes, the mismatched claws, and the grotesque graft-lines—he felt pity tighten behind his ribs.

They’re still incredibly strong to have been able to endure all that heat.

They couldn’t have been more than incomplete prototypes plucked from the lab, so what would a fully-fledged, fully-trained Grafting Bug Afflicted soldier be like?

And then a whisper scraped up from the living grafting bug’s throat.

“... Kill me.”

Zora flinched, because even though he knew it was true, he’d still forgotten that—at the end of the day—these grafting bugs were human. They’d been kidnapped, carved open, and rebuilt into something monstrous before being claimed by the Swarm, but they were still human.

Some scrap of humanity still remained in the man, clinging stubbornly to the last thread of free will he had left.

Zora’s grip tightened around his staff so hard his knuckles ached. The man’s plea was too human in its shape, so he wanted to end it with fire—but his tongue wouldn’t move.

He couldn’t bring himself to kill another human.

So it surprised him when Kita stepped past him and lowered herself to her knees, both blades stabbed into the ground as she clapped her hands in a prayer.

Then she lifted her head, looking the dying man in the eye.

“The Swarm will pay for what they have done,” she whispered. “I will see to it.”

The man’s grotesque head shifted a fraction toward her voice. His eyelids flickered as though he were struggling to even see where she was speaking from, but… his breathing changed. For a second, it became stronger and steadier as if her vow had given him something to cling onto. His mouth twitched, and he attempted a smile that couldn’t quite form on a face grafted with multiple eyes.

“For… the Attini Empire,” he rasped.

Kita dipped her head. She raised one blade and drove it down into his heart with a single, decisive stab, and that was the end.

… Zora and Enki turned their heads to the south as Kita prayed once more for the mutated souls.

Regalia was still there at the very top of the southern wall, hands plunged into the stone beneath her to spread her seemingly endless waves of necrotic ants. It’d taken them ten minutes to deal with the grafting bugs, and in those ten minutes, cracks lined with zombie ants had already spread across a solid seventy percent of that one section of the wall from top to bottom.

In just another minute, her necrotic ants would chew through the last thirty percent, and the wall’s integrity would no longer hold.

The battle wasn’t over yet, and yet…

“We can’t kill her here, can we?” he muttered.

Enki didn’t respond, but he knew the boy shared the same thought. If the two of them were strong enough to kill the Greater Insect God of the South, they would've done it back in the Divine Temple. The truth was, they simply didn’t have the firepower to kill her here and now—but they could stop her from breaking the wall, and they could prevent the Crawling Seas from flooding in today.

“... I have an idea to get Regalia out of the city,” Zora said. “You want to hear it?”

Enki glanced at him coldly.

“I have an idea, too.”
