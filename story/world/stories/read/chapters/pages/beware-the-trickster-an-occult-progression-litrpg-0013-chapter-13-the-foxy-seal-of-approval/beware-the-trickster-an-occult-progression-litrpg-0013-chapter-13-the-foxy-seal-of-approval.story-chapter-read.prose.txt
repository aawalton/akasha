The trip up started in what Agnes proudly called an “elevator,” though I would’ve personally labeled it “a death trap held together by hope and iron bolts.” It was a wooden platform caged in wrought iron, with thick chains disappearing into a shadowy shaft above us. A crank-and-pulley system whined as a counterweight groaned its way down somewhere out of sight.

Steam hissed from a pipe on one side, the metallic tang of hot iron mixing with the faint scent of oil and old wood. Agnes stepped in and pulled a massive lever, and the platform jolted hard enough that I had to dig my claws into Julia’s dress for stability.

Well, that confirms another point. I am definitely somewhere in an early industrial era. And damn, was it strangely unnerving that this clanking machinery sparked more primal fear in me than that deathless guide had?

[It is not an irrational fear, honestly. We can learn to manipulate magical forces, but against a malfunctioning gear or a snapped cable we can only place our hopes entirely on Agnes’s skills to keep us from becoming a stain on the floor below.]

The entire contraption was a carbon copy of those grainy, sepia-toned photos from my high school history textbooks depicting early London. From the gas lamps I’d seen earlier to riding in what was essentially a prototype designed decades before anyone discovered the concept of “safety standards,” the aesthetic was consistent. The only thing missing was a soot-covered chimney sweep’s kid coughing in the corner.

The iron bars rattled, the chains clinked ominously, and yet, after the initial jolt, we began to rise with a surprising, albeit noisy, smoothness. Well, maybe they mix a little magic in with the mechanics, I reasoned. That thought was somewhat assuring. Julia, for her part, seemed perfectly at ease; this was clearly not her first time in the rickety device. She did notice my palpable distaste for the primitive lift and affectionately patted my head.

“I was kind of scared too the first time I stepped into one of these,” she whispered. The admission was not remotely reassuring, but the comfort of her pats certainly was.

We stopped with another jolt and Agnes moved, sliding open the cage door with a screech.

Finally, solid ground! But the room we stepped into was… not what I had expected.

I craned my neck, trying to take it all in. We had emerged into what looked like the back half of a massive warehouse or a very cluttered storeroom. It was a long, high-ceilinged space illuminated by oil lamps fixed to the stone walls, their glass chimneys fogged with a thin layer of black soot. Sturdy wooden racks ran along both sides, packed with neatly folded garments in muted, practical shades of gray, cream, and deep navy. Interspersed among them were finer gowns in rich, luxurious colors like burgundy, emerald, and jet black. There were wigs perched on carved wooden stands, some styled in elaborate curls and intricate braids, others starkly plain. Large, reinforced travel trunks were stacked neatly against the far wall, and a tall, freestanding mirror leaned casually against a heavy support beam.

Huh? Were we in some sort of costume shop? Though it lacked the curated, commercial vibe of one. Julia was staring too.

Agnes walked in without hesitation, like she’d already guessed what was running through her head and was ready to answer it before she asked.

“As Inquisitors, we’re not always marching around in uniform,” she said, looking back at Julia. “In fact, ninety percent of our work is investigation. It’s hard to track down supernatural threats when everyone in the street knows you’re an Inquisitor. So, we keep… alternatives.” She gestured to the racks. “We maintain multiple identities, different classes, different social standings. If you need to move in certain circles, you need the right skin to wear.”

She clapped her hands once. “Now, Julia, you’re changing too.”

I hopped down to the floor as Julia reluctantly stepped forward, shoulders stiff. Agnes moved like a woman on a mission, plucking garments off the racks with unerring precision. Bolts of rich fabric caught the light, deep jewel tones, fine weaves, fitted bodices with sharp seams, collars that could probably slice bread. Honestly… I kind of liked this place.

Drawn by idle curiosity, I padded over to the freestanding mirror and took in the sight of my own floofy glory. If I had to draw a comparison, I looked a lot like an Arctic fox, with fur a near-silvery white, shading to soft gray along my back and deepening to black at the tips of my tail. Against that, my crimson eyes stood out like embers in snow. Striking? Yes, absolutely.

What I wasn’t sure about was how I’d look grown up… assuming “grown up” ever happened. I was almost a year old now and still small enough to curl up inside a bowl. Pretty sure that’s not how normal foxes develop. Then again, I was a demon, so… maybe the growth chart just looked different for us.

Whatever. I spent a while stretching and posing (purely for research purposes) until Agnes settled on her choices for Julia and herself. The hats on a nearby stand caught my interest too, but given my size and the fact that I am, objectively, an exceptionally floofy fox, none of them had a hope of fitting me.

Eventually, a dark wine-red walking dress was chosen for Julia, paired with a matching hat adorned with a tasteful arrangement of feathers. For herself, Agnes picked a deep green gown with intricate embroidery at the cuffs. She swept her hair up, pinned it neatly beneath a wide-brimmed hat, and finished it off with a ribbon and a netted veil.

When they finally stepped out from behind a folding screen, they looked like they’d strolled straight out of a century-old oil painting, perfectly composed, poised, and so thoroughly noble-looking that the dusty warehouse setting felt like a stage backdrop that didn’t quite belong.

Not that the dresses were spotless at first. A thin coat of dust clung to the fabric, and here and there, faint stains marred the hems. Agnes placed a hand on Julia’s shoulder, and the touch bloomed with a warm golden light. Before my eyes, the dust lifted away, the little stains vanished, and even the faint wrinkles smoothed out as though chased off by invisible hands. Julia’s skin gained a faint, healthy glow to match.

“Purification spell has its own unique uses,” Agnes remarked.

Then her gaze drifted down and caught me watching her, ears perked and eyes wide in my most devastating display of foxy innocence. She smiled, crouched down, and set her hand lightly on my head.

Immediately, Trickery whispered the nature of the spell into my mind. Purify, for cleansing and purging. Normally designed for smiting the ‘evil’ right out of something. Naturally, I tweaked it mid-cast so that instead of trying to erase me from existence, it just… cleaned me.

And just like that, I was a fresh, pristine fox.

“Still baffling how differently the Sun’s power reacts to her,” Agnes mused as the glow faded from my fur. I felt lighter, refreshed, almost sparkling. This was going to be very convenient once Julia learned it too. I could spend more time being gloriously lazy and less time licking myself clean.

Agnes shook her head before moving towards the only door out was set into the far wall. It was framed by a reinforced steel band. No handles, no locks, there was just a flat metal plate where a doorknob should have been.

“This door is one-way from the outside,” Agnes explained, walking up to it. “It opens only when it recognizes approved mana signatures.” She turned to Julia. “Place your hand here.”

Julia pressed her palm flat against the plate. A faint glow shimmered under her skin, bleeding into the metal like water sinking into cloth. Trickery stirred in my skull, offering a neat block of information. The enchantment was solid, stable enough that I couldn’t poke around too much. The only tweak available was to piggyback Julia’s mana signature with my own, so I’d get access too.

I took it. Why wouldn’t I?

“There,” Agnes said, stepping back. “You’re keyed in now. From either side, you can open this door. No one else can, not without approval.”

Approval I’d already granted myself.

With that, she pushed the door open. The hinges moved in complete silence, and the space beyond came into view.

Only… it wasn’t the city. We were definitely aboveground, but still inside some kind of structure. My nose told me more than my eyes at first, the sharp tang of coal smoke, damp stone, and a faint briny note that screamed seawater. Docks? Had to be.

Leaning against a stack of crates stenciled with Atherton & Sons – Textile Imports was a broad-shouldered man in a foreman’s heavy wool coat and flat cap. He pushed off the crates as we emerged, then stopped short at the sight of Agnes. His face lit up with a smile, posture straightening in a way that was more reflex than conscious decision.

“Lady Croft,” he said, tipping his cap. “Coach is ready for you and…” His gaze shifted to Julia, who offered a polite smile. “…your guest.”

His eyes flicked to me as Julia scooped me into her arms. For a moment, he looked torn between awe at my floofy majesty and confusion about why I was here. To his credit, he didn’t ask.

“The afternoon shift’s about to start,” he continued, “so the yard’ll be getting busy.”

“Thank you, Mr. Henley,” Agnes replied, her voice now wearing a smooth, aristocratic lilt that matched her refined appearance perfectly. “The shipment from the southern provinces arrived without trouble, I hope?”

“Aye, milady. Cleared customs this morning. The silks are being unloaded now.” He jerked his thumb toward the yawning warehouse doors, where a handful of broad-backed laborers were already rolling handcarts piled with bolts of fabric out of a wagon.

As Mr. Henley moved ahead to clear a path, Agnes leaned slightly toward Julia, voice dropping to a low, conspiratorial murmur.

“Atherton & Sons is a perfectly legitimate textile import firm,” she began. “On paper, it’s owned by a silent partner, a charitable trust managed by the Church. The profits fund our operations, the workers remain blissfully unaware, and it gives us the perfect excuse to move in and out as we please. Everyone on the docks knows the ‘Lady’ from the head office makes occasional inspections.” She finished with a sly wink.

I had to admit, I was impressed and a little proud on Julia’s behalf. Her new employers were competent and were efficient in a way I could respect. The Church had officially earned my foxy seal of approval. If they ever decided to go full corporate takeover, I had no doubt they’d pull it off without the world batting an eye.

Outside was, indeed, a dockyard and the scale of the place hit me at once. The entire structure loomed rather massive, iron cranes groaning overhead as they swung cargo nets heavy with bundled goods from ship to shore. I had no idea how the whole process worked, not that I was in any rush to learn. Still, I felt like I needed a higher perch to take in the full spectacle.

A black carriage stood waiting for us. The driver, a thickset man in a double-breasted coat and tall hat, stepped down in one smooth motion and opened the door without a word.

Agnes and Julia climbed in, settling into plush upholstered seats that looked far too comfortable for dockside grime. I claimed my usual in Julia's lap by the window, eyes fixed on the passing scene. If there had been any doubt before, it was gone now, this wasn’t remotely medieval. I couldn’t decide if I liked the change. The older setting had its own rustic charm, but maybe this era would grow on me… assuming I got to see more of it from somewhere cleaner than docks that smelled like coal smoke, seawater, and horse manure stirred together.

“Only a select few ever manage to unlock their Soul World,” Agnes said suddenly, watching the crowds outside. “The vast majority have no idea the shadowed world that exists alongside theirs. And it’s better that way. As the Captain says, knowledge itself can be corrupting.”

Her gaze stayed fixed on the moving dockhands as she continued, voice taking on a more sober tone. “We get cases all the time, someone catches a glimpse of something they were never meant to see, or a minor Spirit World entity tempts them. It always ends badly. The most recent was right here on the docks, a man killed his own crew after finding a book still tainted with traces from the Spirit World. It opened his mind to the wrong thing, and… well, the rest was a bloody mess.”

She fell quiet for a moment before her tone shifted to less recounting. “There will come a time, Julia, when you might feel lost. You might blame yourself for the deaths, the tragedies you couldn’t stop. Consider this a warning, guilt leaves you wide open to outside influence. It is the most dangerous vulnerability of all.”

Julia nodded, her own eyes drifting toward the view beyond the glass.

I, on the other hand, yawned, curling my tail up over my nose in Julia’s lap. Welp. Philosophical lectures over. Nap time. And naps, as everyone knows, are very important.

The only thing that got me to crack my eyes open was the smell of bacon. I let out an excited little foxy chirp and sprang to my feet. Julia laughed as I bounded over to her. We were back inside somewhere, a proper room this time, with a fireplace and a cozy living room full of warm furniture and worn-in sofas. She fed me by hand, which I accepted without hesitation.

No idea how long I’d been asleep, but judging by the lack of any cooking smells beyond the bacon, I guessed they’d picked up some kind of takeaway along the way. Either way, we were finally in what I assumed would be our place to stay.

Agnes was nowhere to be seen, probably off to do whatever mysterious church business kept her busy. Julia had her own routine to get to. She drew the curtains, lit a gas lamp, then opened the case holding the golden, beating heart and slipped into her cultivation practice.

I decided to explore. The whole house had a pleasantly homey feel, fully furnished and clearly lived-in despite being ours now. On the first floor, I found two bedrooms. In the first, I leapt up onto the window sill for a better view outside.

Evening had settled in. Wherever we were, it was much calmer than the last place, only the occasional person passed on the street, and those who did looked… refined, somehow. Out front, a neat lawn stretched down to the road, with red and white flowers blooming along its edges.

Okay, I took back what I’d said earlier. I could vibe with this place. There were no creepy, perverted phantoms lurking behind trees, staring like they had nothing better to do. Just a pleasant, quiet atmosphere.

Some of the neighbors had left their windows open. My eyes narrowed.

[Are you thinking about sneaking in and pranking them?]

I had the thought, but… honestly, I was ill-equipped for that kind of thing. It worked on Julia because, well, she was Julia. Random strangers? One wrong move and I’d be fox paste.

[Well, you have options now, three… no, two, if you’re discarding the Sun Aspect.]

I’d rather wait.

[Fair.]

I pulled a cushion over to the window sill, set it down, and sprawled across it to watch the sunset. I wanted to nap, but it didn’t feel like the right time. Better to keep watch. Julia’s life had been nothing but chaos lately, and I didn’t like it. To be blunt, it felt… artificially inflated with trouble.

If I wanted my peace back, I needed to keep trouble away from her.

[That doesn’t make any logical sense. Just admit you like Julia at this point.]

I hmphed. Of course not. This was purely selfish, I was only looking out for my own floof.

Curling into a defiant little donut, I peeked through the curve of my tail toward the street.

Seemed my decision paid off.

Around midnight, a figure in black emerged from the shadows, moving low behind the trees, then scaling a garden wall with practiced ease.

He slipped into houses through first-floor windows, locks giving way under his hands without so much as a squeak. He was quick, quiet and very deliberate. Definitely looking for something.

A thief, most likely.

He was working his way down the street, house by house, disturbing nothing and no one.

Two doors away now. My eyes narrowed.
