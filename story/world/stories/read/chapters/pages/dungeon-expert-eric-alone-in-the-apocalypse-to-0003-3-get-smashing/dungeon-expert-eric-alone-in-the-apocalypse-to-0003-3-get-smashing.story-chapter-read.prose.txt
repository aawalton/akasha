Eric pushed the door open and stepped outside. A cool breath of air conditioning followed him out, then abandoned him into moist heat. The air was humid, so humid it felt like he was breathing soup. Heat beat at him from all directions as the humidity trapped it around him. It felt like he was wrapped in a hot, wet towel even though he was still wearing his khakis and polo from work. He’d spent the night in them, and they looked like it, though right now, wrinkles were the least of his worries.

There were clothes in the gas station, but most of them were trucker-oriented, boasting slogans he wouldn’t want to be spotted dead in. It wasn’t like there was anyone out there to see him, but they’d said something about televising this in the opening letter, hadn’t they? He couldn’t totally let his image slide. Though it really was more about his personal feelings about those shirts. He could get behind a little crude humor, but there was a limit. And some of it wasn’t even crude humor, but stuff he considered truly reprehensible. The -isms, as it were.

One or two were fine, he supposed. Like the one with the yellow tire track that said CAT in all caps with the matching yellow sweatpant-material shorts. He wasn’t that jazzed about heavy machinery, but he’d been five once. Everyone had their construction gear phase.

For now, he was sticking to the khakis and polos, but in the near future, he’d probably have to swap. His current outfit was already stinky and getting sticky, what with the one leg already destroyed by cockroach goo. The staleness of the sweat-soaked fabric alone was wearing on him, let alone any further monster blood. If he didn’t change his clothes within a day or two, he’d start getting rashes and blisters from the dirty fabric. He wasn’t an action hero who could wear the same leathers for weeks without a problem. He was a thin-skinned, soft-handed office worker, and his body was going to act like it.

Well, he thought, testing his new boots on the dust of what had once been the parking lot, maybe the System will drop more clothes for me.

He stood there by the door for another few seconds. When nothing attacked, he ventured a little further out. The ground changed from black to white here, where the concrete pad had once supported the gasoline infrastructure, the tanks and the dispensers alike. The very last scraps of the SUV laid scattered in the dirt, along with the shreds from the cockroach frenzy last night: antennae and legs that had broken off in the scrum or somehow escaped the jaws of the hungry bugs. Eric progressed quietly, listening as hard as he could for any sign of life: a snapped branch, a huffed breath.

Sunlight beamed through thick foliage, painting dappled sunlight over the ground. Birds sang songs he didn’t recognize in the near distance. A cool breeze blew, cutting through the humidity and the heat. He closed his eyes, just enjoying it for a moment. Here he was, out in the world, accomplishing things. Not chained to a desk, grinding away at some meaningless vanity project so that his company’s president could make the shareholders happy and fire another half of the office’s staff. This was how life was meant to feel. Not dreary and gray. Not an endless repetition of the same colorless days over and over again. Real. Alive.

Something crunched in the undergrowth, startling Eric out of his reverie. He dropped to a crouch, tightening his grip on the wooden club. The shorter length made it perfect to hold in one hand, which left his other hand with nothing to do but awkwardly clutch at the air.

I need something. A shield, a wand, something.

The thing crunched again, and Eric whipped around to face it. A rabbit hopped toward him… or rather, it looked like a rabbit. It had the usual fluffy white fur and long ears, but it also had two bright red swept-back horns that arced over its skull like a goat’s horns, curling around just in front of its long ears. Its claws were longer than he was used to, and shared the horns’ bright red tone. He eyed it, and it eyed him, quietly chewing away on a leaf.

“Are we cool?” he asked it.

It hopped away, vanishing into the underbrush.

“I guess so.”

He had no problem with killing large cockroaches, but adorable forest creatures were another question. If the rabbit attacked him, or if he needed food, it was fair game, but he had a big duffel bag full of snacks in his inventory and a gas station full of respawning food behind him. He didn’t need to kill it, and it wasn’t attacking him, so why attack it?

Besides, who knew? He was struggling with cockroaches right now. That rabbit might be able to oneshot him.

I’m just making up excuses because I don’t want to kill the cute bunny, he thought, rolling his eyes at himself. Still, if he ever encountered his sister again, and this was actually televised, then she’d kill him for real for harming a sweet baby rabbit, so it was really just practical life preservation on his part.

There would be more monsters. He was currently in a target-rich environment, so there was no worry about not finding something. He was more worried about finding too much than not enough.

He crept forward, keeping his head on a swivel. There was a strange sound nearby, almost like water dripping, and he slowly approached it. The undergrowth was dense, but full of fresh greenery rather than the usual piled-up. dried-out layers he was used to, and as a result, the sleek brown body stood out like a shiny fresh shit. It called out again, shifting ever so slightly, and that water-dripping sound resounded through the forest.

“I see you, you little sneak,” he muttered. He kept it in the corner of his eye and approached it sideways. From many late-night cockroach hunts in his small, infested apartment, he knew just how wily and aware of sight-lines the tiny beasts were. He glanced over his shoulder at the gas station. I can dash there before the horde arrives. Let’s go!

He got within reach of the cockroach and didn’t hesitate; if he hesitated, the roach would run. Instead, he lunged, throwing himself at the cockroach shoulder-first, putting his entire body weight into the swing. The roach jerked back at the last second, but Eric had anticipated that. His club struck home, smashing right through the wings and hardened chitin to pancake the tiny monster with a satisfying, juicy crunch. Eric immediately hopped back, anticipating the horde.

Nothing. No rumbling in the undergrowth. No trembling of dried leaves. Just him and the dead cockroach.

Eric looked around him, confused. Was that a special case? Did there just happen to be lots of cockroaches around, or something? Or was it a time-of-day or weather thing? He didn’t know, and the world wasn’t dropping any tool tips. A little hesitantly, he held his ground and looked around for more cockroaches.

The cockroach he’d just killed burst in a little puff of smoke. Startled, Eric made it halfway to the gas station before he realized it wasn’t the oncoming horde. He recovered and walked carefully back over, craning his neck from a distance to see what had happened to the carcass.

The carcass melted into the earth. A small plastic package sat where it had been. He glanced around to make sure it wasn’t a trap, then closed the distance and picked it up.

It was a tiny sampler-size packet of pretzels. No, not sampler-size: airplane-size. A silver airplane blazed through the blue packaging, with the outline of Seattle picked out underneath and a friendly label informing anyone who didn’t know the city skyline that it was, in fact, Seattle. He squinted at it. Was it really just a packet of airplane pretzels? Why? He could get plenty of food from the gas station—at least, food of this quality.

This time, the System did pop up a tool tip for him. Pretzels (airplane), it read. And beneath that: +5% float for 3s on consumption.

Eric stared, startled. Hold on. Five percent float? That’s not a usual quality of airplane pretzels! What does that mean? And does that mean these things are magic items? Wait, that’s actually kind of awesome? He turned it over in his hand again, then put it into his Inventory. He didn’t need 5% float right now, but maybe he would in the future.

He eyed the I Love Truckin’ bag, then pulled it out and opened it, scanning the bag for tool tips. None appeared. All the food was just that: food.

Yeah? Did you think you were getting magic items for free? That’s not in the budget.

He whipped around again, then narrowed his eyes. Either he was going insane… more insane, or that voice was real. And if it was real, then it was definitely attached to the System, because it got sassier every time he questioned something obvious about the System or the world. He crossed his arms. “Are you talking to me, System?”

Silence.

“Come on. You’ve been sassing me for a while. Are you bored? Lonely, or something? Do you need someone to talk to? I’m bored too, we can chat.”

Still nothing.

Eric shrugged. Nothing he could do about the haters, he supposed. He put the food back in the bag and threw the bag back in his inventory, leaving the airplane pretzels separate. The horde still hadn’t materialized, so he continued on his slow survey of the clearing around the gas station, on the lookout for more things he could kill. Two more individual cockroaches met a grisly end in the undergrowth before a chime played.

Level up! 1 > 2

STR: 4

MAG: 15>18

STM: 4>5

DEX: 10>11

DEF: 2

MDF: 2>3

Skill Choice Unlocked! Please choose a skill.

Mana Sight

Gain the ability to see mana and sources of magic, including spells and field magics. Cost: None

Mana Burn

Burn your mana to enhance your physical characteristics for a short time. Cost scales with the relative enhancement. [Patch Note: this skill will not enhance your body as much as any enhancement spell or skill. Its output has been capped to make it the low level skill it was always intended to be. Do not attempt to abuse it or you will be punished.]

Eric raised his brows. What a conundrum. Choose the un-nerfed skill that was clearly balanced to the System as it stood (or close enough to dodge a nerf), or the nerfed skill that had once been OP, and might still be OP… but also might be nerfed to hell. He’d played games where it swung both ways: both where the nerf completely killed the skill or move, and where the nerf did nothing to actually weaken the skill. The fact that they’d nerfed it meant it used to be strong, but was it still or not? That was the question.

On the other hand, Mana Sight sounded very useful, especially when he had to find spells in the environment around him. Not overpowered, but incredibly useful. It also didn’t cost anything, which also sounded powerful to him right now. He didn’t know if he had any mana. He had MAG, but how did that relate to mana? He didn’t know. But if he could see mana, he could instantly overcome that barrier.

No, I have to play it safe. I’m all alone. I need to pick the one that’s sure to help, not the one that might help if I win the 50/50. And I’m playing directly against the house here. My odds aren’t great. He could definitely find mana and spells with Mana Sight, as compared to rolling the dice that Mana Burn was still worth having. Mana Sight was the missing piece that he needed to make Mage work. Mana Burn might help him, but it also might not. He picked Mana Sight.

A warm sensation passed through his body. It passed from his stomach to his head, then focused in on his eyes. It grew hotter, like a bad headache that settled right behind his eyes. He closed them, pressing his hands over his eyes. Ugh. I need some ice.

His eyes cooled. He blinked them open, then stared. Everything was glowing. Everything. The trees. The grass. Little motes of light floated around on the air. He looked at his hands. Light flowed through them as well, barely any brighter than the light in the plants.

“Whoa.” This wasn’t just the ability to see mana in magical items; it was the ability to see mana in everything. Including, apparently, people and monsters. He swept his gaze over the trees and undergrowth around him. The rabbit he’d seen earlier still picked its way through the undergrowth, but now he could see it. There was a large bird in one of the nearby trees that was watching something hungrily; hopefully the rabbit and not me. And over to the left were more cockroaches, hidden in the undergrowth but still within smashing range of the clearing.

They should really bill this thing better. It’s more like x-ray vision than just “seeing magic in things.” He wouldn’t have hesitated for an instant if Mana Sight had informed him it would give him the ability to see through solid objects.

With the help of Mana Sight, he spent the rest of the morning circling the clearing and taking out cockroaches and a few other small, insect-based monsters that he would’ve missed without Mana Sight. None of them put up much resistance, but neither did he level up again. Lunchtime came, and he returned to the gas station for food and to consider his next move.

Tired hinges squeaked. The now-familiar chime sounded, and delicious AC spilled over his face. He closed his eyes for a second, just letting the cool air wash over him. When he opened his eyes, the entire room was a wall of bright light. He closed them again and deactivated Mana Sight, and the gas station returned to normal.

“You wanted to talk?”

Eric jumped where he stood. He whirled. A man in the gas station’s uniform polo with a nametag on his chest stood behind the checkout stand. He was gaunt, with leathery, worn skin and salt-and-pepper hair, with the hard-living air of someone who’d ended up as a gas station attendant after crashing out of every other employment opportunity out there. Leaning on one hand, he tapped one of the duplicate cigarette boxes against the counter with the casual urgency of an addict.

Eric stared. “Who… where…”

The man tapped his head and raised his brows meaningfully at Eric. You wanted to talk?

“Wait—that’s… huh?” Eric squinted at the man. This guy was the System? All of it? How?

“Not all of it. I’m just the regional Administrator who got stuck with you. Call me…” He looked down, checking his nametag. “Joe, apparently.”

“Oh, okay.” Eric nodded, not sure what else to say.

The man stood, stretching, then pointed at the cigarettes. “Listen, this body is killing me for a smoke. Mind if we continue this outside?”

“Uh… sure?”

“Great. I’ll temporarily extend the safe zone just outside the door.” Joe stepped outside and gestured for Eric to follow.

With no idea what else to do, Eric followed. Joe leaned against the wall and lit up a cigarette, took a smoke, then let out a deep sigh. “That’s much better. Man, mimetic bodies aren’t worth the trouble.”

“Mimetic bodies?”

Joe nodded. He gestured at his body. “This thing is a construct formed from the memories lodged in this place. It remembers all the things Joe was. Even the useless things like the fact that he really wanted a smoke. And the tattoo on his ass.”

“Oh.”

“So. You wanted to talk?”

“Yeah. Uh. What’s going on? I mean, I got the jist of it. World’s over, monsters, dungeons, yada yada. But… where is everyone? What’s the Tutorial? Who’s it being televised for? Basically, what’s going on, but with details this time, please?”

Joe nodded. “That’s fair. Yeah, you kinda got dealt a raw hand.” He took another drag, then gestured with the cig. “Listen. I’m just small-time. I can’t tell you some things, and I don’t know everything. So if I gloss over something, you’re going to have to roll with it.”

“Sure,” Eric allowed.

“So, this whole thing…” he trailed off thoughtfully, then started up again. “Let’s start with the Tutorial. You’re not missing much.”

“I’m not?”

“Nah. It’s more about finding talent. Picking out the stars that shine, choosing the main characters for the run of the live show. There’s several billion of you, in case you haven’t noticed. The audience can only handle a few dozen plotlines, at best. The rest of you will be reduced to B-roll if you’re lucky. Sure, those people in the Tutorial, they’re trying out fighting styles, training, fighting weak enemies. They’ll be level ten by the time they leave the Tutorial. But you can think of it more like acting school or tryouts. Once they pick the hundred or so people they think are the most compelling, they’ll give those specific Players training. How to pose. How to fight, and how to do it entertainingly. They’ll pick heroes and heels, give them gear, and give them pointers.

“The rest… well, they’re chaff. I mean, not that the cameras won’t watch them, but the number of times the execs have picked a hero out of the chaff… I can count ‘em on one hand, and I only have five fingers right now.” Joe wiggled his hand at Eric.

“Right now?”

“Mimetic body. This isn’t me, remember?” Joe drew from the cigarette and sighed. “Damn, I hate how much this body wants this. But it feels so good.”

For a while, Joe just stood there, smoking. At last, Eric cleared his throat, bringing him back to the moment. “So the Tutorial is acting school, and I’m in the deep end?”

“Not as deep as you might think. Well… yes. You’re in the deep end, and don’t get it wrong. But the Tutorial usually lasts a year or three. The best might be out in six months, if they’re lucky. That’s time for the World Dungeon to mature that it hasn’t had yet. If you got thrown out here in a year’s time with zero training, you’d be truly fucked. Right now, you’re just playing, uh, what do you call it… Hard Mode. It’s hard—duh, that’s in the name—but if you’re careful, you can beat it.”

“A year—” Eric stared, flabbergasted.

“Yeah? What, you think you can become a master swordsman or mage in ten minutes? The show wants cool attacks and exciting battles, not idiots getting turned to paste. It’ll get plenty of that anyways, but you know, it wants some people to have a fighting chance. A year isn’t really enough for that, but with the System’s help, it gets closer.”

“Wait… then if I took the Fighter class—”

“Yeah, hate to break it to you, kid. The System is a path to power, but it isn’t instant strength. If you took a melee class, you’d get boosts toward learning how to handle a weapon, and with time, you’d surely master it. But you wouldn’t instantly be a sword-fighting prodigy. That would be insane. I mean, can you imagine? Crazy.”

“Oh,” Eric said, breathing out. Picking Mage had been a bit of a throw, and he knew that, but it hadn’t been as much of a throw as he’d feared if no class gave instant mastery.

Joe glanced at him, then nodded. “No, you picked well. I mean, you could have picked better, but it wasn’t a bad choice. You’ve got an uphill battle to getting your first spell, and don’t get me wrong, but once you manage that… well, you don’t need years to master pointing your finger at someone and shooting a Ray of Fire at them. Any idiot can do that.”

“Thanks. I think,” Eric said, not really sure if it was a compliment or not.

“You’ll need to master manipulating mana, but taking Mana Sight is the first step in that direction. You’ll figure it out with time. Probably.”

“Probably?”

“There’s a very non-zero chance you’re dead tomorrow. I like to hedge my bets.” Joe waved his cigarette in Eric’s general direction, realized it was burned out, and threw it on the ground instead. “Listen. You won’t be a master mage for years. Probably ever. But you know those primitive ballistic launchers your world had?”

Eric stared at him for a while, then realized: “Guns?”

“Yeah, those. Guns. You basically picked ‘gun’ for your class. You’re not going to become a master marksman in ten minutes, but you can still do some damage with ten minutes of training and a ballistic launcher. Gun. Whatever. And you’re less likely to get maimed or killed when you’re twenty feet from your target. Great choices, all around.”

“Thanks, that’s what I was thinking.”

“Don’t get ahead of yourself, kid. Still very easy to wind up dead. Just… a little less easy when you’re not likely to fall on your fire ray and accidentally impale yourself.” He made a face. “God, I need another cig. Man, this guy was an addict.”

“I’m not a kid,” Eric protested. “I’m twenty-four. I have a full-time job and an apartment. Had… had a job and an apartment. I’m an adult.”

“You’re a kid to me, kid. I’ve been around the block more times than this guy has. Haven’t been addicted to as many substances, but you know. Everyone has their own expertise.” He pointed at Eric. “Which brings me to my next point.”

Eric gestured. “Go on.”

“The Great Game is a show. A very real reality show, a bloodsports show, call it what you like, but nonetheless: it is a show. It needs protagonists, side characters, storylines, B-roll… you get the picture.”

“Sure.”

“Right now, you’re in a unique situation. You’ve got early access to the World Dungeon. You can capitalize on that to rise out of the chaff… or blend in and become chaff. Your call, really. There’s upsides and downsides to both.”

“Wait—the cameras are rolling? I thought they wouldn’t start filming until… well, until everyone else gets here.”

Joe shrugged. “The Tutorial is how they decide who shines on camera. They also use it to tell if they’re polishing gems or turds. If the audience doesn’t react, they cut the character before we hit the prime-time meat of the real Game and move on to a new storyline. The cameras are always rolling. They just might not care about you.”

Eric nodded. It made sense, kind of, if he thought of it from a psychopathic, uncaring, inhuman angle. If whoever was making the show didn’t care about human suffering—and, as Joe implied, they were inhuman, so why would they—then sure, it made a lot of sense. “You mentioned upsides and downsides to being in the spotlight?”

Joe had taken a moment to light a cigarette, and lifted his finger as he battled an uncooperative lighter into flame. He straightened as he succeeded at last and nodded at Eric. “Usual shit. Audience is just as happy to hate as it is to love, so if they aren’t happy, they might as well be rabid. Some other benefits too, though. People can send you assistance; wouldn’t count on it, though. It’s excessively expensive. More typically, the execs will lend you a hand if you’re a popular character and you’re about to die…and, generally speaking, just throughout the dungeon, will give you cool things that’ll look neat on camera. Monsters will drop more useful loot. Chests will contain better items.”

“That sounds pretty sweet.”

“Eh. It has its ups and downs. If they can plot twist you into a heel, they’ll do it just as gleefully as they’ll offer you a helping hand. If you’re a popular Player, you’ll always exist one day’s bad ratings away from being the most reviled Player in the game.”

Eric nodded. He’d lived in a world with the internet; he got the idea.

“Also… you get loot that looks cool on camera, but you also get pitched against monsters that look cool on camera. You’re on Hard difficulty right now. If you want to stay on that train until the end of the game, or even upgrade it to Nightmare, become popular. Like I said. Ups and downs.”

“What’s the alternative, then? How do I get back to Normal difficulty?”

“Be boring. Be chaff. Hide at the bottom of the barrel until there’s so few people left you have no option but to become a character. It’s not exciting, but it is a good way to survive. No one’s going to try to kill you, cancel you, or turn you into a shitty meme.” He paused. “You guys have memes? Damn. Maybe you aren’t as much of a backwater as I thought.”

“And the loot will be… bad?”

Joe turned his head to breathe smoke into the forest before responding. “How do you feel about airplane pretzels?”

“Not a big fan, I’ll be honest.”

Joe nodded. “That, and you’ll be behind the curve. Way weaker compared to all the popular Players who spent the whole Game getting pitched against the hardest monsters. If you’re careful, you can basically guarantee survival as chaff until the endgame, but when the endgame comes, you’re fucked. The level curve will go nuts, and you’ll fall off the cliff.”

Eric looked at him. “The endgame. What is the endgame?”

Joe took a drag from his cigarette instead. “Thing is, I want out of this gig,” he said at last.

Guess that’s one of those things he can’t talk about. Eric frowned. “Out of… what gig?”

“Out of being a gas station attendant,” Joe said, rolling his eyes. “What do you think? Out of being a System admin, you fool.”

“Is that… not good?” It seemed pretty good from where Eric stood. It’d been a decent, well-paying gig back at his company, too, as long as you didn’t mind long hours and getting called at 2am on Christmas morning, according to Damien, his company’s sysadmin, anyways.

“Hell no. I’m a mook. Regional admin? Don’t kid me. I’m one step above a grunt. One demotion away from dropping out entirely. I want a promotion. I want to be a manager. Manage talent like you. Control what’s cut and what plays. Shape your narrative. Take a cut of your earnings. That’s where the money is.” He jabbed the cigarette at Eric emphatically and nodded.

He really likes playing with the cigarettes. I guess it’s new to him. Like a toddler with a new toy. Eric nodded, not really listening to Joe, then paused. “Wait, go back. My earnings?”

“Oh yeah. You get a cut of the ad revenue and associated appearances fees if you survive. It’s more than enough to set you up for a long time.”

“For… life?” Eric tried.

“Life isn’t a relevant time span from where I stand. You’ll get it one day, kid.”

“Still not a kid.”

“Still are to me. Course, it’s not relevant if you’re dead. The kid part and your earnings. And you’ll probably die, at which point I get all the proceeds from your appearances.” Joe nodded in satisfaction. “Managing. It’s where the money is.”

I get it for you. I’m not sure I get it for me. “So… you want to manage me?”

Joe beamed and pointed the cig at him again. “Bingo. You’re in a unique position right now, and I think you should capitalize on it. An early preview of a World Dungeon is money, especially when the rest of the screen time is boring Tutorial stuff we’ve all seen a billion times.”

“Who’s “we?” What do you mean, “a billion times?” Do you guys… whoever you are, do this Great Game thing a lot?”

Joe smacked his lips rather than answer. He glanced at Eric. “So. What do you think?”

“About what I just asked? Or about you managing me?”

“The managing part. I can’t help you with the rest of it.”

Eric crossed his arms. I could be in the spotlight. Who doesn’t want that? But it’s not like there aren’t downsides. On the other hand— His stomach grumbled, interrupting his train of thought. He pushed the door open, eyeing the gas station sandwiches display. “Let me think on it. And grab some lunch.”

Joe waved, fumbling out another cigarette. “I’ll be out here.”

Not a big fan of cigarette smoke, Eric sat inside to eat and think about Joe’s offer. He had to admit: as far as life went, he was chaff. He was the coffee gofer. An underpaid junior employee no one respected. He didn’t have many friends and his followers on any platform numbered in the single digits. He didn’t have a lover of any description, pets, or a particularly good relationship with his parents. He didn’t even qualify as B-roll. He was nothing but a loser, a blip in human history that no one would remember. Without this opportunity, he would fade into dust and truly become nothing.

It was danger, but it was also opportunity on a scale he’d never been offered before. It was the original trap; the very first trick anyone in the showbiz used. The next step was to get him to sign a very complicated and unpleasant contract, but as long as he didn’t sign any contracts, he figured it wouldn’t be too bad. It’s not like things can get much worse. He’d already been skipped over by the Tutorial and he was trapped, alone, in an empty world that’d become a massive dungeon.

No, he’d be a fool not to take this opportunity. There were two big points he had to insist on, he decided as he ate the last scraps of his slightly-moist refrigerated sandwich. One: he couldn’t start vying for a big slice of the camera pie until he had at least one spell. He was already fighting an uphill battle. He didn’t need to upgrade the difficulty from Hard to Nightmare yet. Two: he needed to control his image. He got to decide what kind of Player he was. He didn’t want Joe to decide what kind of person he was representing himself as; he picked whether he was hero or heel.

He ate a couple fruits to stave off scurvy and washed them down with half of a Big Pickle (spicy) before he walked back out, rejoining Joe just outside the gas station. The air was thick with smoke, and the pack was mostly empty; Joe seemed to have decided to have the pack of cigarettes for lunch himself. He’d dropped to a squat to fully enjoy them, and tossed his latest cancer stick away as Eric stepped out. He stood, dusting his pants off. “So? What’s it gonna be?”

“Yes. Let’s try this.”

“Great choice. You won’t regret this, kid.”

Eric lifted his hand, cautioning Joe to take it slow. “But I have a few provisions. First: don’t start promoting me until I have at least one spell. Maybe two.”

Joe rolled his eyes. “I want you alive, dipshit. You’re no good to me dead. No, I’m not going to do anything stupid like that. You better hurry up and get your first spell if you want to stand a chance of making something out of this miracle you’ve been handed, but no—I’m not going to get you killed.”

That was easier than I expected. Then again, Joe did make a few good, self-serving points: Eric was no good to him dead. At least not yet. Once he started making money, that was a different equation, but for now, Joe was fully incentivized to keep him alive.

“Two: I control my image.”

Joe sighed. “That’s a harder one. You don’t know what the audience likes. You don’t even know who the audience is, properly. You can’t cater to what you don’t understand.”

“That’s not what I mean. I get that you’re going to have some control over my image, and I get that you know things I don’t. Obviously. If I knew everything, I wouldn’t bother with a manager.”

Joe nodded. “And you’d end up dead real quick, but go on.”

“What I mean is… on the macro scale, I control my image. If I chose to be a hero, you don’t get to try and make me a villain because you think it would “sell better.” If I choose to be the heel, you don’t get to try and twist my actions to make me the hero. Is that clear?”

Joe sniffed, then wiped his nose. He stood there for a second, lost in thought. For the first time, his hand stilled on the cigarette box as he totally forgot to play with his new toy. “Kid, that’s literally the job I’m trying to sign up for.”

“It’s not. You can manage me without having full and absolute control of my image. We both know this, so don’t try to lie to me. I’m not asking for fine-scale control of every little thing. What gets cut, what gets played. You can pick that; hell, I won’t have the time to do it anyways. I’m asking for big, sweeping decisions. You don’t get to pick left if I pick right. You can massage things within the limits of what I pick, but you don’t get to totally control what kind of person you sell me as. If you can’t agree to that, I can’t agree to be managed by you.”

Joe thought for a minute. For two cigarettes, to be precise. At last, he stubbed out the last cigarette in the pack and nodded. “Alright. Fine. I can live with that.”

“Fine? We’re good, then?”

“We’re not good. What we are, is talent without talent, and a manager without management. We’re chaff. But we can fix that. I can help you fix that.”

“Right, so…” Eric gestured, looking for that “fix” Joe was referring to.

“So go find that first spell, bucko. You’ve got Mana Sight: use it. Don’t pick your next skill until you talk to me, but don’t bother me until then. You’ve got a lot of growing to do before you’re presentable. Get to it.” Joe gave him a slap on the shoulder and stepped forward. The air buzzed and his body distorted, stretching impossibly in the space between the lip of the roof and what had been the parking lot, and Joe was gone, blipped out of reality as though he’d never been there at all. The crumpled cigarette box hit the ground behind him.

Eric snorted. All that to be right back where he was ten minutes ago. Still, he knew much more about his situation now than he had ten minutes ago, and that had to count for something.

He dusted off his hands and reactivated Mana Sight as he stepped outside. He still had a few hours until the safe zone closed at sundown, and now he could see the monsters hiding in the forest around him. It was time to go find a spell.
