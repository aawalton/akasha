As far as Ning knew, the acquisition of a supernatural power was entirely random. It didn’t depend on talent, cultivation realm, physique, or bloodline, only on sheer luck.

Of course, supernatural powers were incredibly rare to begin with. Less than twenty percent of cultivators awakened one, and the chances of obtaining something on the level of Eyes of Heaven were even rarer. It was perfectly normal for such a power not to appear even once in a thousand years.

In other words, supernatural powers were essentially gacha, and abilities like Eyes of Heaven were the UR-tier jackpots.

Probably a balancing mechanism put in place by nature. After all, if too many people possessed such absurd powers at once, the world would be one step away from exploding.

Among those supernatural powers, Know Thyself ranked neither high nor low. It was a middling talent, useful, but far from dazzling.

The main reason it was considered mediocre was simple: after achieving the Divine Intention, one’s body held no secrets. At that level, a cultivator could observe and understand themselves with ease, which made Know Thyself somewhat redundant.

But Ning’s eyes lit up immediately when he read about it.

Because Know Thyself resembled his panel.

He’d been shocked the first time a panel popped into existence inside his mind. At first, he assumed it was a cheat, a transmigrator’s privilege, but after repeated experiments, he discovered its limitations.

It simply displayed information. He couldn’t add points, couldn’t manipulate stats, and it definitely didn’t function like the progression screens of RPGs, which allowed for infinite growth as long as one put in effort.

It was… lackluster. A glorified mirror for his own condition.

But if it wasn’t related to his transmigration, then what was it?

After learning about supernatural powers, Ning finally had an idea.

Could his so-called panel be a supernatural power?

It seemed extremely likely. His panel was almost identical to Know Thyself, with only a few minor differences.

The first difference was obvious: other cultivators’ Know Thyself probably didn’t manifest as a game-like status screen.

But Ning’s did because he subconsciously shaped it that way. As a transmigrator, when he jokingly called out “system” in his mind and imagined a panel, his supernatural power must have adopted that form.

The second difference was its focus. His panel leaned heavily toward proficiency tracking, because that was what Ning imagined an RPG-style system to do. The jade slip even mentioned that supernatural powers often manifested differently depending on the individual, influenced by personality, perception, and innate tendencies.

But there was another overlooked detail that made Ning even more certain.

Know Thyself had a secondary effect: maintaining the spirit.

And hadn’t his panel helped him resist an inner demon attack?

A coincidence?

He thought not.

The more Ning examined it, the more another realization slowly surfaced. If Know Thyself helped one understand their true nature… then his panel had been doing exactly that from the beginning.

For some reason, the Ship of Theseus thought experiment came to mind.

From Ning’s perspective, “Ning” and “Ji Ning” were connected, yet distinct identities. He possessed Ji Ning’s body and memories while retaining his own personality and memories from Earth, and as the years passed, those two sets of experiences would inevitably continue blending together. Given enough time, where exactly would Ning end and Ji Ning begin?

The panel, however, had given him a constant point of reference. Every time he looked at it, the name Ji Ning might have appeared at the top, but the person observing, interpreting, and understanding everything recorded there was still him. In a strange way, Know Thyself may have helped stabilize his sense of identity and prevented the two sets of memories from unconsciously overwhelming one another.

Of course, that was only a theory, and Ning had nowhere near enough evidence to declare it fact, but it was a theory he found increasingly convincing.

“Finally, one mystery solved.”

Ning exhaled slowly, a small smile appearing on his face. As expected, the further he advanced and the more he learned about this world, the more those once-impossible questions began to acquire reasonable answers.

Now only the greatest question remained.

What exactly was he?

He would be lying if he said the possibility of being an inner demon hadn’t shaken him, but even that theory had problems. If he truly was an inner demon, then he had to be an exceptionally terrible one, considering he had nearly gotten caught in friendly fire from another “fellow” inner demon.

Then there was Ji Ning’s death.

Ning had originally assumed that the original Ji Ning had suffered some freak qi deviation while attempting to draw spiritual energy into his body, but now there was another possibility. Awakening Know Thyself could apparently cause one’s qi and spirit to fluctuate as the ability manifested and began examining its possessor. For someone as weak and inexperienced as Ji Ning had been at the time, losing control during the awakening and suffering a qi deviation made considerably more sense than an inexplicable once-in-a-thousand mishap during basic qi drawing.

“At least I probably don’t have to worry about hidden enemies.”

Ning sighed in relief. Ji Ning’s sudden death had always bothered him enough that he had considered poison, sabotage, or some unknown enemy lurking in the background. He had even deliberately allowed some of his talent to become visible over time, reasoning that attracting the attention of stronger sect members would make any hidden enemy more cautious about acting against him.

Now, however, all that worrying seemed increasingly unnecessary.

As for the question of his true nature, Know Thyself itself might eventually provide the answer. His supernatural power simply hadn’t developed far enough yet. At the moment, it was excellent at reflecting his cultivation, physical condition, and understanding of techniques, but apparently not quite good enough to answer existential questions about whether he was a transmigrator, an inner demon, or something else entirely.

In hindsight, Ning realized that he had probably misunderstood his own ability from the very beginning. Because he had firmly believed the panel came from his transmigration, he had spent all this time trying to understand it as a game-like system instead of considering what the ability itself was actually meant to accomplish.

A classic case of being blinded by preconceptions.

It was the drunken aphids incident all over again. Old Zhou had later told him that those insects could produce high-grade spiritual wine when raised using a particular secret method, but Ning’s limited knowledge at the time had led him to sell them for a fraction of their potential value. Even now, remembering it made his heart ache as though he had unknowingly thrown away a winning lottery ticket.

Still, mistakes were human. What mattered was learning from them.

“Fortunately, supernatural powers can grow alongside their cultivators,” Ning murmured as he continued examining the jade slip. “And something like Know Thyself should naturally change as the person themselves changes.”

His own ability had already shown signs of that. As his cultivation deepened and his understanding of qi became more refined, the information reflected by the panel had grown more detailed alongside him.

There was clearly still untapped potential waiting to be explored.

But that would take time. For now, Ning was simply satisfied to finally have a reasonable lead, one that might eventually explain not only the panel but also what had happened to Ji Ning and, perhaps one day, what Ning himself truly was.

He let out a long breath and leaned back. Living while surrounded by unanswered questions was exhausting, especially when half of those questions concerned his own existence. At least now he had somewhere to start looking.

“You know, I used to wonder why protagonists could go hundreds of chapters without figuring out what the hell was going on with themselves.” Ning thought about everything he had spent the past year trying to understand and sighed. “Turns out it’s pretty hard when nobody gives you the damn exposition.”

[Shadow Steps]

It was a movement-focused martial art. At the Minor Accomplishment stage, it was essentially a framework of footwork designed not only to increase speed but, more importantly, to minimize the sound and traces left behind by movement.

At its core, Shadow Steps was built around control.

Control over weight, breath, balance, and every tiny shift of the body that most people rarely had any reason to notice.

Ning took a step forward.

Splash.

Ripples spread across the shallow water.

His movement was too heavy.

He frowned and reset his posture. The manual spoke of “placing one’s weight upon the breath,” which sounded like the usual cultivation nonsense until Ning experimented with it for a while and realized that it was essentially describing control over his center of gravity.

He adjusted his posture, shifting his weight slightly forward and centering it around his lower abdomen rather than allowing it to settle toward his chest or heels.

Then he stepped again.

This time, his foot entered the water far more smoothly, producing only a gentle ripple across the surface.

Ning nodded in satisfaction and jotted down the result in his notebook. Even though his panel made practicing martial arts considerably more efficient by showing his progress and understanding, it didn’t magically tell him what he was doing wrong, so his own observations and notes remained just as important.

Training in the shallow stream behind his fields had definitely been the right decision.

If he leaned too far, the current immediately disturbed his balance. If he lifted his foot too quickly, the splash gave him away. If he stepped down too heavily, he received immediate feedback in the form of water spraying all over his clothes.

It wasn’t quite as harsh as the sort of protagonist training where some old master threw their disciple into a monster-infested jungle wearing nothing but a piece of cloth and told them to come back alive, but it got the job done.

More importantly, it was intuitive.

Ning shifted his weight again and stepped forward using the three-phase movement he had gradually worked out during his practice. The outer edge of his foot touched the water first; the pressure rolled inward smoothly, and only then did the rest of his sole settle.

The movement looked slightly unusual, almost like a cat padding carefully through wet grass, but the effect was undeniable. His steps produced barely any sound, and as he repeated the motion again and again, his understanding of the technique steadily deepened.

His current goal was to reach Great Accomplishment as soon as possible. Only then could he begin practicing the technique’s strongest movement, appropriately named Shadow Glide.

Fortunately, between qi and the heightened bodily awareness reflected through his panel, Ning had excellent control over his movements. At the very least, he didn’t have to suffer through the classic problem back on Earth of watching a thirty-second gymnastics tutorial, thinking I can probably do that, and then discovering that while the brain understood perfectly well what needed to happen, the limbs had apparently not received the memo.

The classic brain says yes, body says no problem was considerably less troublesome when the body in question belonged to a cultivator.

“No wonder people say martial arts are easier to learn than spells,” Ning murmured as he continued moving through the stream. “A lot of it eventually becomes muscle memory.”

Cultivators were naturally far more attuned to their bodies than ordinary people, which made repeating physical movements until they became instinctive considerably easier than the tedious process of memorizing incantations, maintaining visualizations, controlling spiritual energy, and understanding the principles behind a spell.

Of course, martial arts being easier did not mean they were easy. A disciple without much talent or an effective training method could still spend years struggling to make meaningful progress.

Even Ning had run into that problem with the Bone Forging Fist. His progress had slowed considerably because he lacked access to some of the natural conditions recommended for tempering the body, such as training beneath waterfalls or against powerful currents.

For now, the best substitute he had found was proper training, plenty of food, and an almost religious dedication to drinking milk.

“If only there were forty-eight hours in a day, my numbers could go brr twice as fast…”

Ning sighed wistfully before stepping forward again, returning his attention to what he considered one of the most important skills a cultivator could possess. After all, being able to fight was useful, but knowing how to quietly get the hell out of there when fighting wasn't an option was even better.
