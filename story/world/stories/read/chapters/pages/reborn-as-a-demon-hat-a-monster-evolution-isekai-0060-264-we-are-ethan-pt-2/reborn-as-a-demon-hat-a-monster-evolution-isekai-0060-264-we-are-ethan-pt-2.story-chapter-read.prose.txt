"Arty!" a woman’s voice cried into the fading light of spring. "Arty! Come home!"

In a deep valley, wreathed in twilight, a single apple tree was on the eve of blossoming; here and there amidst the shadowed boughs one flower had opened early, rose-colored and white, like a faintly glowing star. Down the orchard isles, in the fresh grass soaked by constant rains, a young, white haired boy ran for nothing more than the joy of running. Upon hearing his mother's call he did not come at once but made a long circle before he turned his face towards home and started sprinting in that direction.

And on a withered tree stump on the edge of the valley, an old one-armed warrior watched the scene with detached, aged eyes, resplendent in silvered platemail bearing no symbol or sigils on its surface.

Artorious. The last Lightborn.

He barely even heard Ethan approach, floating through the air in his hat form towards his sagging shoulders. His blade, Ethan noticed, was resting on the thin grass beside him.

“Well,” he said without turning round. “I was wondering when you’d get here.”

Ethan flopped down on the grass beside him, enjoying the feeling of dense vegetation beneath his hem after so much chaos. Even if this memory held nothing real, he appreciated it, nonetheless.

“Sorry I’m late, I guess,” he said. “But then I always did disappoint you, didn’t I?”

The old Lightborn gave him a peeved side-eye and then grunted, turning back to the sight of the child at play below.

“This is a memory,” he said. “Let me guess, your earliest?”

Artorious nodded slowly. “The earliest I can remember.”

“How is it that I’m even here?” Ethan queried. “Soul wipe should have instantly erased all my memories, all my Skills. But I’m still..?”

Artorious seemed surprised by the question.

“I thought you’d know best,” he said. “Your mind is the hub, after all. The connective tissue that keeps us all linked.”

Ethan stared back at him, wide-eyed. The old warrior shifted uncomfortably as he explained,

“You are comprised of not one soul, but many,” he said. “At least, that’s what the lizard-woman says. The Cardinal’s Skill seems to be taking its time to erase one Memory Sphere at a time, traveling through your being like a virus until it reaches the Core Sphere.”

“Spheres…” Ethan mused aloud. “That’s what I saw outside, right? That’s where we are right now. I’m inside your last memory.”

Both of them regarded the playing child below. Seeing him giggle, running between his mother’s legs as she chased after him amidst the trees, no man would have predicted this boy’s life would be nothing but an endless stream of suffering and violence till the end of his days.

“This is all I have, now,” Artorious said quietly. And there was no hate, or joy, behind those words. “I’ve spent quite a lot of time here since I left the physical world. After all my sins, it is a mercy that I am allowed to visit this memory one last time in my final moments.”

“Sins?” Ethan asked. “So you finally admit that your Greycloak quest was fruitless then, after all.”

“I would never admit anything to you,” Artorious scoffed. “But if nothing else, this prison has provided me with the chance to…reflect.”

The warriors hunched forward, placing his chin on his wrists, as he watched the child in the valley playing with his mother without a care in the world.

“We of the Order were pawns all this time,” he said. “Instruments that an increasingly disaffected God threw away when we could not more guarantee him just a few more days of power. Everything he did he did out of fear of your System. And that very same fear was what he transferred to us.”

He paused to stare at the varicose veins on the backs of his hands.

“But it does not excuse our actions,” he said. “There were ways – small ways – that we could have resisted. There were signs in our souls that we could have heeded and chose not to. We deserved what we got. Even if the notion of rejecting our Classes seemed anathema to us.”

“I’d say you got off easy,” Ethan said. “You’ve seen what happened to the rest of your brothers and sisters who resisted.”

“They were never truly my comrades,” the old man muttered. “I was a symbol to them and nothing more. And to me…they were bodies that I was allowed to break for a cause I didn’t believe in myself. Now I simply know that I should have trusted my gut instincts. In the end, it was all pointless. In the final analysis, clinging to dreams is all I have.”

His eyes drifted to the sight of the mother picking up her son and ruffling his thin, silver hair. He nuzzled happily into her chin and she kissed his forehead gently.

“…but there are worse fates than this,” Artorious quietly admitted. “And yet there are still times when I wonder - perhaps, if she had never died, I would not have set out to become the Lightborn. Perhaps I would have found a different path.”

Ethan ruffled his hem.

“Arty, if you hadn’t been the Lightborn you were, I’d have never made it this far. You might not see it, but you did end up helping this world, in a way. You might have inadvertently saved more lives than you helped destroy in the end.”

The old warrior heaved a heavy, very Artorious-like sigh.

“Does this make us friends, now? Because even after all this, I’d still rather see you impaled on a spike.”

“Ouch,” Ethan muttered sarcastically. “And here I was thinking your retirement had mellowed you out a little. I bet you were downright giddy watching me getting beat up all this time.”

Artorious shifted, his lower lip trembling slightly. It might have been the closest thing to a smile that the old warrior could crack.

“…occasionally,” he admitted. “But the joy was short-lived.”

“How come?”

“Because I knew you’d win.”

Both of them watched the mother take her child indoors after that. The sky started to darken just before she cleared the doorway to their house, however, and the child pointed up at the stars that dotted the heavens. They stood for a while on the house porch and watched the moon rise, sprinkling the night with light.

Finally, Artorious spoke:

“I doubt you came here to spend your final moments with me.”

Ethan twisted to face him.

“You mentioned a ‘Core Sphere’,” he said. “That’s where Sys must be. If that’s where my core memories are at, then he’ll be waiting for me there, I know it. Before the Soul Wipe takes all of us, myself included, I need to reach him.”

Artorious looked at him quizzically. But even Ethan wasn’t sure exactly how to explain what he needed to do.

“I want…there’s something I have to do before this is over.”

At that, the old man released one final sigh of resignation.

“Just as cryptic as the snake-woman,” he said. “She really did teach you how to be more annoying, didn’t she?”

Ethan’s thready hem curled up in a hatty smirk.

“She will know where the Core is,” Artorious told him. “Leave this sphere, and I will make sure you end up in hers.”

Ethan met the old warrior’s eyes then, knowing that he meant to remain here and finally perish with the one piece of his past he didn’t regret. Ethan couldn’t say that he necessarily deserved a comfortable end, as such, but it seemed fitting that he was finally being allowed to choose how to leave this world behind. No one – least of all him – could ask for more than that.

“If you see Kaedmon, tell him he is a fool,” he said. Then he rose and palmed the air gingerly, as if he was operating some archaic device Ethan couldn’t see. All at once the air seemed to freeze, and a small bulb appeared at the edge of the forest floor – an entrance, no doubt, to Lamphrey’s mind.

Below, the mother had just taken her son inside their cottage, where some dim candlelights had just been lit and were playing across the windows. In the sky, Ethan saw pieces of the night slip away and fall – being replaced by white patches that started to multiply like bacteria engaged in binary fission. The trees lost their vibrancy. Deer and squirrels nearby fizzled out of existence. And the wet grass beneath Ethan suddenly felt hard and dead as stone.

This Memory Sphere was finally being destroyed. But as the bulb he’d summoned approached them from the valley floor, Artorious simply sat with folded hands, accepting what he saw as though he already knew what his end would look like.

“I still think you’re wrong, you know.”

Ethan eyed him.

“About there being another way – a path to a better world. At the end of the day, we all have our own natures. Removing the Law won’t change what’s already in people’s hearts.”

“Maybe not,” Ethan said as the white bulb hovered before him. “But it’ll let them know that their own heart isn’t all they have to listen to.”

Both Archon Hat and Lightborn warrior looked at each other across the fading world of the Memory Sphere. The trees, by now, had been swallowed by white, as had the grasslands and mountains ranges beyond the valley below. The stars had gone out, and a blank canvas now stretched as far as the eye could see, taking the little cottage down in the valley with it and the mother and son who slept peacefully inside.

And Ethan watched Artorious glance down at his disappearing palms with little fanfare or interest. Both of them knew that saying something as trivial as ‘goodbye’ would mean nothing here, so instead, Ethan gave him a little nod before flopping towards his exit.

But before he left the fading Lightborn to wither away with his dream, he had one final question that he just had to ask:

“Are you the real Artorious? Or just a piece of the old one that the Soul Wipe hadn’t deleted yet?”

He looked back to see nothing but Artorious’s neck and head staring back at him.

And for the first time ever, Ethan saw a smile on the old Lightborn’s face.

“What do you think?”
