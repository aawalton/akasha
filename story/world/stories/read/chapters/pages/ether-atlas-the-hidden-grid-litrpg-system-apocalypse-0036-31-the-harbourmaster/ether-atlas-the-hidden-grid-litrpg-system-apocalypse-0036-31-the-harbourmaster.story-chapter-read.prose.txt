Chapter 31

“Some nodes accumulate their dead. The Grid’s resonance does not distinguish between the living and the buried. It is a consequence of the architecture.” - Codex of the Long Watch, Field Observation, 8th Century, Mediterranean*

The Gardiens du Port had turned the old harbourmaster’s office into the nerve centre of a civilisation that ran on fish, salvage, and the refusal to die quietly.

Someone had reinforced the ground-floor windows with steel plate bolted through the limestone frame. The main entrance had been narrowed to a single-file chokepoint with a barricade of concrete-filled oil drums flanking the approach. The roof carried a spotter’s position made from scaffolding and tarpaulin. The modifications were practical and sound, if rough.

The compound sprawled along the harbour’s edge in a chain of connected buildings. It had an office, a warehouse, a boat repair shed, and what looked like a restaurant kitchen converted into a communal mess. People moved between them with the focused purpose of a community that had figured out, in the space of a week, that staying busy and moving forward was the difference between survival and collapse.

And beside the warehouse, in the open air between the building and the quay, Arthur saw what the Gardiens did with the dead. Their kills, not their human dead.

Three boar carcasses hung from a boat crane, gutted and draining. Two women and a man worked beneath them with knives that moved in quick, practised arcs, stripping chitin plates and stacking them in sorted piles, separating joints and slicing the dark meat into strips. Nearby, a drying rack held rows of salted flesh curing in the Mediterranean air. The blood and brine were the flat metallic undertone that Arthur had been smelling since the transit chamber.

Steve stopped walking.

“They’re eating them,” he said.

Arthur had seen it too, and for a second, he kicked himself for not thinking of this earlier. The chitin plates in sorted piles weren’t waste, they were material. He could see pieces already lashed to leather straps and stitched onto jackets; this was an improvised armour plate made from the shells of the things trying to kill them. The drying meat wasn’t an experiment either. The rack was full. They’d been doing this for days.

A basket of fish sat beside the butchering station. The fish looked almost normal except for the faint luminescence along their scales, a shimmer that Arthur recognised as low-level Ether saturation. Even the harbour’s catch was changing.

Steve caught up to them at the entrance. “If they’ve been eating Ether-saturated meat and fish with no ill effects, that changes the entire survival calculus. We’re not dependent on tinned food and whatever we can scavenge from supermarkets. The food chain is adapting. We can adapt with it.”

“Steve,” Donna said. “Focus. Harbourmaster first. Dietary revolution later.”

The harbourmaster’s office had been a harbourmaster’s office before the world changed, and it still was.

From the looks of it, it had the same desk and harbour charts on the walls. The same view of the Vieux-Port through windows that were now reinforced with steel mesh. The woman behind the desk looked like she’d been running this port for thirty years and the apocalypse was, at most, an administrative complication.

Nadia Adjani was perhaps fifty-five, with broad hands, short, grey-streaked hair, and a face that had been weathered by decades of salt air and difficult decisions long before the sky turned violet. She wore a harbour authority jacket, a real one with the insignia, over a fisherman’s jumper. She did not stand when they entered nor did she smile.

She looked at each of them in turn, her gaze pausing briefly on Arthur’s shield, reading the Ether luminescence the same way the gaff hook woman had.

Then she spoke to a man standing at the window behind her.

He was tall, perhaps in his early forties, with a fresh scar that ran along his jawline from ear to chin. He wore military boots and a civilian jacket and stood in controlled, menacing stillness. He was definitely military, maybe even the foreign legion. Arthur didn’t know how he knew, but the posture was unmistakable, the same bearing he’d seen in ex-military contractors on restoration sites and at the Emirates.

Rémy Touré listened to Nadia, then looked the party over, deciding whether these three strangers were worth the trouble.

Steve translated as Nadia spoke. “She says the patrol reported what happened on the quay. Her fighters told her the man with the shield stopped a charging boar without moving, and the woman with the axe killed two in the time it took her best fighter to kill one.” Steve paused. “She wants to know our levels.”

Arthur looked at Donna and she gave the small nod.

“Seven,” Arthur said. “All three of us.”

Steve translated and the air in the room changed. Rémy leaned forward and a third person, a man Arthur hadn’t noticed so far, sitting in the corner with a ledger and a pencil, looked up. He was in his late thirties, with a compact, and the quick eyes of someone who counted everything. His name was Cédric Vidal. He’d been tallying something in the ledger when the party had come in. Now he seemed to be tallying the party.

"C'est pas possible," Nadia said and followed that up with another question.

“It's not possible," Steve translated. "Level seven. In a week. Her best fighters are five."

Arthur understood that this was the negotiation. Tell too little and they won’t trust you, tell too much and you lose a bargaining chip that you might need later.

“Steve. Tell her the truth, most of it.” He chose his words carefully. “Tell her the old monuments, the temples, the churches, the ancient sites, they’re not just history. They’re part of a network. A system that was built to help humanity survive what’s happening now. We’ve been following a trail through those sites from London.”

He continued after a breath. “Each one has a test. Pass the test, and you get pointed to the next. The trail brought us here through an underground connection between the sites.” He paused before saying the difficult bit “Tell her there’s a site in Marseille we need to reach but we can’t share all the specifics of how we know this. Not because we don’t trust her. Because some of what we know isn’t ours to share yet.”

“You want me to say all that?”

“Give it your best shot.”

Steve translated, it was slow going with a lot of backtracking and hand gestures and even what looked like miming. Arthur watched Nadia’s face as Steve continued. She didn’t interrupt or react. She absorbed information, incorporating it into the operational picture around her.

Then she leaned back on her chair and responded to Steve.

Steve sucked on his teeth, "She says that we are telling her salads."

“Huh?”

"Lies," Steve clarified. "Tall stories. It's an idiom. She does not believe a word of what we are saying.”

Arthur sighed. He had expected something like this. "Ask her if she reckons the boar with eight eyes were a magic tale, then…the blue writing….the attribute points."

Steve did. Nadia did not respond.

"Tell her we're not asking her to believe anything. We're passing through and we'd rather not have any trouble. If she wants to stick her head in the sand, then…” he shrugged. “Her lookout."

Steve arched an eyebrow at Arthur and got a nod from Donna instead.

"I'll translate that loosely," Steve said and did.

Rémy said something sharp. “He wants to know what we’re not telling them.”

“Everything we can’t prove,” Arthur said. “We’re not going to make claims we can’t back up. What we can tell them is that we’ve activated four sites in London. Each one made us stronger, and each one pointed to the next. The trail led south and we followed it. That’s how three people from London ended up on her quay at level seven.”

Nadia listened to the translation and studied Arthur with the look was of a woman who knew exactly what it sounded like when someone was telling you most of the truth and being honest about the part they were holding back. The most respectful way to lie.

Nadia and the two men stepped outside for about ten minutes. When they came back, Nadia asked two questions and Steve translated them one at a time.

“The first question is, will activating the Marseille site make things better or worse for her people.”

Arthur considered. He thought about what he’d seen at every node they’d activated. “It shouldn’t change conditions here,” he told Steve. “The system’s already running. We’re just proving we’re allowed to use it. Though, it could increase the saturation.”

"Bon," Nadia nodded.

“Second question,” Steve said. “What do we need from her.”

Arthur needed information. Specifically, he needed Steve to have information, and Steve needed context that wasn’t in any book he’d nicked from a London university library.

“The riddle. Steve, tell her about the riddle.” Arthur said.

Steve recited it. His French was steadier now, the adrenaline of combat and the focus of negotiation had burned away the rust. It wasn’t elegant but it communicated well enough.

The moment Steve said, “Lion’s Port,” Cédric looked up from his ledger. He said something and Steve’s head snapped towards him.

“What?” Donna asked.

“He said ‘Port du Lion.’ It’s a local name. The old harbour entrance, the original Phocaean anchorage on the south side, was called the Lion’s Gate. There’s a carved lion on the seawall.” Steve had his notebook out. “Lion’s Port isn’t a metaphor. It’s a place marker. The riddle’s telling us to leave the old harbour entrance and go to the soldier’s stone.”

“Saint-Victor,” Rémy said. He hadn’t needed translation. The name had been clear enough.

Nadia spoke quietly at the mention of the abbey.

“She says no one goes near Saint-Victor. Not since the second week.”

Then Rémy took over and he spoke in slow, deliberate French, pitched for Steve to follow. His explanation was clinical and precise, more a military debrief than a story.

Eight scouts, his best people, were sent to assess the abbey as a potential forward position on the south side of the harbour. The approach was bad with creatures concentrated around the building in numbers they hadn’t seen elsewhere. The scouts had managed to push through and reach the abbey, entering the crypt.

Two managed to get back out to the entrance. One of them collapsed in the abbey courtyard. The other made it back to the harbour. He said the thing in crypt had absorbed people and was made of people. The scout hadn’t spoken since.

“Made of people,” Donna said arching an eyebrow.

Arthur looked at the harbour chart on the wall behind Nadia. The abbey was marked on the south shore, across the water from the compound. It looked maybe a kilometre on foot.

“We need to get inside that crypt,” he said.

Rémy said something and held two fingers up.

“He says he’ll give us two scouts who know the route to take us there, but they won’t go inside. If we don’t come back, he’ll mark our names in the same ledger as the seven.”

They were given a room in the warehouse along with blankets, water and a fish stew with a metallic edge that they ate without comment. Fresh cooked food was a luxury.

Cédric had brought the food personally. He said something to Steve.

"It is not bouillabaisse." Steve said with a laugh. “Do not let anyone tell you it is bouillabaisse."

“Tell him, this is the best thing we have eaten in weak.” Arthur said with a smile.

Cédric lingered, counting their equipment with eyes that missed nothing, and asked Steve a question.

“He wants to know if we have anything to trade,” Steve said.

“We’ve just fought for his harbour,” Donna said.

Cédric responded with a small smile.

“Goodwill is poor currency when you have mouths to feed.” Steve translated.

Arthur respected that. A man who understood that gratitude didn’t fill stomachs was a man who’d keep his community alive.

A fourth person appeared while they ate. A young woman in a stained medical coat, dark-eyed and visibly exhausted. She was introduced as Dr Maryam Belkacem. She checked the dockworker’s gash from the quay fight, re-dressed it with supplies that looked rather sparse, and then looked at the party.

“Any of you injured?” Steve translated.

Once she was told that they were fine, she did not argue. She simply nodded, packed her equipment and walked away.

When they were alone, Arthur laid the riddle out.

“Lion’s Port is solved; that’s the old harbour entrance. Soldier’s stone is Saint-Victor. The deep-set crypts is the necropolis beneath the abbey and Lady in Black is the Black Madonna in the crypt.” He looked at Steve. “The last line. ‘Touch the anchor where the shadows fall back.’ What’s the anchor? And why is a bloody anchor in an underground crypt?”

Steve thought about it. “Well….It could be symbolic. A carved stone, a painted mark, a feature. Could be anything really. If there’s one in the crypt, near the Black Madonna, that’s the activation point. But we won’t know till we get there, I suppose.”

“And ‘where the shadows fall back’?”

“I can only imagine that something would happen with the activation,” Steve said slowly. “The shadows retreat. Maybe we see something? No idea till we get there.”

Arthur leaned back against the wall. The limestone was warm and the harbour sounds filtered through the steel mesh on the warehouse windows. Water, voices, the distant clang of someone working metal. A community that had survived a week and would survive another, and another after that, as long as the monster waves didn’t get worse and the food held.

“You're right. No point guessing. We'll see for ourselves,” Arthur said finally. “Dawn, we go at first light.”

Donna nodded and laid back on the cot.

Steve sat motionless, staring at the wall with a worried look on this face. Arthur knew he wasn’t seeing limestone, he was seeing a crypt full of twenty-three centuries of dead, saturated with Ether, and something inside that was made of people.

“Steve,” Arthur said.

Steve looked at him.

“We’ve walked into worse.”

“We really haven’t,” Steve said. He then picked up his Reacher and checked the blade, and that was answer enough.

THE ETHER ATLAS: FIELD NOTES OF STEVEN PARR

Marseille

THE KNOWLEDGE THAT DOESN’T TRAVEL

There are facts about a place that exist nowhere except inside the people who live there.

I don’t mean secrets or anything deliberately hidden. Things like the unofficial name of an anchorage, which stair becomes slick after rain, which quay wall carries a shout, and which alley gives you thirty seconds’ warning before something reaches the harbour. None of it belongs in an atlas.

Archaeology has a hierarchy of evidence, though we pretend it doesn’t. Inscriptions are better than stories. Plans are better than memories. Anything written down is treated as more serious than something a fisherman knows because his grandfather taught him.

I have spent twenty years arriving in other people’s landscapes with maps, credentials and a bag full of books, then asking someone local to show me where the site actually is. I called that preliminary fieldwork and I am starting to suspect that it was the real work.

The old world made local knowledge seem optional. A phone could tell you where you were, translate the sign and calculate the quickest route around a closed road. It made every city superficially available to everyone. Underneath that layer though, the actual city remained where it had always been: distributed among the true locals.

Now the superficial layer is gone.

I need to stop treating every difficulty as a test designed for us. Infrastructure does not have to be sentient to produce dependencies or challenges. A Roman road did not care whether a traveller knew where the next reliable well stood. The traveller still died without that knowledge.

Perhaps that is why so many ancient routes became chains of monasteries, inns, shrines, markets and settlements. The road alone was never enough. You needed people stationed along it who knew the next stretch.

We have weapons, levels and the ability to cross impossible distances. The people here possess something we cannot carry through a transit chamber: Marseille.
