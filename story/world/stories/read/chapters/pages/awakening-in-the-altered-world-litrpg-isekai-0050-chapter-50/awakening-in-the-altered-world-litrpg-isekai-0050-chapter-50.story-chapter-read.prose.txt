Alex’s rideshare pulled up.

Half an hour later he was back, and feeling pretty wrecked, physically and mentally; the effects of the day’s wear, and all that mana usage, had well and truly settled in, and he knew it’d only get worse—the last time he exerted himself like this, the feeling peaked the morning after.

The first thing he did was take a long, hot shower. He also discarded his shirt, and pants, and sneakers, which were absolutely shredded. He’d had the foresight to realize this would likely be the case, luckily, and didn’t wear anything he was particularly attached to.

The sky was deep gray; it was starting to drizzle. He ordered groceries to be delivered, poured a glass of what was in the fridge—orange juice—and chilled for a bit at the counter, watching the official broadcast of the previous 5v5 World Cup to try to familiarize himself with the teams a bit, and tried to relax.

He was eagerly awaiting watching the upcoming World Cup this weekend live. Every World Cup also kind of served as a benchmark of humanity’s best talents—except it was also true there were plenty of folks that’d likely win, or at least perform very well, who didn’t participate. Elite Guild Masters and Vice Masters, for instance, were all widely agreed to be world-level—potentially World Cup-winning level—but losses were so potentially consequential for folks’ reputations, the potential benefits of dueling weren’t worth it. At least, this was the speculated reason for why most Guild higher-ups didn’t participate that Alex felt was most convincing.

From what Alex could tell, there seemed to be only two events that could drag the majority of humanity’s elites into the public eye, all at once—the World Championships every two years, and the Olympics. Which was also why the Olympic Gold was regarded as so prestigious.

A little searching, and he was reading a Reddit thread with less than 50% upvotes entitled, ‘But who’s actually the world’s strongest?’ Which featured arguments like—

‘Of course Guild elites won’t beat elite Duelists heads-up. 1v1 or 5v5 dueling’s its own specialized sport. you can’t just get off the couch and expect to beat pro Duelists, who aren’t even necessarily weaker.’

‘But most Guild elites were former elite Duelists. It’s not like they don’t have the experience? and plenty performed well at World Champs.’

‘read what I said, I said off the couch. the guilders who performed well took a season of specialized dueling training to get into specialized dueling shape. it’s different eras. The meta’s changed so much, especially after the new instance items cycles. duelists are just too skilled and specialized nowadays. It’s not like 2019 era dueling, where if you were elite you could show up after a weekend of delving, and still win a World Cup. these days at the highest levels it’s all optimized, there’s just too much talent and resources. I kind of miss 2019 era tbh.’

‘On the flip side, plenty of people who started in the mid-2010’s like Adrian St. Clair and Aurélian are still world class, so these previous-gen elites can clearly adapt. And Dueling’s still plenty fun. people just think Dueling was best when they started watching.’

Alex was idly interested in the question of who the best in the world was, even if he was aware it likely depended on the circumstances. Like 1v1’s, 5v5’s, specific matchups and such. It seemed like there wasn’t a consensus, but plenty of websites still published unofficial world rankings based on partial data; Alex didn’t take them all that seriously, but—they’re pretty much all Guilders, he realized. Which… isn’t surprising, I guess. It obviously takes insane resources.

Alex smiled wanly. It does kind of feel like wherever I look, the world’s telling me to join a Guild.

As Alex scrolled—apparently there was a stretch between 2011 and 2017 that featured the rise of the current heads of like half the elite Guilds? As well as tons of prominent Delvers and Duelists—the ‘Golden Generation.’ And this ‘Adrian’ was part of that generation.

But these are more world elites, what about my peers?

At which point Alex went on social media, and started stalking various celebrity talents’ accounts—specifically elite talents that’d Awakened in the last two years.

It seems like, on average, folks get to E-rank—that is, to Level 10—in 4 weeks or less, Alex was realizing. There were even very rare examples of truly extreme talents who’d achieved it in 2 weeks.

I’m Leveling pretty quickly, I think on track for that time range.

Levels are harder to get the higher you go, and there’s also the bottleneck.

To get from Level 9 to Level 10, you’d have to accumulate an order of magnitude more essence—at which point it’d all be actualized in a massive influx of stats, at once.

In the modern era, there’s people reaching D-rank, the cusp of world level, in like 1-2 years. With massive support, of course—it feels like it’s all Guild talents or like billionaires’ kids.

Alex leaned back, puffing out his cheeks, and wondered.

He was taking Clarissa’s words quite seriously, and it was the case that typically early in a career, you’d have to make certain sacrifices. ‘You’re not special,’ she’d said.

She’s probably right, he reflected.

If I’m delving paycheck to paycheck, trying to scrape money together all the time, how much more free is that than being a Guild talent? And I’ll need better gear as I go up, whose prices are almost certainly also pegged to their rank, so relatively speaking, good gear’s always going to be expensive.

There’s times I wish I was a nepo baby.

It was an amusing if sardonic idea; he didn’t actually want to be given infinite resources, which seemed like it’d very briefly fun, and then lead to a deeply uninteresting life. Like turning on cheat codes. Ideally, there was a way to get a ton of resources, which he also deserved. I mean—obviously I’d still prefer being a nepo baby over being broke. Well, by Awakened standards.

There were a few potential ideas, besides of course joining a Guild—a choice he’d decided to leave open, at least until after Rising Stars’ Cup. He still wanted to fully evaluate his options.

There was always the social media celebrity choice, which was maybe more possible for the talented than Alex might’ve previously believed—except first, he didn’t have any illusions about his own charisma, and second, on reflection he liked the idea even less than joining a Guild.

Which left the empty Card slot in his soul, which he’d been interested in for a little bit now.

Isn’t Skillscribing supposed to be pretty lucrative? It was an option he’d already researched a little.

And every Profession Level in it does give +Int and +Wis. And it’s supposed to be pretty Mana Manipulation-based, as well as Pure mana-based.

There was also something intriguing about trying to ‘get’ magic at a very fundamental level; you were working with magic in its rawest, most basic form, after all. And he quite liked the symbiosis as well; proficiency in mana types, as well as increasing his Mana Manipulation rarity, was beneficial to his Class and his Profession.

After a little more searching—when you work on Skills and enchants, you also tend to get quite familiar with the concepts involved with them. Which can also help with my own Skill upgrades.

Even if he wasn’t so cash-strapped, he was realizing there was plenty to recommend about Skillscribing.

Of course the price of dual-classing is, I’ll level my Class a little slower.

And the Profession, as a secondary card, only gives half its usual stats per Level versus if it were my primary Card—and Skillscribe specializes in Int and Wis anyway, so the stat gains are quite narrow for all the essence and time I’ll spend on it.

Essentially I’ll level a bit slower, except I’d also have an unusually big mana pool for my Class Level, and the ability to use this mana abnormally well.

You know, it’s not such a harsh tradeoff.

It did require an initial certification, and an aptitude test, to get started; you had to meet the Pure talent requirements, but also various soul aptitude requirements, apparently. He’d need to visit the Association of Skillscribes HQ. It wasn’t difficult to schedule an aptitude test, which he set for the Monday after the Rising Stars’ Cup.

As far as he could tell, the aptitude test was kind of a specialized kind of Mana Manipulation test. And it also depended on the degree of your Pure talent.

Alex suppressed a slightly cocky smile.

I think I’ll be fine.

I’m kind of curious how many Level 5’s walk into their center with Rare Mana Manipulation.

God, I sound so cocky. Surely I can’t screw it up now. It’d be so embarrassing.

Ideally, Skillscribing, along with potential prize winnings from the upcoming talents tourney, would tide him over for a bit—but for that to happen, surely I’ll have to get pretty decent at Skillscribing, pretty fast?

We’ll see.

***

The rest of Alex’s day passed relatively uneventfully, with skills research and build theorizing on Alex’s end, as well as generally dicking around, watching a few streams, and chilling. He didn’t try anything Skills-wise.

Next morning, sure enough, his body felt ridiculously, almost unbelievably sore. Mentally, he wasn’t much better; the soul seemed to experience patterns of soreness after exertion pretty similar to the way a body experienced delayed onset muscle soreness. It did seem to recover a little faster.

Alex’s issue that second day was—he was a day away from meeting his family, which was very much on his mind. And he felt like he’d already researched and ideated himself to tears.

He felt like he was waiting around.

Which was why a little before 6:00PM, he went to the Lake District Training Center with all his new shiny gear, which he was quite eager to play with.

And this time, no overexertion. Like last time.

I’m serious, even if I get matched up against a literal World Youth Champion.

At the Training Center, he tinkered a little with adding Shatter Resistance to his Mana Manipulated hard constructs—kind of like he’d done with Mana Shield.

He felt like he was making very slow progress. Skill Shards were useful for a reason; it’s so much more difficult adding supports to raw Mana Manipulations.

Alex was also trying to tweak his Force cycling, and with a little more success; his idea was to take it from tiny, sharp impulses to a sustained flow, continuously supporting his muscles. A flow of force support which he wanted to be able to intensify and weaken, as he wanted.

And then, there were the v-Duels.

Which went… about as Alex expected.

1-0, WINNER1arcstyrion!

elo increased!

602? -> 656?  (+54)

1-0, WINNER1arcstyrion!

elo increased!

656 -> 699?  (+43)

The second opponent had been an O2 Guilder as well, a wide-eyed Nature mage casting energy-balls; she’d likely have given the Alex of a few days ago a decent challenge.

The Alex of today, with all his new Levels, and Skills, and gear, was probably capable of finishing the Alex of a few days ago in like thirty seconds. And that was generous.

The question was if it’d be sufficient against the best of the best Guild talents.

Alex stepped off the v-Station platform, sweating lightly. After the Devereaux fight, he’d learned his lesson, and besides it was almost closing time anyway.

His was one of a handful v-Stations still in use at this hour.

One of which was that v-Station with the translucent privacy screen walling off its sides, giving it the look of a 50% opacity cube. It always seemed to be at that station near the corner, a few stations diagonally across from the station he typically selected.

Almost every time he’d been here, it always seemed to be there, and usually it was in the middle of a v-Duel; he was pretty sure she was at least E-rank as well. The action definitely sounded quite intense. Some really diligent rich girl, I guess?

Except he wasn’t sure it was always the same person—at times he swore he saw multiple silhouettes flickering behind the screen.

But it was ultimately an idle curiosity, and Alex didn’t really care. He respected the person’s privacy and left, the bright sounds of steels on steels ringing out behind him.

10:56am

Alex went to the infinite fountain fifteen minutes early, trying to calm his nerves.

The minutes ticked by; on two separate occasions he was sure he saw the two of them, only to realize he was looking at someone else. The fountain felt deafening up-close, and it was much bigger than he’d thought; he was having to walk around to get an eye of the plaza.

11:03am.

They’re late.

He was starting to feel a little frustrated—by the environment, more than anything. He pulled out his phone, trying to calm his nerves, or call them.

He walked around fruitlessly, and received a text from Stella—Sry, we’ll be there in 10! client ran long

He let out a tight breath. If they react badly, it is what it is, he said to himself. I mean it has to come out, one way or another.

As he waited, he opened Instagram Reels—pretty much a desperate try to take his mind off the subject.

He’d binged a lot of World Cup content lately, as well as reels about celebrity Duelists in general, so aura edits were what his algorithm decided to show him.

He flicked between two, of essentially pro Duelists saying cold lines while being interviewed, and then cutting to clips of them finishing enemies in spectacular fashion.

A third featured a talent he kind of recognized—isn’t this that really good Echelon talent, from Interguilds? Jackson, or something? The reel had a slow bass beat. The guy was being asked, ‘Of all the talents you faced in Interleagues, who would you least want to face, one on one?’

He laughed and said, ‘Oh, for sure Arden.’

And Alex froze.

It cut to a second, a silver-haired guy in a uniform— ‘Arden.’

The next clip, a girl in an X2 bodysuit—‘Are you kidding? Arden.’

‘Arden.’

‘Arden.’  By this point Alex, of course, had realized these were all elite Guild talents.

Antarctica, Alex realized.

Fucking Antarctica.

A clip of a TV personality’s voice, spliced in—in 2025, Echelon talent Antoninus Rosier, then undefeated, broke through his side of one of the most stacked brackets in history with a perfect, all-finishes record in the Freshman World Youth Championships, defeating what was believed to be a murderer’s row of geniuses across elite Guilds.

In any other year it would’ve been enough for gold, but unfortunately he ran into someone else in the final, the other undefeated talent also on all-finishes run.

At this point, the clip cut to what looked to be a clip filmed for official Guild introduction, maybe a pre-roll before a duel.

A black-haired woman with striking features and sharp light-blue eyes, clad in scaly armor, was staring directly into camera.

“Hi. I’m Isabella Arden,” she said. She smiled brightly. “Templar Guild.”

After which the reel promptly went through clips of her slaughtering every one of the talents who’d spoken, in reverse order.

You’ve got to be fucking—

“Alex!” His head jerked up.

It was his aunt Stella, waving excitedly, with his uncle Matthew next to her. She might’ve been coming in for a hug, but she paused after seeing his face. “What’s up?”

At which point he also noticed the ring on her index finger.

“We have a lot to talk about.”
