Maeve had already said ‘no’ to getting into the suit, but somehow, someway, she was now standing inside the suit.

She wasn’t entirely certain when that change had occurred. There’d been an argument between her and Gael about the Myrmur escaping if they wasted time, and then there’d been more arguments about the safety of the suit, and then there’d been even more arguments about the various safety measures Gael had apparently built into the suit, because to Maeve, none of them really sounded safe enough for her to risk her life getting into a coffin designed by a drunk Plagueplain Doctor. Alas, she must’ve sighed at one point—that was usually where her worst decisions began—because now the final backplate clanged shut behind her, and Gael began fastening the suit’s outer locks.

The sounds echoed through her helmet in dull, heavy knocks, sealing her inside a cramped iron chamber. She shifted one foot, and the whole suit groaned. Her leg did move, but only after considerable effort.

“This isn’t particularly comfortable!” she shouted through the thick glass windows set into the front of the helmet. Gael paused beside her and tilted his head.

“What?”

“I said this is extremely uncomfortable!”

“Yeah, I heard you the first time! I was just asking why you’re screaming!”

“Can you hear me?”

“‘Course I can!” Gael remarked, tapping the thick plates enclosing her from head to toe. “I imported some of the strongest metals I could afford from Ironwych—discounts courtesy of our good friends among the Steelborn—and then I asked them to melt the lot together in a crucible and fuse it into something that wouldn’t crumple under deepwater pressure!

“Which metals?”

“Strong ones!”

“You can’t name them?”

“You wouldn’t know them! Point is, you don’t gotta shout! There are tubes connected to air tanks, buoyant controllers, auxiliary pressure regulators, and speakers up on the barge—you’ll be just fine breathing and speaking at normal volume the entire time you’re down there!”

“So why are you shouting, then?”

“Can you hear me?”

Maeve wanted to sigh. It was a pointless conversation, and Gael knew it too, because he tightened several more bolts beneath her arm, adjusted a valve near her shoulder, then crouched and drove a screw deeper into one of the knee joints before slapping her on the back—a sensation she barely felt.

“Yep!” he declared. “Feel free to take the plunge now!”

That didn’t appear to reassure anyone. Vivi stood several steps away beside Jin, both of them watching Maeve with visible concern. Vivi looked especially troubled, her eyes moving between the suit, the air tubes, and the black waters beyond the railing.

“Um… Gael, are you certain she’ll be safe in there?” she asked.

“Sure she’ll be,” he replied plainly.

Evelyn crouched on the roof of the bridge, staring down at the armour. “Well, it definitely looks heavy enough to drag her straight through the bottom of the lake.”

“That is the intention, yes.”

Liorin leaned over beside Evelyn, studying the helmet. “But if water enters suit, does Maeve become soup?”

“She’d be wet first,” Fergal muttered, “and then she’ll become soup.”

“Have you tested the suit before?” Jin looked at Gael.

“I’ve tested every component separately, yes.”

“But have you tested them together?” Cara asked, her stare sharpening.

“... You know, someone’s gotta take the brave first step, so it might as well be—”

While Fergal and Cara pummelled Gael behind her, Maeve waddled over to the bow of the barge. Each step landed with a deep metallic thud. The weighted boots struck the planks hard enough to shake rainwater from the gaps, and the entire vessel started tilting beneath her.

She extended one arm.

“Mistrender.”

In the end, it was Jin, not Gael, who handed her umbrella over. The thick metal glove gave her almost no sense of touch, but the locking joints around her fingers held it securely enough.

She supposed, after all was said and done, that she did trust Gael’s inventions.

So she took a deep breath and stepped over the bow, and the air tubes tightened behind her as she dropped. Darkness closed over her body. Rain vanished, wind vanished—even the sounds of the barge disappeared, replaced by the dull groaning of the suit and the faint hiss of air moving through the tubes as her weighted boots dragged her down. She’d half-expected the cold of the waters to bite through the suit, but it didn’t. The metal was too thick, and the canvas beneath it trapped what little warmth her body produced. She could barely feel the water at all.

Red pulses flickered somewhere ahead. She narrowed her eyes, trying to locate their source, but the darkness was complete. She could sense the Myrmur through intuition alone, but—

“Turn the dial on the left side of your helmet,” Gael suddenly said, his voice crackling through a tube behind her head. She lifted one gauntleted hand and fumbled around the metal near her temple. “The small one,” he added. “The one with the super deep ridge. Even you should be able to feel it with those gloves.”

She found it at last and twisted. Two powerful beams of lanternlight immediately burst from the sides of her helmet, and the darkness ahead tore open.

Grey-blue water stretched in every direction, filled with drifting weeds, scraps of old netting, pale debris, and dozens of giant mutated fish. The moment the light struck them, they scattered. Shellback flitters snapped their wing-cases shut and darted between the weeds, long gravehorn eels twisted away into the dark, and smaller, but no-less mutated fish vanished so quickly she saw little more than tails.

For several seconds, she forgot about the Myrmur entirely.

This is…

Cool.

She slowly turned her head, and the twin beams swept across the water with her. Fish fled ahead of the light, but more always appeared at its edges, strange shapes swimming through sunken wreckage and forests of black reeds.

Back in Vharnveil, there were places called ‘aquariums’ where wealthy families paid to see fish kept inside enormous glass tanks in a gallery. She’d heard about the shows those places usually held—the colourful creatures, and the divers who entered the water to feed them or play with them by hand—but her parents had never been wealthy enough to afford a ticket. Then she’d become an Exorcist, and life had filled itself with harder things before she ever found the chance to visit one.

This must be what it felt like to be inside one of those rumored giant tanks.

“You’re passing twenty meters, a depth at which an ordinary person’s ears may begin to pop!” Gael announced through the speaking tube. “You still good down there?”

She didn’t reply. She turned her head slowly, sweeping the twin beams across the dark waters, but the deeper she went, the less there was to illuminate. A gravehorn eel passed far beneath her, barely more than a thin shadow before it twisted away from the light.

“You’ve reached forty meters!” Gael continued. “If you dropped an uncorked bottle of wine from the barge, it would now be several feet above you!”

She sank deeper, and the darkness thickened until it no longer seemed like water at all. The lake had become a great empty chamber, with no walls, no floor, and no ceiling visible from where she drifted. Even the small bits of grit and plant matter that had clouded the upper layers had completely disappeared.

“You’re approaching sixty meters!” Gael announced. “At this depth, the pressure would make swimming back up rather unpleasant if your suit failed, so—”

More sounds of Gael being pummelled transmitted through the tube, but Maeve barely paid any attention to him, because she descended another few meters before the tubes on her back pulled taut, and she stopped descending abruptly.

For a moment, she simply floated there in the abyss. No wind battered her coat, and no rain struck her face. No gangsters shouted across rooftops, no Myrmur screamed from inside a stolen body, and no drunkards knocked over bottles while claiming they knew exactly where they’d put their drinks.

It was… peaceful, in a way.

She swept her lights across the empty waters again. Nothing moved inside their reach. The lake around her felt vast enough to swallow all of Bharncair and keep its secrets forever, yet it didn’t frighten her as much as she expected.

Maybe there was a reason why so many locals in Wraithpier longed to become fish, so much so that Myrmur Hosts found their comfort in actually being inside their Myrmurs. Floating down here, she understood it just a little.

She allowed her arms to loosen slightly inside the suit, and she lowered Mistrender beside her hip while she continued scanning the depths. Just before her mind could wander even further than it already had, though, her vision pulsed briefly red—a hot warning beating behind her eyes, originating somewhere to her left. So she blinked, straightened herself, and turned towards the pulse.

The moment she turned her lantern beams, she saw it hanging just at the farthest, brightest edge. At first, she mistook it for a strange piece of wreckage. Its body was long and segmented, covered in pale grey armour that narrowed into a fan-shaped tail. Several thin legs curled beneath its belly, barely moving as it floated in place. One of its claws was small, but the other was enormous—almost as large as the rest of its body—with a thick joint and a hollow groove between the pincers.

She squinted through the scratched glass.

She’d seen something resembling it before—not alive, admittedly. It was usually chopped into small pieces and floating among noodles and black mushrooms in Miss Alba’s frighteningly spicy bowls, but even then, the shrimp she was used to eating didn’t have claws that big.

She relayed the Myrmur’s status interface to Gael, and a moment later, his voice crackled back through the tube.

“... Huh,” he mumbled. “And there’s no umbilical cord or anything that you can see? The Host is really all the way inside that thing?”

“Seems like it,” she murmured back. “It’s also not humanoid in any shape or form. It’s just… a giant shrimp.”

“Well, it is optional for Blight-Class Myrmurs to look more like humanoid bugs wrapped around their Hosts as living armor. The Wraithpier Hosts probably just find it more comfortable to feel like an actual shrimp, like that guy in the giant lobster.” Gael made a thoughtful noise through the tube, and then his interest apparently expired. “Whatever. Just blast it apart with Mistrender and drag the incapacitated Host up to me.”

And she was going to do just that.

She lifted Mistrender, but the motion was considerably more difficult than she expected. Her suit resisted her at every joint, forcing her to raise her arm in slow, clumsy stages. But the pistol shrimp remained where it was. It didn’t move as she gradually forced Mistrender’s tip towards it, and it didn’t move as she brought Mistrender into its usual firing position.

She adjusted half an inch to the left. Then another little distance upwards. The Myrmur still didn’t move.

… Alright then.

She pressed the firing button on the umbrella’s handle, and toxic bioarcanic blood gathered inside the shaft with a vibrating hum. The blood cannon discharged from the tip a second later—but instead of tearing through the water in a sharp emerald blast, the projectile crawled forward in a slow, wavering stream.

Her blood cannon drifted several more meters to the right, lost more speed, and passed harmlessly beside the shrimp before dissolving into the dark water.

She blinked. The shrimp didn’t. Now it was its turn to casually lift its enlarged claw and point it directly at her.
