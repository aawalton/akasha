The giant roc pursues us relentlessly. We refuse to leave Josiah’s ship behind – still the slowest of the fleet – and so the roc gains on us moment by moment. Whether it enjoys the chase or reckons that we’re a good enough buffet to justify its pursuit, the result is the same.

“This isn’t working,” I conclude grimly to the leadership group.

I’m met with several equally-grim looks and an incredibly nervous one from Captain Garous who was reluctant to join the group call in the first place.

“We need te decide how te face this beastie head-on,” Sor’az agrees heavily.

“Shouldn’t we continue as far as we can?” Josiah asks anxiously. “It’s still possible it will give up on the chase.”

“And let it pick the battleground?” challenges Elarion.

“It’s picking the battleground as it is,” Layton points out. “If it’s a Tier four wind-specialist, the open sky is a perfect battle ground for it. And it’s not like we can change that. Unless you’re able to create a shelter of ice?”

“Over all of our ships, against a Tier four? Not alone,” Elarion replies, though it sounds like it costs him something to admit to being incapable of doing something.

“I suppose the question is whether there is more advantage in not running, than in pushing our speed as much as possible in hopes that it will give up before our ships do,” I comment thoughtfully. It earns a short silence.

“If we save some of the sails’ energy, we have a better hope of escaping later,” Valence muses. “If we can injure it enough that it can’t keep up with us, we might be able to escape.”

“It would be better if we kill it,” Elarion points out. “Tier fours are almost always intelligent. If we injure it badly enough, it may hold a grudge and then attack us when we’re least expecting it.”

“Or if we keep going, we may avoid a fight entirely,” Josiah continues to argue. Valence doesn’t seem to hear him.

“Can we kill it? It’s a Tier four.”

“There are seven nobles here,” Elarion reminds him. “Two of whom are scions of Great Houses. And we each have a strong retinue which includes mages and Classer fighters. I think we have a good chance.”

“Meaning no offence, Lord Elarion, but not all Classes are made alike,” Valence counters. “Lady Keshaka is hardly a combat-Classer. Besides, as Lord Layton indicated earlier, this is the worst starting position from which to fight a wind-specialist. Not only does the roc have the whole sky at its command, but every time it sends a wind attack at us, it will agitate the sea below us as well. Though I agree that a battle seems inevitable, killing an Emperor is not a quick affair, and every moment we linger increases the risk that something else will notice us.”

They both make very good points. I reach out to Kalanthia, my local expert on beasts.

If this is a Tier four, what are the chances that I can talk it out of attacking us?

She answers immediately.

Very, very slim. An Immortal will not see you as a threat, but as prey. If it has pursued this far, it will continue unless something about the situation frightens it away.

Not particularly reassuring, but good to know.

And how likely do you think it is that I can Bind it? I suspect I know the answer.

If the exasperated sensation that comes down the Bond from Kalanthia is any judge, she thinks it a ridiculous question to ask too.

You could not hold me if I did not choose to allow our connection. What makes you think that you could hold a beast who is a magnitude stronger than I am?

There goes that idea. If the beast is as strong as Kalanthia is indicating, any attempt on my part to Dominate it would be over before it really began.

What do you think of Valence’s idea about just injuring the beast to delay it and then fleeing?

Kalanthia hesitates a little.

Every beast is different, she replies finally. However, Immortals did not reach that level by suffering threats to live. Just as I would have wiped out the lizard-kin had you not neutralised the threat in a different way, if the air-beast believes you to be a threat to it, it is likely to pursue you until you are dead or too much of a threat to attack.

Thanks Kalanthia, I tell her gratefully, if with some disappointment. As always, her insight is helpful, even when it’s telling me something I’d rather not hear.

“Right,” I start, interrupting the argument between Valence and Elarion which has started to turn a bit heated. “From what I’m hearing, there is little disadvantage to pushing our sails as far as they can go – if the beast gives up before then, it’s only to our advantage. And, as Elarion said, if we fight this beast and injure it, it is likely to pursue us. At which point, we might as well kill it just to be sure that we haven’t left an enemy at our back.”

Honestly, I’d have preferred to do that with Kyrian, who has proved to be far more my enemy than this bird. But politics stopped me there. Here, it’s an entirely different question.

And more than just protecting ourselves against a potential enemy, I can’t help reflecting that a Tier four would offer some incredibly valuable resources.

But I mustn’t be caught up in greed. A Tier four is still an incredibly dangerous enemy, even with as many fighters as we have. And we are vulnerable in our wooden shells floating on the sea, Valence is right about that.

However, it’s clear that for all a fight may not be in our best interests, the decision may not be in our control.

“Come. Let us make a plan – and try to cover all the possible ways this might go wrong.” As impossible as that might actually be.

Cries ring out from around me as a strong gale blows over the deck and brings with it a powerful odour of sea salt and ozone. Classers who weren’t holding onto anything stagger, shouts lost in the wind. Non-Classers in the same situation are knocked over or blown forward several steps.

One sailor who wasn’t holding onto the rigging tightly enough and another who was running near the far railing of the ship are knocked off it entirely.

I run to the other side of the ship and see some of their crewmates throw a rope down to the closest. But, under my frustrated and helpless gaze, a tornado touches delicately down on him. His screams and desperate attempts to claw himself free fade away as he’s sucked upwards – right toward the open maw of the enormous bird floating over us.

I don’t dare take to the skies myself, but I grab the rope closest to me and send my own whisper of air magic along it to take it directly to the second sailor. He grabs it and I haul as quickly as I can. Beside me, both Loran and Sarran haul with me, the three of us working faster than I could alone.

But it’s to no avail. The same tornado touches down over the second poor sailor. Using the traces of my air-magic still coating the rope, I feed it into the tornado. I know already that I cannot defeat the Tier four – I’ve already tried that – but if I can disrupt the current even a little, that might give us enough time to reel the sailor in.

My hope flares as I feel the tornado wobble around my magic.

And then it fails as the beast grips the tornado more firmly. My air-magic is sucked into its mass, feeding rather than disrupting it.

The sailor flies upwards, a vague, screaming blur in a twisting current of air. And then he disappears entirely, entering that massive beak.

The giant roc lives up to its name. I think Sirocco was even underestimating it when she said it was almost the size of the ship – I’d say it’s almost half again as long, its wingspan more than that. As we saw from its flight, it doesn’t even need to beat its wings, but just holds them open, casting a shadow over our fleet. And everywhere within the shadow of its wings is within its domain.

Obviously, the roc caught up with us. We pushed the ships to Josiah’s limits, but the roc was still faster. And when it arrived, our sails abruptly became useless. In the domain of an Emperor Wind-Roc, the currents of air are no longer ours to use.

I’ve already called Noir, Sirocco, and Ivor back into my tattoo because of how tumultuous the skies have become. If there’s a better moment, I’ll let them know they can come out again, but right now, they’re more at risk of having their wings broken, being dashed against something, drowned, or pulled up to feed the Tier four attacking us.

Another wind gust comes and I grip the railing tightly.

“Brace!” I hear echoed across the deck in little snippets of sound that make it to my ears. This time, no one falls overboard when the buffeting wind blasts across our decks.

“Fire!” comes the next cry and a cloud of arrows, ballista bolts, coloured spells, and Skill effects rises quickly through the air.

Personally, I launch one of my spears up in the air. Its head is filled with anti-magic potion and I direct it the same way I did the rope – with a seeking head of air-magic. It flies with the main group of attacks – we’ve learned that we’re more likely to get attacks through if we send up a load all at once instead of one by one.

Some don’t make it high enough in the air to hit the roc. Others are diverted by the winds. Of the few that do strike it, among which is my own bone-spear, little damage is obvious. And most of what is inflicted heals quickly enough. Beasts don’t call Tier fours immortals because they are literally immortal, but because their regeneration rate is usually so high that it takes incredible amounts of damage to give them a lasting injury.

Kalanthia is doing her best to press back some of its influence to help more of our strikes to get through, but she’s struggling – not only is the Tier four far stronger than she is, but its domain seems to offer a counter to her own.

Unlike other domains I’ve felt so far, the roc’s is one of wild fluidness that almost perfectly mimics the winds it controls. It does not try to dominate and impose like Kalanthia’s, but slips in through the gaps and creates a sense of capricious inevitability. It’s disheartening and I can see how it’s already having an effect on my fighters – they’re sending every ranged attack upwards that they can, but the domain, added to the lack of real affect, is starting to affect their strength of will.

But I refuse to give up.

Giving up means allowing the roc to eat its fill and hoping that some of us remain alive afterwards. I’d hope that hiding inside the ship would be enough except that the intelligent glint in the roc’s eyes indicates that it would just peel the ships open like a can if we did that.

Another attack swipes across the deck. It looks like a tentacle made of distorted air up to where it impacts our anti-hostile-magic shield, and then it becomes simply a gust of strong air. But that’s enough to stop everyone from sending another load of attacks as we clutch onto something to stay upright.

And there’s another casualty. One of Maxen’s volunteers wasn’t paying enough attention and is knocked overboard. As before, the roc takes quick advantage.

“Fire!” comes the call. This time, I have prepared three spears, each with their anti-magic head and send them flying upwards. It’s a challenge to control all three at once, but since they’re all moving in the same direction at the same time, I manage. Anyway, it’s not like the roc is hard to miss as long as something makes it up high enough.

Though the odd shifting insubstantiality of its body is something else to manage. I know now why its patterning was changeable – its greys and blues flicker as parts of its body fade in and out unpredictably. The edges of its wings in particular seem to blur into the winds around it.

At first, we tried keeping both mana and physical shields up – the gusts of wind are thick and strong enough to be considered as ‘physical’ by the shields. But that threatened us in a different way – the roc seemed to take pleasure in treating the ships like toys to bat around with its wind limbs. When Valence’s and Elarion’s ships were smashed together, damaging the steering system on Valence’s ship and carving a massive hole in the side of Elarion’s, we realised that it wasn’t sustainable.

Lowering the physical shield means that the roc can send these gusts of wind across our decks, but the anti-magic shield prevents it from directly controlling them, so it can’t just create a tornado on our deck and funnel us up to it straight away. Small mercies.

For now, it seems content to knock people overboard where it can then grab them, like someone at a buffet. But even that is a threat to us, doubly so since every slam of a wind limb into our shields eats away at them. Eventually, they’ll give way and then we’ll have the choice of raising our physical shields again – with all their risks – or leaving our decks open to anything the roc tries to do.

And it’s not only our ship that’s suffering – the roc is capable of attacking all the ships at once. I’ve lost count of how many we’ve lost so far. I curse my earlier arrogance in thinking this might not be so hard. The battle against the two Tier threes near the rift gave me false confidence. I’ve never seen a Tier four before, and it’s become patently clear that the difference in Tier isn’t academic.

This is a battle of attrition with a beast of deep resources; one I’m starting to fear that we’ll lose.
