Within the Jade Silk Pavilion, a place normally filled with lively music and laughter, an uncanny silence had taken hold. The air was thick with anticipation. Patrons, their minds clouded by wine and the allure of wandering beauties, now sat still, eyes fixed on the stage.

At the center of the room stood a raised wooden platform. A single, unadorned chair rested in the middle—yet every gaze was locked on it, as if it held a secret waiting to be revealed.

The soft creak of a door broke the hush.

All heads turned as a woman stepped into view, robed in flowing azure silk. Her long black hair cascaded behind her like a waterfall of ink. With skin as pale and smooth as jade, and lips like a delicate stroke of crimson, she walked with a grace that silenced hearts.

Each sway of her hips seemed to ripple through the room, entrancing nobles and beggars alike.

In her hands, she held a huqin—a bowed string instrument favored by the entertainers of the Jade Silk Pavilion. But unlike the others, this woman needed no introduction.

Fairy Qin.

Escorted to the stage by a member of the Black Tiger Gang, she moved with measured elegance, as if every step was part of an unseen dance. She ascended the platform, her presence alone enough to command reverence.

She took her seat upon the lone chair, resting the huqin on her knee. As she raised her head, revealing her porcelain features, the crowd could no longer contain themselves.

“Fairy Qin!”

“Over here!”

“I’d sell my entire household for a single night with her!”

“Bah, you couldn’t afford even a glimpse.”

The room buzzed with desire and awe, but it fell quiet the moment her lips parted.

“My name is Qin Hu. I hope you enjoy my performance,” she said gently, her voice like wind chimes in spring—soft, melodic, unforgettable.

And then, silence.

The bow met the strings, and the first note rang out.

It was not music meant for mortals. The sound wrapped around the patrons like mist, sinking into their bones. Sorrow, sudden and profound, spread through the room. Yet as her voice followed—clear, haunting, filled with longing—that sorrow transformed into fragile hope.

She sang like a bird caged too long, dreaming of clouds and freedom. Each note was a key, unlocking hearts that had long forgotten how to feel.

Tears welled in the eyes of hardened men. The courtesans, no strangers to beauty and seduction, found themselves motionless, drawn into her web. Time itself seemed to pause.

The performance lasted only ten minutes, but none dared protest.

As the final note lingered in the air, Fairy Qin stood and offered a graceful bow. The room erupted into thunderous applause. With the same silent dignity, she was led away by her escort, vanishing up the stairs to the VIP suites.

For a long moment, the room remained still, as if unsure whether to breathe again. Then, slowly, the usual clamor returned, laughter and chatter pushing back the lingering spell.

Upstairs, in her private suite, Fairy Qin exhaled deeply.

A knock came.

“Fairy Qin, the second prince is here to see you again.”

Her emerald eyes flashed with irritation. She didn't rise.

“His persistence knows no bounds,” she murmured, more to herself than anyone else. “Does he truly not see that Father is merely using him?”

She waved a hand dismissively. “Tell him I’m busy.”

“Young Miss… the boss already informed the second prince that you would attend him,” the maid said softly, her expression tight with concern.

“Father did?” Qin Hu clicked her tongue, irritation flashing across her face.

Still, she didn’t argue further. With a sigh, she turned down the hallway, pausing before a polished bronze mirror to adjust her hair. Her fingers worked deftly, smoothing stray strands before she practiced a flawless smile, holding it for just a moment too long.

From within her robe, she retrieved a small lacquered box. Sliding the lid aside revealed a fine white powder, which she used to touch up her face. Satisfied with her appearance, she set the box aside and made her way to one of the larger VIP suites—the one the second prince had practically claimed as his own.

She stopped before the door and knocked gently.

“Second Prince, it is I—Qin Hu.”

“Come in,” called a voice from within, laced with eagerness.

Qin Hu opened the door slowly and stepped inside, her eyes respectfully lowered. Though it chafed her pride to act so subservient before this man, etiquette demanded it. And survival required it.

“Raise your gaze, fairy,” came the prince’s command. His voice, though still youthful, dripped with desire.

With no choice, she obeyed.

Her emerald eyes lifted to take in Prince Lyon Marxx. Like his elder brother, the crown prince, he bore the fair skin and delicate features of their bloodline. But the resemblance ended there.

Where Crown Prince Leo exuded strength and leadership, Lyon lounged like a spoiled child. His posture was sloppy, his boots—mud-caked and undignified—rested on the table as if the suite were his personal quarters.

“Your performance was masterful as always,” he said, flashing a smug grin. “As expected of my future concubine.”

Qin Hu bowed slightly. “Second Prince, I believe we are not yet engaged.”

Lyon scoffed. “With the Black Tiger Gang’s support, it’s only a matter of time before I take the throne from Father. When that happens, you’ll be mine.”

Qin Hu kept her face serene, but her thoughts sharpened like knives.

You fool. Do you truly believe my father would offer me so easily? You’re nothing but the laughingstock of the imperial court.

Still, her tone remained courteous. “What is it you require of me today, Prince Lyon?”

“I’ve brought you a gift,” he replied, gesturing to one of his attendants.

The servant stepped forward and placed a polished box on the low table. When opened, it revealed a wax-sealed glass vial and a bundle of herbs tied in fine silk.

“These are dried bathing herbs,” Lyon said, licking his lips. “I hear the scent clings to the skin for days. I can only imagine how divine it would smell on you.”

The look in his eyes made her stomach twist.

Though the gift was undoubtedly expensive, it wasn’t the contents that made her skin crawl—it was the man offering them.

She bowed deeply, controlling every breath. “Thank you, Prince Lyon. I have received your gift. Now, if you’ll excuse me, I must take my leave.”

Thankfully, Prince Lyon seemed satisfied enough to let her go.

As soon as she exited the VIP suite, Qin Hu released a quiet sigh, her expression wilting. Her shoulders slumped slightly, and for a brief moment, the mask she wore cracked.

Every encounter with the second prince left her feeling suffocated, as if his very presence drained the air from her lungs.

‘I wish Father would just get rid of him already…’ she thought wearily.

“Young Miss, your father has summoned you.”

The pleasant voice startled her.

Qin Hu turned sharply and was met by an unfamiliar face. Her brows drew together as she examined the woman standing before her.

“And you are?”

“I am a new servant recently employed by the boss. My humble name is Vivian,” the woman said with perfect courtesy, bowing low.

Qin Hu raised an eyebrow, taking in the woman’s figure—ample curves, a graceful posture, and the kind of beauty that rarely went unnoticed in the Pavilion.

A strange pang stirred within her. It wasn’t something she was used to feeling. As the celebrated fairy of Ralis City, Qin Hu had never once felt lesser in the presence of another woman.

Yet something about this Vivian unsettled her.

“Young Miss?” Vivian prompted again, “The boss said it was urgent.”

“R-Right,” Qin Hu replied, shaking herself from the daze.

As she walked past the new servant, questions swirled in her mind. A woman like Vivian—with her beauty and figure—would normally be trained as a courtesan. That was standard practice. So why had she been hired as a servant instead?

It didn’t make sense.

I’ll ask Father about her later, she thought.

Ascending the stairs to the third level of the Jade Silk Pavilion, Qin Hu soon found her father seated near a window. He was hard to miss.

A broad-shouldered man with a spiked beard and a bald crown, his sheer size exuded dominance. Even in repose, he radiated danger. He looked more beast than man, like a bear waiting to strike.

Had he not been her father, Qin Hu might have been afraid to approach him.

“How was your visit with Prince Lyon?” he asked, his deep voice rumbling through the room.

“The same as always,” she replied, her tone flat. “He brought gifts this time.”

She thought of the wax-sealed bottle and the bundle of herbs—such an ordinary offering from such a loathsome man.

“Father…” she began, but he raised a hand, silencing her.

“We’ll act when the time is right, little Qin. I know it’s difficult, but you only need to keep him at arm’s length for another year at most,” he said, his tone unusually gentle.

“Yes, Father.”

There was no use arguing. Her father didn’t make idle plans—he controlled Ralis City from the shadows. The king might wear the crown, but everyone who mattered knew where the true power lay.

And that power belonged to Ming Hu, leader of the Black Tiger Gang.

He shifted in his chair, one massive hand stroking his beard as his gaze lingered on his daughter.

“You’re looking more and more like your mother each day, little Qin,” he said, his voice quieting, tinged with something almost tender.

Hearing her mother mentioned, a flicker of sorrow crossed Qin Hu’s face before she quickly masked it. Her father had only recently begun to speak of her mother again, and each mention reopened wounds not yet healed.

“Thank you, Father.”

Ming Hu was silent for a moment before sighing. “You may leave.”

Qin Hu bowed and turned to go, but a thought tugged at her memory.

“Father, about the new servant—Vivian—the one you recently employed. Why was she not made a courtesan? With her appearance, she could bring in far more coin than working as a mere servant.”

Ming Hu raised an eyebrow. “New servant?”

Qin Hu blinked. “Yes. One moment—I’ll bring her here.”

She left without delay, searching through the pavilion’s many halls and chambers. But ten minutes passed, and she found nothing. Vivian was nowhere to be seen. Worse still, when she asked the other servants, none had ever heard of or seen anyone by that name.

Confused and unsettled, Qin Hu hurried back to her father’s chambers—only to find several men already inside. The atmosphere had turned tense, thick with anger and urgency.

“The Wright brothers were killed in our own territory?” one of the men growled, barely containing his fury. “Who would dare strike at the Black Tiger Gang?”

“Boss, should we start questioning the locals?” another asked, fists clenched.

“Just give us the word, Boss.”

Qin Hu’s breath caught.

The Wright brothers were responsible for recruiting talent for the Jade Silk Pavilion. Killing them was more than an attack—it was a direct blow to the gang’s operations, one that would cut off a vital source of income.

She hesitated by the door, instinct telling her to slip away and avoid her father’s wrath. But she steadied herself. Something in her gut whispered that the mysterious woman and this violent incident were connected.

Perhaps it’s just a woman’s intuition…

“Scour the streets,” Ming Hu ordered, his tone deadly calm. “Find out if anyone saw anything—quietly. I won’t have this scandal drawing the princes’ attention. The succession battle is heating up, and the last thing we need is to give them an excuse to meddle.”

“Yes, Boss!”

The three men bowed and hurried from the room, leaving Qin Hu alone with her father once more.

After a long pause, she told him everything—the strange encounter with Vivian, her disappearance, and the troubling coincidence of the timing.

Ming Hu listened in silence, then nodded slowly.

“I won’t make any moves until we have a lead. For now… keep Hilda close. I don’t want you exposed.”

“Yes, Father.”

Just a few hundred feet away, a lone figure in a black robe strolled away from the Jade Silk Pavilion, her pace unhurried.

She reached up and let down her hair, the glossy strands cascading freely over her back. A soft smile played at her lips as she tucked a ribbon into her robe.

Victoria walked on, leaving the chaos behind.
