Dancing Bells

She found herself, somehow, observing the world through the eyes of a small child.

A younger version of herself. She didn’t know why, but yes—she was looking at herself.

It felt as if those memories belonged to another life entirely: distant and disordered, yet so vivid she could almost touch them, as if touch were a quality she could project onto something as intangible as a memory but she could feel, even if it was in a chaos of sensations from her senses.

There she was—a little girl with dark hair, so small that everything around her seemed enormous, yet she felt it, knew it.

Her mother, a woman whose pointed ears that rose gently from her Asian features, smiled as she stroked the girl’s hair.

Her mother height was modest compared to her... father? A Caucasian male of dark hair, who stood tall and slender beside them. Both looked happy—so happy their joy seemed to radiate like warmth bleeding through the images.

The girl played at their side, in the garden of their home, surrounded by the laughter of other children. Her eyes carried a quiet trust, untouched by fear of the world.

The shift came almost instantly.

The image distorted, blurred, and slid aside as the scene changed—like a canvas where the artist abruptly changed his mind about what to paint. She turned.

And the scene shifted again while the picture dissolved, rearranging itself into a new palette of colors. Suddenly, the little girl was running through the hallways of what looked like a preschool.

The walls were painted in bright colors, and the sounds of children’s laughter, and teachers trying to put order, echoed like a distant melody. On one wall hung a calendar marking the year: 1981. The number was drawn in red ink, and for a moment the girl stopped, as if time itself had decided to take a breath. But that break didn't take long to disappear once again.

Dates and seasons drifted again like windblown leaves, carrying her through memories that blurred at the speed of light. Every step felt like a leap toward an uncertain future—but in those moments everything seemed so simple, so clear.

The images surged onward, unstoppable, and the girl now saw herself in another memory, older this time—perhaps six or seven.

Her father stood beside her wearing a hakama, teaching her how to shoot a bow in the backyard. The same backyard? The precision of his movements, the calm on his face—every detail seemed etched into the air of the memory. She wasn’t yet skilled, but the concentration in her expression promised she would be… maybe.

Her father’s large hands and bony knuckles guided her, correcting the angle of her arm, offering advice she didn’t fully understand—but somehow felt was already part of her.

The bow, seemed like a natural extension of her small body as she pulled the string. She watched the arrow cut through the air, flying toward the target without hesitation. Everything made sense then, as though it were an essential thread of her existence.

“Well done, Suzu!” her father applauded happily.

“What are you two doing?” her mother asked, coming out into the yard with her hands on her hips. “Are you trying to shoot an arrow into the neighbor's yard?”

“Oh, crap! Run!”

“ Roger that!” she said in a childish voice, continuing the game.

“I don't know who the child is,” her mother said reproachfully.

She didn’t even know how it happened, but in the next breath the image shifted again.

This time, her mother was teaching her something else—something just as valuable, though she couldn’t hear her voice. They were surrounded by books as her mother spoke about history and literature, subjects the little girl absorbed like a sponge despite her young age.

Although she could feel that her mother was speaking, she couldn't hear her well.

Her mother’s seemed words flowed with a deep, gentle cadence, as if the stories belonged to an ancient world where everything still rested in its rightful place.

The girl sat at a table, her tiny fingers leafing through pages filled with letters she didn’t fully grasp but which filled her with a strange nostalgia. The woman with pointed ears spoke with patience and tenderness, explaining lost civilizations, forgotten heroes, and epics that shaped... humanity’s? path. No, those not were human stories at all. But now, between those stories, the girl felt a void—a disconnection born from her inability to hear her mother’s voice or see what was written in the books held by her younger self.

The memories pressed on relentlessly, dragging her toward a time when the little girl had no idea who she would become.

With a single blink, the child appeared somewhere entirely different, her body now dressed in traditional garments.

She was a miko, wearing a white-and-red kimono, dancing to the rhythm of taiko drums and flutes while a crowd seated around the shrine’s platform watched her and the other girls. Ceremonial bells chimed with each movement of her hands.

It was a ritual dance, one that seemed drawn from ancient Shinto rites. She saw statues of a wolf decorating the platform and various parts of the shrine.

The wind rose, as if the air itself were alive and dancing with her. Each step, each spin, was marked by a natural grace the girl seemed to understand instinctively.

Her parents were smiling with other adults and seated among the crowd, and her mother held a camera in her hands.

The music resonated deep within her, a distant echo from some forgotten place. Even if she didn’t fully understand the meaning of the dance now, she felt a profound connection to that moment—as though her soul had already been there once, dancing across time. Memories of her mother, her family, and the feeling of belonging to something far larger than herself seemed to weave together as quickly as her small feet touched the ground.

Suddenly, the image dissolved, and the girl found herself wearing an elementary school uniform, her hair tied into a tight ponytail. The chiming bells vanished, replaced by the ordinary bustle of daily life. She sat at her desk, surrounded by other children her age. The classroom walls were covered with drawings and bright posters.

Yet even in that cheerful scene, a faint sense of unreality clung to everything—like pieces of a puzzle fitting together just a little too imperfectly.

Moments later, the door opened softly, and her mother appeared in the doorway. The woman’s warm gaze, her eyes shining with affection, filled the room—and somewhere along the way, the classroom had vanished again without her noticing.

“Suzu! Come! Uncle Jack and Leteo have come to visit,” she said with a gentle smile.

The girl rose immediately, her heart light, running toward her mother with an inexplicable need to be close to her.

She didn’t know when it had happened, but the schoolroom had shifted seamlessly back into her house, changing with the liquid fluidity of a dream.

And now the voices were growing clearer.

“Invading now, Quincy! Piece of shit!” said a deep, but cheerful voice.

“Stop making stupid poses, Jack. How old are you? Twelve?” her father replied.

“Izumi, sorry for dropping in unannounced,” said another voice.

“How’s it going, Leteo?”

She and her mother descended stairs, and as they reached the first floor, the familiar household noise filled the air. The living room was lit by midday sun. Her father stood beside two adult men, chatting like old friends.

As the girl approached, one of the men—a tall man with dark hair—turned toward her with a broad grin.

“Rei! Look at you, how much you’ve grown! Come say hello, we brought gifts!” he exclaimed.

Surprised but delighted, the girl approached, her eyes wide with curiosity.

The man hugged her tightly—a hug that smelled faintly of… beer, maybe? When they separated, she looked toward the other man, who remained quiet. His face was marked with visible scars, as if burned.

Without understanding why, she walked toward him too, and he greeted her gently.

“How have you been, little one? How’s the math going?”

“Don’t even ask her about that, Leteo!” her mother answered.

“How are you, Izumi?” he asked.

“Stop calling us Izumi, Leteo. We’re both Izumi. Use our names,” her mother said.

“Sorry, Hisui.”

Suddenly her mother called her, pulling her out of the trance.

“Suzu, come help me,” she said softly, and the girl obediently began walking toward her.

But then—something strange happened.

As her feet touched the floor, the girl looked down, and the world around her began to tremble.The ground quivered under her soles, and the walls of the house shifted—like the structure of the home itself was coming apart.

The adults’ footsteps turned blurry and distant, and a creeping sense of instability swallowed her whole.

The atmosphere thickened, as if an invisible weight pressed on everything. The air grew dense, and a strange dread seeped into her chest—like something terrible hovered just beyond the edge of perception. But the next second, the trembling stopped, leaving the girl with a lingering unease, unsure whether it had been real or just a passing vision.

It felt like a dark omen settling over everything.

Still adrift inside her memories, the girl heard a faint but unsettling sound: the distant chime of wind bells.

The sound came from the back of the veranda, as though someone—or something—were toying with them, producing a soft, almost melodic tone. But there was an eerie quality to it, as if those bells sounded farther away than they really were.

A shiver crawled up her spine.

She looked toward the source of the sound—toward the garden doors, wide open—and beyond them, something caught her eye.

A shift. A shadow.

An animal, maybe a dog—or a wolf—but not the kind of creature one expected to see in a garden. Dark fur, nearly black, and those ears… those ears red as flame.

That shouldn't be there.

The girl felt her stomach tighten, a cold sweat sliding down her back. The animal stared at her, and she felt the pressure of its gaze—an oppressive awareness, as if the creature didn’t belong to that place at all.

But before she could fully process it, the image shifted again. A blink, a snap—so fast it almost stole her breath.

She was somewhere entirely different now.

Bright lights blinded her, harsh beams coming from every direction. In front of her were her parents, their faces distorted by the glare, their eyes fixed on her—yet not truly seeing her. They were inside a car, the engine rumbling with a steady, guttural sound.

The girl tried to look around, trying to understand what was happening, but something was terribly wrong. The air was thick with tension, and her stomach twisted. Everything around her began to blur, the noise of the engine faded, replaced by a growing sense of dread. That was when she felt it—a blow to her stomach, a violent jolt that made the entire world shudder.

The car windows shattered all at once, as if an explosion had erupted inside, tearing everything apart. Glass scattered in every direction, the sound of breaking shards ringing sharply through the air. The girl screamed, but her voice was drowned out by the roar of splintering crystal. Amid the rain of glass, she saw a massive shard flying straight toward her chest.

Time froze for an instant, and she saw it coming—like a deadly arrow. She couldn’t move, couldn’t do anything but watch as it surged toward her with terrifying speed. The glass gleamed like a phantom in the air, and in a single blink, everything went dark. The shard drove into her chest, piercing her shirt and skin with unbearable agony. It was a stabbing pain. And then, in the exact moment the pain consumed her, everything went out.

The world vanished, and the girl’s vision, like a candle in the dark, flickered out.

And in that same instant, she woke up.

***

The girl awoke, hand pressed to her sweaty chest. The golden morning light filtered gently through a nearby window, bathing the room in its warmth. The faint tinkling of wind chimes outside, accompanied by a bird’s song, drifted into her ears with a melancholic softness—as if the very air were trying to reconnect with her.

For a moment, the scene seemed to tremble, as if the walls themselves were breathing, but she soon managed to steady her vision. She was lying in a Western-style bed, the fine white sheets contrasting with the old-fashioned air of the room. The furniture, though aged, was elegant: dark wood polished to a quiet gleam. The space was warm, yet filled with an uncanny calm, and she couldn’t shake the feeling of being out of place.

She looked to one side, where there was a bedside table that looked really old. Next to the lamp was something that didn't match the table. It was a digital clock. It was 8 o'clock in the morning. May 1, 2004.

2004?

She remembered the dates from her dream. Even though they had been fleeting, she had seen some of them.

She sat up slowly, feeling the cool touch of the sheets against her skin. As she moved, the fabric slid down, revealing her naked body—completely exposed.

Her breath quickened when she looked down at her chest. The sensation of the shard stabbing into her, that sharp flash of pain, was still alive in her memory—too vivid, as if the moment had never ended. She looked at her chest. There, almost imperceptible, was a small scar.

Reality itself seemed to blur, as though those memories belonged to someone else—someone from another life. Then, in a barely audible whisper, she realized something.

She knew her name.

Rei.

Suzu… Suzune… that had been her nickname, the one her parents used with affection.

Suzune… somehow that word echoed in her mind, heavy with something she could no longer reach.

She looked over her body with an uncanny calm, examining each detail. The small scar on her chest, right where she had felt the glass pierce her.

The scar was faint, as if it had healed long ago, yet the memory of that wound was so fresh the pain still lingered on her skin.

Her gaze then drifted to her long hair. Silver. As silver as the full moon in a dark night. Her fingers slid through the messy strands, touching them with a blend of fascination and confusion. In front of the bed, a large mirror reflected her figure.

There she was.

Her eyes—deep green, almost otherworldly—stared back at her, framed by her long, disheveled hair cascading over her shoulders and down her back. Truly… disheveled. But something else caught her attention. Two small antennae, like those of a moth, gently swayed atop her head. And her ears—both were pointed. Rei stared at herself in shock and moved them.

The moment she did, it was as if the entire room reverberated. She could sense things she couldn’t see. Behind the bed, there was a small crack in the wall—she knew it was there without even looking. She could feel the soft sway of the wind chimes outside as well. But a headache began to throb behind her eyes when she tried too hard to perceive everything at once. She stopped focusing on her antennae, and the discomfort faded.

Her gaze dropped again to her bare chest, watching her soft pink nipples. When she pressed one, a small white droplet appeared. The sight startled her—a strange blend of confusion and curiosity washing over her.

Milk?

With a trembling finger, she gathered the drop and brought it to her lips, tasting a sweetness that wrapped her in an inexplicable nostalgia. Something in that gesture stirred a warmth deep inside her—comforting, tender—as though it came from some faraway place, from a time she could no longer remember clearly.

But at the same time, something inside her felt hollow—an ache of profound loneliness settling deep within her chest.

Something was missing.

She didn’t know what it was, but something was missing. A single tear slipped down her cheek, warm and silent. Everything felt so familiar, yet impossibly distant. An unfathomable emptiness surrounded her, and Rei couldn’t help but wonder who she truly was, what had become of her.

Was she someone’s mother? Did she have children? A husband?

At that precise moment, the bedroom door opened with a soft creak, and a woman with long black hair and dark glasses stepped inside. She wore old jeans with torn knees and a simple white blouse. Rei tensed immediately, her heart began to pound violently.

It was her—the same woman who had thrown her to the ground with terrifying ease. Fear surged through her like a torrent, and in an instinctive reflex, she shifted toward the far side of the room, wrapped in a sheet, desperately searching for a way to escape, to flee. But the woman stopped her with a calm, steady voice.

“Easy,” the woman said and raised her hands, her tone gentle but carrying an authority that echoed against the room’s walls. “I’m not going to hurt you.”

Still in shock, she froze, but she couldn’t stop herself from glaring at the woman with distrust, even letting out a low growl like a frightened cat.

“Can you understand me?” asked the woman.

Rei's jaw muscles were tense, and she wiped the tear from her cheek.

“Can you speak? Do you understand what I'm saying?”

Rei nodded. “Y-Yes...”

The woman smiled and approached slowly, her presence was calm, yet unwavering. Then, to Rei’s surprise, the woman removed the glasses she was wearing—and in that instant, something became clear: the woman was blind.

For a moment, the girl didn’t understand, but soon she noticed that the woman’s fixed gaze didn’t shift at all. Her eyes didn’t seem to register anything. A shiver ran down Rei’s spine, but the woman’s serenity, softened the sense of danger ever so slightly.

“My name is Miho Sagara. We met last night. You are in my house,” the woman finally said, introducing herself. “I work as a specialist for the police, advising on… unconventional cases. You could call me a psychic detective, I suppose… they helped me last night, to find you.”

Rei watched her in silence, feeling a strange mix of curiosity and wariness.

“You can understand me, right?”

Rei nodded, and Miho let out a quiet sigh of relief.

Rei studied her, eyes scanned for any hint of truth in her words. Miho smiled softly and continued, as if she already knew what Rei was thinking.

“I know it sounds strange, but I’m not just a consultant. I’m also an itako,” she explained, her fingers brushing the glasses she carefully put back on. “That means I have certain mediumistic abilities… Well, I was a shrine maiden to begin with, but I already had certain abilities since I was a child… presentations are always a pain.” She muttered the last part almost to herself, scratching the side of her head with a faint, embarrassed motion.

The sunlight caught the lenses of her dark glasses, making them gleam as though a spark from another world had awakened behind them.

“These glasses are made from magical crystals. They let me see—but only when they’re in contact with me. It’s… a special connection. Cool, right?”

Rei was still processing what the woman had just said to her with a confused expression.

With a soft sigh, Miho leaned forward slightly as she put the glasses back on, her expression now serious yet warm.

“Now tell me—what is your name? Or rather, how should I call you?”

The question hung in the air, carrying a subtle urgency, as if the answer were somehow important. The girl, without knowing why, sensed that this moment mattered, that something in her life was about to shift, and she couldn’t help wondering what lay behind this woman standing before her.

Rei stayed still, watching the wind chimes hanging outside the window—their gentle tinkling pulling her toward another place, another time. The chimes she remembered, the ones that swayed in the memory of her childhood, blended with the sound of the ones in front of her now. She remembered the soft breeze that made the ceremonial chimes dance while she, as a little girl dressed in a miko kimono, spun with a grace that felt, for a moment, foreign to who she had become.

In that distant memory, she was happy. But now, standing before this woman who waited patiently, the questions returned once again. Who was she, truly? And why did everything feel so unbearably confusing?

The answer came without warning. In a timid voice, as though the air were heavy and the words stuck to her throat, Rei let a new name slip out:

“M-Mai.”

The name, fitting into place like a long-missing piece, felt right—an answer she somehow already carried within her. The girl who had once been called Rei, or Suzu, now stood at a crossroads, surrounded by shadows of a past she could no longer fully recall. But Mai… Mai was her now, an identity that settled softly into her soul, even though everything around her remained shrouded in mist.

Rei…Rei…

She must not say that name.

Miho smiled when she heard her, and when Mai didn’t answer, she invited her gently.

“All right, Mai. Are you hungry?” she asked softly.

The girl, who barely dared to nod, felt a sudden weight drop into her stomach. Then a faint sound escaped from within her —a soft but unmistakable growl— and her cheeks flared with heat. Her stomach was empty, and the shame of such an involuntary reaction, all while still being naked, made her feel even smaller than she already was. It seemed she had inherited her mother’s short stature. Miho, on the other hand, was far taller.

“I’ll see if I have something in your size. You’re tiny.”

Miho walked off, and Mai puffed her cheeks a little. Sorry for being tiny, she thought.

Miho returned after a moment and offered her a shirt, sweatpants, and clean underwear, then guided her to the bathroom.

“You’re a size smaller, but it’s what I have. I cleaned you up a bit last night because you were dirty, but I didn’t want to put anything on you in case you reacted badly when you woke up.”

Mai, at first bewildered, accepted in silence, grateful even if the words wouldn’t quite come out. But then Miho looked at her in a different way —a gaze that seemed to study her— and a flicker of surprise crossed her face.

“We need to take care of that first,” Miho said, her voice carrying a seriousness she hadn’t shown until then. Mai stared at her, confused, and Miho, without shifting her gaze, pointed at her chest. “You’re lactating,” she murmured.

The words echoed in Mai’s mind, senseless at first. But when Miho stepped closer and examined her more carefully, Mai felt a shiver run through her. Miho’s eyes drifted to her stomach, her hips, then back to her chest. What drew Mai’s attention most, however, was Miho’s expression —more in her eyelashes than her hidden pupils— some flicker of surprise, curiosity, and… compassion?

“Does anything hurt? You don't have a headache? Or breast pain?”

Mai shook her head. She had felt pain when she focused on the antennas, but now it was gone.

Then Miho brought a hand to Mai’s head, gently stroking her as if soothing a child.

Mai’s mothlike antennae retreated under her pale hair. The noise she had been hearing quieted more, and her mind cleared.

“You must have had someone very important on the Other Side,” Miho said softly.

Her words weren’t sad, but Mai could feel the weight behind them.

It was as if Miho already knew what Mai herself didn’t want to admit.

Someone important… someone she had lost, even if her memory couldn’t grasp it yet.

The hollow ache inside her —that sense of something torn away— deepened in her chest, but Miho’s words, strangely, offered comfort.

Mai didn’t know why, but they made her feel a little less alone, as if someone, at least for that moment, was willing to understand what she herself could not remember.
