The HUD warning still hung in Riley’s mind like smoke that would not clear.

❖ Warning: Group detected approaching perimeter.

❖ Intent unknown.

She had come straight from the mine. Dust and the faint sour bite of brimstone clung to her sleeves. Her boots still carried the echo of basalt underfoot. Soldiers moved in their usual patterns. Pairs patrolled. A cart rumbled past with fresh quarried stone. Every eye flicked toward the gate the moment the lookout called down.

Riley lengthened her stride across the packed dirt. Valrik was already there. One hand rested on his sword pommel. The other hand raised to hold the archers steady. Bows were drawn. Strings taut. Arrowheads glinted. Swords half drawn. Voices sharp.

“Who approaches?” one of the gate guards barked. “State your business or turn back.”

A woman’s voice answered from the other side. Calm, but carrying the weight of exhaustion.

“Tama. From the village east of the ridge. Riley invited us.”

Riley reached the gate just as the soldier nearest the bar turned his head.

“Warden, they’re twenty-five. No weapons we can see, but they came fast and quiet. Could be scouts.”

Riley’s pulse ticked up. Not from fear, but from the math snapping into place. Twenty-five. Able-bodied adults. No children. No elders. Riley had visited their village days ago inviting them to join her.

She had expected them to send word and a planned exit. Not this sudden group of people standing at her gates.

She stepped forward. Boots scuffed the dirt.

“Lower the bows,” she said, her voice clear and even. “Open the gate.”

Valrik glanced at her, then gave a short nod. The soldiers eased their strings, though hands stayed near hilts. The heavy bar lifted with a low scrape. The gate swung inward.

Tama stood at the front. Tall and wiry, hair pulled back tight, clothes road-dusted and patched. Behind her the twenty-five waited in loose formation. Faces gaunt. Eyes wary but not panicked. No one carried more than a walking stick or small pack. A few leaned on each other. The trek had not taken their spirit, fear had done that.

Riley met Tama’s gaze.

“You arrived earlier than I thought.”

Tama inclined her head once.

“We could not wait. The Clawborn riders came through and demanded double tribute next moon. We cannot even meet what they demand now.”

A murmur rippled quietly through the group behind her.

Riley’s jaw tightened. Double tribute. How much was ever enough?

She scanned the group again. A quarter of what had once been near a hundred.

“And the rest?” Riley felt her stomach turn.

“Still in the village,” Tama said quietly. “Waiting for word. Children, elders, the sick. We came ahead to see your village for ourselves.”

Riley exhaled through her nose. Not raiders. Not Clawborn. People.

The ones she had promised walls and security to. The ones she intended to bring here before the next Clawborn demand turned into something worse.

She turned to Valrik.

“Let them in. Get water and food to the open area by the bulk houses. No one stands guard over them. They are our guests who just walked a long road.”

Valrik gave a crisp acknowledgment and moved to relay the order.

Riley looked back at Tama.

“Come. I will show you what we have built. Then we will talk about the future.”

The gate opened wider and the group stepped through. Shoulders loosened slightly as the gate closed behind them and the tower’s shadow fell across their faces. Some of the villagers looked back once as the gate shut, as if measuring the safety of the walls against the uncertainty they had left behind.

Tama fell in step beside Riley as the group moved deeper into the settlement.

The villagers followed in a loose column, eyes darting to the high walls protecting Riley’s community, the watch posts where archers had lowered their bows but kept arrows ready, and the tower rising at the center. It felt like it stood for something larger than timber and stone.

Late afternoon light poured through gaps in the clouds and painted long shadows across the packed dirt. A faint breeze carried the smell of cut pine and forge smoke.

Riley kept her pace steady. She gestured toward the two large bulk houses that dominated the open area near the inner wall.

“These are the main shelters we have finished so far. Each one holds fifty people comfortably. Bunks along the walls and hearths at both ends. We built them by hand with the timber we have been hauling. No magic. Just work.”

Tama studied the structures. Rough-hewn logs formed the frames. Gaps between them had been packed with clay and moss to keep out the wind. Windows were small and shuttered, but the doors stood open now, revealing rows of simple bunks inside.

They were different from the huts in Hoshin.

These buildings felt solid. Permanent.

“Fifty each,” Tama repeated. “That is a hundred places.”

Riley nodded.

“Enough for your full group when they arrive. We can add more houses as we go, but these two give everyone a roof tonight and tomorrow night. Later we can plan homes for families. For now, the goal is safety.”

She led them past the bunk houses toward the resource areas within the settlement.

Tama looked at the sawmill first, watching the long blade slice steadily through a log. Then she turned toward the ore foundry where heat shimmered above the forge. Finally her gaze drifted to the quarry where workers chipped stone into usable blocks.

Then she looked back to Riley with evident confusion.

“These are resource buildings,” Riley explained. “They produce stone, ore, wood, and over there is the farm for food. The machines work all day and night. The resources accumulate in the silo there.”

She pointed to the tall storage structure near the center of the yard.

“They do not produce enough on their own for the community’s needs. That is why we still gather.”

One of the villagers, an older man with callused hands and a limp, spoke up.

“And the soldiers? They do not gather?”

“Yes they do,” Riley said. “But they are primarily for security. There are raiders, Clawborn, and monsters in this realm. Security is required for survival.”

She paused before continuing.

“But even soldiers help gather when needed. We are building this community into something. Something more.”

She looked across the villagers.

“You would gather for this place. For the walls you sleep behind. For the meals you eat. For the children and elders still waiting back in the village. It goes into shared stores. Everyone benefits and the community grows stronger.”

Tama nodded slowly, then asked another question.

“But more of these buildings could be built. What happens when you no longer need us to gather? What will happen then? Do we have to leave?”

Riley shook her head.

“No. You do not understand.”

She slowed her steps and turned to face the group.

“We are building something different than what you had.”

Her voice carried evenly across the yard.

“When fewer people are needed to gather resources, those people can do other things. This community is not just for producing resources. It is for living.”

She looked from one villager to the next.

“I ask you this. How would you contribute to your community if you did not have to gather every day?”

The villagers exchanged uncertain looks.

Then one man from the back spoke.

“I would like to fish rather than gather.”

Another woman raised her voice.

“I would rather make clothes than work the fields.”

“And I would hunt,” another added.

Soon more voices joined.

Ideas spread through the group like sparks catching dry grass. The villagers began talking with hope and a little excitement at the prospect of having a choice for the first time in their lives.

Tama watched them.

Then she looked back at Riley.

Riley was smiling now.

She led the group back toward the open space between the bulk houses.

The sun had dropped lower, and the light now carried a warmer amber edge that softened the hard lines of the walls. Soldiers had already set out rough tables and benches near the hearths. A few villagers sat on the ground stretching legs stiff from the road. Others stood in small clusters, speaking quietly.

Their voices blended with the distant clang of the forge and the low whinny of horses near the stable.

Riley stopped at the center of the space and turned to face them.

Tama stayed close.

The rest gathered in a loose semicircle.

No one spoke first.

The weight of the day still pressed on them.

Riley waited until the last stragglers settled before she began.

“Now you have seen this for yourselves,” she said.

“This is a community. We have resources, soldiers, walls. We have a future.”

Tama listened carefully.

Then she spoke.

“Yes, Riley. What you said was true. We see your buildings, your walls, and your soldiers.”

She paused.

“But we have seen this before.”

The hopeful expressions in the group began to fade.

“How do we know that once we come here things will not change?”

Her eyes held Riley’s.

“The Clawborn were not always this brutal. They offered protection once too.”

Her voice grew quieter.

“And in the end, there was no one left to protect us from them.”

The group tightened.

The smiling hopeful faces were replaced with sober expressions.

Riley could feel the fear creeping back in.
