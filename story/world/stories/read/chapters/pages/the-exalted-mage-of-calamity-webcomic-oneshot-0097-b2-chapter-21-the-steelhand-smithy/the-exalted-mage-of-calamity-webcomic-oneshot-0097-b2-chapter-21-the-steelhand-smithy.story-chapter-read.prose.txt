The hammer fell.

Once. Twice. Each swing sent a sharp ring echoing through the forge.

Orban struck the glowing blade, driving shape into the metal like always. The steel stretched beneath his hammer, a bright orange glow diffusing along the edges while the center spine remained dark. Sparks bounced along the anvil, scattering over his gloves and apron before dying like raindrops against the stone floor.

He wiped sweat from his forehead.

One strike.

Another.

It was a constant rhythm. The weight and feel of the hammer had been carved into his bones. His muscles remembered each motion before thought could reach them. It should have felt good. And yet, it didn’t.

Orban lifted his hammer, but stopped at the top of its arc. For several seconds, he didn’t move. He only stared at the half-finished blade resting across the anvil. The shape. The weight. All of it was right. Even the balance was close to finished. But the color was wrong. The texture wasn’t correct.

Any experienced blacksmith could tell immediately.

There was unevenness beneath the heated surface. Small veins of dull gray hid beneath the orange glow, refusing to blend. The difference was clear as night and day. Like dirty threads woven through royal tapestry. Every strike pushed the shape closer to completion, yet brought more of the irregularity to the surface.

“Damn it,” he muttered. His voice was deep, almost like a growl. His voice held restraint, barely contained.

He slammed his hammer down on the metal. A strike much stronger than the ones before.

Across the workshop, his apprentice flinched.

The boy had been working the bellows, his face shiny with sweat and soot smeared across one cheek. He looked barely old enough to shave, though Orban suspected that had more to do with being human than being young. Humans always looked too soft around the edges.

“Master Orban?” Theo asked carefully.

“Stop the bellows.”

The apprentice nodded quickly.

The furnace breathed lower, its orange throat dimming as the roar inside the workshop softened into a steady crackle. The shop immediately began to cool as Theo ran across the room, opening the ventilation shafts and powering the cleansing runes.

Orban picked up the blade with a pair of tongs and brought it close to his eye.

There it was. Just as he thought. A hairline fracture. So small that most people wouldn’t notice. Even appraisers might have overlooked it. But during tempering, that was when it became most apparent. The blade would be usable, but only for a short while. Perhaps two weeks of moderate use before the metal cracked and came apart.

Anyone using such a blade would be handed a death sentence.

It was just like the rest.

Orban growled and dipped the blade into water to cool it. Then he tossed it onto the trash heap at the side of the workshop. The blade bounced and clanged onto the other metal blades; it was a pile filled with cracked spearheads, warped daggers, and even chipped axes and broken breastplates. Dozens of them. Every single one—useless.

“Was that one…?” Theo asked.

“Steel’s rotten,” Orban said. His voice was low. Like a dragon returning to its nest and finding its eggs stolen.

Theo froze, lowering his eyes to the floor. “I apologize, Master Orban.”

Orban glanced at Theo for a fraction of a second. He realized his tone had been a tad harsh, yet it wasn’t enough to make him apologize.

The boy had been the one who brought him the ingot. Yet he wasn’t the one who had refined it. He hadn’t been the one who sold it as second-grade sacred steel with a straight face. It wasn’t he who swore that the batch came from clean ore, unlike the others.

No, all of that came from the merchants. Every single one of them.

His apprentice stared at the pile. “That’s… the sixth one this week.”

“I can count, boy.”

“Right. Sorry.”

Orban sighed and took off his gloves. He dragged a hand through his beard.

Six failures. That meant six commissions delayed. Six commissions he probably wouldn’t be able to finish any time soon. And six more clients who would spread the word, the rumors, that Orban’s forge, The Steelhand, wasn’t what it used to be.

That was the part that galled him most. His hands had not gotten worse. His keen eyes had not dulled. His muscles hadn’t lost their edge. Those were skills no dwarven blacksmith simply forgot.

A guard’s spear. An adventurer’s sword. A nobleman’s breastplate, or even a rogue’s plate guards. Orban could create each one.

The craft was not the problem. The material was. A smith like him could work around many problems. Poor heat. Bad charcoal. Even bellows with a loose seam. Each could be fixed or remedied. But impurities hidden deep inside an ingot? Slag veins folded into the heart of the steel?

There was only so much a hammer could fix.

He could draw out slag, mend superficial flaws, and coax as much strength as possible from stubborn steel. But he could not hammer honesty into a rotten ingot. Not without burning more coal than the blade was worth and losing half the metal before it ever reached his client.

He was only an independent smith. He owned and ran his own forge. The other smithies in town were large organizations with reach. They had direct partnerships with merchant houses. They were the ones buying up all the best ore and refining the best ingots.

Orban was left with the low-quality, price-gouged ingots. The ones meant for failure.

And there was nothing he could do about it.

A knock came from the front of the shop.

Theo looked over. “Should I get it?”

The knock came again.

Orban closed his eyes and drew in a deep breath. He knew that knock. Three short taps, spaced apart just enough to sound patient and polite. No craftsman or client ever knocked like that.

Orban turned and walked through the forge, passing empty weapon racks and shelves that should have been full. His tools were off their hangers and lay across different tables and workbenches. Everything was a cluttered mess, and somehow, it still looked empty.

Orban opened the door.

A thin man in a cream-colored coat stood outside, smiling beneath a narrow mustache. Behind him, the street bustled with foot traffic. Porters hauled small crates and large packs. Merchants rode atop wagons and carriages. A pair of adventurers walked past in new armor, bright and shiny. The metal was too new, as if it had never seen the edge of battle.

“Master Orban,” the man said. “Still hard at work, I see.”

“Still wastin' my time, as well, I see.”

The man’s smile did not change. “I came on behalf of Vellum House.”

“I know who you came for.”

“Then you know why I’m here.” The man reached into his coat and withdrew a folded notice. “Your payment deadline is approaching. Furnace coal, tool loans, guild dues, and the outstanding lease on this property. House Vellum has been very generous in extending your repayment schedule.”

“Generous,” Orban repeated. “that what you folk call this?”

“Extremely. After all, we could have shut down this place many months ago, but we assumed a great smith such as yourself would be able to turn his situation around.”

Orban stared at him with cruel, sharp eyes. “You people charge interest like a starving vampire needin’ a fix. Don’t come around spouting such lunacy with me. You know exactly why my forge is behind on pay.”

“And yet we remain more forgiving than the craft guild.” The man glanced past him into the shop, his eyes moving over the empty racks. “Auction season should have been quite profitable for a smith of your reputation.”

Orban’s jaw tightened.

Reputation this. Reputation that. The man in front of him, and his whole damn house, were the reason he had fallen so low. Never had he faced such humiliation.

“I’ll have the money,” Orban said.

“From where?”

“That’s none of your concern.”

“I’m afraid it is very much our concern.” The man’s smile softened into something almost pitying. “Master Orban, there is no shame in accepting a buyout. Come work under House Vellum. The larger forges are taking on workers. With your skill, I’m certain you could negotiate a respectable position. Less risk. Steady pay. And of course… better material to forge with.”

“I’m well aware of your tricks. Buyin’ all the good stuff and leaving me with the rot. I’d sooner die than lose to such cowardly methods.”

The man spread his hands. “I don’t know what you’re talking about, Master Orban. I’m just here to extend House Vellum’s sincerest offer. Of course materials are being bought up by all the other big smithies. It would be very hard for a forge as small as yours to compete. That’s not manipulation. That’s just the truth of business.”

Orban growled.

“Enough. I’ll have your money. Leave my forge. Now.”

The smile on the man’s face faltered, then turned into a frown. “Oh, Master Orban. You are only delaying the inevitable. The longer this farce goes on, the worse your problems—”

“Yeah, yeah. Over my dead body.” Orban cut him off and slammed the door shut before the man could utter another word.

He turned and walked deeper into his forge, while the man’s faint mumbles carried from the other side of the door.

A miserable frown settled on Orban’s face.

Because the man was right. Without good material, he couldn’t make good equipment. Without a merchant’s backing, he couldn’t get good material.

The Steelhand was doomed to fail.

After leaving the dwarven kingdom, he had never expected to be reduced to this. Cornered by sly foxes all plotting his downfall.

And here he was, without a single resource. Without any recourse to change his fate.

“Damn it all…”
