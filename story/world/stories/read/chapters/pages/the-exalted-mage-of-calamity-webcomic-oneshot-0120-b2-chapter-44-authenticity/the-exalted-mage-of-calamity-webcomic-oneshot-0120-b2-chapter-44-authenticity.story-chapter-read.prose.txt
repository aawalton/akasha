Gwen tilted her head, looking a little confused.

Nasha glanced over. “What’s wrong?”

“Hm… didn’t you say your notes would only make about ninety thousand vix?” Gwen asked, looking at Veronica.

“Yeah. Roughly.”

“Then… if my math is right… after that one hundred and twenty thousand, we’re completely out of money. And that’s assuming the notes sell for at least forty and fifty thousand each.”

“You’re really pushing it by betting on the price of your notes. We don’t even know how much they’ll sell for,” Nasha said, leaning back nonchalantly. “Though I’m sure Victor would be proud of you.”

Veronica glanced at her, then sighed. “No, I’m sure Victor would kill me if I were betting with money I didn’t have.” She looked back toward the auctioneer, who was already taking bids for the next lot. “I’ll just have to borrow more funds from Galland.” Her eyes drifted toward another balcony. “Or maybe even Lord Vaun.”

Gwen’s lips flattened. “I don’t know how nobles or rich people deal with being friends with someone of high status when all you’re gonna do is extort them.”

Veronica shrugged. “Favors for favors. I’m sure Lord Vaun might appreciate one from a future tenth-tier mage.”

“You gotta make it there first,” Nasha added.

Veronica stared down at the auction hall.

The next few lots passed without much interest from her. Conversations throughout the auction house had returned to normal, no longer concerned with the feud between the commerce groups and the dwarven smith.

Veronica turned another page in the catalogue, trying to focus on the remaining materials she needed. The starmetal had been secured, though at a price far above what she had wanted. The drakebone ash had also been acquired. There were a few substitutes among the later lots, but none were particularly ideal.

She would have to be careful.

Then a soft chime rang inside her head.

[Veronica. I have finished analyzing the gemstone dropped by Hevestra. However, I require a source of demonic energy to conclude and finalize my data.]

Her fingers paused on the edge of the page. Demonic energy? Why would you need something like that?

[The gemstone appears to possess significant absorption qualities. However, since it was used by Hevestra for an extended period, its affinity for demonic energy is much greater than for other forms of energy. I have already analyzed how it reacts with mana and aura, but I require demonic energy to complete the analysis.]

When did you get the chance to test it with aura?

[I was able to absorb a trace amount while you slept beside Nasha.]

Veronica narrowed her eyes.

Sage.

[No harm was caused. Nasha did not notice.]

That’s not the part I’m concerned about.

[Noted.]

Veronica exhaled quietly through her nose and leaned back against the couch. There was a short pause, followed by another chime.

[Quest Updated: Acquire demonic energy.]

Veronica pursed her lips.

“Something wrong?” Gwen asked, noticing her expression.

Veronica looked down and continued flipping through the catalogue until she found something suitable. “Sage finished analyzing that gemstone from Hevestra—that demon we fought. She wants something containing demonic energy so she can test it.”

“Excuse me, what?” Gwen said, leaning forward.

“Exactly what I said,” Veronica replied lazily.

Most people hated demons, considering them a scourge upon the world, but demonic materials still had practical uses. They were particularly useful in poisons, specialized concoctions, and certain artifacts. Possessing demonic materials wasn’t inherently illegal, though they certainly carried a stigma.

Her eyes stopped on the first demonic item listed in the catalogue: a monster core harvested from corrupted creatures in the north. Most buyers purchased such cores for the original monster core buried beneath the corruption, assuming they had a way to purify or extract the demonic energy.

“How much are you planning to extort from Lord Vaun?” Nasha asked with a smirk. “You keep wanting more and more things. So selfish.”

“Oh, be quiet,” Veronica said. “Worst-case scenario, I can provide the auction house with another set of notes for them to sell afterward.”

“You’re having the Mage Association give all of that away for free,” Nasha said. “Not only will you make some prestigious noble or powerful mage unhappy, but you’ll make an enemy of the auction house too.”

“She’s got a point,” Gwen added. “You can’t just keep pissing people off like this. Especially when the auction house is backed by so many people. You saw all those banners hanging outside.”

Veronica narrowed her eyes and bit her lip.

They did have a point.

Her notes were limited, and she couldn’t keep capitalizing on them forever if she intended to make them public. Worse, doing so repeatedly might damage her reputation, regardless of how powerful she became in the future.

Maybe it would be better to let this opportunity pass and find another later.

“Fine, fine,” Veronica said, closing the catalogue. “But this all depends on how much my notes sell for. They’ll appear before the monster cores do. I’ll make a decision then.”

Gwen leaned forward, looking past Veronica directly at Nasha.

Nasha leaned forward as well.

The two exchanged a look, then shook their heads in mutual disapproval.

“I can see you two, you know,” Veronica said, staring at them.

Several more lots passed in quick succession. The three of them sat and waited for the next interesting one to appear. That particular lot would determine how the rest of the auction would unfold.

The fox beastkin auctioneer snapped her fingers. A new item appeared onstage.

A black box.

Unlike most of the previous lots, the actual item was hidden from prying eyes. The seal of the Pelotema Grand Auction House was affixed to its side.

“Pay attention to this one, ladies and gentlemen,” the auctioneer said, her voice carrying a more regal tone. “For lot one hundred and sixty-seven, we have a set of notes—a manuscript submitted for tonight’s auction. It covers the theory and application of spell compression for mages. Specifically, a method for compressing a second-tier spell into a first-tier spell.”

A few murmurs rippled through the room. It wasn’t much, but there were definitely people interested—particularly mages and researchers. Plenty of them belonged to Maftem’s four mage towers.

“Inside is a full explanation to convert one basic spell, along with notes and theories on how the same principles may be applied to other second-tier spells. Our mages have already examined and verified the manuscript and can confirm that its contents are legitimate.”

Several people turned to speak with colleagues or mentors. Some appeared hesitant, but many showed clear curiosity. The divide wasn’t surprising.

Spell compression was a dead area of study. Not only was it comparatively underdeveloped, but it was also difficult to justify the time required to research it. A fourth-tier mage, for instance, wouldn’t spend countless hours converting a second-tier spell into a first-tier one. There was little point. They had already surpassed the tier where such a spell would be useful.

That problem only became worse the more advanced the mage was. An archmage certainly wouldn’t care about compressing a spell from such a low tier.

“For this lot, we will begin the bidding at twenty thousand vix! Minimum bid increment: one thousand!”

For several seconds, no one bid.

Veronica, seated above in the VIP balcony, narrowed her eyes faintly.

Then, breaking the silence, a mage in the lower section raised his paddle. “Twenty-one thousand.”

Another mage, a much older woman, lifted her paddle moments later. “Twenty-three thousand.”

A third followed from a side section. “Twenty-four.”

The bidding moved carefully, far more cautiously than with many of the previous lots. Most of the bids came from the lower sections, particularly from mages seated in tight-knit groups.

Mage tower representatives.

The real appeal of the notes lay in the knowledge behind them. They were a foundation for further research into the field—a breakthrough in an area of magical study that few had bothered exploring. Being among the first to build upon that knowledge was an attractive prospect.

Unfortunately… it wasn’t attractive enough.

“Sold for thirty-six thousand!” the auctioneer announced.

High above, Veronica frowned.

“Um, that’s bad,” Gwen said.

“That’s not the forty thousand you were hoping for,” Nasha added. “Seems like there wasn’t much interest.”

Veronica sighed. “I suppose this isn’t surprising. Not many people research spell compression.”

Onstage, the black case disappeared with a snap—only to be replaced by another.

Immediately, murmurs rose from the crowd.

“For lot one hundred and sixty-eight, we have a second manuscript! Like the previous lot, it concerns the theory of spell compression. This time, however, the manuscript demonstrates the method using a third-tier spell compressed into a second-tier framework!”

The murmurs grew louder. Now their attention had been caught.

Throughout the lower sections, and even among the balconies, people began to whisper. If a third-tier spell could be reduced to the second tier, then in theory, it could potentially be reduced again. Down to the first tier—if one possessed both manuscripts, that was.

“Excuse me.” A voice carried from one of the balconies.

Everyone turned.

Someone had used a manual voice-amplification spell.

Veronica’s gaze settled on the speaker.

Far from her own balcony stood a large man wrapped in a purple robe lined with black along the seams. That marked him as a senior member of the Mage Association, but more importantly, a badge bearing seven stars was pinned to his chest.

Each star represented a significant degree of authority and standing. With seven stars, that placed him at roughly the level of a vice-director, second only to the master of a mage tower.

He appeared to be in his fifties, with a white mustache that connected to a full beard. Wrinkles marked his face, yet he possessed a strong physique uncommon for someone his age. His voice was deep, and the confidence in his posture made it clear he had no concern about interrupting the auction.

“You say these manuscripts were verified by the Grand Auction’s mages?”

The auctioneer, caught off guard by the sudden question, did her best to straighten. She coughed. “Ahem. Yes, sir. We reviewed both the previous manuscript and this one and confirmed their legitimacy.”

“How so?” he asked.

“Our mages reviewed the notes and confirmed they were able to produce the promised result. Specifically, the ability to cast a higher-tier spell at a lower tier.”

The man’s posture didn’t change. If anything, his eyes narrowed. “How does that confirm legitimacy?”

She opened her mouth, then froze. People stared at her.

“I… apologize, but I don’t understand what you mean.”

The man grunted quietly.

Below, everyone had focused their attention on him, gazes flicking between his balcony and the stage. A few VIPs in the surrounding balconies merely shook their heads. Veronica spotted Lord Vaun staring at the man before looking down and pinching the bridge of his nose.

“You say these manuscripts were given to you and contain information on spell compression—a field of research long forgotten. One that is no longer seriously studied, aside from the occasional examination of its methods and theories.” He paused. “So I am curious how you received two separate manuscripts solving a problem that has never been solved before.”

Onstage, the auctioneer stood frozen. Veronica glanced at her and noticed her large, fluffy ears twitching every few seconds.

The auction staff were probably communicating with her, telling her how to handle the situation.

“Can you guarantee these notes actually allow for true spell compression?”

“Of-of course,” she replied, her ears twitching again. “These notes were registered legitimately, and we tested them before—”

“There are already methods for refining spells into lower-tier frameworks,” the man interrupted. “But those methods come with no real benefit. Exorbitant mana costs. Unstable, diminished effects. They defeat the entire purpose of spell compression, where the goal is to reduce a spell’s cost while maintaining its effectiveness.”

His gaze hardened. “These notes—can you guarantee that they reduce the mana cost while preserving the spell’s original strength?”

“I… um…”

The auctioneer struggled to answer. It was obvious she didn’t know the finer details of the agreement the registrar had made. She probably wasn’t a mage, either. The benefits and drawbacks of spell compression meant little to her. If a spell could be compressed… was there more to say about it? That’s probably what she thought. That’s probably what most of the non-mages thought.

She was only following a script.

“I assume the Grand Auction will act as guarantor for these manuscripts?” the man continued. “That they are as effective as advertised and have been legally authorized for sale?”

Silence settled over the auction house. Everyone stared at the woman. She stood stiffly, ears twitching wildly as someone continued speaking to her.

“I… we of the Grand Auction can—”

“It would be a shame if the notes turned out to be fraudulent,” the man interrupted. “Or worse, stolen.”

He stepped to the edge of his balcony, hands clasped behind his back. It was then that Veronica noticed someone standing behind him.

Harris. The arrogant mage appraiser from the Starfault Archives.

This must be his master, she thought. Apple doesn’t fall far from the tree, it seems.

“It might have been overlooked if this concerned only a second-tier conversion,” the man continued. “But now that you have presented a third-tier conversion as well, I have concerns that the consignor may have stolen these manuscripts and intends to profit from another mage’s work.”

A wave of murmurs washed through the auction house.

Many of the mages below began whispering among themselves. The VIP sections were no different, with representatives from each commerce group turning to private conversations. Even Lord Vaun leaned back to speak with one of his aides.

“Unless you can guarantee these manuscripts are authentic and not stolen,” the man continued, “I worry for the reputation of the Grand Auction if you anger the original author—or force the Mage Association to intervene.”

He paused, allowing the threat to settle over the room. “That is, unless you can reveal the name of the consignor.”

“Veronica,” Nasha said, leaning forward.

“I know,” Veronica replied. “He’s trying to bait my identity out.”

“Is he allowed to do that? Are they allowed to do that?” Gwen whispered, glancing between Veronica and the man.

“No,” Veronica said. “But he’s using the Mage Association’s reputation to pressure the auction house into revealing my name. It’ll be difficult for them to ignore a threat like that.”

“But… why? I know your notes are valuable, but the first manuscript didn’t even sell for forty thousand vix.”

“It’s not about the notes,” Nasha cut in. Her eyes had narrowed, fixed on the man across the hall.

“What do you mean?”

“He doesn’t want the notes,” Veronica answered instead. Her gaze remained on the balcony. “He wants me.”
