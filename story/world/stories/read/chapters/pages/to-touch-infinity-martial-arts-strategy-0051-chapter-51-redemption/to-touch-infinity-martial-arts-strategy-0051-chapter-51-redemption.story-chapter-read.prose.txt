The trip inside the supply wagon was more tumultuous than Chae-ha had expected. Almost constant jostling blurred together with a slow, repetitive rattling of the wheels, and every few moments it felt like the vehicle intentionally hit a large bump. Each and every motion caused a dull warning ache to flare up in her side.

After five more agonizing days of being confined to her room in the infirmary, the young Inspector was finally well enough to travel. Fairly soon, the injury in her side would be reduced to nothing but a scar, alongside a lingering tightness that would slowly improve with training.

Marching outside the supply cart was the captain of her newly appointed personal guard, Noh Gu-shim. His eyes constantly scanned the tree line with the focus of a man who was giving his all to an assignment. The other three squad members marched behind, ready to deal with anything that might come their way.

When the small group of travelers finally stopped for a short rest break, Mu-cheon poked his head inside the wagon. “Are you doing okay in there, little sister? Do you need us to slow down?”

“The Meng-shan aren’t going to slow their march to accommodate my stitches, senior brother,” Chae-ha responded, although her face looked green. “Don’t you dare slow down because of me.”

As their journey continued, Elder Eom Tae-seok rode in silence, his remaining good eye fixed firmly on the path ahead. As the mountain road rounded one last, steep cliffside, the thick canopy of the Laiwu timberland vanished. The path opened up into a man-made clearing buzzing with industrial activity.

Chae-ha paused as she exited the wagon, taking in the sheer scale of what was being built on her behalf.

The hundreds of lumberjacks, stonemasons, and engineers who had been press-ganged into service had apparently been busy; in just a few weeks, they had successfully erected an outline of their new defenses. The skeletal form of a new gatehouse stood right in the center of a newly created glade. The men had already finished clear-cutting almost all of the trees, although a few hundred scattered stumps still needed to be removed. The man-made clearing spanned all the way from a sheer rock wall west of the road to a deadly mountain ravine to the east of it.

The scent of fresh cut pine filled the air. Extending outwards from either side of the gatehouse, a long timber-and-stone wall was forming, as if it was slowly rising from the earth. Groups of laborers worked under the watchful gaze of Tai-shan swordsmen, digging up stumps, carrying stones, and dragging massive logs through the mud. As a final touch, lines of abatis – sharpened wooden spikes made from small tree trunks – were being arranged in a pattern outside the walls.

“It’s coming along well,” Eom Tae-seok commented, alongside a grim nod of approval. “Once archers are stationed up top, this place will be a deathtrap.”

“If it’s finished in time,” Chae-ha added quietly.

“Inspector! Young Master!”

Vanguard Commander Shin hurried towards them from the center of the gatehouse scaffolding. He looked hollowed out, as much from the hard work as from the responsibilities of command. His oxblood silks were dirty and stained with sweat, and violet shadows hung beneath his bloodshot eyes.

“Commander,” Chae-ha greeted him with a polite nod. “The scale of the construction is impressive. It can’t have been easy holding everything together.”

“We are running the crews in three overlapping shifts, twenty-four hours a day,” Shin panted, wiping a layer of sawdust and sweat from his forehead. “The abatis layout will be finished by dusk. The primary gatehouse structure is stable. But look at the stone mortar on the lower tiers, Inspector. It hasn’t even dried yet. We need another week, at minimum, before this place will be defensible. Preferably, ten days.”

Chae-ha’s gaze drifted past the wall, looking down the long, empty valley stretching towards the south.

“It will take at least three more days for the Meng-shan to capture the mine. But once they do arrive, and find no garrison there, they will realize we sacrificed it deliberately to buy time. At that point, there are two possible scenarios that could play out: one, they decide to prioritize the mine anyways. They remain defensive, establish their supply lines, and entrench a proper garrison. In which case, they will be unable to make any further moves for several weeks.”

“Or two, they get worried about the deception, determine we’re up to something, and attack immediately – hoping to preempt whatever it is,” Commander Shin finished for her.

“Exactly. In that scenario, they arrive here in about six days.”

“That’s cutting it close. Isn’t there anything else we can do to buy another few days?”

“Not without affecting the retreat path of our decoy column. Let’s wait a few more days, and see what the construction progress looks like, before deciding just how desperate a strategy we should employ.”

“Speaking of the decoy column, a bird arrived about an hour ago. The two hundred men we sent are nearly at the junction.”

“Perfect. Thank you, Commander.”

“They are getting closer and closer to an engagement by the hour. Our boys are about to make contact with an army that outnumbers them five to one. This decoy strategy of yours had better work, Inspector. Because if the Meng-shan realize those two hundred men are a distraction before this wall is dry, we are all going to die in this ditch.”

“Don’t worry, Commander. They won’t. And even if they do, I have a backup plan. So for now, let’s just wait and see everything unfolds.”

Chae-ha wondered how Commander Shin would react if she told him that her backup plan involved sacrificing those two hundred men to buy another day. He’d seemed perfectly happy to throw lives away under the orders of Commander Mok, but somehow, she doubted he would react positively to her doing it.

It didn’t matter. The most likely outcome was still that, when the Meng-shan saw the fortifications, they would simply refuse to engage. They wouldn’t charge recklessly; they would turn around, leave, and prepare a counter strategy.

After all, it’s not like they had any way to tell how dry the mortar was.

The beat of approaching hooves rose above the synchronized stomping of the marching soldiers. Lesser Elder Wi Mo reined in his black stallion, his hand instinctively raising his fan to protect his face as a panicked scout skidded to a halt right in front of him, throwing up a cloud of dust.

“Elder! The Tai-shan column has been sighted!” the scout panted, executing an awkward bow from his saddle. “We counted at least two hundred. They’re about ten li from the junction.”

Wi Mo’s eyes shifted towards the horizon. The crossroads they were aiming for – the vital three-way junction that led east to the Deer Valley mine and north towards Laiwu – was near. If the Tai-shan claimed that intersection first, they could anchor a defensive line and stall his entire army.

“Pass an order to the captains,” the strategist commanded, snapping his fan shut. “Tell the men to burn their caloric reserves. Double-time! I want every spearman running, if necessary. We have to take the junction before the Tai-shan even see the clearing!”

The troops groaned – but obeyed. The noise grew louder and more disorganized as they sped up their march. But Wi Mo’s ruthlessness paid off. After a few more hours, the Meng-shan column was spilling into the rocky expanse of the crossroads, completely uncontested.

"Establish a defensive line! Immediately!" Wi Mo commanded from atop his horse. "Dig in, brace yourselves, and set your spears! And…bring the machine to the front!"

Six oxen strained, mooing in protest as they dragged the enormous siege ballista into position at the center of the newly established line. Its payload was aimed directly down the empty northern approach. Wi Mo smiled, imagining the look on the Tai-shan general’s face when he marched around that bend, straight into the path of a weapon that could punch through a fortress wall.

His reverie was interrupted by a second scout, who had descended from the ridges overlooking the northern path. The man looked deeply confused.

“Elder...per your orders, we took a closer look at the approaching column,” the scout reported hesitantly. “Neither the Tai-shan Young Master nor the one-eyed master seem to be with them. The group appears to be commanded by a regional captain.”

Wi Mo immediately stopped moving, his fan still half-open. That…didn’t make any sense.

The strategist’s mind raced, pouring over every possible explanation he could conjure up. Why would the Tai-shan send troops to protect a vital intersection without sending their martial powerhouses?

The answer hit him like a lightning bolt, bringing instant clarity to his thoughts.

They were late.

The Tai-shan must have realized they couldn’t mobilize their main force in time to protect the intersection. In a panic, they had scrambled a light, fast detachment of ordinary disciples to rush ahead and try to claim the crossroads, while their main force lagged behind. Given that the scouts had seen no sign of them, that larger force must be at least a day away still.

But their ploy had failed; Wi Mo had taken the crossroads first anyways. And his actual target – the Deer Valley mine, less than a day’s march to the east – was now completely isolated. Its garrison was permanently cut off from the main Tai-shan army.

Wi Mo’s chest swelled with a predatory thrill. Two hundred greatswordsmen were no threat; even if they threw themselves against the Meng-shan line, it would easily hold. Meanwhile, he could take a small strike force, march east, slaughter the isolated garrison at the mine, and be back before the dawn.

Turning to his second-in-command, the Lesser Elder pointed his fan towards the barricades. "Hold this line with your lives. The Tai-shan are desperate; an assault is imminent. But they are badly outnumbered, and we are entrenched. Just stay here, and whatever you do – do not break formation."

Wi Mo whirled his stallion around and sent his lieutenants to gather up a group of three hundred spearmen. It wasn’t long before they were all arrayed along the path heading east towards the mine.

“With me! It’s time to claim our prize.”

As the strike force moved out, Wi Mo couldn’t help but feel pleased. Strategy, he reflected, always came down to a question of timing. And his timing today had been flawless. They had secured the junction. They had capitalized on a magnificent stroke of luck in the Tai-shan’s complete lack of coordination. And now, they were about to claim the prize they had come all this way to take, having barely spilled any of their own blood.

After an hour of constant marching, the twisting path through the mountain woods finally began to level out. Wi Mo and his strike force rounded a sharp stone bend, exposing a long, straight section of the road directly ahead.

The Lesser Elder pulled hard on his reins, his stallion letting out a sharp whinny as it skidded to a halt.

The Elder’s jaw dropped. He stared ahead, his smug composure collapsing into bewilderment.

The road was blocked.

In front of his strike force, stretching across the entire width of the narrow valley path, lay a chaotic jumble of felled lumber. Dozens of trees had been dropped haphazardly across the path, their overlapping branches interlocking to form an impenetrable wooden wall. It clearly wasn't a natural collapse; the cuts on the trunks looked clean and deliberate. Clearing a barricade of this scale would take his men hours of backbreaking physical labor.

Why would the Tai-shan do this? Wi Mo’s mind reeled. Wait. How could it even be the Tai-shan? Their column hadn’t reached the crossroads yet!

He stared at the arrangement of fresh-cut pine, the scent of sap filling his nose. It made no sense. Unless...

A sneer slowly grew on his lips as he figured it out. Of course! The garrison.

The pathetic, terrified dogs stationed at the mine must have received a bird, warning them that the Tai-shan reinforcements were running late. Realizing they were helpless against the oncoming storm, the miners and guards had panicked. With no other ideas for how to save their own skins, they had tried to sabotage the road, fervently praying that their saviors would arrive before the Meng-shan would be able to clear the path.

Wi Mo looked up at the position of the sun, estimating the remaining daylight. There was still enough time.

“Send runners to the supply train. Have them bring back axes!” the strategist commanded, pointing his fan at the obstruction. “And when they get back, start chopping! I want a path cleared through these logs by mid-afternoon!”

He settled back into his saddle, staring coldly at the barricade. He was going to break through their pathetic little wall, march up to that mine, and show those frightened little fools that their fears were indeed warranted.

This engagement wasn’t going to be a battle.

It was going to be his redemption.
