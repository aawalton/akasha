One hour before the dusk of the yellow sun, we arrived at our staging ground.

It was a spot Raik had picked after doing some research and asking Captain Osar for advice. The hill rose above the surrounding terrain, its sides rough and uneven on three of the four approaches, making it naturally defensible against ground assaults, at least to some degree. The fourth side sloped more gently, but even there the climb was rough and slow. It was especially hard for Shingo and me, and even Vena’s robes hampered her mobility a bit. Ja’a decided to skip climbing altogether and fly… cheater.

At the very top stood a lone sequoia tree, standing firm like a patient sentinel.

It felt old in the way only certain trees do, its trunk wide enough that five people linking arms would struggle to wrap around it. The branches stretched outward, needles whispering softly in the breeze. I couldn’t say whether it had been planted here intentionally or if it had simply survived long enough to claim the hill as its own, but its presence made the place feel more majestic.

The boys started making camp almost immediately.

Katar and Calr began clearing the ground, stamping down grass and removing loose stones, already mapping out where tents and the bonfire would go. Raik circled the perimeter, eyes sharp, checking sightlines and approaches.

Ja’a had Shingo help unpack the ritual supplies under Vena’s supervision. Mostly stakes and a coil of rope, as well as a bag of sand and a large wooden basin, or maybe a kneading tray. It looked like the platter you’d use to serve couscous in a communal setting, flat and large enough to feed ten people.

I approached Vena, who stood and slowly turned in place as she examined the site.

“Does this place work well for your ritual?” I asked.

She nodded without hesitation.

Then she turned toward Raik. “Is this spot temporary,” she asked, “or are we going to spend a lot of days here?”

“Does it matter?” Raik replied.

She folded her hands in front of her, thoughtful. “I can perform a simple ritual for a temporary shelter, or go for something stronger and more permanent.”

Raik considered that, then nodded slowly. “That depends on whether the intel I got is right. There should be a few ruined villages a couple of hours from here, an old mine, and a lake. All of them could be interesting to explore.”

Calr straightened and turned toward me. “Can I borrow your spyglass?”

“Right, here you go.” I handed it to him without hesitation.

He climbed the tree like a spider monkey; his agility had improved a lot since acquiring his Soulbook. He even double-jumped a few times to reach a higher elevation. Bracing himself against a branch, he peered through the lens, taking his time as he checked the western horizon. After a moment, he shifted position, relocating from branch to branch, and looked again northward, eastward, then southward.

He descended as quickly and gracefully as he had climbed.

“It’s foggy,” he said, handing me back the spyglass, “but I can confirm the lake to the west and at least two of the three villages. Southwest and north.”

Raik exhaled. “Okay then. I guess we’re staying here for a few days.”

Vena nodded once and got to work.

We joined Ja’a, who had already finished unpacking the supplies with Shingo’s help. Shingo left to help Katar finish the camp, and Raik and Calr joined them shortly after.

“Can I help you with anything?” I asked Vena.

“Not with the initial phase,” Vena answered. “I will enact a temporary sanctified space. You can help me with the ritual to upgrade it to a permanent one.”

I watched as Vena selected seven wooden poles and began planting them into the ground, spacing them evenly around the hilltop. The pattern formed an even-sided septagon, wide enough to encompass the main camp area while still leaving room for movement and defense. Once the poles were secure, she connected them with rope, tying careful knots at each junction. The structure looked like a fence, serving more as a symbolic barrier than a physical one, kind of like the keep-out police tape at a crime scene.

She went back to where the supplies were unpacked and picked up a bag of assorted items. She advanced to the first pole and knelt beside it.

She rummaged in the bag and pulled out a carved wooden ball. No, not a ball, a fruit carved out of wood, a peach, if I wasn’t mistaken. Using some rope and impressive knot work for a pure maiden, she turned it into a bola-like charm and attached it to the pole. Then she spoke, her voice calm and steady, carrying easily in the open air.

“Oh, Lady Laurel, grant us safety.”

At the name, the peach glowed faintly. The glow transferred to the nearest pole, then traveled through the rope, jumping from pole to pole, each one glowing for a moment before briefly fading.

That display made everyone pause to watch the pretty lights, forgetting their work for a bit as they focused on what Vena would do next.

The cleric stood and stepped clockwise to the next pole. This time, she placed a wooden carving shaped like the sun. It was attached with a cord like a pendant. It wasn’t too expensive, but it was clearly crafted with care.

“Lady Sofia, grant us light.”

The light display repeated with the same intensity.

At the next pole, she placed a wooden crescent moon pendant, warmth spreading through the wood.

“Lord Kent, grant us dependability.”

The glow effect continued.

Next came a book.

“Lady Sarah, grant us wisdom.”

Then a sheer veil.

“Lady Mary, grant us compassion.”

Then, a wooden dagger, more like a child’s toy.

“Lord Kain, grant us decisiveness.”

Each name caused a brief pulse of holy light to travel through the rope. The camp felt subtly different with each invocation, as if the air were being gently tuned, or gravity running lighter.

At the final pole, she placed two white feathers, attaching them symmetrically to either side so they looked like wings. Then she spoke the last name.

“Lady Henrietta, protect us from the undead.”

The reaction was immediate and unmistakable.

The rope flared brighter than before, the light lingering longer, sinking deeper into the poles and the ground beneath them. I felt it even without any holy sensitivity, a pressure in the air, firm and resolute.

I wasn’t sure whether it was because Henrietta had died fighting the undead, or because her name was spoken last, but that final invocation definitely carried more weight. The effect was stronger, clearer, and more decisive.

Vena lowered her hands slowly, breathing out as the ritual settled into place. The glow faded, but the presence remained, an invisible boundary drawn with faith and intent.

“Are you done? And why the number seven? Seven poles, seven sides, seven saints. Did Henrietta’s name provide more power?” I asked quickly.

“No, this is just a temporary sanctified miracle,” she replied. “I can answer your questions while we work on making it permanent. Come, follow me. We have a lot more work to do.”

We started with the bag of sand. Vena told me to get it while she brought the large wooden basin closer. She nodded for me to open the bag. When I untied it, I realized the sand inside wasn’t the fine, powdery construction sand I was expecting. This was thick, grainy beach sand, uneven and rough, with tiny shell fragments mixed in.

“Pour some in,” Vena said.

I tipped the bag and emptied about a quarter of it into the basin. The sand landed with a dull, gritty sound, forming an uneven mound. Vena then added water from a skin, pouring slowly until about an inch covered the surface.

She gestured for me to start stirring.

As I worked my hands through the mixture, the water quickly turned brown, muddy, and opaque, thick with silt and fine debris. The sand felt abrasive against my fingers, scraping slightly as I agitated it.

“Why seven?” I asked, taking advantage of the moment. “Seven poles, seven sides, seven saints. Why not eight?”

Vena smiled faintly. “Seven is a good number.”

“That’s it?”

She shrugged. “Seven realms. Seven legendary Holy weapons. Seven Grand Temples. It’s a number that repeats in the structure of the world. It feels… stable.”

I frowned. “And six?”

She didn’t even hesitate. “Definitely worse.”

“Is the fact that six gods are leading the pantheon influencing this designation?”

She chuckled. “I don’t know what you mean. The Holy religion has no ill will toward the pantheon.”

I opened my mouth to push her further, but she closed her eyes and placed her hands over the basin before I could speak.

She cast her cleansing miracle with a small whispered prayer.

Golden light flowed down from her palms, sinking into the murky water. The effect was immediate as the brown water began to separate, impurities rising to the surface like smoke trapped in liquid, while the sand beneath grew visibly brighter. Within seconds, the water above turned clear, while everything unwanted simply disappeared, vanished from existence.

I guess miracles can say fuck you to the conservation of mass principle.

Vena agitated the sand once more, and the water remained clear, making sure the sand was clean. She started scooping it from the basin and spreading it across a tarp she had laid out earlier. I joined her. Vena directed me carefully, making sure the layer was even and not too thick so it would dry properly.

“Again,” she said.

We repeated the process. Sand, water, stirring, cleansing, and drying. Each cleaning produced more clean sand until the bag was empty.

As we worked, I asked, “Did Henrietta’s invocation actually provide more power?”

Vena hesitated. “It’s possible,” she said slowly. “Saints tied closely to a domain sometimes resonate more strongly when that domain is directly involved. Henrietta died fighting the undead. That struggle shaped her faith and sacrifice.”

She glanced at the poles. “But ritual placement and sequence matter too. Final invocations often feel stronger because they seal what came before. I can’t say for sure which mattered more.”

“I thought the faith was all about the Holy flow, yet your society is idolizing individuals. Isn’t that a contradiction?” I asked.

“Not really, no. All those individuals dedicated their lives to the community. They are just examples of a one-for-all approach rather than an all-for-one.”

As we worked, she smiled to herself. “I remember that I was helping Lady Sana create sanctified sand when we first met. Now you are helping me.”

I blinked.

That memory surfaced instantly. Sana is inviting me to the temple, and Vena is in the office, cleaning sand. Then another detail clicked into place.

“There was a sand jar at the entrance of the temple,” I said slowly. “Some kind of ritual everyone was doing? I have never seen it again after that first day.”

Vena nodded. “Yes. Spirit ablution.”

She continued spreading the wet sand on the tarp. “Once a month, people with a Faithful class are allowed one wish. We do it at the temple on the days after a new moon. But before wishing, it is recommended to do a spirit ablution.”

“Oh, that happens every month? But I’ve only seen it one time,” I said.

“Well, it’s not like you spend that much time in the temple,” she said in a somewhat reproachful way. I had a feeling it wasn’t on purpose. I guess deep down she wanted me to convert and become a cleric like her, but that wasn’t my path. I decided to refocus the conversation.

“So a wish. What does that entail?” I asked.

“It has to be small,” she said. “And not greedy. The Holy doesn’t answer requests that seek to take from others or distort fate.”

“What kind of wishes do people ask for?”

She spoke calmly, as if this were common knowledge. “Most women ask for a more manageable monthly cycle, or better fertility if they are trying to conceive. Men usually ask for luck or strength. Knights in training might ask for courage or resolve.”

“Does it always work?”

She shook her head. “No. But it helps… a little.”

It made sense. From what I understood, the Faithful people were mostly dependent on knights, clerics, and justicars for their miracles.

“What did you ask for?” I asked. “Before you made it to cleric, I mean.”

She paused.

“I didn’t,” she said.

I stopped spreading sand and looked at her. “Never?”

“I always offered thanks,” she replied. “I never asked for anything.”

I blinked.

Somehow, I should have expected it. I thought about how she managed to ascend that young. How naturally the Holy miracle responded to her. How she never seemed to hesitate or doubt her faith.

I frowned, half joking, half serious. “You know, that sounds suspiciously like saint behavior.”

She laughed softly, shaking her head. “That’s not what the people of Hano are saying. Don’t try to pass that title on to me.”

The boys were already done with the camp by the time we finished cleaning the sand, and it was already getting dark. A small bonfire provided light, along with a few mana lights spread around the camp.

We took a few-minute break to join the team for dinner.

The meal was simple. Dried fruit and strips of jerky passed around as we watched the darkening landscape, weapons kept close at hand. This was the first time anyone had tested the Sanctify Miracle in the undead dead zone, so we were prepared for things to turn sour. This was one of the shorter nights, after Fireday, so only six hours of darkness, which had been accounted for in our plan. We were far more likely to hold defensively for six hours if the miracle failed.

Luckily for us, the miracle didn’t fail.

We were completely ignored by the forming horde of skeletons heading east toward Ectamel. The camp was sanctified, if only temporarily. Still, we didn’t rest on our laurels and decided to keep watch just in case. It was mostly Raik, Calr, Shingo, and Katar splitting the watch, as the rest of us girls were busy making the ritual permanent.

That’s right, Ja’a and Kan joined us so we could finish faster, especially since the rest was mostly busy work and didn’t require faith to perform.

“Oh,” Kan said, glancing at the tarp and the pale sand spread across it. “You’re making sanctified sand.”

Vena blinked. “I didn’t know you were familiar with the Holy faith.”

“My uncle’s wife is Faithful,” Kan explained. “I spent some time with them after my mother died, before my older sister was old enough to take custody of me.”

The word she used for uncle was the one reserved for a maternal uncle. The common language here had two distinct words depending on which side of the family you meant.

We moved on to the next step once we made sure the sand was dry enough.

The four of us poured sand into small cloth bags, careful not to spill any. Once filled, Vena instructed me to write glyphs into them. One side bore the Holy glyph for evil, the other the glyph for begone. The work was slow and repetitive, and we worked as we talked.

Mostly about light things. Favorite foods, small happy memories, and old friends. Ja’a and I did most of the talking, probably because we had the most normal childhoods of the four of us, despite me being a literal alien in this world. Kan and Vena had rougher pasts, and I could feel them appreciating the distraction, even if neither said it outright.

Ja’a talked about life with a father who had a large harem of wives, growing up with sixteen sisters, and a father desperately trying and failing to get a son.

I kept my own stories vague, focusing on events that didn’t betray my Earth origin, mostly drama about university politics.

Once the bags were finished, we tied them to the rope fence, spacing them about twenty centimeters apart. They swayed gently in the breeze, pale against the darkening grass.

The remaining sand was scattered by Vena across the camp, spreading it like a farmer sowing seeds. When she was done, Vena stepped back to the center of the septagon.

She raised her hands and began to pray again.

This time, she didn’t name saints.

She named the Holy cities. I didn’t hear the full prayer, but I caught Helm’s Peak, Kain’s Crown, and Virgin’s Veil, the respective capitals of the three Holy kingdoms.

Holy places that had withstood the test of time, each city existing for at least two thousand years.

As she prayed, another glow spread.

It traveled from the poles, through the rope, and into the ground itself. The light rippled across the hilltop, sinking into the soil, spreading toward the center. Even the sequoia tree reacted, its bark faintly illuminated, its needles glowing softly for a brief moment before fading.

The hill felt different afterward. Familiar, or maybe safer. I couldn’t describe the feeling exactly. It was like getting the same hotel room on your second visit to a city, no longer completely unfamiliar, but still not home.

“That should hold,” she said.

“So it’s permanent now?” I asked.

“It’s as permanent as a brick house is permanent compared to a tent.” She yawned, unable to fully mask the fatigue.

We had spent half the short night working on the ritual. The boys had already rotated through the watch twice, so we decided to get some sleep, and both Ja’a and Kan had gone down to sleep one hour earlier.

I looked once toward the sky, hoping to see the stars. Sadly, the fog made visibility terrible. I slipped into the tent I shared with Ja’a for some much-needed rest.

I woke up fully rested, which was a bad sign. That meant I had overslept.

Ja’a was already gone. I left the tent and immediately saw that the blue sun was well above the horizon, maybe four hours past dawn.

Only Calr was in the camp, standing just inside the sanctified rope fence and watching northward. He didn’t look particularly worried, so I figured the rest of the team couldn’t be far.

“Where is everyone?” I asked.

“Vena is still asleep in her tent,” Calr replied. “The rest of the team went out to clear the surrounding monsters. Ja’a detected a few nearby aberrations that weren’t moving toward Ectamel, and Raik decided it would be better to deal with them now, just in case.”

“Shouldn’t he have taken the whole team?” I asked.

Calr shrugged. “Nah. They’re between ten and twenty SB. Shouldn’t pose much of a challenge.”

I pulled out my spyglass and scanned the foggy landscape. The mist made it hard to see clearly, but after a moment, I spotted movement a couple of kilometers away. I thought they were fighting some kind of suit of armor, or maybe an armored zombie. It was hard to tell at this distance.

Still, it didn’t look like it posed much of a threat. Kan appeared to be holding it in place while Shingo repeatedly bashed it with his hammer.

They would be gone for at least an hour.

That left me with time to kill.

I considered heading to the Sunless Reach to practice with star mana, or maybe hopping toward Ectamel to look for fresh supplies. Then I glanced at Calr. Leaving him alone on watch felt rude, especially when he was already stuck babysitting the camp.

My eyes drifted to the campfire, still warm from the night watch, and an idea clicked into place.

Why not cook lunch?

I checked my bag of holding and took stock of what I had left. Fresh apples, tomatoes, fire roots, cabbage, a couple of dozen eggs, dried meat, and bread that was already going stale.

The tomatoes wouldn’t last much longer, and neither would the eggs. The bread was on borrowed time, too. If I didn’t use them now, they would just go to waste.

Luckily for me, Manal had taught me exactly what to do with this kind of ingredient list.

I set a pot over the campfire and poured in some oil, then sliced the fire root thin and tossed it in; those worked here as a substitute for onions and garlic. I stirred until the root softened and browned, releasing a sharp, aromatic smell.

Once it was ready, I added a tablespoon of a local spice blend that smelled like black pepper mixed with cumin. Then I added the tomatoes to the pot and crushed them with a fork, stirring everything together.

At this point, you might think I was making shakshuka.

I wasn’t. This was its sadder cousin, shtiteha, which roughly translates to little dancer. With a generous amount of water added, the tomato paste turned into a thin, savory sauce, closer to a watered-down marinara.

I tasted it and immediately facepalmed.

Salt. I had forgotten the salt.

I added a generous amount, then kept stirring as I waited for the mixture to come to a boil. I had picked this recipe because it was easy to make and didn’t require precise heat control, perfect for cooking over a campfire.

Once the red liquid began to bubble, I cracked the eggs into a bowl one by one, giving each a quick sniff to make sure none had gone bad. Then I gently slipped them into the sauce to poach, spaced evenly apart. I put the lid on the pot and let them cook.

This dish worked because the tomato's natural umami took time to seep into the egg whites. Overcooking the yolks wasn’t a big problem either, since the acidity and moisture of the sauce balanced out the dryness. The excess sauce also doubled as a dipping base for the stale bread, making it far more palatable.

It wasn’t a perfect meal by any means.

But it was savory, filling, rich in protein, and it used up almost all of my perishable ingredients. The only thing left was the cabbage, and honestly, I wasn’t losing any sleep over that. I wasn’t a cabbage kind of girl anyway.

I stirred the pot once more and glanced toward the foggy horizon, hoping the team would smell it on their way back.

Vena woke up next, drawn by the smell. I served her a bowl along with some bread, toasted bread to make it less chewy.

When the rest of the team arrived, I served each of them a bowl with three poached eggs, except for Shingo, who got six. The mute half-giant thanked me with a wide smile.

“This tastes good for a peasant meal,” Ja’a laughed. “Chef Sisso would gouge his eyes out if he saw me eating this.”

“You should pass the recipe to the temple soup kitchens,” Vena said. “This would be a nice addition to the menu.”

I knew she meant it as a compliment, but it still sounded a little backhanded.

“So,” I asked Raik, “what did you find nearby?”

He showed me five monster cores, each the size of a billiard ball.

“This place is amazing for climbing the guild ranks,” he said. “Each of those would count as a mission-level challenge.”

“Does that mean we’re not going to explore the ruined villages?” I asked, almost disappointed.

“Of course we are,” he grinned. “We are here to see what secrets this land holds.”

I couldn’t help but smile at the call to adventure.
