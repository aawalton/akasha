Vir stared at the expanding cloud of darkness looming over the street mere blocks away, unable to understand why it still existed.

His gamble had been simple. Show the Fateweavers a future where the Prana Swarm destroyed everything, forcing them to revert to a timeline without it. And he’d been proven right. Reality had reset, Amara’s plan was restored, and the Vimana had returned, even if they’d returned to some point in the future. When they’d left, Amara’s Vimana launch was some months away.

The question was, why had the Prana Swarm come back too?

“It doesn’t make sense,” Vir muttered, watching the creature devour a marketplace, the fresh produce wilting before their eyes as their prana was sucked dry. The humans who’d been in the streets at the time had run at the first sign of the arcane terror, but it would not be long before the Swarm claimed more victims.

Maiya pulled him aside as panicking citizens fled past them. “Maybe they think its presence will foil Amara’s plan?”

“But that’s bound to happen anyway,” Vir said. “We know that Amara’s compromised. They wouldn’t have allowed this timeline to exist if her plan had any chance of succeeding.”

“Seems likely,” Maiya muttered. “You think it’s her? Or her people?”

“I think it might be all of them,” Vir said. “We know that’s how they work. Amara probably believes she has a real chance, and that belief is exactly what makes her predictable to them.”

Maiya’s face went pale. “Then we’re back where we started. Except now there’s also a Prana Swarm eating the city.”

Vir watched the Swarm pulse and grow, consuming prana from every living thing it touched. Which, in an Imperium city, meant the buildings too. Spires collapsed and people screamed, fleeing the destruction. And through it all, the Vimana continued its lumbering ascent, Amara’s grand plan proceeding exactly as the Fateweavers wanted it to.

“Maybe it wasn’t intentional at all.”

“How could it not be intentional?” Maiya asked. “They control Fate itself.”

“They influence it,” Vir said. “But we both know they’re not omnipotent. Nobody knows how Prana Swarms even exist. All we know is they consume energy continuously. What if it’s outside their influence? What if they couldn’t erase it?”

It was the only thing that explained its continued existence. The Fateweavers clearly did not want a rampaging Prana Swarm, or they wouldn’t have rewritten reality.

Maiya stared at him. “You’re saying we accidentally created something they can’t control?”

“I’m saying we might have a chance,” Vir said, already moving. “Shan! Help anyone trapped by the quakes! Get them to safety! We’ll draw the Swarm.”

Shan barked an order to the others, and the wolves bounded off without hesitation, their forms blurring as they dove into the chaos.

Vir turned to Maiya. “We need to get to that Vimana before it’s out of reach.”

“And do what?”

“Stop Amara from wasting her one shot,” Vir said, black flames erupting around him as he activated Balancer of Scales. “And maybe get our foes to reset this reality again. To something better.”

He pulled Maiya into his arms and jumped.

Balancer of Scales flared to its maximum, making them both as light as a feather.

Buildings blurred past them, with only the wind slowing them.

But Vir hadn’t forgotten about the Swarm. It hungrily sucked up the trail of prana Vir left, following in tow.

On its own, Vir’s lightened weight would’ve proven insufficient to cross the gap to the Vimana, but he’d picked up a few tricks over the years. Tricks not even Cirayus could pull, owing to his lack of Ash Affinity.

Vir learned to trick the ability into thinking something other than the Earth was the ground, so long as it had enough Ash prana. And Vimana were nothing if not beacons of it, blazing like a sun.

Concentrating, Vir activated the ability again, boosting his weight. Except instead of falling to the ground, he and Maiya were pulled to the Vimana itself, landing on the Vimana’s exterior hull with a bone-jarring impact that would have shattered normal bodies. Vir cycled prana through his legs, absorbing the shock, then carried Maiya through the nearest access hatch he could see.

Inside, mejai walked with purpose in every direction, some toward defensive positions, while others headed for the command center and other stations. All of them had the hollow-eyed determination of people who knew they were about to die but had accepted it. Not one of them paid Vir and Maiya any mind.

Even if they hadn’t been compromised, they must have known what they were about to attempt—flying the Vimana into the Ash, if Vir’s guess was on point—was tantamount to suicide. Even if the Vimana’s shields protected them from the prana density, Vir wasn’t certain at all the Imperium structure could withstand the might of the beasts that roamed that realm.

They found Amara in the central command chamber, surrounded by her inner circle, all of them staring at a projection showing the Vimana’s collision course with the Ash Boundary.

“You shouldn’t be here,” Amara said without turning around. Her voice was cold, though she couldn’t hide the undercurrent of hopelessness in it. “Leave now, or you’ll doom everything we’ve worked for.”

“Your plan is already doomed,” Vir said.

That got her attention. She spun, and even through the fox mask, Vir could feel the intensity of her glare. “Explain.”

“You’ve been compromised,” Vir said, addressing not just Amara but everyone in the room. “You or your people are the unwitting agents of our enemy. They wouldn’t have allowed this timeline to proceed if your plan had any chance of succeeding. You know this.”

“That’s impossible,” one of the mejai said. “We’ve been careful. We’ve taken every precaution—”

“They don’t need to know your plans,” Vir interrupted. “They can see the future. They know whether you succeed or fail, and they shape reality to ensure the outcome they want. If you were a real threat, you’d already be dead. Or erased. Or you’d have never been born in the first place.”

Silence fell over the command chamber.

“Then what do you suggest?” Amara asked, her voice tight. “That we give up? Let our universe die?”

“No,” Vir said. “I suggest we change the game.”

He turned toward the nearest window, where the Prana Swarm was visible in the distance, a sphere of absolute darkness growing larger by the second.

“I’m going to make them an offer,” Vir said. “Either they let your plan succeed—whatever it actually is—or I feed that Swarm until it consumes everything. Including them.”

“You can’t negotiate with them!” Amara said. “They’re not—”

“I know what they are,” Vir said. “And I know they can hear me right now. Through you and through your people.”

He raised his voice, addressing the air itself.

“You wanted to preserve this universe by eradicating intelligent life. But we’ve given you a new problem. That Swarm out there? It came through your reset. It absorbed some of your power. And every time you rewrite reality to stop me, it’s going to absorb more.”

He extended his hand, and a torrent of Ash prana flowed toward the approaching Swarm.

The creature surged forward, doubling in size in seconds. It crashed into the Vimana’s shields, which flared under the assault.

“Stop!” Amara shouted. “You’ll destroy everything!”

“That’s the point,” Vir said calmly, still feeding the Swarm. For the longest time, they’d hid from the Fateweavers. That skyship had sailed long ago, and now that they knew their enemy couldn’t simply erase their existence, continuing to sneak around would only guarantee the end of this world, and them with it. “Either you let Amara succeed, or you watch me turn your universe into an apocalypse you can’t undo. Because eventually, it’ll be strong enough to threaten even you.”

The Swarm grew larger, its darkness beginning to consume the Vimana’s defenses. Lights flickered inside and ancient alarms blared.

Vir fed it more.

“Last chance,” he said to the air.

For a heartbeat, nothing happened.

And then the world began to unravel.

Reality twisted, colors bleeding into each other, sounds stretching and compressing. Vir felt the reset beginning—the universe preparing to rewrite itself again.

So they can listen, he thought. But whether it was his words or the rapidly growing Prana Swarm, he couldn’t say. Vir somehow got the impression these beings existed in a space outside of rational thought.

Regardless, this time, he was ready.

He looked at Amara, wanting to say something. To warn her about what was coming… and caught a glimpse of red beneath her mask.

Not the dull red of cloth or paint, but the vibrant, flaming red of hair he’d seen only once before, on a girl who’d changed everything for him.

“Mai—” he began.

The world went white.

Sensation returned gradually, and Vir felt solid ground beneath him. The smell of old parchment and dust was what he noticed next.

Vir opened his eyes to find himself standing in a library. Not the grand libraries of Alt Ashani, but something smaller and more intimate.

Maiya stood beside him, looking as disoriented as he felt. His Maiya.

Had that really been her? The her of another universe? Was that why she’d been so desperate to hide her face from him?

The thought had scarcely crossed his mind when he beheld the figure across from them, sitting in a worn chair with a book open on his lap. A man Vir had thought he’d never see.

Janak.

Not the grand prana projections of the god he’d always seen, but an old man of flesh and blood, looking haggard and spent. His long white hair and white beard were ragged, and his hands trembled slightly as he closed the book.

His eyes opened wide, shock and disbelief warring with something that might have been hope.

“You survived,” Janak croaked, his voice rough from disuse. “After all this time, you actually survived!”

Vir couldn’t speak. Couldn’t process what he was seeing.

“Where is—” Maiya began.

“Welcome to my prison,” Janak said, rising slowly, like every movement hurt. “I admit I had lost hope. After you disappeared in that world, I…”

He stopped, his gaze shifting past them to something behind.

His expression changed from hope to something else. Fear?

“And you’ve brought something with you,” Janak whispered.

Vir turned, and suddenly Janak’s reaction seemed understated.

For behind them was the very same Prana Swarm Vir had fed.

It had come through the reset once again, even stronger than before.

And if Vir understood the situation correctly, they’d left Amara’s universe behind entirely.

This wasn’t a universe controlled by them.

Which meant there would be no resets.

This time, there was nowhere to run.
