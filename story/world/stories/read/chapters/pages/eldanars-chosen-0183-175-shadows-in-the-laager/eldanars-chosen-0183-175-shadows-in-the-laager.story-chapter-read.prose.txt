The midnight frost settled over the refugee camp like a shroud, turning the muddy ruts of the frontier road into treacherous ridges of hardened iron. Within the great, circular laager of wagons, the fires had been allowed to die down to smouldering, ruby-red embers, casting long, wavering shadows across the huddled, sleeping forms of the survivors.

For Brett, the first four hours of the watch were a masterclass in slow, agonising torture.

He was a mage of no small talent, a scholar of the arcane arts whose natural habitat was a well-lit library with a crackling hearth and a cup of perfectly brewed spiced wine. He was not built for crouching behind the icy, splintered wheel of a merchant’s cart, his breath pluming in the freezing air, staring into a wall of impenetrable darkness.

"I swear to God," Brett muttered, pulling his thick, fur-lined cloak tighter around his shoulders, "if my toes fall off from frostbite, I’m charging Josh for a set of enchanted, self-warming boots."

A few paces to his left, leaning heavily against the side of the wagon, Bhel let out a low, gravelly snort. The dwarf seemed entirely unbothered by the plunging temperatures. He had one of his heavy axes resting head-down in the dirt, his thick, calloused hands resting on the pommel.

"Stop your whingeing, lad," Bhel rumbled, his eyes never leaving the treeline. "This is barely a brisk autumn evening. The cold keeps the blood pumping. Keeps the mind sharp."

"My mind is perfectly sharp, thank you, Bhel," Brett retorted, his teeth chattering slightly. He held up a hand, his fingers glowing with a faint, mesmerising blue light as he absent-mindedly traced the somatic components of a minor ward. "It's my physical form that objects to being treated like a side of beef left in an icehouse."

"Drink this," Carcan said softly, appearing from the shadows between the carts with the silent grace of an alley cat. She handed Brett a small, leather-wrapped flask. "It is not wine, but it is a herbal infusion that… just don’t drink too much of it. It will warm your core and keep the fatigue from settling in your bones."

Brett took the flask with numb fingers and took a tentative sip. The liquid tasted of crushed pine needles, bitter root, and raw honey, but as it hit his stomach, a bloom of genuine, radiating warmth spread through his chest. He sighed, a long plume of white vapour escaping his lips. "My eternal thanks, Carcan. You are the only civilised soul in this entire party."

Carcan offered a serene, albeit weary, smile and took up a position on Brett's right, her heavy staff resting comfortably at her hip.

For a long while, the three of them stood in silence, the quiet camaraderie between them a stark contrast to the profound, oppressive isolation of the dark plains. But as the first hour bled into the second, the silence of the night began to fracture.

It started subtly. A sharp snap of a dry branch from the dense pine forest to their east.

Brett stiffened, the blue light fading from his fingertips as he gripped the side of the cart. He peered into the gloom, but the trees were just a wall of jagged, black silhouettes against a slightly less black sky.

"Did you hear that?" Brett whispered.

"Aye," Bhel growled, his knuckles whitening on his axe. "Heavy tread. Too heavy for a fox or a badger."

Ten minutes later, it happened again. This time, it was a prolonged, erratic rustling, like something large dragging itself through a patch of dense, dead bracken. It sounded close—perhaps only fifty yards beyond the reach of their dimming firelight. Then, an abrupt, unnatural silence fell over the woods, as if the forest itself was holding its breath.

A profound jitteriness began to claw at Brett’s nerves. He was used to the chaotic, explosive violence of a dungeon delve or a pitched battle, where spells flew and steel clashed. This waiting—this blind, sensory deprivation while knowing something was out there—was infinitely worse. It was psychological warfare.

"I am going to cast a luminescent flare," Brett announced, his voice tight. "Just a quick burst. We need to see."

"No," Carcan cautioned gently, placing a hand on his forearm. "If there is a hunting pack out there, a sudden light will only blind us while highlighting our exact positions. Let the darkness work for us as much as it does for them."

"She’s right," Bhel agreed, though he sounded frustrated. "You throw a light, and you make yourself the biggest target on the frontier. If there’s anything out there, let's let them come to us."

So, they waited. For two more agonising hours, the forest played a cruel game with them. There would be a low, guttural cough that sounded almost, but not quite, human. Then, twenty minutes of silence. A sudden, violent shaking of a sapling on the edge of the treeline. Then, nothing. The wind would howl, masking any approaching footsteps, leaving them straining their ears until their heads ached.

Brett spent the time discreetly casting minor spells, lacing the mud thirty feet out from their position with barely visible flame traps, waiting to snap up the ankles of anything coming nearby. But nothing crossed them. When the moon finally dragged itself to its zenith, signalling the midpoint of the night, Brett felt physically drained, his magical reserves barely touched but his nervous system utterly frayed.

"Four hours," Carcan murmured, checking the position of the stars. "We should wake Josh and Perberos."

"Aye," Bhel spat, shifting his weight. "Let them deal with these dancing shadows. My axe is getting cold."

The three of them picked their way carefully back through the maze of wagons, stepping around the sleeping forms of the refugees. The stench of unwashed bodies and fear was thick in the enclosed space.

They found Josh and Perberos near Geth’s wagon. Neither man was asleep.

Josh was lying on his back, fully dressed in his padded gambeson and chainmail, staring blankly at the underside of the wagon above him. His heavy plate armour was piled neatly beside him, but his longsword was clutched in his right hand, resting across his chest. Across the dying fire, Perberos was sitting cross-legged in a meditative trance, but his eyes snapped open the second Bhel’s heavy boot crunched on the gravel.

"Time?" Josh asked, his voice rough with exhaustion. He sat up, rubbing his face with his free hand.

"Past midnight," Brett replied, dropping his staff into the dirt and collapsing onto a crate. "And I am officially resigning from nocturnal guard duty. I am entirely unfit for it."

"Ok," Josh said, ignoring the wizard’s complaints. He began the arduous process of strapping his heavy steel greaves over his boots, his movements stiff but practised.

"Something is out there," Carcan answered, her tone serious. "There is movement in the eastern treeline. We heard snapping branches, rustling undergrowth, and a few vocalizations that I could not identify. It sounds like a pack of some kind, but who knows if it's a pack of squirrels or something much bigger."

"Did anything break out of the treeline?" Josh asked, buckling his breastplate into place.

"No," Bhel grunted. "The cowards stayed in the dark. Pacing. Trying to spook us. It worked on the flameball."

"I was not spooked," Brett said indignantly, though he didn't put much effort into the lie. "I was tactically apprehensive. I was all set to light whatever it was up, but they must have known I was ready. Whatever is out there is keeping its distance for now."

Josh nodded slowly, grabbing his shield. He looked awful. The dark circles under his eyes rivalled those of the refugees, and the tight set of his jaw spoke of a man running entirely on adrenaline and sheer willpower. "Alright. Good work. Get some rest, all of you. You'll need it for tomorrow's march."

Perberos had already gathered his bow and quiver, his movements entirely silent. He didn't say a word, just offered a curt nod to the outgoing watch before vanishing into the shadows, heading straight for the eastern perimeter.

Josh took a deep breath, the freezing air burning his lungs, and followed his scout.

When Josh reached the edge of the laager, the psychological weight of the camp hit him anew. He was standing between more than a hundred terrified, exhausted civilians and a forest that stretched on into the unknown. If he failed, everyone behind him died. It was a suffocating pressure, one that made the biting cold feel almost insignificant.

Perberos was crouching behind a stack of spare wagon axles, his eyes fixed on the black wall of the pines. Josh knelt beside him, his plate armour clinking softly despite his efforts to be quiet.

"Talk to me, Perberos," Josh whispered.

"The others were right to be uneasy," the elven ranger murmured, his eyes glowing faintly in the starlight. "But they were listening with human—and dwarven—ears. They heard the noise, but they didn't hear the pattern. Carcan’s ears don’t count by the way, she was never any good at tracking, she’s basically human in that respect. Don’t tell her I said that though."

"What pattern?" Josh asked, ignoring the rest.

Perberos closed his eyes, tilting his head slightly, filtering out the sound of the wind, the creaking timber of the wagons, and the distant snores of the camp.

"A natural predator—a wolf, a bear, a mountain cat—moves with a specific rhythm," Perberos explained softly, imparting the ancient wisdom of his people. "It hunts, it tracks, and it strikes. If it is pacing, it is because it is confused or cornered. But the noises the others heard... they were deliberate. Synchronised."

Josh frowned, scanning the darkness. "Synchronised how?"

"A branch snapped to the north-east," Perberos said, pointing blindly into the dark. "And exactly three seconds later, a patch of bracken was disturbed to the south-east. The noises are designed to draw the eye, to keep the sentries looking at the most obvious threat. They are testing our sightlines, Josh."

A cold dread, entirely unrelated to the temperature, began to pool in Josh’s stomach. "They're probing our defences. Looking for the weak point."

"Precisely," Perberos said, his eyes snapping open. "And I think they've found it already."

For the next hour, Josh and Perberos stood in tense, agonizing silence. The forest continued its terrifying performance. The occasional heavy footstep, the phantom rustle of leaves. Josh could feel his heart hammering against his ribs, the exhaustion of the past week trying to drag his eyelids down, but the pure adrenaline of an impending attack kept him hyper-focused.

Then, the wind shifted.

Instead of blowing from the freezing north, it gusted from the south, sweeping across the length of the massive wagon circle.

Perberos’s nostrils flared. His pointed ears twitched sharply, and he turned to look in a completely different direction, abandoning the eastern treeline entirely. He dropped to one knee, pressing his ear against the frozen, muddy ground.

"What is it?" Josh demanded, keeping his voice to a bare whisper.

Perberos looked up, his face an unreadable mask of elven stoicism, but his eyes were blazing with deadly intent. "The noises in the woods are a feint. A distraction to keep the competent guards looking the wrong way."

"Where?" Josh asked, drawing his longsword, the steel whispering as it left the scabbard.

"South," Perberos said, already moving, slipping between the wagons like water over stones. "The southern side. Who’s got watch duty there now?"

Josh cursed violently under his breath and broke into a heavy, metallic jog, trying desperately to keep his footsteps light while moving as fast as his heavy armour allowed. They wove through the sleeping camp, dodging the dying embers of the central fires and the sprawling legs of exhausted draft horses.

The southern edge of the laager was furthest from the forest, facing out towards the open, frost-covered plains. It was supposed to be the safest direction, which was exactly why Josh had stationed the traumatised adventurers there.

As they approached the designated southern watchpost, Josh felt a sickening lurch in his gut.

The watchpost was located near a particularly large, heavy merchant’s wagon. Sitting on an overturned bucket, his head tipped back against the wagon wheel, was the adventurer Josh had argued with earlier—Kaelen. The man’s spear had slipped from his grasp, lying uselessly in the mud. His mouth was slightly open, a soft, rhythmic snoring escaping his lips.

Next to his spear lay an empty bottle of alcohol.

He had obviously drank too much and fallen asleep. The exhaustion, fear and the trauma had simply overridden his sense of duty. The southern flank was completely blind.

Josh gritted his teeth, stepping forward to kick the bucket out from under the man, to wake him with a furious reprimand.

But Perberos’s hand shot out, grabbing Josh’s steel-clad shoulder with a grip like a vice. The elf didn't speak. He just pointed upward.

Josh froze, his breath catching in his throat. He followed the line of Perberos’s finger, looking past the sleeping man, up to the thick canvas roof of the merchant's wagon.

Against the faint, starlit sky, silhouettes were moving.

They were crawling over the carts.

They were monstrous. From what Josh could see in the gloom, they were roughly the size of large hunting hounds, but their anatomy was horribly, terribly wrong. They possessed elongated, double-jointed limbs that allowed them to flatten their bodies almost entirely against the curvature of the canvas roofs. Their skin appeared hairless and slick, absorbing the starlight rather than reflecting it.

The most terrifying thing about them, however, was their absolute silence. They didn't have hooves or normal paws; their feet seemed to end in fleshy, splayed pads covered in microscopic, hook-like barbs that gripped the timber and canvas flawlessly.

Four of the beasts had already crested the wagon above the sleeping Kaelen. They moved with a disturbing, fluid synchronicity, like a swarm of terrible insects. They were slowly, painstakingly lowering their elongated necks, their eyeless, triangular heads swivelling blindly as they tasted the air, homing in on the scent of the sleeping refugee families huddled in the dirt just a few yards away.

They hadn't noticed Josh and Perberos in the shadows below. They were entirely focused on the vulnerable prey inside the circle.

One of the beasts, a particularly massive specimen with thick, whip-like tails, opened its maw. It didn't have teeth; it had rows of jagged, serrated bone-plates, dripping with a thick, viscous saliva that sizzled faintly as a drop hit the frozen canvas. It reared back on its hind legs atop the wagon, preparing to launch itself down into the camp.

There was no time to wake the others. No time to form a shield wall. The breach had already happened.

"Perberos," Josh breathed, his voice barely a vibration in the air, his grip on his shield tightening until his gauntlets creaked. "Take the one on the left. I'm going over the top."

The elven ranger didn't reply. In one impossibly smooth motion, he drew his bowstring back to his ear, an arrow already nocked and waiting for the kill.

The long, terrifying night was over. The bloodletting had begun.
