Hua was dealing with another bewildering realisation about mortals: they did not understand naming conventions.

She was seated with a furious Master Huang, at a Master Huang’s needlessly large table that had been served by a Master Huang’s now-embarrassed wife, and now Hua was being interrupted by a servant of Master Huang.

Hua raised her hand before the mortals could do something stupid like brawl in her presence. It was too hot and humid for that.

“Forgive this lord his ignorance, but is he not speaking to Master Huang?”

The servant of the other Master Huang smiled broadly, even as he casually had three guards hanging off him. “There are two merchants named who call the Western Reaches home named Huang, my Lord.”

Hua doubted there were only two Huangs across such a vast expanse of territory, but she was also unsure given that they were mortals and mortals, as she was continually reminded, were very strange creatures.

“One serves the Great Shang Huang, who runs the largest refineries within the honourable Liao’s territory and controls trade across the mountains. One’s master had hoped to provide you the services of his hospitality for your stay here.”

Hua wasn’t sure she wished to be led to another mortal. But the Master Huang before her looked fit to commit murder and she was curious if he would at being snubbed. Leaving him to simmer in his resentment might expedite that decision.

And she really wanted to be anywhere away from this Master Huang’s vile presence.

“Ah, of course. Wu Yan, this lord believes you can negotiate lodging with Master Huang for your people.” She glanced at the ostensibly lesser Huang and offered her most contrite smile. “This lord would consider it a personal favour should you  assist the shaman Yan in the matter of the mine.”

Huang the Lesser did not hide his fury at the snubbing. Not much Hua could do about that. She was not staying within a li of that man. They’d scale the plateau and make camp up there if it was necessary. Build boats and sleep on the lake. Climb the mountains. Anything and everything to not be so near Huang the Lesser was worth it.

The lesser Huangs’ embarrassment was why you didn’t have ostentatious tables of such size you could not fit them in your manor, behind the walls, where things were private. Appropriately sized furniture in appropriately sized homes. If you had a sprawling estate occupying a hillside then you could have obscenely large tables.

And also, why you had more guards.

She squeezed the Ranger’s hand again beneath the table. They were still connected. Hua regretted having to let go and she more especially regretted having to leave her behind, but surely she could handle killing one or two mortals?

By mortal standards, the speed with which Hua left the manor with its barking dog might be considered fleeing. Hua could only be judged by Cultivator standards and those standards tended to be determined at sword point.

“We’re never going back there,” Weiji said lowly as they rode down streets long ago blackened with coal dust.

“Agreed.”

“I’ve never met so greedy a human. I thought he would mortgage my soul somehow.”

“How lucky, I felt he had sold all my lives past and present for a pittance.”

“Usually, one would speak of their past and future lives.”

“Only if one isn’t an immortal as I plan on being.”

“Arrogant,” he said fondly, his mood lifting as they rode in the sun’s warmth, towards the lake shimmering yellow, a rotted sun that had dripped upon it.

Coaltown was an unpleasant experience beyond the wet heat clinging to her. Even as the walls blocked a wave of dust from sweeping through the city, fine dust was sticking to their clothes and their skin, and might be like mud given enough time. Even now, brushing her horse’s mane, her fingers came away grey. Give it a few hours and they would all be wearing a mask of charcoal. The leathers people wore made sense—easy to clean, easy to wipe down, no chance of coal drying into wet clothes. The layers of fabric coverings were sacrificial, the better to catch finer dust while the architectural designs blocked the majority of it. There was an efficiency to it, a conformity of a tried and tested design that took up much of the buildings. Simplicity that was squat, cramped, and ugly to Hua, but if it meant avoiding constant clouds of dust, she might be willing to accept it.

The estate they were led to carried none of these hallmarks. To begin, it was an oasis of greenery.

Huang the Greater’s estate sprawled along the soil banks of the lake, a compound of many connected buildings whose vined roofs peeked out amidst tree cover. The Shang Family Estate was an evergreen paradise, with trees grown tall and vines crawling across the many rooves with their deep eaves. Artificial waterfalls could be heard, the powerful churn of water familiar. He was wealthy enough that he used silk for his dust traps. Wealthy enough for glass windows and the servants to scrub them clean against the onslaught of dust.

They were ushered through large wooden gates, the kind of wood from a tree centuries old, capped by delicate designs of wrought iron. Beyond those gates lay an oasis. Hua was hit by a new wave of moisture, but this one lifted the mood, cutting through the muggy heat. A combination of the plateau’s shade and the light misting produced by the Greater Huang’s many waterfalls stole away the heat before it could envelop her again. Hua was grateful. Her robes were clinging to her back, sweat drying in layers on her arms. It was a miserable experience.

With water came life. The buzzing of insects, the flies and mosquitoes caught in webs, spiders on the water’s surface, crawling aphids running up trees and armies of ants waging a war in the soil. Fluttering wings, birds calling, flitting about; toads in ponds, croaking, jumping; fish teeming, scheming in their schools.

Life.

Full, vibrant, resplendent and represented in its fullness.

It was not as clean as the other Huang’s estate was. But it was lived in, a quality that would have made it preferable. And yet it continued. They were greeted by a full compliment of servants. Dozens of them who bowed and offered Hua welcome in unison—called her lord Liao Weilong and that would stay with her a lifetime. Her horse was stabled. Hua trusted it would be treated well. Drinks, refreshments, and cold cloths to wipe away the fine dust of the city were offered. Before they had even come close to meeting the master of the manor, they were honoured and given a chance to recuperate. This, here, was what she had desired from the mayor. What she had been denied by the other Huang.

The receiving room was open to the gardens of the estate leading to the lake. Layers of silk curtains shivered in the light breeze, a way to keep the dust out, a way to give privacy from the walkway wrapping around the estate, but one sheer enough that the lake’s waters glittered a crystal blue against all sense, as though the waters were clean only for a man so wealthy.

“Be welcome you to the Shang Estate, honoured lord Liao,” boomed the master of the home. “This little merchant Shang Huang greets the lord Liao.”

***

Huang the Greater was not an ugly man. Tall, wide, cheeks pockmarked with old scars, he did the best he could with what he had and was impeccably dressed as well. Objectively speaking, Huang the Lesser had stronger features, a clearer face, and a strength that came from a civilian dedicated to mastering a weapon. But Huang the Greater was jovial, affable and, if the tapestries hanging from the walls were his, then he had mastered his calligraphy. More than that, he had the careless ease of someone who knew they were important and felt no need to force that importance upon another.

Seeing Huang the Greater, she finally understood why the Lesser was so despicable. Imagine your rival achieving everything you wished and having a much prettier wife. And what a lovely woman she was, her figure slender and what Hua could see of her tiny bird-thin hands made her out to be someone Hua could have lifted with one hand before she’d learned to touch Qi. Her blush showed through her white makeup and matched well with the red painting her eyes. Hua could not look away from the sway of her robes as she walked with that distinct gait of lotus feet.

Did the wife also have to be an excellent guzheng player? It was Hua’s favourite instrument so she knew Lady Shang was as great a talent as her husband was rotund. Sometimes, as she drew her hand across the strings, her robe would slip just slightly, and grant Hua a glimpse of her neck.

“This minor merchant hopes a lord so venerable might accept these paltry offerings. I hope the lord accepts this gift in the spirit it is offered.”

Servants who had been waiting along the walls arrived in a procession, kneeling before Hua and revealing a variety of treasures. One after another, pausing long enough for her to examine the gifts they held in boxes.

Tea and wine and rice, gold and ivory and jade, rolls of calligraphy paper, bars of superlative ink and beautiful brushes to write with. A longbow suitable for hunting the mythical beasts they spoke of in the far west. It was a king’s ransom, a lord’s bribe, and Hua found herself very tempted by the honour. It was… she knew what piles and piles of taels looked like, but no one had yet brought her an offering like this. Made her feel the grandness of her title instead of reminding her that it was a badly fitted mantle she stole from her father, her brother.

The tea alone was so great a gift as to be insulting. The Emperor might serve tea like this. There was a point that humility became arrogance. Calling oneself a minor merchant whilst gifting a king’s random may have crossed that border.

Hua may have pointed such out except for the great prize that was the smile Lady Shang offered Hua. Her features were heightened, sharpened. She was a mature woman and did not try to hide this fact. All she did, all she spoke with her fingers on the strings and the drape of her robes accentuated her experience. Made strength of age.

And if Lady Shang smiled at Hua one more time, Hua would do something very foolish.

“My lord, is there an issue?” Huang the Greater asked pointedly. Weiji wheezed out a high-pitched sound that he masked as a cough.

Hua forced her gaze away from his wife. It was an incredibly difficult experience. Was… was the wife a sorcerer of some kind? Hua had encountered a shaman, why not a sorcerer?

“This Lord has seen no issues with Master Huang’s hospitality. It is the greatest this lord has been offered in the Western Reaches.”

A meaningless, backhanded compliment and they all knew it. The Western Reaches was a backwater. Hua was from Liao capital. This king’s random, fine as it was, beautiful as it was, represented a regular tribute offered to her father. A frog in a well staring at the sky might think they knew all there was of the great vault of the cosmos, but never know that the stars changed as one journeyed. But perhaps a fragment of heaven was still heaven enough to satisfy a frog. Great ambitions were held by those like Hua, who knew ambition enough to reach Heaven, no matter the cost, no matter the passage of ages.

“This Master Huang is grateful that the lord is pleased,” the mortal said, his wife playing another pleasant note. It took all of Hua’s concentration not to stare directly at her. Hua’s gaze was shifted to the side, just enough to get the full bounty of Lady Shang. “He wonders if he might dare ask what brings the Lord Liao here?”

Refusing would be petty of her, though she wished it. It would harm her host’s face and in turn, the dignity of his wife who had done nothing but offer hospitality.

“Have you received word of events in Liaojiangkou?”

He shook his head no and so Hua explained an abbreviated version of events, barely touching upon the true scale of destruction. At times in her story, the delightful notes of the guzheng turned sharp, discordant. Hua was helpless to turn to Lady Shang in those moments and offer consoling words.

“And so, this lord chose to inspect the extent of his domain while the Lord Patriarch remained in his seclusion. Liao stands sentinel. It shall leave no part of its territory unguarded.”

“Well, now that Lord Liao is here, surely the Western Reaches are safe,” he said, rallying with a smile. A jovial man was Master Huang. “Hopefully the grisly business of many skulls is over.”

“Only once those who acted to allow Kang to run freely are dealt with will heads stop rolling. This Lord hopes the Master Huang is willing to provide aid.”

“This little merchant is available at your leisure. Let it not be known that he did not desert his sworn oaths. Indeed, truly, if you are to investigate such a web of corruption, then you require a place to stay. The lord is free to use this household at his leisure,” Huang the Greater declared, louder.

Were they playing a game of generosity now? Usually, Hua wouldn’t bother, but she was in a relaxed mood. After Huang the Lesser, she was willing to accept a lot.

At least this estate did not have the feeling of worms crawling beneath her skin.

“And where would the generous Master Shang reside? This lord could not imagine displacing such a gracious host.” She was still looking at Huang’s wife.

“Please, do not concern yourself. This lowly merchant has another residence he might use.”

“Surely this lord would stay there and avoid displacing the lady of the household.”

“Do not concern yourself with this mere wife. She had hoped to spend time renovating the inn and this shall be an excellent opportunity.”

“This lord bows to Lady Shang’s wisdom.” Hua smiled at her. “I am eager to see what the lady chooses. By the decorations in your home, her tastes are surely well-developed. Perhaps the inn might be a place worthy of this lord to use next he visits.”

Huang the Greater paled, wincing at that declaration. If he wished to play a game of generosity, then he could pay for it. Would his pockets and generosity for his wife be as great as his girth and name implied?

“Perhaps the lord speaks true,” Lady Shang demurred, but her smile was mischievous. “She hopes the lord is satisfied with the renovations.”

Hua smiled back, helpless. “This lord hopes the same.”

***

A careful system of walls, plants, and rocky filtration systems had created an oasis of blue amongst the yellow lake. An artificial atoll had been made through tall beams of wood so waterlogged they had fused together, massive hewn stone transported at undoubtedly great expense, and what Hua imagined to be sand imported from a different region. Lotus and lilies gave the atoll greater colour whilst vines crept along trellises, emerging from the waters just as the many varietals of reeds did so. It was an extreme expense to simply have blue waters in a yellow lake. Hua approved greatly the expense Huang the Greater had gone to if only because she benefitted from it. If she must spend weeks or months here, at least she could do it with clean air and the shade of tall trees and the refreshing spray of mist to cool her.

Hua sat with her feet in the clear waters of the atoll, savouring the coolness against the scorching heat of the sun. Her horse happily ate from a bag of feed, happy now that he was no longer exposed to coal dust and ash.

The estate was being investigated by her soldiers. For spies, for weakness, for hidden paths and greater secrets. Hua doubted they would find anything more incriminating than a few smuggled goods. He’d given her his home and bribed her with gold, tea, ivory, and supplies. Whatever they found would be of such minor illegality that it would diminish Hua’s standing to punish him. She’d lost the game of generosity before it even began.

She said as much to her cousin who was enjoying the sun and relative privacy. He’d removed his robes and laid on the ground in a lazy sprawl. Feet dipped in water, robes bunched up like a pillow, he looked every bit the layabout he desired to be. He wasn’t a twitchy mess for once.

“I feel slightly guilty.”

“Please stop trying to give me a heart attack with impossibilities.”

“Huang the Lesser did nothing wrong to us yet I feel more comfortable here than I did there. I’ve been bribed and functionally told to ignore any illegal dealings I notice. Huang the Lesser was polite and mostly courteous, offered only his aid and compliance, and I still fled from it. Him.”

Qing sat on the pier, one foot dipped in the waters. Swaying back and forth. A hypnotic pattern Hua could not look away from. The water was disturbed but such that Hua could not tell if it was true or merely done in sync with the waves.

“I’ll be shocked if his home wasn’t built on a burial ground. Fleeing was the correct option with him. The only thing you could have done better was to leave the skulls in his courtyard.”

She had them mounted in the town square. Well, gave the order at least. It would be a suitable reminder of what Hua could do. The Mayor had already betrayed Huang the Lesser by directing Hua to him instead of this much wealthier Master Huang. She would let fear grow taut until it snapped and people came to her to confess the sins of their enemies.

“Still, it’s odd.”

“I suspect I know why you chose this place, if not why you feel guilty. I am surprised my lord did not ask Lady Shang to stay behind as our host. More control than I expected from you.”

“Why would I ask that? She’s a married lady.”

“You certainly didn’t look at her that way. I can see why Huang took her as far away from you as possible. First the Ranger, now Lady Shang. You have a big appetite.”

She kicked her foot out and flicked cold water at him. It landed across his chest and neck and face, startling Weiji into sitting upright, his glare petulant and childish.

“We are not behaving like children today,” he warned, as though he could tell Hua what to do. But, well, he’d done nothing but work to her benefit, so maybe she could offer him some minor reprieve today.

“Do you think Ranger Xue would appreciate these accommodations more?”

“I’m sure she would rather complete her duty without interruption and, if I must remind my lord, her duty is to guard the Shaman.”

“We could hire her. More cultivators are always a good thing.”

“Don’t. Just… you’ll be harming her pride and honour if you do that. Let her do her work in peace.”

“But—”

“Trust me for once. Please. As your adviser, just trust me. You would harm her pride if you don’t let her do her job.”

“Fine. I’ll listen to you for once.”

“Thanks. It’s only what you hired me for. Being your advisor.”

She looked to the west, where the mountains towered over everything. The most distant had peaks floating above the clouds. A wall that shielded the Amber Sea from imperial control to the west. It tempted her, the idea of climbing those peaks and staring down the Yongtai Emperor. But she was not quite so strong as to declare herself like that.

“What do you think of that mass of fire Qi beneath the mountain?”

“I am pretending it doesn’t exist. It’s a problem for later. Today, I am doing nothing but relaxing. I will have this one day of peace.”

“Only today,” she agreed.

The last time Hua had felt such Qi, the world broke beneath her feet.
