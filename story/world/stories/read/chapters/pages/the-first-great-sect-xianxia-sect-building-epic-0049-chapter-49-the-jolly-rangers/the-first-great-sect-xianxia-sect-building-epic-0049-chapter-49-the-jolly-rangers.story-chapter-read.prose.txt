It would be hours yet before dawn arrived to illuminate the violence inflicted upon a village that should have been protected by the name Liao. But the name Liao held no power here. Speaking the name of that Great Clan had not saved other villagers, and the arrival of Liao Hua was far too late to make a difference.

The village was a silent ruin. Thatched roofs burnt down. Carts overturned. Doors smashed through. Bandits had swept through like a tidal wave and showed greater malice.

It was a vision Hua had become unfortunately inoculated against, the shock of devastation no longer bright and sharp, but a dull tapping that was far too easy to ignore. Just two, maybe three days ago, they had found a mass grave for a village. This was just another.

“I don’t like any of this,” said her cousin, picking at a thread dangling from his robes. He was squinting in the dimness at it instead of staring at the monument in the center. “I don’t like any of this.”

Hua found she agreed. The silence weighed and the smell of rot and blood were pervasive beneath the chill.

They entered the nearest home.

Old, was Hua’s first thought of the couple. They had died together, one slumped above the other. A futile attempt to protect one another. The man reaching out in his final moments. Scraps of metal and carved wooden beads were strewn about the floor. Hua found the pieces and brought them together, brass and greenish copper and carved beads. The largest copper square had been sheared at an angle, turned into a sharp-sided triangle, and when Hua turned it just right, it fit the angle of the cut along the old woman’s chest.

A promise carried in copper and wood, sealed in blood, and all for nothing.

Love. A foolish thing. It kept them here, in the homes they had built, all so they could die on soil that would not remember their stubbornness or care for their defiance.

“Who would even see this? Who would even care about this? Why go to the trouble?”

“I thought Kang was more precise,” Hua agreed, pushing aside a cabinet. It scraped loudly against the floor. Behind it, where the man had been reaching out, lay a fallen box, popped open, hinges broken. Inside, were copper coins and a single silver tael. And a matching necklace. Hua suspected they had been killed after the paltry offering was found.

A hundred silver taels would not have been enough to move Hua to help mortals. Perhaps a single silver tael was not worth sparing a mortal even for another mortal?

“Don’t go admiring him now.” Part joke, part admonishment, fully nervous.

They continued examining the brutality Kang inflicted on this hapless village. Not every house was a picture of ruin. Many had fled and joined the convoy. Only those too stubborn or too infirm to leave had stayed behind. They had borne the sin of calling for aid and paid for it.

A tall tree had survived the destruction.

One with deep roots that reached the knees, gnarled and twisted. The trunk was knotted and had burs throughout its length. Thick rope wrapped around the trunk, blocky warding talismans attached to it—good luck, prosperity, hope, filial piety, forgiveness—in a traditional way that Hua had seen more commonly in depictions from the southern regions closest to other nations.

The tree was the only thing that endured. The village had been built around the towering tree. She could tell it had been the centre of many a festival, a place for lovers to meet and siblings to play. The old couple would have made their vows here in the springtime of their youth and reaffirmed them in the autumn of their lives.

Decorating the tree were the Jolly Rangers. They had been strung up on the branches of the tree. Hua had been ignoring the display but could no longer.

The last time Hua had seen so many corpses hung as she had in the past week, she had been on her first journey through the lands of petty nobility in the south. There, a minor lord had been suffering a peasant revolt and Hua had thought it her duty to aid a noble who paid their taxes to his Liao overlords. Rows upon rows of bodies had been her answer to a question she had not thought to ask, questions of lords and authority, of mortals and how easily they died. Creaking nooses and wailing children had been her only answer, an answer she had kept with her and understood to mean ‘the affairs of mortals are not the business of those blessed by lightning’ and ever since, she had tried to ignore them.

Cultivators had been hung today. No Cultivator deserved so ignoble an end and here Hua was faced with half a dozen. The remaining Jolly Rangers, swaying where they had been hung.

The Jolly Rangers were jolly in so far as their bellies smiled for them. If their guts were tongues, then it might even be a doggy smile. The analogy fell apart since Hua was certain a dog couldn’t hang itself by the tongue. Maybe they could, but she severely doubted it.

Maybe a demon dog could.

“Hua, what are you on about?”

“Did I say that out loud? Sorry, just considering the complexities of doing this and whether the relative effort is worth it.”

“You are in a very strange mood,” Weiji said slowly, elongating the sounds. Calming. Reassuring. “This is a lot to experience all at once. You can leave and let me handle this.”

“Don’t treat me like your little sister?”

“Don’t be petulant.”

She noticed that the Ranger’s guts had been tangled up in rope nooses, the true strength needed to support the gruesome display. Only because a seventh noose swayed in the wind. Six corpses and seven nooses. Kang was considerate. He made sure all the Rangers had a space, even the archer he had strung up as a different warning.

Hua glanced eastward. The sky had lightened just a hint. A tint of navy to the otherwise pitch-dark sky. Before the heavens struck her, she doubted she would have noticed such an incremental change. She shook her head and they continued their search.

They soon came across a bandit.

Hua knew he was a bandit immediately. His death was brutal by the standards of the civilians. Head smashed to a pulp. A crater beneath his neck filled in with blood and chunks of flesh, bone and teeth and brain matter all frozen.

“He killed his own man,” Hua said, finding a serpent branded upon the man’s arm. The only way to remove it would be to so thoroughly brand it again that the skin would be more akin to leather.

“There would have been a reason for it. This… it doesn’t confirm my suspicions, but it gives them credence.”

“Explain.”

“Do you notice something? Only the Rangers were hung like this. Not these other villagers.”

“The same villagers who were violently killed in their homes?” Hua asked, gesturing at one villager with a slit throat.

“Yes. But consider also how clean the kills are. People were always going to be killed for being defiant—it’s what I would have done were I in his position and someone hired Cultivators against me. But he didn’t torture them. Didn’t make them suffer longer than needed. Execution, not torture. Retaliation instead of retribution.”

“So why kill his own man?”

“Disobedience, maybe. Maybe he’d crossed a line and needed to be made an example of. Maybe it wasn’t even Kang that did the killing. You decapitate someone with a blade, same way you gut them. Crushing a skull is a hammer’s job. Any blunt instrument, really.”

“This body also wasn’t made into a display or buried,” Hua realised. “So, someone else made this kill, even if Kang approved it. Pushed too far for Kang to accept. What pushes a man as brutal as Kang too far to accept?”

Weiji aggressively looked away from her. “Same reason you castrate forty bandits.”

“Oh.”

“Yeah.”

“So, he kills these villagers cleanly because he doesn’t care about murdering villagers and then makes monuments of terror against real threats and those that disobey him. I don’t truly see the moral difference.”

“Maybe he cares about his next life. Karma can weigh on you. Revelling in evil can affect your karma.”

“But ordering the deaths of possibly hundreds of civilians is somehow more acceptable than a bit of light torture? Another reason I don’t believe in the gods.”

His smile was bitter. “Just another reason to hate them.”

Hua did not want to think of Qing who was under the tree, fiddling with the seventh noose. “But you’re right, this doesn’t make any sense. Someone’s stolen my yunzi stones and thought I wouldn’t notice. I don’t like things that don’t make sense when they aren’t in my favour.”

“So you haven’t liked anything for the last month?”

“Did the world break then?”

“Close enough that it doesn’t matter. So, what are we going to do with the Jolly Rangers?”

“After we examine the corpses and search them for any clues? Do what the bandits should have done and burn them.”

Weiji levelled her a considering look, one that quickly morphed into disappointment. “You just want to see a fire.”

“Not at all. It’s the only way to dispose of them cleanly.”

“Then why not the villagers as well?”

“Because mortals can go to the soil easily enough. Their dantians still have some Qi. It might create a plague of Qi-rich insects and those could grow into a Gu.”

“Then we pop their dantians and bury the bodies. Say the words, give them proper rites, and anything else that doesn’t involve throwing up a massive signal for Kang to know we’re coming.”

“Weiji.”

“Fine, we’ll do this and ruin our chances of a clean victory. And when we all die for it, I hope you consider it worth it”

His shoulders were hunched, the nature of his Qi snappish. He approached the tree with grudging steps, each loud and ponderous, each an intentional sign of disapproval. Weiji could be silent if he wished it. Hua held strong. She’d have her way or—

Qing slipped into one of the nooses.

“Wait,” Hua said unthinkingly, her heart stuttering to a pained stop. Her ghost refused and began pulling the noose secure. “Stop!”

“Hua?” her cousin asked, stopping to face her.

She ignored him, looking over his shoulder at her ghost. A challenge, a threat. Hua could not win against it. “Take them down. We’ll… we’ll bring them back.”

Between one blink and the next, Qing was gone.

“Good.” He paused. “You’re certain about this? I’ve never known you to change your mind.”

“I thought it through.”

His expression was gentle. Too damned gentle. She hated it.

“Hua—”

“Don’t. Let’s just get them down and move on.”

She could indulge her missing ghost a little.
