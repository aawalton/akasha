Slowly, I lowered my hands away from my mouth. I could feel my breath coming in short, sharp gasps through my nose as I looked down, reluctance thrumming within me. My eyes found my hands, and I stared at them in horror.

Red, viscous liquid coated my fingers. I could feel it smeared across my face where I’d touched it, and the breeze reaching in through the window that I must have opened served as a constant reminder of its presence.

Blood, I realised as my heart began to thud in my ears. My hands were covered in blood.

Panic surged within me, and I looked around frantically, searching for an explanation. I expected to find a body, or a weapon, or something else to explain what happened, but my gaze only made it as far as the duvet before me. It got caught by the red pattern on the cover that I’d noticed earlier, before my gaze had fully focused. It was not flowers like I assumed. It was splatters of blood.

My eyes widened, and I tried to scramble backwards, but I was already pressed against my headboard. I couldn’t move any further away, and the attempt just caused even more blood to be smeared on the duvet cover, smudging the already-drying droplets that marred the fabric, but that was good, I realised. It had rubbed some of it off my hands, and I needed that. I had to get it off me.

Quickly, I wiped my hands on the duvet cover again, trying desperately to clear as much of it off as I could, but it was impossible to get it all. The blood had already been on my hands for too long, and it was starting to dry. Some flaked off or smeared, but too much remained, and I could see how red my skin had been stained beneath the blood.

I had no idea who or where it had come from, I realised as my breathing grew even more shallow, making me light-headed. I had no recollection of what happened, of what I did, and I tried to think back, but I couldn’t. My memories were locked away from me.

It felt like they weren’t even there, like they didn’t exist. No matter how hard I tried, how desperately I strained, I couldn’t access them. I could vaguely recall what happened in the morning, going upstairs to my room, but that was it. It felt like a chunk of time was missing from my day, but it couldn’t be. I must have just not been in the world for long enough to access it all yet, but that would come with time, I tried to tell myself.

A question came to me, making my stomach clench. Was it self-defence? I hoped it was, that I had not been the one to attack anyone, but that they had tried to hurt me, and I’d had to fight them off. My window was smashed. I hadn’t noticed it before, but someone could have smashed it and climbed into the house before trying to kill me. As much as that idea scared me, it felt better than the alternative, better than if I were the one who’d struck first.

I doubted that I could have done that. I wasn’t the type of person to start fights, and I didn’t attack people for no reason. Or, at least, I hoped I wasn’t. The world I was in was new to me, and I didn’t know what I was like there, but I couldn’t be that different from my normal self.

My eyes found the message written on the wall again, and ice slipped down my spine. My arms wrapped instinctively around my legs, and I hugged my knees tightly again. Had I written those words, I asked myself. I couldn’t remember it, and I couldn’t think of any reason why I’d do it, but I wasn’t certain. It seemed like it was a message to me, though. Somehow, I just felt like I was the one who was being watched, but I wasn’t sure why, and that sent a shiver of fear through me.

It wasn’t just the words themselves that scared me, though. It was the size too. Each letter was huge. The words took up almost the entire wall, and I had no idea where I or whoever had written the message had gotten that much blood from. There was nothing in my room that indicated where it could have come from, no container or body.

Surely, it was more than a single person’s worth of blood. I knew humans contained a lot, like five litres or something, and I didn’t really know how much had been used to paint the wall, but it seemed like they must have drained more than one person for it. Multiple people had been killed just to send me a message.

My stomach dropped, and my eyes darted towards the door. I threw myself off the bed, lunging towards it as my head spun. White spots exploded in my vision, and a whooshing sound pounded in my ears as I staggered, fighting to regain my balance as a single thought repeated in my head.

Was it my parents? Had I or whoever it was killed my parents to send the message to myself? Or maybe they weren’t dead. They could just be lying somewhere in the house, the life slowly draining from them, and I needed to find them. I had to get to them before it was too late.

But the moment I reached my bedroom door and stepped out into the hallway, a sound came from below. A door slammed, the noise echoing through the house, and I fell still. Indecision thrashed within me in time with my racing heart, but I did not move.

I had no idea what to do. I was terrified, and I didn’t know who had slammed the door or if they were entering or leaving my house. It could have been the attacker, whoever they were, fleeing the scene, or perhaps it was the police, arriving to arrest me.

My heart leapt into my throat. Regardless of what had actually happened, whether it was self-defence or not, I knew it looked bad. I was covered in blood, and the writing on my wall was terrifying. I wasn’t sure that I’d be able to convince anyone that it wasn’t me, and simply saying that I didn’t remember what happened would not be good enough. It would be seen as suspicious. I knew it would. I had to leave.

Guilt flared within me as I shut my eyes. I felt awful for even considering abandoning the world, and I knew that I’d be leaving the other version of me to deal with whatever had happened, but there was nothing more I could do to help them. Staying in the world wouldn’t make things any better for them, and I just hoped that they’d be okay.

They would, I told myself as I reached out for the dizziness once more. They’d be completely fine. I didn’t entirely believe that, but it gave me the strength to slip out of the world, tumbling into the nothingness that awaited me.

It was easier that time. I didn’t get stuck in the world, and the other realities didn’t recoil away from my grasp. They just crowded around my mind, surrounding me as I plummeted through the unknown.

My heart continued to pound as I fell, and I could barely breathe. Each breath felt too shallow, like my chest was trapped in a tight vice that stopped me from inhaling fully, and I had no idea how long I was there, but I could feel other realities rushing past me as I dropped deeper and deeper into the darkness.

I couldn’t stop, though. Vertigo had gripped my head so strongly that I could barely think. It was impossible to concentrate hard enough to think about one of the worlds, much less choose which to enter, and it was a relief when I felt the dizziness begin to clear. Something was approaching, coming into focus, and I could feel myself being pulled into another place.

My steps faltered, and my knees almost buckled, threatening to send me down onto the gleaming white sand below. I stumbled, trying frantically to remain on my feet, but the movement sent me falling sideways into the thick plastic wall beside me.

The people on the other side leapt backwards, but they did not look away from me. They simply watched silently as I pulled myself away from the sticky vinyl that was almost as hot as the sand that burnt my bare feet. Despite the pain the impact caused me, the sting that lingered on my skin even after I moved away, I was glad it was there. If I had been out in the open and stumbled, I would have fallen properly, and that would be worse.

I couldn’t stop, though, I told myself as I gritted my teeth and forced myself to continue running. If I stopped or let myself slow for even a minute, I would die, and I’d already fought too hard to stop that from happening. Even though I had no idea why I was running or where I was trying to get to, I couldn’t give in.

The sand slipped beneath my feet, as soft and sinuous as silk. It made it hard to move as quickly as I knew I needed to, but I tried to ignore that as I dashed past another giant vinyl bubble, also filled with silent watchers. Their eyes followed me as I darted past, but they did not speak nor offer help.

I could taste blood. I was pushing myself too hard. My lungs burned, and each too-quick breath that I gasped in only stoked the fire that was roaring within me. It did not help that the air was so hot either. It felt as though I were trapped in a flaming vortex, but somehow, it was also humid. The world was muggy, and each inhale felt strained. Difficult.

My body was not used to it, I realised as sweat poured down my face. I was not used to the heat, to running so hard, or being outside. It was the first time I’d ever done it, the first time I’d set foot on the sand, and it had come too soon, I realised.

Another giant vinyl ball loomed before me, and my gaze met a woman’s. She stood inside the cool dome, watching my attempt, and the urge to shout to her soared within me. I longed to cry out, to beg the people lining the clear walls to help me or throw the doors open, but somehow, I knew it was pointless. They would not and could not help me.

Instead, I just had to keep going. If I did, there was a chance I could make it to Salvation.

The pure white sand blinded me as I sprinted out from beneath the shadow of the bubble. It was late in the day. The sun was beginning to set, but for some reason, that only made the light bouncing off the sand brighter. It made my eyes water, my vision blurred, and my skin felt as though a flame had been passed over it or held against my body for far too long.

If I did make it to Salvation, I had no doubt that I would be burnt and blistered by the sun. Their doctors would be able to heal that, though. They would have a balm or something that would fix my poor, baked flesh. I was certain they would. They had more than we could even dream of, and I knew they would not allow me to suffer. They cared too much.

But perhaps that was the price I had to pay for being so unprepared. I had been training for that day ever since I was an infant. I knew it could come at any moment, that I could be thrust into the desert surrounding my home and entrusted with the task that so many have faced before, but I did not expect it to come so soon. I should have still been inside one of the bubbles with my family or in the tunnels deep beneath the sand, but I was not. I was alone, exposed, and I was not ready.

I thought I had more time, I remembered as a stitch ignited in my side, making each breath even more painful than it already was. How long I could keep going, I was unsure, but I could not let that stop me. I pushed the thought, the pain, aside and focused on the memories that were trickling into my awareness as my legs continued to pump.

When they called the meeting not even twenty minutes before, I assumed it was about something else. After all, someone had been sent out last month. I had stood inside my bubble, my parents on either side of me, watching as they reached the peak of the dune before faltering, and had mourned, like the rest would do for me if I were to fall.

We should have had at least two months before the next person was selected. The signal was random, but it usually came roughly every three months, so I assumed the meeting was just an update. I remembered praying that they would announce that the restriction on the weekly showers had been lifted, that we would be able to wash for five minutes, rather than the current two.

But the moment I saw the governor standing behind the podium that had been erected long before my time, I realised two things. Firstly, I was wrong, and secondly, someone from our bubble had been selected. The governor only attended the meeting of the bubble where someone was being cast out. He needed to be there. He was the only one who could perform the blessing and last rites, but still, I did not expect for my name to be read out.

Everything that had happened since was like a dream, a terrible, unending nightmare, but the memories were growing clearer. I could remember the sensation of the guards’ hands gripping my biceps, stopping me from running away as they marched me out of the meeting hall and into the infirmary. Before I knew what was happening, I was standing in the space between the inner and outer doors.

My thigh still ached from where the microchip had been injected, and heat leached into the small space as I stared at the governor, barely able to take in the words he was saying. He spoke quickly, his tone hushed and his expression intense, and it had been hard to get a word in, but I managed to point out that I was not properly dressed for the attempt.

I had informed him, my face alight from the shame, that I was in my pyjamas, just like so many of the people who were watching me from inside the bubbles. I was not wearing shoes, and I begged him to allow me to grab them or to take the ones that the unknown person standing at the inner doors held, but he simply shook his head.

His face was solemn as he told me it was too late. As always, there was a short time frame for the attempt to have any chance of being successful. It had already been too long since they received the signal because the governor had to journey all the way to my bubble, so I needed to go immediately. If I waited even a minute to grab the boots offered to me, I would lose my window, and Salvation would be beyond my reach. Certain death would be the only thing awaiting me in the desert.

A grunt slipped from me as I passed the final two bubbles, the ones closest to the gateway, and I couldn’t help but wish I had started there. If my family had been assigned to the rooms below that bubble, my attempt would have been so much easier. I would have already been halfway up the dune, but it didn’t matter anyway. It was too late to think about that.

Beyond the bubbles, the sand grew uneven. I had been warned of that, seen it in pictures, but I had never witnessed it with my own eyes, nor had I experienced it myself, and I did not expect it to be so much harder to traverse.

My breathing became even more ragged as the sand began to slope upwards, the angle quickly becoming much more steep, and I was forced to slow. It had been hard to maintain my speed on the sand whilst it was flat, but that was nothing compared to how difficult it was to run up the dune. Frustration and panic soared within me as my foot came down, sinking into the sand, and I almost fell.

But with each laboured step, I rose higher above the vinyl domes that dotted the desert behind me, further from the place I once called home. I was staggering. Sweat dripped into my eyes, making it even harder to see, but I knew I was moving towards the gateway that heralded the end of my attempt and would bring everyone I loved closer to safety, and I held that knowledge close, letting it fill me with determination.

My head snapped up, and the air caught in my chest. I held my breath, straining my ears, as a noise became audible over the deafening thump of my heart beating in my ears. Panic flooded me, and my steps slowed as I listened, trying to work out what I could hear.

The soft buzz was gradually growing, but I couldn’t quite place it. I couldn’t figure out whether it was the hum of locusts that would swarm me and strip the flesh from my bones within seconds, or if I could hear the gentle vibration of machinery. My chest burned from the lack of oxygen, but I refused to breathe as my mind raced to figure it out.

It was hard. I’d only heard the recordings, and they were old, but I was almost certain that the noise was unlike any the tutors had played for us. The pitch was wrong. It was too low, but it sounded more like…

Machinery, I decided. An engine.

I surged forward, renewed fear coursing through my body. As a child, I had feared the locusts more than the others. I thought the idea of being devoured by those mutated beasts would be the worst way to go, but now that I was sixteen, I realised I was wrong. The others were worse. Unlike the locusts, they may not kill me immediately, but I did not doubt that they would make me wish they had.

A faint fear flared at the back of my head as I fought to climb the dune. I had somehow found my way into another terrifying world, just like the last, but that thought was quickly pushed away, replaced by another.

How did they know someone would be sent out tonight? They could have been monitoring the bubbles, watching for the moment when a teenager was thrust out onto the sand, but surely the patrols would have noticed. There had been no reports of an encampment nearby. The closest one was half a day away, which meant they must have known.

My blood turned to ice within my veins. Perhaps it was just a coincidence. The others I could hear may have simply been passing the area when one noticed my attempt, but part of me feared that was not true. I feared that they had managed to hack the signal.

That was not possible. We had been told it was protected, safeguarded. The people of Salvation were much more technologically advanced than those of us forced to reside in the desert, so the others should not have been able to do that, and if they had…

I stumbled again, almost falling, but thankfully, I caught myself. The sun-baked sand burnt my palms, and I snatched my hands away quickly, forcing my feet to keep moving. If the others had managed to access the signal, it could mean that Salvation had been compromised, that it had fallen. The others would have access to the gateway, be able to pass through at will, and I feared what they would have done to the place that was meant to be my haven.

But I could not think of that now. I had to believe that they were safe, that they were waiting for me on the other side of the gateway, I told myself as the buzzing grew louder. The sound was so close that it was making my teeth vibrate in my skull, and I could not stop myself from glancing back over my shoulder, needing to see how far away the others were, as I continued to climb upwards.

My heart dropped into my stomach as my foot came down on nothing. There should have been sand beneath it, but there was not. Instead, it just kept moving downwards, and I realised too late that I had reached the top of the dune. It was already plummeting downward, just as I had been warned.

I threw my arms out, trying to regain my balance, but my body had already tipped too far forward. My foot hit the sand hard, sinking into it, and my knee gave way. I fell, landing so heavily that the air was knocked out of me, and I began to roll.
