Even the crooked have lines they won’t cross.

The thieves slunk down the alleys behind the inn and shops, steps almost silent among the rotting boards and garbage-stained cobbles. Like every town market, the polished facade faded once you stepped behind it, revealing the ugly bones of each building. Scaffolding, timber beams, and the detritus of slapdash industry. Men and women in coarse wool and thick boots tromped between loading bays and crate-laden carts, cursing as they slipped on crumbling steps or pushed through doors set on crooked hinges.

The drain pipes, however, were quite well made.

Once out of view of the square, Harker ascended the nearest heavy iron pipe. The brackets were stout and bolted to the wooden beams—it took his weight easily. In less than a minute, he’d made the roof, and then he was loping across the tiles. He momentarily lost the kids due to the warped eaves, but balacing himself precariously on the edge cleared his line of sight.

Below, the thieves scurried between wagon wheels and broken piles of stone. Like little streams joining a river, the first three became four, then five, until seven of them moved in a gang down an empty side street.

Harker followed from above, easily hopping the narrow gaps between tavern and tenement. He held Sovereign Sight, letting the minor drain of its 5-foot sphere drench the mossy tiles. It kept his footing secure, identifying loose or sheared nails that would otherwise send the tiles sliding off. It also watched his back, a curious sensation that made Harker feel a bit like he was floating. Peering in every direction at once was a trip.

Down three blocks and across a narrow canal, the thieves ended up within the hollow shell of a burned out tenement. The charred remains were sturdy enough that it wasn’t falling down, but Harker paused on a neighboring rooftop, crouching so that he wasn’t silhouetted against the false stars.

Here we are, then. Harker leaned forward. The ratlord himself.

The six thieves crowded around a gaunt young man with limp, shoulder-length black hair and skin as pale as a fish belly. A thin cloak hung off one shoulder, and the rest of his clothes were almost the exact same as the children. The only difference were the three heavy blades at his hips.

“Ratlord!”

“Don’t call me that, Bucket,” said the young man. “Makes me sound old.”

“You are old,” said a toothless child no more than seven or eight.

“Young enough to beat all of you.” The children laughed, clearly unafraid. The ratlord grimaced. “Blighted kids. How’re the crowds?”

“Thick.”

“How’s that, Knees?”

An angular boy with scabs across his shins gestured wildly. “Big sailors got off their ships. And those fat merchants were crowdin’ the stoops.”

“You took from them before they made it to the inn and taverns?”

“We hit the sailors,” Bucket said. “The merchants closed ranks. Like they knew we was there.”

The ratlord pursed his lips. His already gaunt face hollowed out and his long, aquiline nose crinkled. “Just my luck,” he muttered. “Enough. Give me the take.”

One of the older ones handed the young man a collection of pouches, purses, and satchels. “This is all we got, Rags.”

The ratlord hefted them all. “Light.”

The thieves stared at him, their unease simple to read across their bent backs and hunched shoulders. They had the look of fawns ready to bolt.

Rags sighed. “How long’ve ya’ll been out?”

“Ten hours,” said Bucket.

“Ten…” The ratlord lowered the take. “I bet you haven’t eaten yet either.”

The children shuffled, their hunched shoulders lowering as they raised their chins.

“No, didn’t think so.” Rags shuffled the collected pouches under an arm, and from the satchel at his side he pulled out a loaf of bread. He tossed it to Bucket, then followed it up with several cloth-wrapped packets.

“Bread!”

“Salted fish!”

“Slow down.” Rags clucked his tongue as the young thieves tore into the food. “Don’t hurt yourselves. It’s gone a bit stale, but soak it in some water and it’ll be alright. The fish is fresher. Just lifted it today.”

“It’s so good!” Knees sucked on a hunk of bread, alternating with tiny bites of fish.

"Enjoy it. And if you can, see what else you can grab. Not tonight,” Rags added sternly the moment one of them stopped eating. “You kids’re dead on your feet and I don't need you gettin’ caught. You understand? You eat. You rest. Then you take this town for everything it’s got.”

“You got it, Rags. We'll get you more. Don't worry."

Grins stretched the face of the thieves, and a few were even bouncing on their toes. Harker had been that hungry before, and the joy of eating anything could be a giddy rush. The tension he’d seen in the children had melted away.

"It's a shame, Rags,” Bucket said between mouthfuls. “After you, reportin’ to Figs or Mudlip is gonna be downright dreary.”

“They’re boring,” said another.

“Mean too,” Knees agreed.

“Not as mean as some. If I could keep lord’n over you rats I would, but things gotta change sometimes.”

The smallest of the crew leaned forward, big brown eyes almost shining. “Can we see it?”

The others joined in a chorus of agreement.

“Quit yammerin’!” Rags shoved the sleeve of his right arm up to the elbow. “There. Happy?”

Immediately the crew was transfixed by the gleam of silver lines etched across the man’s forearm.

Chartermarked. Just like Oswick, this Rags would have had to take the mark back during Threllsnacht. Why haven’t either of them left already?

“It’s beautiful,” Knees sighed. “Like you tuck a silversmith’s whole stock into your skin.”

“Alright enough gawkin’.” Rags let his sleeve drop. The thieves all let out disappointed jeers. “I’m goin’ whether you like it or not. Figs and Mudlip might be boring, but they don’t beat their rats. That’s about as honest as any cutpurse is gonna get, yeah?”

“Yeah.” The response was almost in unison, but it wasn’t happy.

Rags considered them. “Look. In a few days, you can take the roost.”

“Really?” Buckets stared with wide eyes. “No foolin’?”

“True as a crossed money lender. You can all stay there for good. You know the way up?”

“We do!”

“Wow, the roost!”

“Go,” Rags pointed off into the distance. “Rest. Before I change my mind. I got places to be.”

Excited and charged with newfound energy, the thieves scurried off. The six of them headed not toward the market, but further into the tenements. Harker didn’t bother to follow, not even with his eyes. He stayed glued on the ratlord.

The young man, this Rags, was no older than Harker himself. He had several scars across his face, rough hands, and the look of someone who'd never had a full meal in his life. Once the children had gone, he deflated, sagging all the way to the ground. He set down the purses and started going through them.

"Scour me!" Rags dropped the last satchel with the rest. He scratched his head, lank hair tangling around his fingers. "This ain't nearly enough."

Groaning and grunting, he bundled all of them up into his own pack and stood back up. He headed south, away from the river and in the direction of the Lighthouse.

Strange. Harker followed across the rooftops. I’d figure he’d head back dockside.

He kept his distance as the ratlord shuffled through rough streets and crevices so narrow it was a miracle the thief managed not to get stuck. All the while, Harker kept his attention fixed firmly on Rags’ back. Ratlords were just as skittish as the name suggested, and Rags took several unexpected detours that had Harker scrambling to keep up. He didn’t suspect he was being followed—he never looked up, even—but evasion appeared to come second nature to the man. Harker could appreciate a properly cautious individual, though.

His mother had described ratlords as petty kings over a domain of the desperate. She’d told him they existed in every major city, though some islands called them by different names—glibhands, slicks, crookfingers—they all performed the same function. The Shroud was an organization Harker hadn’t heard of until he’d encountered Miriam, but they didn’t change how thieves worked. Ratlords organized the aimless crews of the streets, taking their spoils and distributing rewards—but those spoils had to go somewhere.

Rags headed somewhere deeper into the city, steadily and with purpose, but not in any particular rush. He made his way through the tenements and into another area of small shops and taverns. It was quieter than the docks, but people still milled about, a number of them loudly carousing, their voices a drunken slur echoing off the walls of the building.

Rags veered toward a shop, hustling up a set of stone steps. A chipped and stained facade of stone marked the building from the taverns near it, though the front door was simple oak weathered by a thousand dirty hands. Brass hardware protested when the ratlord pulled it open, and the rare window rattled when he slammed it shut behind himself. Letters had been painted across the glass, most of it peeling off, except for the name Boggs.

Harker stepped closer to the eaves, waiting a long while as he studied the place. Across the next few ten minutes, not a single soul entered or exited the shop. There was no movement in the upper windows or even light. What he did notice, however, was that a series of scratches had been marked into the lintel of the doorway. Unlike the markings across the door’s base and knob, these were too even and deep—too purposeful—to be simple weathering. In fact, the longer he stared at them, Harker recalled seeing similar scratches above Miriam’s doorway.

If there are cutpurses, then they must have some place to sell their ill-gotten goods. Fences or scrappers, his mother had taught him to look for the signs. They can be useful, in their own way. If you need information, find a fence. But nothing’s free. Mind you bring something worth trading.

Harker sat back on his heels, perched on the edge of the building across the street. His cloak wrapped around him, covering his bent knees and providing some bit of warmth. That sticky humidity still lingered, but unprotected by the buildings, Harker found himself at the mercy of a ceaseless wind. He endured and watched for any sign that the ratlord would finish up his business. He had no interest in having witnesses.

For thirty minutes he waited, yet the door to Boggs remained firmly shut while the countdown ticked at his wrist.

I don’t have time for this.

He climbed off the roof, slipping down the nearest drain pipe into an adjoining alleyway. The main street still had some stragglers, but they fled when a pair of drunken guardsmen stumbled unsteadily down the cobbles. They clung to each other, a skin of wine passing between them, still dressed in their tabards and armor.

"And then I said, I said: hands off the councilor’s property!"

The other burped. "Bet they didn’t like that!”

“No they didn’t! They went all pale and splotchy across the cheeks, like those rats do.” The guardsman made a noise like something was fighting up his throat. He put up a hand until it passed. “Then guess what he tried?”

“Pulled a knife—”

“He pulled a knife! Said he made if for the councilor and the old coot didn’t pay. So he was, get this, ‘takin’ it back’!”

They both laughed. Harker curled his lip.

"I assume you improved his manners."

"Oh, I did. Put my truncheon through his rotten teeth.” The drunk patted his hip, smacking into the helmet clipped there before his fingers found the heavy oaken stick at his waist. “I told him, I said, what the councilor says is theirs is theirs. It don’t matter whether they pay.”

“So long as they pay us!”

The two shoved each other, guffawing too loudly. Harker kicked a stone and it slid under the guardsman’s foot. He tripped, banging into a parked wagon on the street, truncheon and helmet clattering. It came loose. The helmet tumbled toward the street, unseen by the stumbling guards.

Harker snatched it up before it landed.

“Gimme that wine!”

“You’ve had enough, I think!”

“Nearly twisted my ankle on that loose cobble. I need it for the pain!”

The guards meandered on, arguing over their wineskin without ever looking back. Harker lifted the helmet and sniffed it. He grimaced. The padding inside stank of sweat, but he had limited options. He put it on—backwards. It was an awkward fit, and his nose was a little squashed, but it worked. The clean steel plate covered his face completely, rendering him impossible to identify. He pulled his tattered cloak up around  himself and tugged the hood up as he flared Sovereign Sight around himself.

Harker was tired of waiting. It was time to make some deals.
