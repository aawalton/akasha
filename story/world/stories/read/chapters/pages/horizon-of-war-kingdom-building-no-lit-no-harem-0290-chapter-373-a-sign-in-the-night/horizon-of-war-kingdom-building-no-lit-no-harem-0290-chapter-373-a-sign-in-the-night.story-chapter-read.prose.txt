Chapter 373

A Sign in the Night

Skodra Region

While the world’s attention was fixed on Edessa and the war against the Navalnian Empire, the war with the Mountain Clans continued in the west. Throughout 4427, the Shogunate, alongside the Gray Skull Legion, waged a separate campaign against the Clans in the Five Peaks. Despite several setbacks, the Clans remained entrenched in the mountains, and the region remained in turmoil.

The balance only began to shift after reinforcements arrived from Umberland, bringing forty-four half-kins and a hundred men under the command of Sir Hugo. One of the Lord Shogun’s first knights, Hugo had once come close to disgrace and had gone to Umberland in penance. Now married to a half-kin woman, he sought to redeem his name.

Yet Sir Hugo’s road to redemption was far from smooth. Before the Lord Shogun fell into his long sleep, he had given Sir Hugo direct orders to reinforce Clavieras. Despite those orders, Sir Hugo made a grave misjudgment. Believing the city could easily withstand a siege, he diverted his relief force toward the Gray Skull Legion instead.

The decision, made without informing anyone in the Shogunate, nearly ended in disaster. Clavieras survived only because Sir Farkas, his Black Bandits, and the freedmen rallied in time to turn back the Clansmen’s assault from the underground passages.

Once again, Sir Hugo stood on the brink of disgrace. Yet during the summer and autumn of 4427, he redeemed himself by leading an effective northern campaign against the many fortified settlements of the Five Peaks. His leadership freed Sir Servius, the latest to join the ranks of the Shogunate’s bannerets, to concentrate on the perilous northern and western borders, where the surviving mercenaries of the fallen King Nico remained a constant threat.

Fortunately, aside from several raids broken by swift and forceful counterattacks, the Skodra region saw no major conflict for the remainder of the year. The war-torn lands governed by the Gray Skull Legion were given room to recover. Fields returned to cultivation, abandoned settlements stirred back to life, and refugees settled wherever the land was safe, putting down new roots among the ruins left by the war.

By the summer of 4428, commerce had begun to flourish across the region for the first time since the Gray Skull Legion pacified the area.

Adding to this revival was a growing craze for a new dish brought from Lowlandia. Famed as the Lord Shogun’s creation, it had helped sustain the people through the past two winters. By then, the cooks of Skodra had already given it a flavor of their own. They served the pasta with small cuts of smoked meat, long peppers, and sour cherries, then covered it with grated cheese. To many, the result tasted nothing short of divine.

Many had learned the art of making pasta, and each town soon developed its own variation. The dishes became so popular that, for once, people had reason to travel simply to taste what was being served in neighboring cities. The roads grew lively, commerce increased, and inns and taverns were rebuilt, reopened, and furnished anew to welcome the growing number of travelers.

Sir Servius

The Nicopolan mercenary legion had no hereditary succession, and command rarely passed from father to son. Yet after receiving his enfeoffment as a Banneret of the Shogunate, Sir Servius was required to follow the Shogunate’s system of alternate residence. Since Servius was already too old and badly maimed to make the journey regularly, he resolved the matter by adopting his second-in-command as his son.

Servius’ family and that of his adopted son were sent to live in Korelia.

His adopted son, as the man expected to assume command of the legion after him, would travel between Korelia and Skodra in Servius’ place. Under the usual arrangement, he would spend the summer governing in Skodra, then return to Korelia before winter to rejoin his family.

However, with the war in the Five Peaks still demanding the legion’s attention, the Shogunate had granted them time to settle their affairs. The regular cycle would begin once the fighting had eased and conditions in the east had become more secure.

The arrangement satisfied everyone. Sir Servius and his legion had reclaimed East Nicopola and spent the last few years rebuilding it. The last thing they wanted was to see all that work undone by a succession crisis.

Their trust in the Shogunate had not been given blindly. Sir Servius had known the Lord Shogun only briefly, but it had been long enough for him to judge the man’s character for himself. Lansius was not like those nobles who demanded loyalty before offering anything in return. Since then, the Shogunate he founded had proven that it followed the same principle. That knowledge put the old condottiere at ease.

In truth, what the Lord Shogun had given them was so bountiful that even the Nicopolans in Skodra who had never met him could feel his benevolence. Grain, pasta, horses, weapons, and even formal recognition had all been given fairly beneath the banner of Blue and Bronze.

And despite all they had done, the Shogunate had never sent anyone to rule over them, nor burdened them with endless demands in return.

Thus came the saying in Skodra that only an ingrate would reject the Blue and Bronze.

For Sir Servius and his people, the House of Korelia was worthy of their trust, even while its Lord remained asleep.

Seated before his workbench, Sir Servius sighed at the thought, drawing the attention of the man half his age beside him.

“Just a little longer, Sir,” the soldier-infirmarian assured him.

“No, no,” Sir Servius replied quickly. “Take your time. I was merely thinking about the lord.”

The infirmarian nodded. He was tending to the end of Sir Servius’ right arm, carefully washing the healed stump with warm water and a little olive-oil soap. Once it was clean, he dried the skin with a soft cloth and rubbed in a thin layer of beeswax salve to keep it from cracking. Only then did he wrap it in clean linen and begin fastening the wooden hand into place.

Sir Servius had long since discarded the iron hook he once wore. Its replacement had been carved from light wood into the shape of a hand, then fitted with leather so that, from a distance, it resembled a gloved hand rather than a lifeless piece of timber.

More than just a fixed sculpture, the hand had several practical functions. A set of tensioned wires ran through its interior, each connected to the jointed fingers and accessible through a leather flap on the back of the palm. By pulling one wire and latching it in place, Servius could draw the fingers into a particular position suited to a specific task.

One position left the forefinger extended while the others remained closed, allowing him to point or use it much as he once used the hook. Another formed a narrow grip around a spoon. A stronger setting closed the fingers around the hilt of a sword, while the simplest left the palm open, whether to steady an object or wave to someone across the room.

Its use was limited, but it was far better than the crude hook he had worn before.

Sir Servius examined the prosthetic hand and turned his wrist slightly, testing its weight and fit. Finding it firmly secured, he gave a satisfied nod and rested it upon the arm of his chair.

With the hand finished, the infirmarian moved on to tend his missing leg.

The old condottiere relaxed into his seat. It was still early in the day, and although matters of governance continued to occupy him, life had grown calmer than it had been. The concerns of war had gradually given way to disputes over land, debts, contracts, and inheritance. Fortunately, he now had the Shogunate bailiffs to deal with much of it.

Settling disputes had never come easily to him. Each judgment drained his patience and his wits, while almost always earning him the resentment of whoever lost the case. Matters became especially dangerous whenever one side included members of the legion, for even a fair ruling could be taken as favoritism or betrayal.

Now, he could leave such work to the bailiffs.

Their presence also strengthened Servius’ authority to govern.

While he was a lesser noble, as were his second-in-command and many senior members of the legion, their titles did not grant them the right to govern a territory as vast as East Nicopola. With the Imperium gone, no surviving authority remained to formally grant them such a right. In this regard, the Shogunate’s recognition lent their rule some legitimacy and made their claim far more credible.

And since the Lord Shogun accepted the title of Count from the Duke, his House and all those sworn beneath it gained greater standing.

On Sir Servius’ desk lay the letter that had brought Lord Lansius to mind. It had been dictated by the man himself nearly two years ago and later given to him by Sir Hugo as proof of the campaign in the Five Peaks. Since then, the old condottiere had kept it close at hand among his most important papers.

He remembered reading the letter for the first time and immediately recognizing the boldness of its strategy. Only Lord Lansius would have dared propose such a thing.

One line read, “It doesn’t matter if Clavieras falls. With the Clans determined to retake it, our attack from the north will have its chance. Now is the time for a breakthrough.”

He couldn’t help but smirk. The same letter explained that the Grey Legion lacked the men needed to complete the northern attack. Lansius had also rejected a general assault that might have left the legion badly mauled, declaring that he would rather see those men alive in Skodra than dead for no good reason in the mountains.

Only a highly capable tactician could have made such a decision. Most commanders cared only about victory, especially when they believed their cause justified the cost.

Not Lansius.

The man thought of his soldiers first, even at the expense of victory.

And that was what made him dangerous. He would not squander the lives of men who were not even his own. In return, many within the Grey Legion had come to love him and would willingly die for him.

Yet now, the Lich’s curse had condemned him never to wake again.

“Such a young soul,” Sir Servius lamented.

“Still thinking of the Lord of Korelia?” the infirmarian asked sympathetically.

“Often,” he admitted. They had spoken of the matter before.

The infirmarian nodded. He, too, admired the man. He had received his medical training from Dawn's men, made possible through the cooperation of the Shogunate.

“We might have risen to heights beyond even the Third Imperium, had such a man been granted a long life," Sir Servius muttered.

“He is only asleep,” the infirmarian said.

“I long to go to Korelia and see him with my own eyes,” Sir Servius admitted. “But I fear I would find him cold, with little left but bone and skin.”

“The letters from Korelia say otherwise. His flesh has not wasted, and his limbs remain sound despite the long sleep. The physicians there consider it remarkable.”

Sir Servius nodded, and the infirmarian continued wrapping the bandages.

After a moment, the old man chuckled as another memory from the campaign returned to him. “He is such a clever bastard. Who would have thought the Clans could be beaten that way? He practically made them dance to his tune. First, he used our Gray Legion as a diversion, allowing Farkas to seize Clavieras. Then he used Clavieras itself as the diversion, giving the Gray Legion its chance to attack after the half-kins arrived.”

“So that was how we won the Five Peaks?” the infirmarian asked, his surprise plain.

“I suspect there was another part to it as well...”

This was something they had never discussed before, and the infirmarian naturally looked at him with curiosity.

“You see, the Lord was already on friendly terms with Lady Beatrix, yet he never asked the half-kins to support us. Not until the main attack. I suspect he had always intended to keep the Umberlanders as his reserve. He even placed Sir Hugo among them, giving him time to earn their trust and become familiar with their ways.”

The infirmarian paused, visibly taken aback. “Sir, you believe the Lord had planned that far ahead? That would mean preparing for this campaign years in advance.”

“If it was him, anything is possible.” Sir Servius chuckled, his wooden right hand trembling lightly against the table.

Clearly pleased to have pieced the strategy together, he continued, “He deliberately gave us only the barest assistance, enough to keep us in the fight but not enough to alarm the enemy. That allowed the Clans to believe they faced only the Gray Legion. Then, when the moment came, he suddenly sent us three dozen half-kins as reinforcements. It was deception laid upon deception.”

For a moment, the infirmarian said nothing. “That was ruthless.”

“Indeed, he is.” A note of longing entered the old man’s voice. “I enjoyed his strategy immensely.”

As the infirmarian finished the treatment, he said, “Perhaps the day will come when he wakes again. If the old songs are to be believed, such men return when the realm stands upon the eve of its greatest peril.”

Servius allowed himself a scoff. “If he did, that would make him a legend.”

A knock sounded at the door.

“Come in,” Sir Servius called.

His squire entered with a grave expression. “Sir, an urgent report.”

Sir Servius braced himself. “Speak.”

“The eastern front has flared again. Reports say the Margrave has betrayed our forces.”

Sir Servius and the infirmarian could only stare at him.

“Betrayal?” Servius echoed in disbelief. His face seemed to age in an instant. “The Margrave?”

After a moment’s hesitation, the squire nodded. He too wished the report were wrong.

“The Shogunate marched to their aid, and this is how they repay us?” Servius demanded. “What manner of ally begs to be pulled from the mire, only to bite the hand that saved him?”

The squire could only answer weakly, “The full dispatch is still being deciphered, sir.”

The situation was grave, yet it was also so absurd that a dry chuckle escaped Sir Servius. Somehow, the eastern war had managed to descend into an even greater disaster.

“But there is one thing, sir.”

“What?”

“One portion has already been deciphered. Castle Korelia reports that every available reinforcement is on its way—”

“Of course they are. Those men in Edessa will need all the help they can get. By the Ancients, they can’t stand against both the Margrave and the Empire.”

The squire added after a slight hesitation, “The dispatch also says that Korelia is leading the force.”

The old condottiere frowned. “Korelia?”

The squire nodded. “I checked twice. That is what it says.”

“What does that mean?” Sir Servius murmured. A possibility stirred in his mind, bringing with it a sudden glimmer of hope, but he dismissed it almost at once. He sighed and muttered, “Well, there is nothing we can do from here but pray to the Ancients. If anyone could turn defeat into victory...”

His voice trailed off. He leaned to one side in his chair, murmuring, “Korelia...” Then he looked directly at the infirmarian. “Is it even possible?”

The infirmarian had no answer. This time, however, Servius chose to believe.

Turning to his squire, Sir Servius ordered, “Have the full dispatch deciphered at once. I’ll prepare our reply. They can leave the defense of this region to the Umberlanders and us. They need those Black Bandits more than we do.”

Edessa - White Lake Border

Corna Castle

It was near midnight when the Bat, the largest airship they currently possessed, arrived over Corna. Its gondola carried a full load of agents, officials, and injured men, some accompanied by their families, evacuated from Ostaria and the surrounding area. The rear guard under Karl had fought bravely, but withdrawing in good order had proved a nightmare, especially while protecting the refugees. More than once, the retreat had nearly become a rout.

Under the relentless attacks of the Empire’s mounted irregulars, the timely intervention of the airship had helped prevent the rear guard from breaking and being annihilated.

The airship had not escaped unscathed. The defensive line had grown dangerously thin in several places, allowing the irregulars to break through and nearly reach the airship from the flank. Arrows still jutted from its gondola, while its delicate outer skin was pierced by countless small holes.

Only when the fields stretching south of the road from Ostaria had all been set ablaze under Sir Harold’s orders did the thousands of enemy horsemen finally withdraw, driven back by the intense heat and smoke. When the Bat departed for Corna, the dry summer grasslands below were still burning.

Now, with the mage-aviator Angelo at the helm, the Bat slowly descended toward the field outside Corna Castle.

From the castle battlements, Lord Avery rested one fist upon the stonework and watched the airship descend.

“Move, move!” his captain shouted below, urging his men forward as the Bat touched down.

Lord Avery watched Angelo rise from the pilot’s seat and immediately begin directing the people he had rescued from the Empire as they were helped out of the battered gondola.

Beyond the landing field, mounted patrols continued circling the perimeter even in the dead of night. No one dared grow complacent. Corna might remain in Shogunate hands, but it no longer stood amid friendly territory. The ambush of the relief column a few days earlier had made the Margrave’s betrayal plain for all to see.

His own guards were no less alert. Along the walls of the castle and the city, sentries watched the darkness while armed men stood ready near the gates.

Soon, they too would need to brace for an attack.

The Margrave would likely attempt to cut off the Shogunate’s retreat. Corna, after all, was their main staging ground in the province.

The cold night wind swept across the battlements, tugging at Lord Avery’s cloak and cutting through the tunic beneath. He had witnessed enough tragedy for one day, and this kind left a bitter taste. The Lord of Dawn was a man accustomed to visiting ruin upon his enemies, not watching tragedy consume his allies. With a weary sigh, he turned toward the stairs, hoping to claim a few hours of sleep before the next wave of grim reports reached him at dawn.

Before he had taken more than a few steps, the lookouts began to stir. A murmur passed along the battlements, then quickly rose into a commotion. Several guards pointed upward, while another broke into a run toward the old lord. There was no need. Avery had already turned to follow their gaze.

Far in the distance, an unmistakable light moved above the darkened plains.

His squire promptly offered him an Ekionian optic. The old lord raised it to his eye and made out the vessel beneath the brighter light. Its main lamp, powered by a gemstone, cast a broad radiance over the road and surrounding plains.

He recognized the airship. It was his House’s greatest treasure, a legacy envisioned by its first generation, then built and finally made to fly by his own.

To his surprise, another, smaller vessel followed behind it. Its lamp was weaker, but still cast enough light to illuminate the surrounding area.

“Oh, interesting,” Lord Avery muttered. “So, the Bank has decided to throw its weight around too.”

The captain of his guard stood beside him, a stout man who was shorter than most but powerfully built, with a square jaw and the thick neck of a bull. “My Lord, do you think this will make a difference?”

“Anything that helps me paint a beautiful tragedy for the enemy is always welcome,” he jested.

Yet beneath the jest, he knew the truth. The Shogunate positions across Edessa were already as good as overrun. Corna was their last foothold in the province, and they would have to hold it while keeping the road to Derenza open.

The arrival of two airships was certainly welcome, but Avery, more than anyone, knew their limitations. Over the past two years, new workshops had opened, and many skilled hands had entered their service. Yet the workshops supplying and supporting the airships still struggled to keep pace with demand.

The near-constant warfare of the past several years had not allowed their precious stores of naphtha to recover. Of all their weapons, the sticky fire was the one the Empire truly feared.

Even with assistance from the powerful Alchemy Guild of Midlandia, refining naphtha remained a delicate, dangerous, and time-consuming process. Moreover, the same raw materials and skilled alchemists were needed to maintain the production of volatile oil, the fuel upon which every airship depended.

The House’s Office of Works had sanctioned further research, but such experiments could not be hurried.

Three burners of a new design, inspired by the engine aboard the Silver Airship, had already been built in Ekionia and delivered to Dawn. A workshop had been set aside to test them on the ground, for none of their airships could be spared for experiments. Even the three smaller vessels ordered by the Bank and completed over the past two years were frequently borrowed by the Shogunate to support the war in Edessa.

With these thoughts weighing upon him, Avery turned toward the stairs. He needed to descend and make ready to receive the new arrivals. The presence of the silver airship almost certainly meant that members of the Shogunate Council had come, most likely Sir Michael and the Lord of Galdia.

They would want to know what had happened and what might yet be done. Lord Avery feared they would soon discover the truth for themselves.

Edessa was a disaster waiting to happen.

By his own calculation, 7,000 infantry, 2,000 horsemen, and some 100,000 refugees were fleeing across the province. That was before counting the forces stationed at Ostaria and Derenza, or the people still sheltering within their walls. Knowing the Shogunate’s commanders, they would do their damnedest to play the heroes and save as many people as they could.

But sooner or later, after enough blood had been shed, Sir Harold and his column would have to make their escape. Their dense pike squares might march stubbornly onward and keep the enemy at bay, but the road to safety was long.

“Eight days,” Lord Avery muttered to himself, calculating how long the column would need to reach Corna from Ostaria by way of Derenza.

The Margrave’s betrayal had left their prospects bleak. For now, the best Avery could do was load as much naphtha as he could aboard the Bat and put the Margrave’s castle in Buda to the torch. Still, he doubted the Margrave slept beneath his own roof. The man was likely staying elsewhere out of fear for his life.

As Avery made his way down, one of the lookouts called from above, “My Lord, there are lights on the road.”

Lord Avery stopped and turned. “The road?”

His guards exchanged uncertain glances. The captain of his guard climbed back to the battlements, peered into the darkness, and then turned to his lord. “Carts and carriages are coming this way.”

The old lord frowned. The approaching airships he could understand, but the convoy on the road was another matter. By its very nature, the Shogunate Council rarely acted with such speed or initiative. That was also why its members trusted men like him and the bannerets to conduct the war.

Moreover, to time the carts so they arrived at Corna alongside the airships was no easy task. Airships were much faster, did not tire as horses did, and were far less hindered by terrain. The difficulty would have been even greater at night, when horses often slowed or refused to continue if they could not see the path ahead.

Even with the light from the airships, the carts would still have needed riders ahead to guide them. The more Avery thought about it, the more convinced he became that this was a complex operation, requiring careful planning, decisiveness, and, above all, great authority. The Council rarely displayed all three.

This is no mere inspection by the Council.

Someone has a plan.

For the first time that night, a spark of hope stirred within him. As he continued down the stairs, Avery reached into his inner pocket for the dull silver locket, only to find it missing. He had left it in his chamber. It had been days since he had last communicated with his granddaughter.

He quickened his pace toward his chamber, issuing orders as he went. “Wake the staff. Prepare the hall. We have great company coming.”

His grin returned. He could feel it in his bones. The wind was changing, and he could smell blood on it again. His enemies’ blood.

...

The preparations had taken time. By the time everything was ready, Lord Avery stood upon the landing field with a small delegation of the Men of Dawn, several Shogunate officers, and a detachment from the garrison. Angelo stood beside him, visibly exhausted and still troubled by the horrors he had witnessed in Ostaria. Even so, he was no less curious about who was coming.

Dozens of tall torches and lanterns illuminated the field. Patrols had been doubled around the perimeter, while lines of men-at-arms stood ready to protect the arrivals.

The silver airship had broken away from the smaller vessel and the convoy below. Now it approached the landing field and began to descend. From the way it maneuvered, they recognized the hand at the helm. The pilot could only be the Hunter retained by their House, the one assigned to Lady Ella.

Once it touched down, the landing crew burst from the rear of the gondola and scrambled to secure the airship with ropes. The ground crew immediately rushed forward to assist them.

Even before the passengers began to disembark, the ground crew had already set to work. Small gemstone lamps set within polished silver housings were directed at the vessel, illuminating its delicate skin for inspection. Meanwhile, several hundred paces away, another group continued to work on the Bat, whose outer skin had been torn in places and punctured by arrows.

“Who is coming?” Angelo asked.

“Korelia,” Avery replied, deliberately keeping the rest to himself. He had already communicated with his granddaughter through the dwarven locket and knew exactly who was coming.

Angelo did not press him. He assumed the Lady of Korimor herself had come. She was fearless and fierce, and her presence alone would raise morale. Many of the Shogunate’s officers and soldiers would gladly risk their lives at her bidding.

Then Ocelot emerged from the covered gondola. The half-kin’s appearance only strengthened Angelo’s belief that the Lady had come. Murmurs and startled gasps passed through those assembled as two more half-kins emerged behind him.

Nine men dressed in dull black followed, their figures difficult to distinguish in the poor light. Those waiting recognized them as the SAR.

“Quite a number of them,” Angelo remarked, unable to hide his surprise.

Hidden by the darkness, Lord Avery smirked.

Nearly a dozen guards emerged next, followed by five young squires.

Then two cloaked figures stepped out of the vessel together.

The guards immediately closed around the pair. Behind them came Sir Michael, the Lord of Galdia, and Lord Jorge, with the hood of a heavy cloak drawn over his head. Their squires and command staff followed close behind.

Angelo frowned. His night sight clearly revealed two cloaked figures walking side by side. He had caught a glimpse of the Lady’s face, but he did not recognize the man beside her. “Who is with the Lady?”

Lord Avery did not answer.

Puzzled by his silence, Angelo turned toward his lord and found him wearing the widest grin he had ever seen. It was as though someone had just given him a thousand amphorae filled with naphtha.

A wild suspicion seized the mage. He took an involuntary step backward and cried out, “Impossible!”

His reaction drew the attention of the officers, guards, and other men around them. Several followed his stare toward the cloaked pair, while others leaned closer together and began trading guesses in low voices.

Angelo quickly returned his gaze to the man walking beside the Lady. To his astonishment, the stranger seemed to feel his scrutiny. Even at that distance, and despite the guards surrounding him, the man turned and looked directly at Angelo.

Their eyes met for a heartbeat, and it unnerved Angelo. Not even a mage-knight could have sensed a gaze across such a distance, amid so many moving bodies and so much confusion.

The hooded man offered Angelo a courteous smile before his guards closed ranks and blocked him from view.

Only then did Angelo feel he recognized the face beneath the hood. His spine went rigid, his jaw fell open, and both hands flew to his head.

Noticing his expression, Lord Avery broke into laughter, startling the ranks of men already murmuring over the grand procession before them.

Angelo remained speechless. He forced himself to look again and noticed the man’s cloak bulging around a small shape cradled against his chest. A sleeping baby, wrapped snugly in cloth, rested in his arms.

A gasp escaped him. That erased all doubt.

Amid the grand procession walked a man who had not walked the lands of Aqua Terra for nearly two years. Angelo had tried several times to awaken him, and every attempt had failed. In the end, he had judged the man a dead end, a hopeless cause.

But now, the man walked with firm steps. His back was straighter, his shoulders broader than Angelo remembered. Strong enough, it seemed, to bear the weight of the collapsing Edessan campaign.

Behind him, beneath the distant airship lights, what appeared to be a hundred carts drew near, escorted by hundreds of nomad riders. Only as the convoy approached did its dozens of lanterns and torches become visible, stretching along the road toward Castle Corna.

A hard night wind swept in from the plains, whipping the blue-and-bronze battle standards along the city walls and atop the castle.

Korelia had come.
