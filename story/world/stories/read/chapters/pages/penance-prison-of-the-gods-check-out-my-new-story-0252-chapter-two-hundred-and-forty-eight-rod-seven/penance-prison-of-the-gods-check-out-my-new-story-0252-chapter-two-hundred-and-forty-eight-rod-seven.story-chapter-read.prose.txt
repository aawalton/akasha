We found each other by accident.

One turn through a jagged corridor, and suddenly Halver was there, and Syla wasn't; He was half-slumped against a wall of cracked glass, hammer dragging behind him, his face streaked with sweat and blood. I let out a shaky breath I didn’t know I was holding. He didn’t speak, just nodded once, jaw a vise holding back more than pain. The bruises on his arms told enough of a story.

The room we stepped into was wide and circular, some kind of warped arena. The walls weren’t stone but mirrors, broken and stacked in crooked tiers, each shard catching the light and throwing it wrong. My reflection stared at me from a hundred angles, all a little off. When I moved, they didn’t move with me; some moved with a delay, while others blinked when I didn’t. One version of me stood completely still, eyes wide and mouth open in a silent scream. I turned away, unsettled; every motion, however, was echoed wrong: tilted heads, twitching fingers, a loop a second behind real time. It was a projection of memories onto broken glass, none of them quite me.

The floor clicked with every step, humming low, a grudging acknowledgment of our presence. And worse, the air pulsed. It seemed to breathe, a thick, rhythmic pulse pressing against my skin with the dry heat of a forge.

A deep, resonant gong echoed once, from no discernible source, just sound. Then words rippled across the nearest wall, etched in pulsing silver:“Only rage may open the way.”

“Great,” I muttered. “That’s exactly what we needed.”

{Oh, how delightful. A trial of anger. My least favorite kind: too loud, too wet, and never half as clever as they think,} Aurentum drawled.

“You want to come down here and try it?”

{Oh no, Roddy. I prefer the gallery seats. More dramatic lighting,} Aurentum replied.

I glanced at Halver. He hadn’t read it; he didn’t need to. His shoulders were already tensing, fingers tightening on the grip of his hammer.

“I don’t like this place,” he muttered.

I nodded slowly, eyes sweeping the arena.

“It likes you.”

I could feel it watching us, whatever it was, not the gladiator or the copies, but the room itself. It felt aware, expectant, as though it had curled itself inward to study us, the kind of attention that prickled across your skin, devoid of hatred or hunger, showing only interest, the way a child might study a bug before pulling off its wings. This wasn’t a fight; it was a question.

Something behind the mirrors shifted with a low grind. The smell of dust and sweat filled my nose.

We weren’t alone anymore, not by a long shot.

It stepped out of the wall as if it had always been there, waiting. Eight feet tall, wide as a forge wall, covered in mirrored plates warped by heat, a gladiator, but wrong. Its head was too small, its arms too long, and in its hands: Halver’s hammer, or a twisted version of it, bigger and bloodier, crackling with red veins of light that pulsed in sync with the room.

Halver didn’t hesitate. He roared, surged forward, and brought his hammer down in a clean, brutal arc, enough force to split stone, enough rage to shake the room.

The copy caught it. It wasn't blocked or deflected; it was caught.

There was a shimmer of light where the two weapons met, and the sound wasn’t a clash; it was a thud, a sound deadened as if by a sponge soaking up the force. Then the copy shoved Halver back with one massive palm, hard enough to send him skidding a foot across the glass floor.

I moved to cover him, but another shimmer caught my eye: more shapes, stepping out of the walls, two, no, three more of them, all different, all wrong. One grinned, relishing pain. One was already bleeding. The third looked calm, and that one was the worst of all.

The arena was copying him, not just his strength, but his states, every version of Halver it could pull from memory, twisted into something cruel.

He growled beside me, eyes narrowing. “They want a fight?”

“No,” I said slowly, watching the way the reflections waited, breathing with us. “They want to see if you break first.”

The first one charged, fast for something so massive. I barely had time to react before Halver met it mid-sprint with a full-body slam that cracked the floor beneath them.

Glass exploded outward.

I dodged behind a broken pillar, a desperate barrier to deflect the shards, and even then one sliced a line across my cheek. Halver didn’t notice the shrapnel, or maybe he didn’t care. He was locked in, grunting with each blow, his hammer a pendulum marking brutal time, steel on glass, muscle on muscle. The copy grinned as it fought, grinning wider when Halver’s blows landed. Pain meant nothing to it; it thrived on it.

Another version stalked closer, its hair blood-matted, a wild gleam in its eye. It looked feral, dragging its weapon along the ground with a scraping screech. I stepped forward, staff raised, but Halver barked, “No.”

His voice was hoarse and low. “I got this.”

The floor vibrated again, responding to his anger, and I realized what the message on the wall meant. This place wasn’t just testing strength; it was feeding off it.

“It’s feeding,” I said under my breath. “It’s actually feeding.”

{Mmm. Look at it eat. Rage is such fast fuel, don’t you think? Burns hot, burns bright, leaves nothing behind but ash and ego,} Aurentum observed.

“Shut up and let me focus.”

“Halver,” I said carefully, “if you lose control…”

“Then stay out of my way.”

Another blow rang out, louder this time. He caught the feral copy mid-leap and shattered it, glass and gore splattering the arena. But as it fell, two more stepped forward, molded from the fragments. There was no pause, no mercy, just more fuel for the fire. And I realized the trap: the more Halver fought, the more he gave them to become.

I’d never seen Halver falter before.

He fought like a man trying to outrun his own shadow, hammer flying, blood spraying, the floor cracked and steaming. But for every reflection he shattered, two more stepped forward, sharper and crueler, forged from the fragments he left behind. They weren’t just mimicking his strength; they were distilling his anger.

Sweat ran down his temples. His knuckles were bleeding, his shoulders heaving with every breath. I could see it now: the way his grip faltered between swings, the way he flinched when glass caught his ribs. He was still moving, still striking, but the rhythm was wrong, not the measured cadence of a warrior in control, but the panicked tempo of someone trying to outrun something inside. The line between Halver and the copies was starting to blur. And worse, I think he felt it, too. He struck a reflection so hard it crumpled and screamed as he did, not in pain, but in frustration, the kind that eats you from the inside.

“Halver…” I clenched my jaw. That scream hadn’t been pain; it was worse than that.

{Ah. There it is. The good scream. That’s not rage anymore, is it? That’s failure trying to pretend it’s still in control,} Aurentum purred.

“You don’t get tired of the sound of your own voice, do you?”

{I’ve been in the heads of better men than Halver; warriors, kings. You know what broke them? Swords didn't, nor did sorrow. Shame did,} Aurentum said, his voice smooth.

“He’s not broken.”

{No. But he’s close enough to taste,} Aurentum taunted.

I stepped forward, staff tight in my grip. “Halver, listen to me…”

“Don’t,” he growled, a warning in his voice.

The floor pulsed beneath us, the walls shimmering, the surface of water about to boil. And on the mirrored wall, the inscription shifted, new words, clearer now:Power without control is just destruction.

Halver didn’t even look. He was breathing hard, wild-eyed, as another copy lunged. He met it mid-swing, but he was too slow. The thing’s blade raked his side. He staggered, hissed through clenched teeth, and hit it anyway, shattering it with a downward slam. But his footing was off. His hands were shaking, his stance slipping, blood pooling in the crook of his elbow.

The next reflection stepped forward, bearing no weapon, just calm. It looked like him, with the same scars and tired eyes, but it stood straighter, quiet and grounded. Something about this one was different. It didn’t posture. It didn’t bleed. It just stood, calm and unshaking, a memory that had never flinched.

“You forgot what kept you standing,” it said. “You think this rage is strength?”

Halver’s shoulders slumped.

And I realized the trial wasn't about rage; it was about owning it.

I moved before Halver could stop me, stepping between him and the reflection. The copy didn’t flinch; it just watched me, as if it had already won. It didn’t need to swing. It just needed to exist, to be the version of him that hadn’t lost control, and that was enough to break him.

I slammed the butt of my staff between them.

“Not like this,” I said, quiet but firm. “You’re not losing because you’re not strong. You’re losing because you forgot who you are.”

Halver glared at me, teeth grit, blood trickling from his lip. “You think I’m holding back?”

“No. I think you’re giving too much away.”

The copy watched us silently, unblinking. I stepped forward and faced it, not to fight, but to understand. And I saw it then: this was the truth the room had been baiting Halver toward all along, not rage or fire, but the stillness underneath it, the control that forged the fury into purpose.

I turned back to him. “It’s not your anger that makes you dangerous, Halver. It’s the way you carry it.”

He stared at me, breathing hard. Then something in his stance shifted, just barely, his shoulders squaring, his hands steadying on the haft of his hammer.

The reflection nodded, then, impossibly, bowed, an act of respect, not mockery, as if acknowledging the truth Halver had almost forgotten.

The arena rippled. Mirrors cracked, their fractures long and graceful rather than violent, ice melting in the sun. The floor stopped pulsing, and the trial was over.

{Well, well. That’s a first. Usually your kind flail until the room chews them to paste,} Aurentum commented.

“Maybe he’s not like the others.”

{No, Rod Argent. He is exactly like the others. He just got lucky enough to have you in the room,} Aurentum countered.

I didn’t answer. It wasn't because I agreed, but more because I wasn’t sure I could disagree.

I looked around the arena, half expecting the walls to reset or the floor to swallow us whole, but nothing happened. The silence felt earned. The reflections were gone, vanished rather than shattered or dismissed, as if they’d never been real; maybe they weren’t, never separate from Halver to begin with.

I glanced at him. He hadn’t moved yet, just stood there, hammer lowered, staring at the place where the last version of him had bowed. Halver exhaled, long and low, then grunted, his voice dry. “Guess I forgot that part.”

“Yeah,” I said. “That’s what I’m here for.”
