We were past the winter solstice, and the sun wasn’t setting nearly as early, but it was still dark by the time I met with Moonhand. I found him behind the cooper’s shed. This time, he wore no cloak, and there was no illusory veil covering his face.

As soon as I saw him, I whispered, “Why couldn’t you tell me?”

“It would have made the test different. You would have guarded your mind better. I wouldn’t have been able to examine your will to win.”

I scowled. “You looked into my mind.”

“Yes, and I cannot say I particularly approve of some thoughts you have had about Princess Alis.”

“Hey,” I hissed. “If you tell her—”

“We have bigger problems, and less time than you think.”

I inhaled through clenched teeth, then said, “Fine, then. So you are a Subtle Man?”

“I was. I was one of the best, and one of the organization’s early founders. I built it in service to King Aelfwine, my friend. As Subtle Men, we were to serve the king, investigating other nations, being his eyes and ears all around the world. But Aelfwine, no matter how powerful he was, could not live forever. Two centuries passed, and his maegen dwindled. His heirs were not as strong-willed as him. As the Warlord and his lineage kept gaining power and kept abandoning our old institutions, our gaze drifted within our borders. Paranoid about rebellion, we spied on our own people, stamping out discontent wherever we saw it.”

I tilted my head. “Men can’t become immortal, even if they’re really powerful?”

Moonhand hmed, then said. “Of course not. They must keep getting stronger if they are to live forever, and this world cannot sustain them. So they are forced to ascend beyond it. Aelfwine chose not to ascend, and he died an old man on his throne.”

I nodded. “So…you joined the Witenagemot?” As I spoke, I drew half my mind into my memory palace. It would take months to sort all of those memories, but I could at least do my best to not make it worse. I imagined thousands of tiny shelves lining the inside of the sphere, and each of them was home to a certain category of orbs. I sorted this current memory into what I was calling ‘profiles’—what I knew about the people I spent the most time around. Slowly, I was building up files of information about them.

Moonhand continued. “I wished to be free from the Subtle Men, and I had served the nation well for centuries. I played my part, and I didn’t let my discontent show. With all I knew, and all I had done, the Warlord could not simply send me back to Ohnethil. So instead, he gave me a comfortable position on the Witenagemot. Over the centuries, I have garnered a reputation for picking Ealdormen well. Sometimes, I am overruled. However, Lord Ceonwin was one of my choices, and it seems he has done his job handily.”

“How’d Hinlaw get in charge, then?”

“I don’t always get my way.”

“But you’re certain you could put me in charge?”

“There is discontent on the council regarding Hinlaw. They see him as weak after the trial-joust fiasco. Were I to suggest a replacement, they would agree with me in a heartbeat, trusting my experience to repair the reputational damage done by appointing Hinlaw. I would tell them what they want to hear. Whatever is necessary for them to agree to grant you the position of Ealdorman.”

“Once he dies,” I began.

Moonhand shook his head. “Before. We are already looking for a replacement. Your position will be ratified as soon as he dies. Of course, the council won’t know that we are planning on slaying him.”

“We?”

“You. My part is in Eraen-Kalora.”

“Of course,” I said. “Then it’s settled? I landed a hit on you? You will get me appointed?”

“Yes,” he replied. “You…may not be the wisest, nor the strongest, however, you have a deep resolve below the surface. You must nurture it. Bring it forward. Cast aside the doubt. And when your moment comes, don’t hesitate.”

“My moment? Like…when I face Hinlaw?”

“Perhaps. All great men experience a choice. No matter how impossible things seem, how hard it feels to keep trudging through the darkness, they keep pushing. That’s what separates the great from the mediocre. That’s the moment. It’s when you’re at your most desperate, lowest point that you have the choice.”

I folded my arms. “That almost sounds like a threat.”

“I call it experience. There’s greatness in all of us, if you choose to nurture it. If you do your duty and listen to my wisdom, Hinlaw shall die. Then, next, the Warlord.”

“You sound like you’re leaving. We still have a week of training—”

Before I could finish, a bell tolled from the keep. It wasn’t to signal meal times or a wakeup call. It wasn’t the right time.

“I wouldn’t be so certain about that,” Moonhand said. “It is likely I will not see you again. Be careful. Survive, and execute your will. If you fail, you will doom the entirety of this nation to decay.”

I glanced over at the keep, then back at Moonhand, but when I looked back, he was gone.

I stepped back, moving out of the shade, and turned to run back to the keep, hoping to find out what the commotion was.

Before I could move, though, I came face-to-face with another Dupe. Habit.

“Who were you talking to?” Habit demanded.

“I wasn’t talking to anyone,” I countered.

“I heard you talking to someone. I didn’t hear much…” His heart raced. I could feel it in my Perception. That didn’t mean much, though. There were hundreds of reasons someone’s heart could be racing.

Namely, the tolling bell.

“Look, we don’t have time for this. What’s the bell about?” I asked.

“There’s a wagon approaching. Bad news, it seems. Lord Ceonwin wants a full muster in front of the mess hall.”

“Then we should go,” I said.

“Levi, who were you talking to?”

“No one,” I insisted. There was really no way I could explain it, and there was no excuse he would believe. Some lie about me talking to myself? It would just make me look more guilty. The only option I had was gaslighting. “Look behind the shed if you want. There’s no one. Hell, I know you have a Perception Skill. Use it. Try to sense something. You can’t. So let’s go.”

Without waiting for Habit, I set off toward the mess hall.

Dupes streamed toward it from all across the castle, and only about half of us had arrived by the time I made it in. However, a wagon had stopped in the open space in front of the keep. It had an open top, and the horses pulling it were ragged. The drivers had pushed them too hard.

I rushed over to the wagon and peered over the edge.

Four lieutenants lay on their backs inside the wagon, wrapped in bandages, lips stained green from healing potions. For three of them, it hadn’t been enough. Their flesh was pale, and their limbs had stiffened up. One, Lieutenant Ersatz, breathed shallow, ragged breaths, but he wasn’t conscious.

I rose up on my tip-toes. Lieutenant Owl was among the dead.

The blood drained from my face, and I stepped back. Something had killed Owl?

I had been the one who suggested sending them out. And now they were gone.

Lord Ceonwin was talking with the wagon’s drivers, but when he finished, I rushed over to him. “What’s happening, sir?”

Lord Ceonwin wrung his hands together. “Orc Hangmen ambushed them. The lines finally broke. Greencloaks intervened, helping some of our men escape, but they lost most of their force in the ambush.”

“Hangmen are strong?”

“You don’t want to meet one, Sir Levi. For most, they are a death sentence. It sounds like the most powerful Greencloaks, including Askan, were busy elsewhere, dealing with a distraction. By the time they intervene to slay the Hangmen, it will be too late. The line has already crumbled. Orcs are already heading our way, and they will arrive by morning. The surviving lieutenants were sent to warn us.”

I glanced around. The space in front of the mess hall was almost full. “What about the 294th?”

“Slowbend is under siege. They are holding, but they are currently isolated, and we can’t afford to help them at the moment. The trainers are already departing, citing Presence treaties and frankly, an unwillingness to be involved in Gate’s war.”

“Cowards,” I grumbled. At least Moonhand had a good reason to be leaving, though—he needed to get back to the Witenagemot. After a short pause, I said, “Alright, but the 294th—”

“They are well-armed, Sir Levi. They have the best gear of any southern battalion, and they possess far more artillery than they ought to. They can hold on for a while, thanks to your efforts. Now, get into position, and listen for the announcement.”

“Yes, sir.”

I met up with my squad and took our formal positions along the edge of the walkway. As we waited, my mind churned. Orcs were attacking, Habit was suspicious of me, and Moonhand was gone.

What could I do about Habit? I could try to talk some reason into him. With my senses, maybe I could detect if he was lying, but I didn’t exactly have practice with that. Should I just make him disappear? Could I bring myself to do that, after how hard we’d fought beside each other? And I didn’t even know whose side he was even on.

Before I could spiral too far, Lord Ceonwin stepped to the center of the aisle, between the remaining sapper candidates. “A sizeable orc force is heading our direction. Our intelligence suggests they have Black Captains and some remaining Hangmen. They are two days out.” He crossed his arms. “Our castle is currently unarmed. Sixty candidates and the remaining staff are not enough to hold these walls. Your trainers may be Golds, but they are unwilling to fight this battle any more than I am.

“If this force of orcs bested our lieutenants, there is little we can do about it right now, without a sizable force to support us. I am ordering a full retreat. We will withdraw our training efforts from Castle Urcia and relocate them to Hyrdford.”

My eyes widened. He had to be bringing us back to my city on purpose.

“It is along the road to Steelshore. Our smithy will relocate to Hyrdford, as well as the rest of our staff. The candidates and the Drillmaster will travel to Steelshore on their own afterward to finish their training.”

The silence weighed down on me. I stared at Lord Ceonwin, and he clasped his hands together. “There is no time for debate. Head to your rooms, and follow your sergeants’ orders. I want an orderly evacuation. No one sleeps. By midnight, I expect us on the road.”
