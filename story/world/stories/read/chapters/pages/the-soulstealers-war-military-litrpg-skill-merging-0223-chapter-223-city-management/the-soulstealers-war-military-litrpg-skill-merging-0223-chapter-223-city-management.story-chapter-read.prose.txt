Though I didn’t have much time left in Steelshore, I still had plenty of other tasks to get done.

Next, I headed to the garrison to check on the local guards. Out of about the hundred militia members we’d gathered and trained in the past few months, about twenty of them had died in the siege.

Among them, the only Path-walkers were the Greencloaks, and a pair of twin brothers from a ranch who the guards had found a few months ago and had given a position within the walls. Right now, both were Steels—between the guidance from the squad I’d left behind and the Greencloaks, they’d advanced quickly.

I met with the squad I’d left behind. All of them were still alive, and each of them had taken charge of a section of the city guard.

After a few minutes of catching up, they mustered the entire city guard in the open space at the center of the barracks. They packed in, and there was just enough space for all of them inside the atrium.

As they filed in, I turned to the squad from the 294th who had led them. All of them pestered me with questions about their battalion, and all I could do was tell them I didn’t know. I wanted to promise that the walls of Slowbend were holding, but I didn’t know that either, and I couldn’t make any promises.

Next, I asked them how long they’d been under attack. As it turned out, the siege had lasted about a week and a half. Hinlaw must’ve had most of his force ready by the time he’d threatened me, hence the timing.

Finally, I said, “You performed admirably against the cultists, and on average, they’re more powerful than orcs. Slowbend has an entire battalion of Dupes like you to hold it.”

“We also had the help of poisoned arrows,” one of the Dupes provided.

“Hm?” I tilted my head.

“Hardric built a little bit of an internal economy,” the squad’s sergeant replied. “He offered to make poisoned arrows with the regular river water. They boiled it down, turning it into a paste laden with mineral dust. Really not any difficult alchemical processes, but we got some more sceats flowing inside the city, and even just a nick from one of those arrows made the cultists sluggish. For them, it made them about the same strength as a regular man again.”

“Smart…” I said.

“He was a very good investment, sir.”

“Now, onto business.” I turned to face the crowd of guards. “You all did phenomenally protecting your people, and I couldn’t be more proud of you. I have to extend my heartfelt thanks to everything you have done…”

I paused. I knew people here didn’t clap, but there had to be a way to express my gratitude.

They gazed at me with expectant eyes. No one was in the mood to cheer. Lots of them had lost friends in the siege.

“In a matter of weeks, the threats from the east will be gone,” I said. “I will bring justice to those who did this to your city. It’s a promise. I hope by now, you know I keep my promises—always. Those who fell today and in the past few days that you’ve been under siege will not have died in vain. They’ll have made a better future for Hyrdford.

“I can’t promise a smooth ride, though.” This time, I pointed to the east. “Orcs are coming. I don’t know when they’ll arrive, or how many, but for the foreseeable future, Hyrdford will be a bastion on the frontline. I know everyone here will continue to fight as if your lives and futures depend on it.

“Lord Ceonwin will be leading the defence, meaning you are in good hands. I’ve known him for the past year or so, and I know he’ll do everything he can to hold the line here.”

I was about to add, I want you to follow him as if he was me, but given how little time I’d spent here, I didn’t know if that would really appeal to them properly. Besides, how much of Lord Hinlaw’s whispering had they heard?

“I hope most of you can see through Hinlaw’s lies,” I said. “To him, there’s no such thing as truth. But you all saw the cultists with your own eyes. He’s lying about them. He’s lying about me as well. When the time comes, and when my training is finished, I will return here to keep fighting. We will make sure that Hyrdform remains a city of Gate until our dying breaths.”

Most of the militia members stared at me with somber gazes. I gave them a nod and backed away, then dismissed them.

As they dispersed, the squad’s sergeant tapped on my shoulder and said, “Sir, we have the Presences you requested from us.”

I nodded, then followed him to his office inside the barracks. He placed five leather pouches filled with Presences on his desk. “For you, sir.”

My eyes widened. “That’s…a lot.” It would have to be enough to push me over into Gold.

“It was valuable training,” the sergeant replied. “We ventured into the Labyrinth daily, and we stocked it with whatever we could find. Apparently, sir, the cultists have been trying to breed ruin-hounds.”

“It wouldn’t surprise me.”

“There were a few wild packs in the hills that had gotten free from the cultists, which we captured and brought into the Labyrinth. Just like you did back at Slowbend.”

I chuckled. “If it works, it works. I’m assuming you’d like compensation?”

“We would appreciate it, sir.”

“I’ll check with Hardric, and see what we can do, funds-wise. If not, we might have to reward you with armour.”

“Sir?”

“We’ve got a great blacksmith in town, and he can forge armour for all of the militiamen.”

The sergeant’s eyes widened. “Proper armour and a uniform? So long as it’s theirs to keep, they would very much accept that as payment.”

“I’ll see what I can do, then.”

“This is what Gytha created,” Cuthward said, holding up a rawhide canvas with my new crest on it. At the moment, he held it backward, so I couldn’t see the design. “She made two copies, all with press-ink. You can transfer it to a surface of your choosing, imprinting it and copying the design with ease. You’ll just have to get it wet.”

We stood at the edge of the city’s great hall, while Lord Ceonwin rushed around, putting out tables and trying to keep everyone organized, while planning to repel the orc attack. Aside from the crest, Cuthward had brought a bunch of laws and bills, which I signed and made official with the new signet ring he gave me.

Technically, none of it had been official until I’d given it my seal of approval, but no one had to know that. The new portreeve hadn’t looked closely enough, and by now it was too late.

So, with that out of the way, I could move on to the fun stuff—like checking out my new crest. I had an idea what it would like, given my signet ring’s design, but I wanted to make sure.

“Don’t hold back on me for too long…” I said. “Can we see the sigil?”

Cuthward spun the piece of rawhide toward me, revealing a raven-like crest. It depicted a blackbird in flight, with its talons outstretched. It held a wild rose in its talons. Everything was black, save for the rose’s flower—it was pink and yellow.

“Excellent,” I breathed. “That’s excellent.”

“She takes tips.”

“I’m not sure if we have the funds for that right now,” I said. “But I’ll spread the word. She did a great job.”

Cuthward smiled. “Give this to your blacksmith, then. He will imprint it on your shield.”

“Of course,” I said.

“Now, one last point of business,” Cuthward said. “Come with me.”

I followed him around the edge of the hall, dodging the commotion. We navigated to a room located at the very back of a hall, along the same hallway where my room was supposed to be.

When we stepped inside, three Path-walkers in armour were waiting for me.

I took a fighting stance, expecting an ambush. They wore scale mail, each detailed with different patterns. One’s scales looked like they belonged on a fish, and another’s were shaped like feathers. Their helmets’ plumes were all different colours, but they matched their cloaks.

Though they all carried great warbrands at their hips, they didn’t draw the blades.

“Apologies for the fright,” Cuthward said. “They arrived this afternoon, on short notice.”

“We mean you no harm,” one of them said, holding out his hands. “We are the thegns from your three neighboring lands to the west.”

I lowered my hands, then stood up straight. “If only you’d gotten here a little sooner, we could’ve used a few more hands against the Path-walkers.”

“Perhaps,” a different thegn said. “We rode as quickly as we could when we heard that you were to be passing through.”

“So it is true?” the third thegn asked. “You have been fighting off the cultists?”

“I take it your lands have all had the same problems?” I asked.

“Indeed,” The first thegn folded his arms. “We have been hearing of your…relative success.”

I raised my eyebrows. “It’s still early.”

“We wanted to know what you were doing. We…were curious to use it ourselves.”

Blinking, I took a step back.

“If, of course, you were willing,” a second thegn provided.

“Sure, I can tell you what I’ve done, but…” I exhaled. “The bigger problem is the willingness to fix what’s hurting you. I know it’s not easy, and it takes will to oust people like Tunmod, who rule alongside their cultist thugs…but that’s the bigger issue.”

The thegns looked at me expectantly.

“Well, if you want, I can tell you what I did,” I said. “We have all evening.”

“Sir, there are rumours you are making a play for the position of Ealdorman. And that Hinlaw is to be replaced soon…”

“Well, perhaps…how do they think I’m going to get in power?”

“That you will slay him in a duel, of course. It wouldn’t be uncommon. His body will be burnt in a typical Domain transfer ritual, and you will take charge.”

I narrowed my eyes. So they knew. Of course they would know. But…they weren’t concerned about my loyalties?

After a few seconds, I said, “Perhaps. I won’t say anymore—”

“We won’t ask you to,” the first thegn whispered hurriedly. “But suffice it to say, we are looking forward to the day you take power.”

“Power…” I muttered.

That word still made me itch. I didn’t like the notion of wielding that much power over people. Not the way Hinlaw did it, nor the way the Warlord did. Would people be afraid to speak about me?

“In the meanwhile, we would be curious to hear your advice regarding our lands…specifically, what you did to improve them.”

“Of course.” I motioned to the chairs. “Care to take a seat?”
