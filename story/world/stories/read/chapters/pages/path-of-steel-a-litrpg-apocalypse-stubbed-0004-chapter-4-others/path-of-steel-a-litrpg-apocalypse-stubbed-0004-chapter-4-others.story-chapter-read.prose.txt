For the first time in his life, Gary experienced the fear of imminent, unavoidable death.

He blinked away the sweat and held himself as still as possible. He didn’t even dare think, in fear it would be too loud. The creaking grew closer. Through the gaps between the leaves, Gary spied a colorful shape passing in the distance. There was a small wooden cart—the source of the sound—pushed by something he couldn’t quite make out through the leaves. All he saw was bright red and yellow. He heard a new sound, a faint jingle.

The thing paused.

Gary felt an ice-cold aura stretching towards him, submerging the surrounding forest in its cold embrace, sinking it into stillness. He could sense the creature’s malice. It was sickeningly sweet, a sugar cane forced down his throat. It was visceral, too. He fought the urge to run.

A breeze blew through the forest, uncaring of the creature’s presence. The creaking resumed, and the creature departed, slowly making distance until that horrible sound disappeared. The aura didn’t—or maybe it did, but part of it had seeped into Gary’s heart, freezing him up.

He did not leave the bush.

“Shit,” he whispered, grabbing his face. Thorns dug deeper into his skin as he shivered. He heard the tears falling. “Shit.”

Adventure had overtaken him for a bit, but the cold fear of death reminded him of the before. Of the beautiful Clara splattering into a blood stain. All those stains. That woman on the verge of breaking, who’d rushed Gary and begged him not to leave, even fought him for it. He’d abandoned her.

I can’t even save myself, lady… he thought. Did she make it?

My family… My friends… My world…

There had been explosions. Moving cars turned driverless. Half his family had the magic gene, but where were they at the time of the slaughter? What time was it? Could they have been driving to work?

“No, it was too late. My second coffee. Around 12. They weren’t in the car,” he muttered, but the unknown fate of his planet hung heavy over his head. There could be riots. An alien invasion. It could be war back there, and he was stuck in a forest deep in enemy territory playing Scottish squirrel warrior.

“FUCK!” he cried out, smashing his fist into the dirt, sensing the thorns rip through his skin. That shook him awake. He crawled out of the bush, seeing the blood on his hands. Could it be the blood of innocents? What had those monsters even done to him? They spoke, and he killed them. That orc wanted to be a chef, and it had looked at him with hatred.

Gary knelt to the forest floor, inspecting the blood in the dirt.

“Either human, or a monster that isn’t dead yet,” he said. The blood emitted a faint iron smell. He considered licking it before remembering he wasn’t an eccentric movie detective.

The forest stretched in all directions. This was roughly where he’d met the orc, so there was no clue where to go next. He eyed a tree.

Soon, he stood on its higher branches, overlooking a slightly wider range of forest. Climbing is so much easier when you’re fit, he thought, but the view soon arrested his attention.

A mountain rose far to the west, the sun setting behind it. To the north, he spotted a lake. Everything to the south and east was trees.

“North it is,” he said, then smoothly made his way down. When he jumped from the lowest branch, maybe five feet off the floor, the landing was effortless. “Huh.”

It didn’t take him long to hear human speech. Though he couldn’t make out the words, the relief that washed over him was reward enough. He broke out of the bushes to find three men staring him down, weapons drawn.

One held a videogame controller. The second had no visible weapon, while the third wielded a sledgehammer, the lucky bastard.

“Hey!” Gary said. “It’s so great to meet you. I’m Gary!”

The three man glanced at each other. The one with the hammer was the first to speak—he was tall, gruff, and hard-eyed, probably in his forties, with tanned skin and blood still covering the end of his hammer. All wore the same white clothes as Gary, though theirs were considerably cleaner.

“Hey,” the hammer man said, giving him a slight upwards nod. “The name’s Goran. Will you join us? The more the merrier.”

“Absolutely!” Gary said.

“Do you have a weapon?”

Gary held up his pencil. The three men exchanged glances, then snickered. “Guess you’re no longer the pariah, Webley,” Goran said, motioning to the man with no discernible weapon. That man—presumably Webley—raised his shirt to reveal a laminated playing card tucked into his trousers. It was the jester of hearts.

“That’s not a real card,” Gary said.

“I think it’s time to reconsider the definition of real. Welcome, I’m Tom,” the third man said, the one wielding the playstation controller.

“There’s a lake up north. I assume you have water?” Gary asked, shaking Tom’s hand.

Again, the men glanced at each other, and Goran spoke. “We have no way to carry it. You’ll need to drink from the lake.”

“Got it. What about food?”

“Are you dumb?” Goran asked. “The energy keeps you fed. Or did you not hunt a single monster?”

“I did get a few, actually,” Gary replied. “Did you guys kill the one-legged orc?”

Tom snorted. “The motherfucker tried to choke me.”

“And failed, unfortunately,” Webley said, he and Goran barking a harsh laugh.

“With you here, we can accelerate our hunt,” Goran said. “We need to kill as many of them as possible.”

“To get that energy, right? Do you know how to use it?”

Tom and Webley glanced at Goran, who frowned. “You sure ask a lot, and I don’t see you contributing any.”

“Fair enough. Remember that orc? I met it before you and took its sword without killing it. That way, the sword didn’t disappear, and I had a weapon. Of course, it did disappear when you killed it…” A shadow passed over his gaze. “Can you tell me, how was it in death?”

“Who, the orc? Webley slit its throat and it bled out like a pig. I’d already broken its arms,” Goran replied. Ignoring Gary’s troubled look, he continued. “That’s actually a great trick. Taking their weapons without killing them. We’ll do it. About the energy, we haven’t figured out how to use it yet. It sits inside us like a stubborn mule. It does make us a little stronger, but we all sense it can do so much more. We just don’t know how. I suppose you feel the same.”

Gary nodded.

Goran continued. “Until we figure it out, we need to kill as many monsters as possible, gather more of that energy, and use it to kill stronger monsters. That will give us a leg up.”

“A leg up in what?” Gary asked.

“I don’t know yet, but there has to be something. The monsters, the killing, that promise of power. This is a competition, we just don’t know the rules. I can promise you, whatever those rules are, more power will always be a good thing. When the idiots stop flooding the chat, maybe we’ll know more.”

“Sure,” Goran replied.

“Do you mind if I go to the lake now? I haven’t drunk anything since morning.”

“Help yourself,” Goran said, and Gary stepped around them to enter the bushes.

A group of five prowled through the swamp, three men and two women. They kept their heads low and eyes open—level 1s were rare here, with 2 and 3 being the norm. Those who traveled alone died quickly.

As if to spite that thought, a single man appeared, walking nonchalantly through the mud. He whistled, even. As soon as the group caught sight of him, they paused. The lack of a System description marked him as a human, and he did look like one, but he was clearly different from them.

Where they wore non-descript white clothes, he donned leather armor. His blond hair was tidy, and his gait as confident as they came.

The group froze. He kept walking. “Hey,” one of the men said. “Nice clothes.”

“Wanna steal them?” the odd man asked.

“That’s not what I meant,” the man from the group replied quickly.

“You didn’t need to. I can read eyes, you know. You saw me all alone and careless out here and your first thought was that I’m an idiot, right? But then you saw my clothes and thought, “Oh wow, this guy must be strong to be so well-dressed in this place, unlike our fashion-crime weak asses.” Well, I am pretty strong, and I don’t take well to insults.”

The group backed off. The man who spoke before held his hands up. “We never thought those things. It was all you.”

“And now you’re blaming me? Oh wow, you really want some. Fine. Let’s fight.”

A ball appeared in his hand. It was the size of a juggler’s, except riddled with crevices. The man’s thumb clicked a button, and spikes emerged from the crevices, buzzing as they rapidly span around the ball.

The group’s eyes widened. “Please wait!” the leading man said, while everyone else broke off running.

The odd man flicked the ball. It flew fast, ricocheting off the trees and ground without losing speed. It smashed into the back of an escaping woman and turned it into a bloody mess. Before she even collapsed, the ball had bounced into another direction and shattered the head of a man.

Of the five people in the group, four were hit in the blink of an eye. Only the man who’d spoken remained, frozen in horror. The ball bounced back into the odd man’s hand, who caught it expertly and clicked off the spikes.

“Please spare me,” the remaining survivor said.

“Have you had dinner yet?” the odd man asked, walking closer.

“Uh… No.”

“Wrong answer.”

The odd man flashed before the other. He grabbed the survivor’s jaw and forced his mouth open, then his other hand raised the inert ball over it. The movement was casual, yet the weaker man was completely powerless to resist.

The odd man grinned. “Enjoy your meal,” he said. He clicked the ball and dropped it.
