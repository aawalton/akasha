[Time]: Summer Break, Day 48 — 9:45 PM[Location]: White City · Pedestrian Promenade

Tonight, the White City had surrendered.

With the Greed Umbrella hoisting the championship trophy on their soil, the capital's cultural immune system had initiated a synchronized grief protocol. The crystal spire arrays throttled back to a muted, sullen pearl. The floating street luminaires burned at bare minimum output. For the first time since she had transmigrated, Hathaway was looking at a sky that contained something resembling night.

They sat on a wrought-iron bench near the closed kiosk. The residual spice of the Vissel hand-ground still radiated a phantom, scorched heat against the roof of Hathaway's mouth.

The silence between them stretched, thick and unmapped.

Her developer brain, idling in the quiet of the aftermath, snagged on a logistical variable she had not yet scheduled for processing.

Where is Victoria spending the night?

She ran the arithmetic. The Greed Umbrella had leased the entire Wisteria. Victoria could walk through the lobby right now, claim any vacant room, and sleep without physically intersecting with whatever extraordinary post-championship administrative restructuring her sister was conducting on the top floor. Hathaway stared at the dark cobblestones. Logical. Completely, thoroughly insufficient.

Her processor immediately spun up the alternative. She could come home with me.

The thought triggered a catastrophic, instant firewall block. She slammed every emergency brake she possessed.

Abort. We confirmed this relationship status less than two hours ago while actively crying from industrial chili burns. You do not bring someone home on the first night. That is a sequencing error. The progression tree does not support skipping that many dialogue checkpoints.

She sat very still, staring at the unlit lamppost directly ahead.

Although, a quieter thread noted, we have known each other for three months. And she is already my girlfriend.

By any objective metric, the pacing was terminal. Three months. One fiscal quarter. In the span of a seasonal term, this woman had somehow rewritten the entire operating system of her life. But sitting there on the bench, acutely aware of the space between Victoria's shoulder and her own, those three months felt like they possessed the geological density of an entire previous lifetime compressed into a single season.

Victoria stood up. She smoothed the front of her coat with unhurried grace.

"I should go back," Victoria said quietly.

The words bypassed every logic filter Hathaway possessed. "Back?" she blurted out, her volume slightly too loud for the quiet street. "Back where?"

Victoria paused. She turned her head and fixed those pale, clear eyes on Hathaway. She did not offer a single syllable.

Hathaway's throat tightened.

"...Oh."

Victoria did not move. She just kept looking.

The realization bloomed, slow and unavoidable, in the center of Hathaway's chest. She gathered every ounce of courage she had been issued in this life, gripped the edge of the iron bench with both hands, and shoved the words out.

"Do you want to come home with me?"

Before the last syllable fully cleared her mouth, the anxiety subroutine flooded the gap. She launched into a frantic, breathless cascade of justifications, hands gesturing to build the structural framework of the excuse.

"I mean, the townhouse is significantly closer than the hotel, and navigating the transit grid this late is highly inefficient, plus the civic hostility toward your roster has reached an absolute statistical peak right now, so walking alone carries a measurable environmental hazard, and I can—"

She stopped. Victoria Wellington was not a person who required an escort through a dark street. The excuses were collapsing under their own weight in real-time.

Her logic engine cast around desperately and found exactly one lifeline.

"The cat!" She pointed a slightly frantic finger toward the general direction of her neighborhood. "Your cat is still at my house. It is definitely, absolutely experiencing severe separation anxiety right now!"

Victoria looked at her.

A microscopic softening moved through the architecture of her jaw. The corner of her mouth shifted, nearly imperceptible.

"Her name," Victoria said softly, "is Vida."

She lowered her gaze. With slow, measured care, she raised both hands to her opposite wrists and adjusted the cuffs of her immaculate gloves. Then she turned on her heel and began walking.

Directly toward Townhouse 107.

Hathaway scrambled off the bench and hurried after her, her heart hammering in double-time, profoundly and entirely grateful that she had never gotten around to assigning the enormous, cow-spotted biological phenomenon a placeholder name.

Victoria stepped out of the entryway shadows and into the hall light.

The expression that rendered across Margaret's face was magnificent: forearms corded, jaw locked, the magazine crinkled under a sudden vice-like grip.

Then she looked at her daughter's face.

The blood feud code and the motherly love subroutine entered a violent computation war. Motherly love bullied the ancestral code into begrudging, grinding submission by a fraction of a percentage point.

Margaret carefully, deliberately placed the crumpled magazine on the side table.

"The guest rooms are clean," Margaret stated. Her voice was dry as pulverized chalk. "You may stay whenever you wish."

Victoria stepped forward. She placed one hand over her heart and offered a precise, faultless bow, radiating the exact measure of respectful humility required to defuse a hostile sovereign.

"Please forgive the sudden intrusion," Victoria said quietly. "I am deeply grateful for your generosity."

Margaret's jaw worked. A low, compressed sound emerged from somewhere deep in her chest, the sound of four syllables fighting their way out against significant internal resistance. Hathaway's audio processor barely decoded the syntax.

"You're welcome."

Having exhausted her diplomatic stamina for the foreseeable fiscal quarter, Margaret spun on her heel and marched directly into the kitchen without another word.

Hathaway stared at the empty archway.

She had never witnessed her mother execute a tactical retreat in such profound silence.

The Ludwig estate possessed a significant surplus of guest suites. As they ascended the carpeted staircase, their boots making soft sounds against the runner, Victoria glanced down the quiet corridor.

"Which room is yours?" Victoria asked.

"Far left," Hathaway said. "The last door."

Victoria nodded. She walked the hall without a sound, stopped directly in front of Hathaway's door, turned exactly ninety degrees, and opened the room immediately across the hall.

She stepped inside.

Hathaway stood rooted on the landing. Her heart rate had executed an unauthorized spike and was currently running somewhere fifteen beats per minute above any situation warranted. She couldn't parse why the simple geometric reality of Victoria occupying a mattress one hallway's width from her own was flooding her system with this volume of adrenaline.

As she stood there buffering, the air in the hallway displaced.

Vida drifted out from the ceiling shadows of Hathaway's half-open bedroom door. Victoria caught her. She buried both gloved hands into the thick, glowing fur.

She looked across the hallway at Hathaway.

"Goodnight," Victoria said.

"Goodnight," Hathaway said. Her voice sounded thin to her own ears.

Victoria stepped back. The door closed. The brass latch clicked into place.

Hathaway remained standing in the empty corridor, looking at the polished wood panels of the shut door. A strange, weightless sensation was spreading through her chest. Even the way she closes a door, Hathaway's developer brain supplied, helpless and entirely without irony, is fatally beautiful.

She leaned her forehead against her own doorframe and stayed there for a while, listening to the quiet of the house.

[Time]: Summer Break, Day 49 — 6:00 AM[Location]: White City · Townhouse 107 · Hathaway's Bedroom

Hathaway opened her eyes.

She had actually slept: a full, uninterrupted system hibernation, no paper boats, no void, for the first time in what felt like a geological epoch.

Her brain booted up at half speed, pulling the previous day's event logs in sluggish, sequential order. The control room, the radar, the red dot. The sealed void, the slave contract. The street corner kiosk, the fire-breathing, the kiss.

Victoria.

Her eyes snapped wide open. She scrambled for her terminal: six in the morning, appallingly early. She burrowed back under the blankets and attempted to force a system standby. It failed. She wiggled against the mattress, stared at the ceiling, rolled over, rolled back. Sleep was mathematically off the table.

She opened the financial dashboard. The account balance had updated.

Prior to the Grand Masters Final, she had placed a quiet wager: eighty thousand on the Greed Umbrella to take the championship, at odds that had given the Golden Iris a modest edge. The deposit line glowed back at her. Net profit: ninety-two thousand.

Hathaway committed the exact figure to memory, then locked the screen.

I am absolutely going to need every coin of this operational budget.

She lay there in the quiet for another ten minutes.

Then the social anxiety subroutine kicked in.

Victoria is across the hall. In approximately ninety minutes, Margaret, Anna, and Rory will all be at the breakfast table. If Victoria comes down alone, with no buffer present, the diplomatic pressure will be significant.

She threw the covers off, pulled on a light shirt, crossed to the guest room door, and raised her knuckles to the wood.

Her hand hovered, and the internal calculus spiraled. What if she is still sleeping? Waking her up at six is a monstrous violation of basic guest etiquette. But if she is already awake and waiting alone in an unfamiliar room because she doesn't want to wander my house unescorted, I am a terrible host. The logic engine spun in frantic, fruitless circles, building justifications and dismantling them at equal speed. She stood there, hand raised in the empty air, for a truly unreasonable length of time.

The handle turned. The door swung inward before her knuckles could make contact with the wood.

Victoria stood in the threshold: fully dressed, dark jacket pressed without a single crease, silver hair flawless, posture radiating an aristocratic readiness that made Hathaway acutely aware of her own unbrushed bedhead and wrinkled shirt.

Hathaway's heart skipped. Her raised hand dropped to her side.

Of course. Townhouse 107 had excellent soundproofing, but no internal wards. Victoria had Mystic Eyes. She had known the moment Hathaway opened her eyes.

"Morning," Hathaway managed.

Victoria's pale eyes moved from Hathaway's dropped hand to her face. A subtle, unreadable light passed through the blue irises.

"Good morning," Victoria replied. Her voice was soft, carrying a dangerous, velvety quality. She tilted her head very slightly. "What exactly were you waiting for?"

Hathaway's vocal cords executed a complete shutdown.

She stared at her girlfriend for exactly one desperate second, deleted the entire responsible host dialogue tree from her cache, and executed an emergency pivot.

"What do you want for breakfast?"
