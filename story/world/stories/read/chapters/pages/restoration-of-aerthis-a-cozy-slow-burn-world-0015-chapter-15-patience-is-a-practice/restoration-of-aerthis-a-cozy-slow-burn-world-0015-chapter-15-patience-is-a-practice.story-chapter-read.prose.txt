A clatter of metal on stone in the kitchen woke her up just before sunrise. Then there was the sound of something wooden dragging, and behind it were two or three muffled voices. A dull thud came next, then chirping trickled in from outside, too sharp and high to be one of the farm birds.

Elynara didn’t move at first. Her eyes stayed closed. The blanket was pulled up to her chin and the mattress hadn’t shifted enough to demand a response.

Then a soft, invisible weight bounced once on her stomach.

She groaned.

Another bounce, this time higher. Then something flicked her forehead with deliberate smugness.

She cracked one eye open. Starpuff bobbed directly above her face, silent but unmistakably gloating. Its color shifted green around the edges, bright and irritatingly proud.

“Fine.”

She pushed herself upright with more effort than style, pulling the blanket off and sitting there, hunched and blinking. Her hair was already a mess and she knew it. The fire in the wall had burned low, and the morning chill had crept into the room.

There was no need to rush. But the smells drifting in now were enough to keep her moving. Bread, real bread, and something roasted over wood. She stood, stretched once until her shoulders cracked, and shuffled into her boots.

The door creaked under her hand as she opened it. A rush of warm air greeted her, laced with kitchen heat and damp wool. Someone was humming nearby. Footsteps moved lightly across wood.

She stepped out.

“Morning!”

A young boy trotted past with an armful of napkins, a bit too proud of not dropping any. He vanished around the corner before she could reply.

Rina’s voice called from deeper in the hall.

“Elynara! If you’re up, grab the utensils from the shelf near the hearth!”

Still not entirely sure how she ended up volunteering, Elynara blinked twice, then turned and did as asked.

Her limbs moved before her mind could catch up, the wooden spoons, simple bowls, clay cups set carefully along the long table. It was the sort of task her body could do without needing permission from her brain.

The kitchen door thudded open, and the sharp scent of chopped roots carried in on a plume of steam.

Elynara moved from place to place with mechanical focus, the weight of sleep still clinging to her shoulders. Starpuff followed overhead, occasionally poking at a spoon like it was helping. She didn’t swat it this time.

Gradually, her thoughts aligned.

She thought back to the shrines and her own musings. To how they behaved around mana and the offerings.

Her brow furrowed slightly as she set the last of the bowls. What if she had been wrong about how things worked? About what mattered to the spirits?

The offerings at the altar had been deliberate. Not just ceremonial. And more importantly, not all the same.

She turned toward the kitchen just as Rina emerged, wiping her hands on a towel.

“Thanks for the help. Hungry?”

“Yes. But–”

Elynara hesitated, then stepped a little closer.

“I’ve been meaning to ask. The shrine near the forest… how often do you leave things there?”

Rina glanced at her, then nodded.

“Sometimes. Depends on the season and whatever we have on hand to spare. Fresh fruit, mostly. Once carved a little wooden bird and left it; looked terrible, but someone said it’s the thought that counts.”

“So it’s normal?”

“It used to be. Fewer folks bother now. Doesn’t mean it stopped working.”

She gave a small shrug.

“We’ve had luck with the crops since spring. Some folks still thank the spirits for that.”

Elynara considered that, then gestured loosely.

“Do the offerings have to be… special?”

“Not really. Intent matters more than value. It’s not about impressing them. It’s about sharing.”

She mulled over the reply, though it revealed little in truth.

Starpuff zipped around her elbow like it had something to say on the matter. She flicked her fingers at it in mild warning.

They sat at the table not long after, porridge steaming in wide wooden bowls, slices of fresh bread on the side. Children drifted in one at a time, yawning and wide-eyed, their sleep-mussed hair slowly giving way to giggles and jostles.

Rina took her seat with a sigh and waved her spoon lazily.

“You’re curious about spirits. What else?”

Elynara hesitated again.

“Mana. Can you…?”

“Use it? A little.”

Rina gave her a look over the edge of her cup.

“And the kids?”

“Finnie is a little too young for it yet, but the other two are getting there, I’m so proud of them!”

Saying so the matron ruffled the heads of all of her kids, to their clear displeasure. Though if their fluffed up ears and swishing tails were any indication, it wasn’t quite so clear.

Elynara smiled at that, even giggling when Finnie pouted.

She stirred her porridge once, then looked up.

“I think I’d like to see how you leave offerings. If that’s alright.”

Rina’s answer came with a crooked grin.

“Sure. But I’m not walking you all the way out there before breakfast.”

That earned a small laugh from Elynara and an approving trill from Starpuff, who had just nibbled a crumb from someone else’s plate.

The rest of the meal passed with quiet talk and the occasional interruption from a chair leg scraping too loudly, or the kids loudly debating whether cluckers could cast magic.

By the time the bowls were scraped clean and the table cleared, Elynara felt fully awake, her mind already sketching plans across the coming day.

Shrines. Spirits. Mana.

And now… more questions worth asking.

Still warm from the kitchen, wrapped in cloth and tied with a bit of twine, the bundle was light in her hands. She caught a whiff of dried berries and something sweeter beneath. Rina had passed it over with little more than a nod.

“Finnie’ll show you where to take it. Don’t let him lose the fox again.”

The warning hadn’t made sense at the time.

Finnie was already waiting by the door, bouncing lightly on his toes, tail swishing widely. His hand gripping the strap of his overstuffed satchel like he was off on a grand expedition.

“You ready?”

She gave a tired nod to the enthusiastic boy.

“I think so.”

“Cool. I’ll go first. I’m the best at not stepping in chicken poop.”

And with that, he was off.

They passed through the back gate and into the dew-soaked grass beyond. The morning was cool and bright, the sun just high enough to catch on the edges of spider silk and turn them to threads of gold. Her boots sank slightly with each step, the earth soft and springy beneath them.

Finnie didn’t seem to notice. Or care. He darted ahead, then backtracked, then walked sideways for a bit, pointing things out like a tour guide with too many thoughts and no filter.

“That fruit’s from the trees by the water ditch. Mama says it’s not good enough for jam, so we give it to the shrine instead. That’s fair.”

He paused to kick at a clump of mud.

“The dry stuff’s from the pantry. I think it used to be pears. Might be carrots. It’s kinda mystery food.”

She raised a brow.

“And the carved pieces?”

“My sister made those. Mine looked like a blob so she said I wasn’t allowed to help. The fox is new. We lost the old one after it fell behind the stones and got moldy.”

“That’s… very specific.”

He grinned.

“Yup.”

The shrines came into view just beyond a low thicket, ringed by bent grass and the quiet patience of ancient places, with two weather-worn stone markers half-sunken in the earth. A chicken clucked somewhere behind them. Wind stirred the reeds.

Finnie dropped his bag beside the stones with a practiced thud, already kneeling in the flattened patch of earth in front of them. He held out his hands.

“Gimme the bundle.”

Elynara passed it over, curious now.

He untied it a little messily, smoothing the cloth flat. Then, very carefully, he set each thing in place, arranging the carved fox and bird as if they were on guard, the fruit on one side, and the dried slices in the middle.

“Okay.” he whispered, mostly to himself. Then, louder:

“Hi again. It’s just me this time. Here’s your food. Please make the plants grow and keep the cluckers from being dumb.”

Elynara blinked.

“That’s the whole prayer?”

“Yep! Pretty much,” he said, already brushing off his knees. “Sometimes we add extra stuff if something’s broken or someone’s sick. But I don’t think we broke anything this week.”

There was little to no ceremony to it. No strange wind nor sudden silence. Just the sound of leaves and the faint snap of cloth as Finnie rewrapped his bundle.

He stood up, then looked at her with a small shrug.

“That’s it. You can stay longer if you want. I gotta go feed the chickens or they’ll scream.”

And just like that, he grabbed his satchel and marched off, boots squelching in the grass.

Elynara let out a quiet breath and sat beside the shrine, the scent of fruit and wood still lingering in the air. The offerings looked simple. A little messy, but sincere.

She didn’t know what she expected.

Maybe something bigger.

But the important part was how the spirits answered, not the ceremony.

She closed her eyes and began to reach inward, letting her mana settle as she prepared to watch.

Elynara shifted once, then settled in near the shrine, legs crossed, staff laid beside her. The offering still looked fresh. The fruit sat with its skin intact, beads of moisture collecting at the base. The carved fox leaned at a shallow angle, its surface catching stray light from between the stalks.

She adjusted her posture until her spine rested straight. Her palms faced downward, resting lightly against her knees.

Starpuff lingered for a moment beside her shoulder. It pulsed once, then drifted off in a loose arc toward the glimmer near the stones.

She closed her eyes.

Breath passed through slowly. Her pulse softened behind it. Focus fell into place without resistance.

The mana in the background field was thin. That hadn’t changed. But closer to the shrine, the texture shifted. Small, slow fluctuations rippled through the air like heat off stone.

The fruit was releasing mana.

It wasn’t much, just a passive trace. Delicate enough that she would have missed it if she wasn’t looking. Barely a trickle, really.

Movement followed. The two spirits reacted in their own slow way. They circled the fruit, easing into it like small animals around a dropped crumb, but with no urgency she could detect.

She shifted focus toward the dried slices.

No change.

The air near them didn’t move. They sat steadily, untouched by everything except the breeze. One spirit drifted close, passed over the dried fruit, nibbled on the fox carving, then curved back toward the fruit without slowing.

She adjusted again.

The carved fox pulsed weakly.

It didn’t move mana around itself like the fruit. The carving of the bird did too, though less. The mana wasn’t released in a flow, but it hovered in a shallow layer, like moisture trapped inside bark.

The two pieces didn’t seem much different from each other. Or at least she couldn’t detect a big difference in mana. The fox carving was flatter and released more mana faster. While the bird was roundish and slower to give back.

The same material was used for both too.

It seemed geometry held sway over mana as well. Her old memories were happy at that for some reason she couldn’t quite pinpoint.

But it revealed one more thing. The pieces had mana in them, despite being effectively dead wood. The dried food didn’t have any, so clearly it was something else.

Her thoughts turned towards what the matron said this morning, on how they handled the pieces themselves, but only she had the mana skills for it, so how…?

The answer was quite obvious, now that she thought about it.

Even if the person carving it had no skill with mana, the will and focus given to it while handling it was probably enough. After all, that’s all she needed to handle mana. Will, focus, patience, and imagination.

She drew the comparison carefully.

People here weren’t trained. They didn’t manipulate mana directly. But they clearly still used it. Anything done with attention or emotion left a trace. In a place like this, those traces were all the spirits had.

That explained why the mana in the town was so low.

Low ambient mana didn’t necessarily mean the land was failing. It meant the people were pulling from it, constantly. Just through living and using mana in their own little ways. It was a small drain, but one that needed replenishment.

She sat with that.

The fruit’s signal began to fade a little bit. The spirits stayed close, but their pull softened.

No change near the dried pieces. Still blank.

The carvings would hold much longer. Their signal wasn’t steady enough to attract them yet, but it was there.

It looked like that was all she would figure out this time.

She would return again later, to check on things.

For now, she leaned forward slightly, grounding her palms once more before rising. Her legs ached faintly, but the rest of her stayed steady.

Elynara reached for the carved fox. It was a more uniform piece; its surface felt smooth under her fingers, worn in places where handling had flattened the grain. She let a thread of her own mana slip into it.

The release began slowly. The energy clung to the curve of the figure, rolling along its sides before seeping in. The shape seemed to hold it in, but the release happened over its entire flat surface.

She withdrew her hand and took the bird next.

The figure was rounder, but carved with shallower cuts. When she let mana pass into it, the difference was immediate. The flow was calmer and smaller than that of the fox. But steadier. It would last much longer.

Elynara leaned back slightly, watching the air shift as the mana dispersed. No big shift from the spirits, but they seemed curious. And now that she had their attention, she fed them mana directly too.

She couldn’t help but think about stuffing the hamsters silly. Those puffy spirits could hold quite a lot!

Satisfied, she turned back to pondering over her findings.

Shape seemingly altered behavior. The round one held mana longer. The flat one let it go faster, over more ground.

Intent, material, mana control, and apparently geometry all joined together, mattering in their own little ways.

And… it was beautiful. She could hardly wait to unravel all the little secrets of mana she could.

How many were left? How many more she would be able to figure out?

She didn’t know, but was excited all the same!

Starpuff returned from somewhere overhead, spinning once above her before veering off again, trailing faint green flickers. Its presence always made the air feel lighter. The spirits responded too, drawing slightly closer in its wake before settling again.

Elynara stayed still for a moment longer before shifting her weight to stand.

A small flicker blinked into the corner of her vision, and she smooshed it immediately.

【New Skill Gained: Meditation Lv. 1】

She blinked in surprise.

It wasn’t the first time she had meditated. She had used breathwork, stilled her mana before, and even sustained long stretches of focused calm.

So how was this time different?

Oh, right. She hadn’t pushed. Hadn’t shaped or monitored her internal flow. She had let things unfold and paid attention without trying to guide the result, rather than using it to train, for once.

But the system had only marked it now.

She straightened and brushed the loose grass from her sleeves.

It raised a better question: how much else had she been doing the hard way? How many other pieces were missing just because she didn’t do things the ‘right’ way?

Hard to say. But now she had one more thing to ponder.

She would follow it up later.

For now, her hands were steady and the rest of the day was waiting. She had requests to complete!

Picking up her staff, she checked the position of the sun, and turned towards the edge of the farm, following the dirt path there.

Starpuff floated lazily overhead, trailing just above her hood. It nudged her hair once, then spun forward and tugged at her sleeve.

She gave the little troublemaker a suspicious glance.

“I know, I know. I’m going.”

The quest sheet crinkled slightly in her other hand. She kept reading as she walked, eyes scanning the inked list again. Three herbs were listed, with only one that could possibly be found in the fields around the forest edge, and the others marked as strictly forest-grown. Most of the descriptions were handwritten, the plant names underlined in sharp strokes.

She smiled at what was clearly Alicia’s handiwork.

Adjusting her grip on the paper, she let her pace slow. Grass pulled higher now, brushing against her boots. Bird calls rang further out, spaced and sharp. The background hum of the farm had faded behind her.

Ahead, the field rolled unevenly toward the tree line, still quite a ways off.

Elynara scanned the ground around her as she walked, hoping she might spot the requested herb somewhere.

Starpuff bumped her again, more gently this time, then tucked in closer to her shoulder. Its glow had dimmed slightly.

She slowed to a stop.

The field had grown a little too quiet.

Her eyes swept forward. Grass shifted in small waves with the breeze, but no other movement stood out. The wind carried dry leaves and dust.

Focusing, she prepared her spell, just in case.

It took another couple minutes of walking before she understood where that sense of wrong was coming from.

Just around the bend, the grass stirred again, louder this time, other sounds mixed in.

Then she saw them.

With their curved tusks, two Bristleboars tore through the ground with their snouts deep in the soil. Bristled backs twitching. Large, low to the ground, all thick hide and quick power.

They hadn’t seen her yet.

She inhaled once, slow. Then exhaled and shifted her stance.

Could try dual shaping with its split focus and fast hits. But it was still a fresh skill. Risky if one of them faltered, if she lost control. Or she could empower the first one, drop the lead, and prepare another one before the second monster could react.

She glanced at their spacing, and charged her first spell fully. They were four strides apart, moving in opposite loops.

Mana gathered cleanly in her palm, moving through the staff. She could feel the heat gathering there. All the while the Bristleboars kept tearing at the ground, unaware.

Waiting just enough to make sure she was on target, her spell was cast.

The flame took the first boar in the shoulder. It crumpled hard, the force of the explosion flipping it sideways, legs folding under.

The second one jolted, confused. But still turned towards her nearly instantly with no hesitation whatsoever. It just charged at her.

Not even waiting for the first one to land, Elynara pulled again, shaping with speed. Mana gathered, and her second spell took form, taking a moment longer to fully charge. By the time it was ready, the boar had already closed half the distance.

She responded all the same.

The explosive magic caught it at the chest. It staggered, squealed once, then slammed to the ground, skidding into a short trench before coming still.

She stayed where she was, arms held, waiting for movement, making sure the monsters were truly dead.

The field slowly buzzed with activity again, the insects uncaring for what had transpired.

Breath left her in a long exhale. Mana burned soft against her fingers, the last traces flickering out as she released her focus.

She straightened slowly.

Both bodies lay ahead, still and dark against the grass. The soil around them was torn where they had rooted earlier.

There were no other beasts in sight.

She turned her head slightly, eyes catching the edge of the forest again.

It was much closer now.

Elynara shook out her arms once the glow left her fingers.

The grass still smoked faintly near the second boar’s body. She stepped closer, boots slipping slightly where the soil had torn under its hooves.

Both Bristleboars were down. They were big, solid things. Thick and heavy. Just standing beside them made her legs ache in sympathy.

After poking the closer one and getting no reaction, she crouched and pressed a palm to its shoulder. It was still warm, but losing that heat already.

Thinking about it, what could she possibly do here?

Dismantling them here wasn’t realistic. She had practiced on bonefangs. These were double the size, with a hide her knife wouldn’t slide through easily. Carving off the valuable bits might work, but the rest would go to waste. The meat alone could be worth hauling if she could get help.

She pulled one of the small, treated tarps from her pack, meant for laying out work she got specifically for dismantling.

Instead, she unfolded it over the first boar, tucking the edges as best she could. A second tarp followed for the other. It wouldn’t stop decay, but it would hold off the worst of the flies.

“It’s kind of funny. Prepping for dismantling, became prepping for delivery.”

Starpuff buzzed once overhead, trailing a lazy circle before curling into her hood again. No complaints this time.

She stood and exhaled through her nose. No point in staying on task if the task had changed. The herbs could wait. This much meat couldn’t.

Still, if there were more in the area, she might as well push things a little further. She stepped back, raised a hand, and fired a short burst of low powered fire spell against a far away boulder. It was louder than it was strong, but that was the point.

The sound carried a little, to no visible response. She moved further in, found her mark, and fired again. Then again.

By the time she reached the last bend before the tree line, something answered.

A sharp growl. Thin, ragged. A Bonefang broke from the brush and charged. She dropped it with a fast spell strike to the chest, then caught movement in the tall grass and pivoted.

The second one, closer. This one took two shots since she released her first spell too early, making it a glancing hit. It still stunned the beast, and despite her foot slipping on the uneven ground, the second spell landed true.

She only had a moment of respite before noting sounds of something getting closer. The commotion and multiple explosions in a row had attracted another monster.

A heavier snort accompanied the third shape, which revealed it as another Bristleboar. She sighed once before casting again.

By the end, her arms ached. Mana flickered lower in her chest. Nowhere near empty, thanks to all her training. But had she not trained her capacity, she would be all spent now.

Still, no point in waiting around. Starpuff was already eagerly tugging one of her bangs forward, this rascal!

She collected both Bonefang fangs quickly, then turned toward the fresh Bristleboar, panting. She had no tarps left, and no time to lose.

Grimacing, she grabbed it by the forelegs and started pulling.

The drag was slow. She found the flattest route she could, moving with short bursts, using every downhill stretch to get a few more feet for free. Dust caked her sleeves. Her boots picked up every thorn along the way.

By the time she reached the tarp-covered pile, she could barely lift her arms.

She left the last boar half-folded beside the others, then straightened with a grunt, dusted herself off, and started for the farm at a jog.

That lasted four minutes.

She slowed to a walk, breathing hard, head down, pace steady. Her shirt clung to her back and, where the hilt had pressed into her palms too long, her hands had begun to sting.

The path stretched wider the closer she got. Fences appeared again. The sounds of clucking picked up in the distance. A door slammed somewhere on the main house.

By the time she made it to the yard, someone had already spotted her.

Rina leaned out from the side door, wiping her hands on a towel.

“Back already?”

Elynara leaned forward, catching her breath.

“Um, I thought you would be able to help. I hunted three boars, and two Bonefangs. But I can’t possibly dismantle them all alone. They’re in the field. East edge.”

That brought movement and soon she saw the matron ordering things with full force. Her oldest son and the father soon emerged, pulling a cart behind him. They moved fast.

She followed, slower now.

They reached the bodies just as the first flies had started testing the corners of the tarps. The family took one look, exchanged a few impressed whistles, and started breaking down the nearest one with practiced ease.

She pitched in where she could, helping to hold corners steady, transfer tools, and drag limbs. Still, she flinched once when the knife tore too deep and the stench hit.

The father didn’t seem to notice. One of the girls handed her a cloth for her face, and that helped.

They didn’t leave the Bonefangs behind either.

Finnie pointed out that the cluckers didn’t care if the meat was strange, just that it was meat. Variety was important, apparently.

The whole walk back, the family chattered.

How rare it was to get this much meat at once. How old the biggest boar might’ve been. Whether bonefang bones could be turned into handles or tools.

Theories about why so many monsters had shown up this early in the season. No one agreed, but they chattered away anyway.

Elynara kept quiet through most of it. Too tired to add much, but she didn’t mind the sound.

By the time dinner rolled around, the table was packed. The matron had made a stew with boar meat already, fast and spiced. The room buzzed with new energy.

Every other sentence was about field pests, sharpening tricks, strange root growth near the orchard, or some poor cousin who once mistook a beehive for a wild melon.

She ate slowly, refilled her bowl once, then again.

By the time her chair scraped back, her arms were heavy. Her back ached from hauling, and her hands smelled faintly of dirt and salt even after washing.

She made it to her bed. Starpuff dropped onto her chest without warning and didn’t move again.

Neither did she, as sleep took her.
