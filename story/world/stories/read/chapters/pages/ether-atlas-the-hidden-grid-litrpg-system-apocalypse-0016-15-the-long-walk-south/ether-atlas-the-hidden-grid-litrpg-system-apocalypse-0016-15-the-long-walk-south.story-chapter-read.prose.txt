Chapter 15

"The children play with dice and call themselves warriors. They do not know that they are training. They do not know that the monsters they imagine will one day be real. We gave them the game. The game will give them the reflex. The reflex will keep them alive." - Codex of the Long Watch, Field Observation, 21st Century

The violet sky felt worse when you'd been underground. Stepping back onto Walbrook was like walking into a wrong world. The air tasted of ozone and something faintly sweet that Arthur couldn’t recall. The familiar grinding roar of London traffic was gone and in its place was a silence so complete that Arthur could hear the Bullguard Shield shift against his forearm when he adjusted his grip.

They fell into formation without conscious thought. Donna on point and a little to the side, her eyes sweeping the shadowed shopfronts; Arthur centre with the shield forward; Steve at the rear with the billhook bobbing on his shoulder. The arrangement had developed over the last few hours without anyone naming it.

"Right," Steve said, once they were moving. "There's something we need to discuss."

"The attributes," Donna said, without looking back or slowing her stride.

"How did you–"

"You've been twitching since we left the temple. You look like a man who needs the toilet but won't admit it and you should take up your grievance with the guards who threw us out."

"We have three attribute points sitting around," Steve said, leaning forward slightly as he walked. "That's like excavating a coin hoard and leaving it sitting in the trench."

"Not in the open," Donna said, her head turning towards an alley mouth before settling forward again. "Find us walls, a door, and a defensible line of sight. Then you can play with your spreadsheet."

***

They crossed London Bridge in silence. The Thames was black beneath them, reflecting the violet sky like a wound in a mirror.

Halfway across, Arthur saw the pram.

It was wedged against the stone balustrade where the bridge curved. It was empty, the canopy still up, and a cotton blanket folded neatly inside. Undamaged. He didn’t look inside or look for the person who’d been pushing it. He kept walking, and the part of him that had been holding him together since the pub, developed another hairline crack.

Bermondsey was dark on the south side. Victorian brick warehouses and sprawling council estates stood side by side, every window unlit, staring down like empty eye sockets. The streets looked like a film set after the crew had gone home.

Donna's hand came up, signalling a halt.

There were two shapes in the road ahead. Dogs, or what had been dogs. Staffies, by the blocky look of their heads, before the Ether had thickened their shoulders, elongated their limbs, and widened their jaws into clamps. They stood in the middle of the road, fifty metres away, facing the party with the confidence of animals that had learned in the last few hours that fear was no longer required.

The dogs moved together towards the party; they weren’t as fast as the foxes but moved with a low centre of gravity that Arthur read as battering-ram mechanics: mass times velocity, concentrated at shoulder height. The first one hit the Bullguard and the impact jarred Arthur’s wrist.

The second went wide, circling to the flank with its claws skittering over pavement. Donna met it with a brutal downward swing of the axe, which she followed up with a knife into the wound. Two clean strikes and it dropped.

The first dog scrambled backward, and came straight back at Arthur's knees. Arthur didn't wait for the lunging weight this time; he swung the shield edge-on into its ribs and the impact folded it sideways, legs scrabbling on tarmac.

Steve was over it before it could roll and reset, and drove the billhook down two-handed into the dog's spine. The curved blade punched through fur and muscle and the dog stopped moving.

They turned to find Donna crouching over the first kill. She'd seen something in the chest cavity, a faint glow barely visible through the matted fur and ruptured muscle. She reached into the gore without hesitation and pulled it free.

It was a crystal the size of a glass marble. Cloudy, weak, its internal light flickering like a dying bulb.

"Core, smaller than the bull's. Barely there." She said, holding it out on her bloodied palm.

“You should start carrying a pair of gloves.” Steve said, looking at her hands.

Archie's geometry angled towards the crystal. "Ether matrix density: twelve per cent of the Mithraeum specimen. Consistent with ambient-saturation fauna rather than node-proximity fauna."

Steve took the core carefully off Donna’s palm and held it to the light, turning it between his fingers. He frowned. “The bull’s core was the size of a walnut. This is a small pebble." He looked at Archie. “Is it weaker because the creature was weaker, or because it wasn’t near a node?”

"Both variables contribute," Archie replied, his voice flat and clinical. “The Mithraeum specimen was a challenge manifestation and it developed within sustained high-density Ether proximity. This specimen formed through ambient environmental saturation.”

“So proximity matters,” Steve said, half to himself. “The bull was a trail manifestation and soaking in concentrated Ether at the node’s centre. These dogs had street-level exposure. If the pattern holds, cores from node guardians will always outclass cores from street fauna.”

“We need a few more data points before I make a fool of myself, but that's the working theory.” Steve concluded, nodding to himself.

And then Arthur heard it.

Claws on tarmac and this time there were far more than two sets. The sound came from the alley to their left, from the side street to their right and from somewhere behind them. A rising clatter of nails on road surface.

At least eight. Coming from three directions. This was a pack. The two they'd killed had been the scouts.

Donna saw them first. Shapes pouring from the alley mouth, low and fast, spreading into a loose crescent. More came from the right. The formation was predatory. A hunting pattern refined over ten thousand years of canine evolution and now running on bodies that were twenty per cent larger and far denser than they should have been.

So they did the logical thing and ran.

Three humans sprinted down a Bermondsey side street because the arithmetic was simple, brutal and fatal. Three fighters, two with melee weapons and one with a shield, against eight enhanced creatures in open terrain with no chokepoint, no walls, no way to control the engagement was a death sentence.

Steve stumbled on a raised kerb stone. The billhook caught on a bin and he nearly lost it and his balance along with it but managed to keep hold of the shaft and kept running. Arthur could hear the pack behind them. They weren't rushing to sprint them down; they were running them the way wolves run a deer herd. Keeping pace and waiting for the slowest set of legs to flag.

Donna raised a hand, pointing towards a corner shop fifty yards ahead. "There!"

It was an old laundrette on the bend. It had a wide glass front, but the main door was heavy, solid wood set into a thick steel frame. Arthur didn't slow down. He rammed the door with his shoulder. The latch groaned, but the frame held. He stepped back half a pace and hit it a second time, this time leading with the Bullguard. The lock sheared and the three of them tumbled through into the dark room.

Donna kicked the door shut behind them and Arthur jammed the bottom edge of the shield against the baseboard. Then he threw his weight against the door and braced every muscle in his legs.

The first dog hit the door four seconds later and the impact transferred through the door into Arthur's arms. A second hit. A third. Then came the awful scratching. Claws scraping against steel trim and reinforced glass as the pack circled the exterior, looking for a gap but finding none.

Inside, the three of them sank down onto the linoleum floor and breathed.

Steve was sitting with his back against a row of dry-cleaning machines, holding the billhook across his knees with both hands. His arms were trembling violently. Arthur couldn't tell if it was adrenaline, exhaustion or pure terror but suspected it was a combination of all three.

"We killed a guardian," Steve gasped out between ragged breaths, wiping sweat from his forehead. "In the Mithraeum. A Seeder construct. And we just – we just ran from dogs."

"The Mithraeum was a room. One entrance. One enemy. Walls on every side. The encounter was designed." Arthur said, listening to the pack outside. The scratching was fading. "The street doesn't design encounters. The street is eight creatures against three, in the open with no terrain advantage. That's not a fight we are going to win at Level 2."

"Dungeon versus open world. The dungeon was a controlled environment. Scaled for us. This is the overworld, and we're underlevelled for it." Steve said, nodding.

Donna was at the window, watching the street through the glass. "They're leaving. Moving east." She waited another thirty seconds. "Clear."

***

Steve had the dog core in one hand and a dead mobile he'd picked up off the laundrette counter in the other. He stared between them, chewing the inside of cheeks.

"The bull core transformed the bin lid," he said, his voice thoughtful. "Vessel, core, intent. That's the framework. So logically, any core and any vessel—"

“Steve, experiments can wait,” Donna said. “The pack could be back at any moment, and I’d rather not trust our lives to a room secured by shoving a shield under the bloody door.”

"Thirty seconds, I promise. I want to test something."

He held the core against the phone's back panel and closed his eyes. Arthur watched him focus, his eyes closed, hands steady on the dead phone.

Steve thought of communication, signal, reach, trying to push the core's energy into a dead piece of technology. Nothing happened.

The core's glow flickered. A brief warmth spread over Steve's palm, then the light guttered, dimmed, and died. The crystal turned grey in his hand, cold and inert.

Steve stared at the dead crystal. "The material couldn't hold it. The Ether tried to restructure the vessel but I think the vessel's molecular composition rejected it."

"The substrate lacked a crystalline lattice structure," Archie said. "Ether restructuring requires molecular regularity. The previous vessel met that threshold. This one did not. Ether binding cannot restructure materials that have no affinity with the system that had produced the core.”

Steve glared up at the floating geometric shape. "You could have told me that before I burned it."

Archie's geometry rotated unhurriedly. "You did not ask."

Steve turned the phone over. "Plastic and silicon don't respond to Ether the way metal does. The bin lid worked because it was galvanised steel. A material with a crystalline grain structure the Ether could rewrite."

He put the dead core in his pocket anyway, as evidence of the first failed experiment of the new world.

"One core, wasted." Donna said.

"Not wasted," Steve countered immediately. "That was data. Expensive data, but essential data. We could still test with ceramic. What about wood? What about bone?”

"You just used our only spare core as a science experiment," Donna said.

"And now we know something that probably nobody else in the world knows. Every core we find from now on, we'll know what to bind it to and what not to. That's worth a marble-sized crystal from a dog."

Donna turned her gaze to Arthur. Arthur shrugged, adjusting the grip on the crowbar. "He's not wrong, Donna. Better to ruin a pebble on a dead phone now than ruin a proper one on something useless later."

***

They found a Boots pharmacy two streets south, its front security shutter twisted sideways from someone else's earlier panic. Arthur levered the door open with the crowbar.

Donna moved through the aisles pulling bandages, antiseptic, painkillers, and rehydration sachets into a carrier bag; Steve replaced his scratched glasses from a rack near the till.

The convenience store next door was half-looted. Whoever had been there first had taken the alcohol and the crisps, leaving the things that would actually keep a person alive. Protein bars, tinned tuna and water.

They sat down behind the main counter, hidden from the street, and ate their first meal since it had all started. The protein bars tasted like compressed sawdust. Arthur had never eaten anything better in his life.

Archie hovered near the door, his geometry angled towards the street. Arthur had started to recognise the configuration. Outward-facing with his lines spread thin. It looked remarkably like Archie was keeping watch. He'd never said he could detect threats, but the geometry always oriented towards them before anyone else noticed.

Arthur filed that away.

Steve had the guidebook open on the blank pages at the back. His handwriting was small and precise, angled to fit between the printed margins. There were numbers, observations, the bull fight, broken into mechanical components. Theory on attributes. The dog fight. The failed binding. A note beside Archie's name: provides data on request. Withholds context on principle.

"You're taking field notes," Arthur said.

"I'm an archaeologist with a front row seat to the most significant event in human history. Not documenting it would be practically criminal." Steve said, not looking up as his pencil kept moving. "Besides, someone is going to want to read this."

***

The streets of Deptford were darker than Bermondsey. The buildings here were lower, the gaps between blocks wider, leaving them feeling horribly exposed every time they crossed an open intersection. The pull behind his eyes had sharpened. Greenwich was close, maybe two miles.

Steve stopped midstep.

He was staring at the walls of an old Victorian church with soot-darkened bricks. But Steve wasn't looking at the architecture. He was looking at a mark on the stone beside the entrance. Three lines, intersecting at equal angles.

"I've seen this before," Steve said quietly. "Gobekli Tepe in Turkey, on a Phoenician stele in Malta. And today on the stonework near the Mithraeum stairs." He looked at Arthur. "Someone marked this church with the same symbol. Separated by thousands of years and thousands of miles."

Archie's geometry pulsed with a single, brief flash of blue but he said nothing.

Steve sketched the symbol in the guidebook margin. Three lines at one-twenty-degree intervals. A convergence point.

"That's not a coincidence. It can’t be." Steve said softly, staring at his drawing.

They kept walking. Two streets later, Donna slowed her pace, raising a hand to hold them back. Through the gap between a row of terraced houses and a boarded-up pub, Arthur could see a shop front with its metal shutters pulled three-quarters down. Behind the gap, furniture had been stacked against the glass. Through the barricade they could see shapes moving. People.

"Survivors. Six, maybe more. Barricaded in." Donna murmured.

Arthur studied the structure. The shelving unit on the left was bearing most of the lateral load. If anything hit that from the street side, the whole arrangement would fold inward.

"That barricade needs work," he said.

Arthur thought about the people in the pub who'd died while he was rolling dice. He thought about the flaw in the barricade, one he could see from where he stood and fix in ten minutes to save lives.

"We approach," he said.

Donna nodded once and started walking.

THE ETHER ATLAS: FIELD NOTES OF STEVEN PARR

London

THE THREE-LINE MARK

Three lines. Equal angles. 120 degrees apart, meeting at a single point. It is called the three-pointed star. And I can name from memory at least four places I’ve seen the same symbol appear.

Göbekli Tepe, Anatolia. Pillar 18. Around 9600 BCE. A Phoenician stele on Malta, around 800 BCE. The Bloomberg stonework beside the Mithraeum stairs, Roman London, around 240 CE. And tonight, I found one on the outer wall of St Nicholas, Deptford, date unknown. Four sites, across three continents separated by Eleven thousand years between the oldest and the newest. One mark, cut to the same angles every time, into stone harder than the tools that were meant to exist when the first one was made. Pre-Pottery Neolithic A and B period, Göbekli had no metal. None. You do not get a clean 120-degree bearing in limestone from a flint scraper.

My field has a way of explaining something that turns up in two places that never met in one of two ways. Either a person carried it between them, or separate people reached for it on their own.

Diffusion, or independent invention.

Both are dead here. Nobody walked a mark from pre-pottery Anatolia to Roman London across nine thousand years of no contact. And independent invention gives you a rough family of shapes, a common cross, a common spiral, never the identical proportion and the identical convergence point, four times, to the degree. When both of your explanations fail together, you are left holding the one they train you never to say aloud.

Then there is what it is for. It is not decoration and it is not writing. Decoration drifts over eleven thousand years, and writing changes with the languages around it. This held its exact proportions at every site, across all that time, which means it was doing a job too precise to allow any drift.

Three lines converging on a point is a technique and we have been looking at it like a picture. You fix a place you cannot reach by taking bearings on it from places you can, and where they cross, you have it. Surveyors call it trilateration. The mark is a benchmark, the reference a surveyor cuts into bedrock so a coordinate can never move again. Someone was surveying these sites.
