The core flared once more, and almost instinctively, Vir retreated into his Crown Chakra mindscape, summoning every one of his predecessors as he’d done a thousand times prior.

His mental world bucked and shook, but Vir weathered the storm, hardly even noticing it. It never failed to impress Vir how one could grow so used to something so absurd.

He’d almost lost control those first few times, panicking desperately, entirely at a loss for how to control such an impossible amount of prana.

But then Siya had come up with a solution, as she always did. The girl possessed a mind that far outstripped Vir’s, and he would never know how she came up with a tenth of the ideas she had.

As genius and innovative as they all were, however, most had failed. From suppression Artifacts to Artifacts that converted Affinity, taking advantage of the incredibly inefficient process to aid with Vir’s problem while simultaneously increasing the prana density of the atmosphere, the list had been nearly endless.

Not did her knowledge stop at Thaumaturgy and the intricacies of prana. Siya understood Chakras on a level even Vir didn’t despite the very concept not existing during her time. Despite only having studied it for a handful of weeks.

It was she who had given him the idea to imagine the rupturing core as cracks in his mindscape.

Something she knew he was intimately familiar with from back when his soul had shattered.

A small thing, to be sure, and yet one that allowed him a functional, if almost impossibly difficult, way to fight back.

It was good that time flowed slower while inside his mindscape. The effect had impressively slowed time to a tenth of what it normally would have been… that was, before the core.

Through a mechanism neither understood, the core magnified Vir’s Mindscape to levels he’d not thought possible.

Not only did the time effect grow far, far stronger, he was even able to bring others into the mindscape.

He’d done so with Siya and every other Samsara in the camp.

It seemed there were no limits to this ability, and if he tried hard enough, he might very well be able to bring the entire world into his mind, though for what purpose, he didn’t know.

As it was, Vir had lost count of the years he’d spent in there, fighting battle after endless battle each time the core got out of hand.

He was, thankfully not alone. The endless prana had augmented every one of his predecessors, and they fought side by side to mend the ruptures, often flying to the far corners of the realm to do so.

From Rani to Brij to Vraj Parah, Vir had the opportunity to revisit several places he’d not set foot in in years, even if it was all in his head.

In reality, each flare-up took perhaps a day. A week at most, as Siya confirmed, and when he exited the mindscape, it was usually to a new invention. Each as unlikely to function as the last.

Unfortunately, Siya’s prana absorbers hadn’t functioned at all since the core had first ruptured all those months ago.

She’d rebuilt it four times since, yet while they did an admirable job most of the time, none could handle the violent spikes it randomly discharged. Sometimes thousands of times stronger than anything she could build to handle.

Not only would those discharges break the crystal and force Vir to fire off the energy at a nearby hill, melting his entire arm, plenty of excess prana didn’t obey his command. Sometimes careening harmlessly to the sky, and sometimes creating craters in the ground.

It was precisely why he couldn’t have Maiya, or anyone and anything valuable near him.

A significant issue considering their journey to seek Janak’s many laboratories for information. Laboratories that had been lost to the Ash, scattered throughout the new unified realm.

Upon mending the final crack to his mindscape, Vir bid his predecessors goodbye, closing the Mindscape and rising to his feet. Then he took to the sky, flying west at maximum speed as vast trees blurred under him.

Several minutes later—the minimum distance he felt was safe—Vir he arrived at the entrance to the latest of Janak’s laboratory, buried underground in the middle of a jungle.

Penetrating the dense canopy, Vir landed on the damp ground and injected his prana into the ancient Imperium metal door cleverly disguised as an overgrown tangle of brambles descending into the tunnel beyond.

Siya was in the same spot she’d been at every day for the past week. Cross-legged on the floor of Janak’s study, surrounded by no less than two dozen glowing tablets haphazardly placed around her.

Every single one was lit up with projections of diagrams or dense text, casting their pale blue light across her face.

Her father’s work. The original Janak, that was. Not the copy Vir had known and interacted with.

The last legacies of a god neither of them had ever met.

“You handled it again,” she muttered without looking up. “This was the twenty-eighth occurrence.”

“Was it?” Vir asked with a wry smile. “I lost count long ago.”

“It is,” she said, putting away her tablet to gaze up at him. “I have been keeping track, and the frequency has increased as of late.” Her voice might have been cold and analytical, but Vir knew by now that erecting an ice wall was how the girl masked her worry.

It was something he always stopped her from doing, and this time was no different.

He went up to her and began ruffling her head.

“Wha-what are you!?” she asked, cheeks reddening.

“That’s no way for someone your age to talk, Siya.”

“Well, I’m different!” she snapped, crossing her arms and puffing her cheeks.

“See? Much better.”

Vir crouched down beside her, so they were closer to the same height.

“I know that look. You’ve made progress, haven’t you?”

Siya’s cheeks somehow grew even larger, but she didn’t disagree.

“I have,” she said, clearing her throat.

She reached across her audience of tablets and grabbed one. While written in a language Vir knew, he could actually understand perhaps one word in ten of the words that covered the page. As much as Vir had learned and experienced, technical manuals of the Prime Imperium were beyond him.

He somehow suspected they would always be, even if he had a thousand years to study them.

“I found something we might use,” she said. “My father built three prana storage devices akin to your Prana Core… though admittedly only a fraction in scale. This was long before the fall, and was part of several different experiments.” Her finger stopped on a cluster of symbols near the bottom. “Two failed the exact way yours is now. As he sought to introduce more prana, it outgrew the vessel meant to hold it.”

“And the third?”

“In a way. That one used… something I still don’t understand.” Siya looked up at him for the first time since he’d come in. “The vessel broke, mind you. Just that he found a means to bypass the problem altogether. A means he found exceedingly risky. So much so he abandoned the research altogether.”

Vir waited for the rest of the explanation, but Siya remained silent.

Which was entirely unlike her curious self. When she latched onto something, she wouldn’t stop talking until she’d covered every last point.

“Siya?”

“No, Vir,” she said, shaking her head. “There are so many parts of his method I do not even understand. It would be irresponsible to even attempt a solution with so many missing pieces.”

“That’s never stopped you before. You’ve told me about a dozen ideas you weren’t sure of.”

“Those were different. If those failed, they melted a crystal or took the top off a mountain at worst. I understood those, more or less. But this…,” she said, setting the tablet down. Vir gave it a hard look, as though all its mysteries might be revealed with a sufficiently powerful glare.

“This one is not like those. If I make even one misstep, everyone dies. And even if it works… I’m unsure it is worth the cost.”

Vir went silent at that. In all the months since the core had ruptured, through every failed absorber and every night he’d spent fighting his own mindscape, Siya had never once been scared to pursue Janak’s inventions.

She treated them more as puzzles than anything. A way to reconnect with her long-lost father. Puzzles she was happy to fail at, only to try again. Whatever she had found in this tablet was clearly different.

“But you can at least tell me what it involves, yes?” Vir asked.

For a moment the girl looked far older than eleven, and far more tired. Then she shook her head and frowned stubbornly.

“If… if you attempt this, and even if it works, you cannot go back to what you once were,” she said slowly. “Ever. You will be forever removed from the Great Cycle.”

Vir looked at her for a long moment. For Siya to be so cautious meant what she’d found must be truly significant.

Vir crouched down so he was at her height, then squeezed her shoulder. “You know that this is not my decision alone to make. Whatever we decide, whatever path we take, it will involve the three of us. Yes?”

“Thank you,” she said, deflating as some of the tension went out of her shoulders. “But I worry that you will manage to convince me…”

“It’s that terrifying, then?”

“That and more,” she replied.

“I tell you what,” Vir said with a gentle smile. “Why don’t you get some sleep? You’re always watching over me, it’s time I did the same, yes? Then, when you’ve rested on it, we can chat about this further. Or not. It is your decision.”

The girl looked ready to protest, but Vir stood and put a hand on her head. “Even geniuses sleep, Siya. Your father must have said the same thing, too. I’m sure of it.”

She took his hand and let him pull her up, trying and failing to hide her smirk.

“He did not, in fact,” she said smugly. “He worked for eleven days without rest once.”

“Then he was a fool, and I’ll not have his daughter following his example.”

Some time later, after Siya had finally lain down among her tablets and gone to sleep, Vir left the laboratory for rock overhang some distance away. He’d made his home there to protect Janak’s workshop while Siya worked her magic inside.

The sun was just beginning to set, and nightfall would not be long coming.

Vir started up at the already-blueing sky and wondered how many demons still struggled with the change. He’d always found the stars magical. Ever since they were children, he’d stare up at the stars with Maiya late into the night well past their bedtime.

He wondered about the war he’d left, unfairly, in Maiya’s hands. A burden to shoulder alone when all he so desperately wished was to rejoin her side. To ease her burdens.

He had been gone far longer than he’d ever meant to, and still it was not long enough.

Even now, after all his progress, the core flared up with alarming regularity. More frequently than ever, as Siya pointed out.

The girl seemed to underestimate his ability to weather bad news. Whatever Janak’s plan was, Vir doubted he’d flinch. After all these months, they’d finally stumbled upon the makings of a solution, where there had never been one before. A possible path out of this desperate labyrinth.

Be it permanent or lethal, whatever the cost, Vir already knew he was ready to pay it.

After all, what was one life in the face of the realm?
