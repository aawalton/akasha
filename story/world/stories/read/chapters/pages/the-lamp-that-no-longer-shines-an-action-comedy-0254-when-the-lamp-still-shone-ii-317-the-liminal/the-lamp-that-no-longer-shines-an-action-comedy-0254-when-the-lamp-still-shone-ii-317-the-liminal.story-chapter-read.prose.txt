[Time]: 1980

The cork of the 1812 vintage yielded with a sharp, definitive detonation, and the cascade of pale gold champagne arcing through the air of the private lounge failed to alarm anyone present.

Irene Habsburg leaned back against the mahogany armrest, swirling the foam settling in her crystal flute. She held a thick sheaf of parchment in her other hand, tapping one page with a manicured finger.

"Third paragraph," she said. "The line about how standing on the shoulders of giants only matters if you aren't already taller than them." She tilted her head, golden eyes alight with the satisfied pleasure of someone who has already won and is simply choosing how to announce it. "Too obvious? Or just enough false humility to make the faculty feel they contributed something?"

The witches surrounding her, heirs to ancient bloodlines, the generation supposed to produce the era's defining names, leaned forward and laughed. A Ghost Witch of the Harkins line adjusted Irene's lapel with the chilling, aristocratic grace of a high-altitude phantom, her expression a flawless mask of frosted glass. The syllables slipping from her bloodless lips were measured and clinical: murmuring that the speech was a divine revelation, that the faculty were fundamentally unworthy to weep in its presence, and that she would personally liquidate anyone who applauded too softly.

Irene flashed the smile that had earned her the underground title of [The Darkly Rakish Lady Berenice]: all predatory warmth and dangerous promise, a thing that could be neither taught nor earned.

She accepted the adulation as her natural, geological due.

This was the shape of her time at the Yggdrasil Academy, the crown jewel of Witch civilization. Born in a barren, low-magic backwater, she had walked through these gates starving, and the faculty here were the most gifted minds in the Inner Sea, working the bleeding edge of arcane theory. As long as a professor still held a fragment of the arcane she had not yet taken, Irene was a masterpiece of devotion. She lingered in the twilight of their offices, leaning over star-charts with her golden eyes reflecting the astrolabe's glow, breathless and captivated, asking the precise, brilliant questions that proved she understood a scholar's work better than their own colleagues did. Intoxicated by a reverence that was entirely unfeigned, they poured their most guarded research into her hands, and believed they were shaping the architect of the next era.

They were. But the tragedy of teaching Irene Habsburg was the velocity of her consumption: paradigms that had cost their authors a career to assemble, she devoured and deconstructed and outgrew in a span measured in days. The thrilling, electric resistance of a superior mind pressing back against hers, the shape of a genuine ceiling, would last a week at the outside. Then she would correct the flaw in a celebrated formula on the chalkboard, watch its author stare at the chalk in her hand in silent, ruined awe, and feel the warmth evaporate out of her own chest. She acknowledged the vital scaffolding they had given her; she had simply drained the vessel, and no longer possessed the patience to pretend they were standing on the same floor. She navigated the treacherous hierarchies of the high academy the way Rastignac conquered Parisian salons, and her so-called rivals among the students were merely courtiers competing for slots in a schedule she had already optimized for extraction.

The heavy brass bells chimed across the quad.

Two hours later, the grand auditorium functioned less as a ceremony than as a hostage situation.

Standard protocol: the Dean announced a name, the recipient ascended the carpeted steps, accepted their medal, gave a gracious nod, and descended.

Irene walked up the marble steps for the first award. She never came back down.

She stood at the center of the stage in the intersection of twelve arcane spotlights, her posture a masterclass in careless aristocracy, as the continent's most decorated professors formed an orderly administrative queue and approached her one by one to deposit their various pieces of engraved metal and sealed parchment. The only witches who set foot on stage were the recipients of group or runner-up categories, and they stationed themselves at the extreme edges with the careful, self-effacing energy of stagehands who understood they were not the sun.

When the final applause subsided, she descended with her arms full and handed the entire stack of priceless academic artifacts to the first passing student she encountered, who received them with the blank, disoriented expression of someone handed a live explosive.

The crowd surged. Silk, perfume, breathless invitations to three competing after-parties. Irene raised one hand, and the crowd stilled.

"I've had enough of this," she said. The warmth had gone out of her voice entirely, replaced by the flat authority of an empress confronting a roomful of jesters after an overlong performance. "Go play somewhere else. I'll find you tonight if I decide to."

She turned and walked away, and the elite of her generation parted without a single murmur of dissent.

The campus pathways were lined with students who stopped when they saw her coming. Bows. Stammering, breathless greetings. Irene offered half-second nods without breaking stride, her golden eyes scanning ahead for a route to silence.

Something had arrived, and it was settling into her bones with the patient permanence of something that had been waiting a while.

She had swept the board, and there were no hidden bosses, no secret endgame content the registry hadn't disclosed: just an endless plateau where her teachers could no longer follow, and the hollow exhaustion of standing at the peak of the civilized world and finding the ceiling still low enough to touch.

She veered off the main paths, taking a narrow staircase into the upper spires of the instructional towers, where the crowd noise thinned and the altitude wind began to assert itself.

A violent gust swept down the seventh-floor corridor, snapping the hanging banners hard against the stone. Irene stopped.

At the far end of the hall, the double doors of an empty classroom stood open. The massive windows beyond had been unlatched, and the high-altitude gales tore through the space. Outside, the sky was bleeding: a violent slash of bruised violet and crushed gold, the dying light pressing long, warped shadows across the empty desks and floorboards. A flock of dark birds wheeled sharply through the burning air.

Sitting on the windowsill, back to the room, legs dangling over the sheer drop with the comfortable ease of someone in a garden chair, was a figure.

Irene's breath caught. The woman looked impossibly light: so perfectly still amid the howling wind that Irene felt a sudden, irrational certainty the next strong gust would simply carry her into the sky. And at the same time the whole room leaned toward that ledge, walls, ceiling and the last of the light bending gently round her, the way an enormous mass bends the fabric of the universe.

The recognition arrived before any deliberate thought could form.

Ovelia, the Witch of Eternal Slumber, Head of the Grand Witches.

The elders had been very thorough on this subject. In 1939, the family had looked at this exact woman and made a calculation: the Habsburg dynasty, one of the proudest military powers in the region, would formally surrender without combat rather than face her in the field. They had written it into official military doctrine and left it there. If you encounter Ovelia in wartime, surrender immediately.

Irene had met other Grand Witches before. She had measured their auras, catalogued their operational limits, and filed them neatly under "ceiling of the civilization."

For the first time in her life, Irene understood that she had been comprehensively, terrifyingly wrong. The others were Grand Witches. The woman on the windowsill was a cosmological event.

The figure turned her head. Eyes the deep black of a midnight sky scattered with stars caught the dying light. Starlight fractured and danced within those irises, moving with a slow, internal rhythm that had nothing to do with the ambient glow.

"Irene," she said.

How does she know my name.

Then Ovelia smiled, with the quiet, absolute joy of someone who has been watching a horizon for a very long time and has finally seen the right ship appear.

And something in Irene's chest answered, by no mechanism she could account for: a phantom ache, and the bone-deep warmth of a reunion she had no memory of earning.

First meeting, she told herself, because she was precise about things. This is the first meeting. You have never been in the same room as this person.

She opened her mouth to speak, and blinked, and the doorframe was gone, the distance erased.

Irene's mana field spiked in the same instant it was already irrelevant. In the foundational laws of Witchcraft, teleporting a living, shielded, high-tier caster without a casting delay, without ambient mana displacement, without a single observable frame of preparation, was impossible. The resistance coefficient alone should have required—

The axiom does not apply here, her logic concluded, in the flat, airless tone of a proof invalidated mid-step.

The wind pressed against Irene's face. Even through the gale she could smell Ovelia: a scent so thin it came close to nothing at all, and beneath the nothing a stillness, like a water lily opening at night on a pool at the bottom of some deep cave, where no lily had any right to grow. She was close enough to feel the faint stellar chill coming off Ovelia's skin.

Ovelia leaned forward, closing the last inch of space with an unhurried ease that suggested the universe had rearranged itself for this and was content about it.

Her voice was low and warm, meant for no one else, carrying the weight of something being said for the first time and also, somehow, the ten-thousandth.

"Be my disciple."
