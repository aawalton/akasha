With a wave of Janak’s hands, a projected display materialized, covered with a flowing script that Vir recognized as Imperium. Or the language of the Gods, as some would call it.

The ancient god scanned it quickly, his fingers moving through the interface faster than Vir could track.

He was about to work some unknowable Thaumaturgy to seal the Swarm when he paused, his hand hovering over a final glyph.

“What are you doing!?” Maiya shouted, backing against the wall as the Swarm expanded to fill half the chamber.

“I think,” Janak said slowly, studying the readings, “that will not be necessary.”

Vir turned to face the Swarm, watching as it wrapped around him, threatening to devour him.

He braced himself for a war of prana… that never came. Its touch was cold but not painful. It didn’t consume him, didn’t tear at his flesh or even drain his prana, other than lightly pulling at it, as if to indicate it wished for more. It was an out-of-place gesture. Almost affectionate…

“How?” Vir breathed.

Janak dismissed his display with a wave. “This Swarm seems to be composed of your own prana, and prana is the essence of life itself. With enough of it concentrated in one place, it has the potential to gain a degree of intelligence. Potential that is almost never realized. With most Swarms, the prana is polluted with fragments from thousands of living beings. Each screaming for dominance. Like thousands of voices in your head, talking at once, they devolve to their basest instincts, seeking only to grow and consume.”

He gestured at the dark cloud hugging Vir. “This one is different. It has quite a bit of you in it. Not you, exactly, but it carries your essence. Your will.”

Vir raised a tentative hand, and the Swarm flowed around his fingers like water. “It doesn’t seem to want to harm me.”

So all that time before, it just wanted to come closer? It wasn’t trying to destroy the Vimana?

Then again, the Swarm probably would have destroyed the Vimana just to get close to him. It wasn’t his threat at all that had caused the Fateweavers to eject him from that universe. It was the genuine danger of an entity that cared about him running rampant if they did anything to him.

“Can I command it?” Vir asked.

Janak shook his head. “I think not. It cannot understand your words, but it will likely share your will in most things. Whether or not you wish it to or not, it will follow you everywhere.”

“Great,” Vir muttered. “Yet another reason for people to think I’m a living god.”

“Except,” Janak said quietly, “we are.”

Vir’s head snapped up, eyes narrowing. “What does that mean?”

“Akh Nara is more than just a title,” Janak said, and there was weight in those words. “But we can discuss that later. First…” He moved past Vir to kneel beside the wolves who had crowded around Ashani’s still form, prodding her with their muzzles. “Don’t worry, my friends,” he said softly. “She will be all right.”

He picked Ashani up as though she were a delicate princess and moved toward a doorway that melted out of the walls as he approached. The wolves followed immediately, and Vir and Maiya exchanged a glance before trailing after them.

“Welcome to my prison,” Janak said bitterly from up ahead.

“Some prison,” Maiya muttered in awe, and Vir had to concur.

The grand hall beyond the corridor took Vir’s breath away.

Having lived in Mahādi for years, Vir was no stranger to Imperium construction. Marvelous, yes, but even the greatest wonder had a way of losing its sheen when one gazed upon it every day.

This, however, was something else entirely. The walls didn’t just weave prana into their aesthetic as most Imperium structures did; their very structural integrity seemed reliant on stretches of prana linking arches that floated to their supports, on stairs that were suspended midair, and the list went on. Nearly every surface incorporated prana on a level Vir had never seen.

“Gods,” Maiya whispered, her eyes wide as she took it all in, and Vir wanted desperately to show her that what she was seeing was less than half of it.

To Prana Vision, Fire affinity prana created what looked like burning lines in complex designs across the walls, and Water affinity prana pooled on the roof, making it seem as though it was defying gravity. And through it all, ash prana—or Origin prana as they called it—coated the surface of every object, contrasting the other colors with its deep, pitch-black hue.

Not only that, but the scale was staggering. The vaulted roof of the structure must have soared a hundred stories above their heads, visible only through some optical magnification magic Vir could only guess at.

Even still, the grand hall felt intimate rather than the cavernous space it was. Maybe it was the balconies and platforms that jutted from the perimeter above them, connected by bridges that crisscrossed overhead. Or maybe it was the lavish rugs that dotted the floors under their feet.

“I’ve seen my fair share of Mahādi,” Vir said, unable to keep the awe from his voice. “But I've come across nothing like this.”

“This was our crown jewel,” Janak said without turning around. “The seat of power of the Imperium the world over. No expense was spared in making it as lavish and impressive as possible. Now it stands as testament to my folly.”

His folly? Vir wondered.

Exiting the grand hall, they passed through a chamber where the stars were projected onto the ceiling, except they were all wrong, showing constellations that didn’t exist in any realm Vir knew.

Another room held what looked like a garden, except the plants were infused with prana of all affinities. An experiment on Janak’s part, perhaps? Vir hadn’t even considered how the god supported himself here. He’d assumed he didn’t need to eat, but perhaps that had been a premature assumption after all.

“How is this building still intact?” Maiya asked. “Isn’t this the epicenter of the explosion that destroyed the rest of the city?”

“A combination of luck, good design, and restoration inscriptions,” Janak replied, glancing back at Vir. “I assume Ashani showed you the Fall?”

Vir nodded, feeling how strange it was to talk to this being of myth and legend in the flesh after communicating through projections and brief interactions for so long. There was a time when Janak had been nothing but a figure of the scriptures that Apramor, Maiya’s father, would use to encourage Vir to grow past his fears.

How times change…

“As she would,” Janak replied, oblivious to Vir’s musings. “This was indeed the epicenter and was thus spared much of the force of the blast. Not enough to preserve the lives of the denizens within it, however. I am but a copy of the original Janak. Given life upon his death, to continue his mission.”

Copy or not, Vir couldn’t imagine what he’d been through. Ashani’s story was horrific enough as it was… to be trapped here for millennia for an event caused by someone else?

“Do you resent it?” Vir had spoken before he’d realized it, cursing himself for letting his thoughts slip.

Janak didn’t reply for a long while, bringing them to a smaller hemispherical chamber with white walls that glowed with a soft white light in the way most Imperium surfaces did. But the table at the center was clearly some sort of medical bed, surrounded by machinery whose purpose Vir couldn’t even begin to guess at.

“I used to, once,” Janak said. “But I share his mind if not his soul, and his mission became mine millennia ago. Even if it is futile. Even if I must live out my days in this prison of his daughter’s making.”

Vir was about to ask what he meant when he laid Ashani gently on the table and activated something. Through Prana Vision, Vir watched as precise threads of Ash prana entered her mind, carefully stimulating specific pathways within that network that had made Vir’s head hurt back when he’d attempted to understand it.

More than any architecture, more than any other creation he’d seen, Ashani might well be the most miraculous creation the Imperium had ever made.

The goddess’ eyes fluttered open and she sat up slowly, as though waking from a thousand years of sleep. Her gaze swept the room, briefly registering Vir and Maiya, but when her eyes landed on Janak, she went still.

For a long moment, neither moved, and the tension in the air seemed to press down upon the room. Even the wolves felt it, whimpering with ears folded back.

Janak opened his mouth as if to speak, but before he could, Ashani rushed forward and threw her arms around him, embracing him with a desperation that made Vir’s chest tighten.

“I am but a copy of the one you loved,” Janak said somberly, his voice hoarse as he stroked her hair.

Sorry? Loved? Vir thought, glancing at Maiya, who had the same incredulous expression.

And then Ashani did something Vir had never once seen her do in all their years together.

She cried.

Not tears of grief or loss, but deep, wracking sobs that shook her entire frame, weeping as she clung to Janak as if she were drowning and he was her lifeline.

“Come now,” she half-laughed, half-gasped between sobs. “After all we have been through, how can you possibly say such a thing?”

Though Vir didn’t fully grasp what was going on, it was clear to all that, in that moment, she was less a goddess and more an innocent young woman reuniting with someone she’d thought lost forever. The composure she always wore, the serene otherworldliness, all fell away, leaving behind the true Ashani.

A being that, he now realized, he had scarcely ever known.

He felt like an intruder. The wolves had the good sense to look away, pressing close to Maiya’s legs, and maybe it was his imagination, but even the Swarm seemed to dim slightly, as if sensing the tenderness of the moment, and Vir wanted nothing more than to be anywhere else.

When Ashani finally pulled away, her face might’ve been streaked with tears, but her expression was more peaceful and cathartic than ever before. She looked at Vir and smiled—genuinely smiled—for the first time he could remember. It was… beautiful, and they truly looked as though they belonged together, despite the rather stark difference in appearance.

“Who are you?” Vir breathed, the question encompassing so much more than just Janak’s identity.

Ashani stepped back, wiping her eyes. Janak chuckled, the sound surprisingly warm despite his earlier attitude.

“I am you, Akh Nara,” Janak said simply.

Vir was about to ask what that meant, but Janak held up a hand.

“The question you should be asking,” Janak continued, his expression growing serious, “is who is she?”

Vir and Maiya turned to Ashani. The goddess had composed herself somewhat, though her eyes were still red. She gave them a genuine smile and an expression that was equal parts relief, guilt, and happiness.

“I said it when we first met, did I not?” Ashani asked gently. “A servant to whom keepeth Fate. By way of the shepherd.”

The words hung in the air, their meaning slowly crystallizing in Vir’s mind. Because her words, as ludicrous as they were, needed no clarification.

Maiya stepped back, immediately on guard. “Vir? Is that true?”

Vir stared Ashani in the eyes, his mind reeling. “It… is. Those are the exact words she said when we first met. I’d simply forgotten.”

“No,” Ashani said, her smile turning rueful. “You simply did not know enough to understand. Janak?”

She looked at the god, who nodded. “There is nothing they can do now. Not in his presence,” he said, glancing at Vir, which only made him more confused.

“Then allow me to reintroduce myself, Akh Nara, Maiya. I am Ashani. The First of all agents of those who seek to control Fate… and Janak’s concubine.”
