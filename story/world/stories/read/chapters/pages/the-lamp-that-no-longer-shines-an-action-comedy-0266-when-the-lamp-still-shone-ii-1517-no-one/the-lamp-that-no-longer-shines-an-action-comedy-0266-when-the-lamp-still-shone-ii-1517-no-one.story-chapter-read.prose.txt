[Time]: Twenty-three years, concurrent[Location]: The court that was never convened

The courtroom was vast, and vaulted in cold marble. There was no judge upon the high bench. There was no jury in the tiered boxes. There was no gallery, no clerk, no bailiff at the doors. The gavel lay where it had been set down by nobody, and it had never once been lifted.

The blood came up through the seams in the stonework, warm and arterial and unhurried. It spilled down the aisles in slow heavy sheets and rose through the architecture of justice the way water rises in a sealed room, and it kept rising.

Where it reached the toes of the accused it parted, and went around her, and closed again behind her heels, held off by a law older than physics and written for her exclusively.

None of it was happening anywhere. The court ran on a single thread, one of several, opened once and never closed since. It cost her nothing she needed elsewhere, and nothing on it had ever been permitted anywhere near her face. Irene Habsburg stood on the dry marble, and the court began to present its exhibits.

The blood on the floor of the Habsburg great hall was warm, and real. The Matriarch lay face-down across the lower steps of the dais, her spine opened below the shoulder blades, the edges of the wound still withdrawing as the toxin ate outward through the bone. Her mana bled into a carpet that had been in the family four hundred years, and the blade in Irene's hand had come out as clean as it went in.

Along the eastern wall stood five witches of the dead woman's own grade. Behind them, fifty of the rank below. Behind those, filling the galleries to the upper balustrade, three thousand of the commons. The law asked only that the doors be open wide enough for that many to see it done, and the Inner Sea had packed the room out by noon. Kinslayer, kingslayer: she had been both as a mortal, and she was both again as a witch.

Then, from the empty air behind her, two arms came around her shoulders for the first time. They were made of nothing at all, and Irene felt the warmth of them go into her marrow, and the instant she did they were gone.

The phantom stepped around to her front and stopped an inch above the floor, hands clasped behind her back, chin tilted up. Raven-black hair, and eyes with the slow turning light of a distant cosmos in them. She smiled the way cats smile when they have decided to be a problem.

"Congratulations," the phantom purred. "You are more Habsburg than the Habsburgs now."

Irene laughed. To every witness on the record, it was the laugh of a new sovereign standing in her predecessor's blood. But Irene was only remembering a joke. Ovelia had once reviewed the entire dynasty in five words, without lifting her head from the cushion: Rabid. A jar of spiders.

Which was, on the merits, accurate. Mary Noeffler, former head of the Grand Witches, had been executed for murdering her own sister; the species did not rank many crimes above it. There was also the Death Duel: sanctioned killing, for anyone patient enough to satisfy the preconditions.

Nowhere in any of it had anyone thought to specify that the two parties could not be related. The Habsburgs had found that silence four hundred years ago and built a dynasty inside it. Kinslaying, filed as a duel. No unfair war, so the challenger must hold certification at the defender's own grade. No unannounced war, so the galleries must be full. No unjust war, so the Association must examine the grievance and stamp it. The Matriarch had recited the law aloud to Irene's face the day she was named heir, which the Association accepted as the filing. Correctly certified, correctly witnessed, correctly archived.

Irene held the bright, sunlit smile perfectly in place and looked out across the galleries. "So," she said. "Does anyone have any objections?"

The hall came apart. The five of the Matriarch's own grade brought their staves down against the flagstones in unison, and the commons went up onto the balustrade and screamed for her.

Not one objection was entered into the record.

The blood in the courtroom closed over the first tier of the jury box.

Two years into her reign. Irene was already the darling of every parlor worth entering, and in those rooms information fell toward her the way water finds a drain.

"Did you hear?" An Arch-Witch, leaning in over a crystal flute. "The Witch of Eternal Slumber kissed the second Wellington daughter. They say when the child is older she'll take her as a disciple."

Irene took a slow sip of her wine. The curve of her mouth did not move. "Is that so? Who is saying it?"

"Everyone. And the kiss on the child's cheek is genuine, at least. The Witch of Whimsy saw it herself."

Irene exhaled, gently, through her nose. "How truly fascinating."

Not long after, she went to the Wellington estate and sat through the business with perfect grace, and then her luck arranged the coincidence she had come for, and delivered Cecilia into a garden corridor. And, standing a few steps behind her, Evangeline.

Irene looked at the child's face and found the mark at once. A simple symbol under the skin, something close to an all-seeing eye. It was Kara's. It radiated a boundless, patient warmth: a ward to let a child walk deeper into the dreamlands than a child had any business walking, and to burn anything that came for her there down to ash before it got close enough to be noticed.

Kara has always been far too soft on children. Irene's gaze stayed on the mark for exactly one second too long anyway.

Cecilia's hand came up to her own cheek, unthinking. "Are you looking at it too, Lady Irene?" She gave a small, slightly rueful smile.

"I imagine anyone would be curious," Irene said.

"It's probably just because she's so gentle," Cecilia said.

Not a single crack appeared anywhere in Irene's face. "You are a very lucky child."

Too young. Grown, it could have been arranged. A grievance can be built out of almost anything, but no Association on the continent would stamp a challenge against a girl of six.

Evangeline had not moved, and had not stopped watching. When Cecilia had been sent on, Evangeline looked up. Her mouth curved, and the amethyst eyes carried a light, weightless malice. "What are you enduring?"

Irene's gaze came down and settled on her. The corner of her mouth moved by a fraction of a degree. "That smile," she said quietly, "is the one Avenka wears when she is curious."

So she has met Avenka. One child wearing Kara's mark, and the other standing close enough to Avenka to take the face off her.

Evangeline produced a small, slightly rueful smile. "I don't know what you're talking about."

"And that one," Irene said, "was Cecilia's. A moment ago."

Evangeline stopped smiling. "Disgusting."

Irene tilted her head, gave her a flawless one, and walked out.

Only when the corridor was entirely empty did the phantom, which had been absent every hour since the rumor first reached her, finally arrive.

She drifted along at Irene's shoulder. Nothing in the corridor appeared to require her attention. "Those two children really are adorable, aren't they."

Irene stopped walking. Adorable. The real one would have said it in exactly that voice, and somewhere among all the lives Ovelia had seen, there was one where she had taken them both.

"I detest them." The words came out through locked teeth, and they hissed. "I truly do not know what is wrong with your eyes. The elder is a hollow shell with rot leaking out of it. The younger is worse. The younger adores the shell."

The phantom made a sound, half amusement and half complaint. "Are you jealous?" Then she sighed. It was small, and there was genuine regret in it. "Ah, well. You aren't a child anymore."

Irene stepped into the phantom's space, until her own shadow went over the hem of it. "Is that the better one?" Her voice had gone quiet and level, and nothing answered it.

"Then look your fill." The gold had gone to slits. "I will take her the first morning the law allows it, and you can come down off your seat and collect what is left of her off the flagstones. However many lives you go through, this one will only ever have me in it."

The phantom reached out and took Irene's face in both hands, with the tiredness of somebody who has turned out her pockets onto the table and been called a thief anyway. The warmth of a thing that was not there went straight through Irene's skin, and half the fire in her chest went out.

"Always looking at what other people have," the phantom said, and there was no amusement anywhere in it. "A mark on a child's face, and you stand in the middle of a world I cut out of my own marrow and tell yourself you were starved."

Her thumbs moved slowly along Irene's cheekbones. "Who else alive would survive you. There is no better one, Irene, and there was never going to be one, and that has never once been enough for you, and I have yet to work out what is left to hand over."

The hands let go, and the phantom was gone. Irene stood in the empty corridor with her breathing gone ragged, and put her trembling down the way one puts down an animal.

The blood in the courtroom came up over the witness stand.

[Exhibit C]

Irene stood in the center of a ritual array that had cost her ten million units of accumulated mana and thirty million Solars in casting materials. Any sane witch in her place would have cut the phantom out and called it a cure. Irene had no intention of parting with the only piece of the cat she still had, and she was not going to put one hair of it on a hook either. She went down through herself for something she could spare, and found the part of her that could trust another person. She had used it once, on the cat, and the cat had put her out on a pavement in Selvantara; she would not be needing it again.

She baited the hook with that, and cast.

The light broke, and what stepped out of it wore three pairs of topaz horns, a demon's, a ram's, and a dragon's, with a matte-gold veil falling from a mithril band to the small of her back. Her court dress ran through six shades of gold and was hung with deep-blue lanterns that golden butterflies held aloft. Over all of it lay a cloak of tens of thousands of engraved topaz leaves, liquid witch-gold turning slowly beneath the stone, and from its back hung six crystal hemispheres, each holding a folded pocket world with more bullion inside than a district holds.

Merely by standing there, she was the answer to a question most fortunes never get far enough to ask: what one does once being rich has stopped being interesting. Then she took her skirt in one hand, turned a full slow circle, and sank into a curtsy.

"I am the symbol of wealth. I am the Daughter of El Dorado, and for exactly as long as anything anywhere still desires riches, I will exist eternally."

Under the horns and the veil and the four hundred million Solars of cloak was a face: grey-gold hair, golden eyes, a trace of softness still in the cheeks. She was the sort of witch one could be spoiled by, the sort who, if you came to her and confessed something ruinous, would shake her head and smile and pat your hair and say oh dear, well, that's quite a mess, isn't it.

Irene asked her the only question she had made her for. "How did you lose her?"

The Daughter of El Dorado looked at her, and a little of the softness went out of her face.

"I could not keep our child safe," she said. "After that she put everything down, herself last of all, and in the end she died by my hand."

"Our child," Irene repeated. "Impossible. She would never put me down for a child. Give me your memories."

"No," said the Daughter of El Dorado.

She stepped down out of the light, reached up, and tucked a loose strand of hair back behind Irene's ear. "A possibility is only a possibility, dear. I would rather you did not carry mine as well as your own." Her hand stayed where it was a moment longer. "But I will be living in your house, and you ought to know why I came.

"In my life the old crown never fell, and she wore the other half of it. Every witch alive wanted her. I saw her happy, but never once with the crown off." The softness came back into her face. "Then I looked into you, and there she was, biting into a grapefruit sweet in the afternoon sun. I could not have stayed away."

The old crown had only ever had one heir. Ash.

Our child. With Ash in the room, it read two ways. The obvious one: the cat had come to this woman's bed with the crown still on her head, and the egg had not survived the discovery. Irene knew she would have done exactly that in that palace, inside the first year. She also knew the cat, who would sooner have stopped calling herself Cat than take a ring and lie under it.

Which left the second. The egg had been Ash's, and the woman in front of her called it ours.

"What did she name it?" Irene asked.

"Irene," said the Daughter of El Dorado.

For a breath the array made no sound at all.

The gold in Irene's eyes flared so wide that the air above the sigils cracked. She crossed the array in two steps and had the front of the court dress in her fist, six shades of silk and a string of deep-blue lanterns crushed together, and the six crystal hemispheres rang against one another like bells.

"She named her after me," Irene said through her teeth, "and you let her die? Is that how you kept our child safe? And you have the gall to go on existing?"

The Daughter of El Dorado did not lift a hand to the fist in her dress. The last of the softness had gone out of her face, and what lay under it was old and very tired. She looked back into a pair of eyes identical to her own without flinching.

"Not by choice," she said quietly. "She asked me to kill her, and there was nobody else left in that palace she would ask. I did it. And somewhere, somebody went on wanting gold."

Irene held her a second longer. Then she opened her hand, let the silk fall, and stepped back onto the cold stone, her chest rising once before she locked it down.

At the sound of Ash's name, the cat had once burrowed into Irene's collar like a child waking in the dark. Hand her a thing that is finished and she will hold it up by the scruff and swear it is still breathing.

Ash turned her into somebody's queen, Irene thought, and her hands closed at her sides until the leather creaked. With me she can be as much of a cat as she pleases, and the only thing she will ever lack is the door.

The logic was thin enough to see the floor through, and Irene stood on it anyway.

The Daughter of El Dorado had sat down on the edge of the array while Irene thought, six shades of gold spilling across the sigils, her chin propped in one hand. When Irene looked at her again, she asked the thing she had come all this way for.

"May I see her?"

"No," said Irene.

The Daughter of El Dorado looked at her for a long moment. Then she shook her head, and did not smile, and said very gently, "Oh, darling. What a mess you are going to make."

The blood in the courtroom reached the hinges of the doors.

Twelve years. The circle at the top of the Inner Sea was small enough that the arithmetic put everyone in a room with everyone else sooner or later, and the First Seat had spent all twelve of them staying out of every room that held Irene Habsburg.

A Grand Witch was entitled to a peerage, and only the First Seat could grant one. Irene knelt on the stone, lowered her head, and said, "I fight for you, my lady." Every witch in the hall considered it the honor of her life.

The throat was four feet away. She knew where the pulse ran and how the skin took a fang, and what came out of it, because the woman had put it in her mouth herself. Irene set her teeth into the inside of her own cheek until the flesh opened, held what came up, thin and salt, and waited to be told to rise.

"Rise," said the First Seat.

Irene raised her head and looked Ovelia in the face, and the worst cat in the world was smiling.

It was the unsupervised one, the one that had been on that face over a sheet of parchment in the upper sanctum half a second before the world went out, and what stood behind it now was a woman who had laid stones across an abyss and waited twelve years in the dark for the only creature alive who could survive her to cross them one at a time.

Then Ovelia tilted her head, a fraction, no further than the ceremony could absorb, and the long white line of her throat came open in a hall of three hundred, for one of them.

Irene took the hand that came down to raise her, and rose, and held those eyes, and made her a promise with nothing in it but appetite.

The blood in the courtroom took the gavel off the bench and carried it away.
