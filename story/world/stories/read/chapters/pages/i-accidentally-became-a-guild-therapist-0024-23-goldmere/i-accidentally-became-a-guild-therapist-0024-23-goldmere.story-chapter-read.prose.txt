I ACCIDENTALLY BECAME A GUILD THERAPIST

Chapter 23 – Goldmere

ARC III: THE WORLD REMEMBERS

SYNOPSIS: Livia goes to Goldmere Market District for the first time with money she actually owns. She does not know what she wants. Nyx is there. Nobody asked him to be there. He does not explain himself. They walk around for three hours and Livia buys things she has never bought before, including one item that will change the planning room permanently.

The morning after the salary meeting, Livia woke up and lay in bed for eleven minutes thinking about the number.

Not anxiously. Just—the number was there, in her inventory, and it was real, and she had never had a real number before. Her whole life she had operated in the vocabulary of not quite enough: not quite enough savings to feel safe, not quite enough margin to make a decision, not quite enough left over at the end of the month to ask what she actually wanted because asking the question without the ability to act on it was a kind of cruelty she had learned not to inflict on herself.

The number made the question possible.

The question was still: what do I want?

She still did not know.

There is no wrong way to do this.

The system is being encouraging.

I am going to go before it starts offering coupons.

She was in the guild hall putting on her sandals when Nyx appeared from the direction of the eastern corridor.

He was wearing his regular cloak. His Hello Kitty flashlight was clipped to his belt. He was not going anywhere in particular, by all appearances, except that he was clearly going somewhere and had timed his arrival in the guild hall to coincide with hers.

Livia looked at him.

He looked at the wall.

“I’m going to Goldmere,” Livia said.

“I know,” Nyx said. Still looking at the wall.

A pause.

“Are you—” she started.

“I was going anyway,” he said. “To the market. For things. Separately.”

For things.

Separately.

He timed his departure to match mine because he was going anyway for things.

Separately.

“Okay,” Livia said.

Nyx finally looked at her. The expression he made was one she had learned to read as I was hoping you would say that and I am not going to confirm that I was hoping that. He pushed off the wall and walked toward the door. Livia followed.

Goldmere Market District existed in the eastern quarter of the main hub, past the portal cluster and through an archway that had clearly been designed by someone who believed commerce was best accessed through the suggestion of grandeur. The arch was carved stone, slightly too large for its surroundings, with the words GOLDMERE: WHERE AETHELGARD DOES BUSINESS etched at the top in letters that were impressive if you did not notice the typo in the third word that had been there since launch and apparently nobody had flagged.

Beyond the arch: organized chaos.

The cobblestones were the intentionally uneven kind, the type that suggested history rather than poor maintenance. The buildings on either side were three stories at minimum, each one a different configuration of stall fronts and display windows and holographic signs in competing colors. Vendors called from doorways. A street musician played something that was technically a recognizable melody if you were generous about tempo. The smell was a combination of roasting meat, alchemical reagents, fresh paper, and what one merchant was advertising as "premium atmospheric essence, bottled at source, 40 gold, limited supply."

Livia stopped inside the arch and looked at all of it.

I have walked through markets before.

Camp market. Nexus Bazaar.

I have never walked through a market with money and permission.

That is a completely different experience.

I do not know where to start.

Nyx stopped beside her. He was doing the thing where he scanned the space without appearing to scan it, his rogue habits running automatically even in a marketplace. His flashlight caught the light of a passing holographic sign and scattered it briefly in pink.

“North side first,” he said. “Better quality. South side is cheaper but most of it is tourist.”

“You come here often?”

“Sometimes,” he said. Which, from Nyx, meant yes but I don’t want to quantify it.

He knows the market.

He has opinions about the north side versus the south side.

He came along because he was going anyway for things.

Separately.

Sure.

They walked north.

The north side was, as Nyx had indicated, better quality in the specific way that better quality meant more expensive and more considered. The stalls here had proper fronts rather than open tables. The vendors did not call from doorways. The merchandise was arranged with intention: equipment on one side, materials on another, and a section midway through that seemed to be dedicated to things that were neither equipment nor materials but simply things—furniture, lamps, objects, items whose function was not to enhance stats but to exist in a space and make the space different.

Livia slowed at this section.

In my previous life I never bought things for a space because I never had a space that was mine.

Rented rooms. Dormitory. Always temporary.

Here I have a room. A planning room I use for sessions. A space I have been in for nine months.

I have never bought anything for it.

She stopped in front of a stall selling lamps.

Not mana lamps, not enchanted combat lighting—just lamps. Desk lamps, standing lamps, hanging lamps. One in the corner was small enough to sit on a table and produced a light the color of late afternoon, the warm yellow-gold of a sun that had decided it was done being dramatic and was just going to be pleasant for a while.

Livia picked it up.

It was not heavy. It felt like the sort of thing that had been made by someone who understood that light mattered not just for visibility but for how a room felt.

“That one’s good,” Nyx said, from beside her. He had been looking at something on the next shelf but had apparently been watching.

“You’ve used it?”

“Seen it in other rooms,” he said. “The light doesn’t—” he paused, searching, —“intrude.”

The light doesn’t intrude.

That is exactly what I want.

A light that is present without demanding anything.

She bought the lamp.

The system notes: it is a good lamp. The system notes: the system has opinions about lamps now. The system notes: Livia Marcelline Quinn is responsible for the system having opinions about lamps.]

Three stalls down there was a clothing vendor.

Not armor. Not robes. Actual clothes—the kind that existed to be worn rather than to provide protection ratings. Livia had been in the same gray robe, increasingly upgraded, since she arrived. The robe was fine. The robe had blue threading now and a diagonal pattern and moved better than it used to. The robe was functional and she had stopped hating it months ago.

She stood in front of the clothing stall and looked at it for a while.

I do not need new clothes.

My robe is fine.

But I have never bought clothes I wanted before.

I have bought clothes that fit the budget and covered what needed covering.

That is different.

The vendor, an NPC with the patient expression of someone who had watched many people stand in front of a stall in existential negotiation with themselves, said nothing. Gave her time.

Nyx appeared at her shoulder.

“You’re doing the thing where you talk yourself out of it before you’ve decided anything,” he said.

Livia looked at him.

“How do you know what thing I’m doing?”

“You do it in sessions too,” he said. “You get to the edge of something and then you start adding qualifications.”

He noticed that.

He has been noticing things about how I work.

I do not know how to feel about this so I am going to look at the clothes.

There was a set in a soft off-white, the kind of white that was comfortable rather than pristine. A blouse with slightly too-wide sleeves. A pair of trousers in a grey so light it was nearly lavender. Simple. Nothing that announced itself. Nothing that performed anything.

She bought them.

Nyx said nothing. But when she came out of the changing alcove wearing them instead of the robe, he looked at her for a moment and then looked away. The tips of his ears were pink.

Filed.

Absolutely filed.

I am not saying anything.

The system notes: you can keep the robe in your inventory. The system notes: the robe is still yours. The system notes: having more than one thing is allowed.]

The armchair was in a furniture stall at the far end of the north district, tucked between a stall selling enchanted rugs and a stall whose entire inventory appeared to be different sizes of the same jar.

It was oversized. Upholstered in something that sat between fabric and something more considered, a deep warm neutral that looked like it had opinions about comfort. It was not decorative in the way that decorative furniture usually was—no carved legs, no ornamental trim. It looked like it had been made to be sat in by someone who needed to stay in one place for a while.

Livia stopped in front of it.

I have two chairs in the planning room.

Both are guild-issue.

Both belong to the guild.

Nothing in the planning room belongs to me.

She sat in it.

It was, without qualification, the most comfortable chair she had encountered in this world or the previous one. The kind of comfortable that was structural rather than plush—it supported rather than absorbed. You could sit in it for two hours and not realize you had been sitting for two hours. You could sit across from it and feel, without entirely knowing why, that the person in it was genuinely present..

The vendor materialized at her elbow.

“Diagnostic upholstery,” the vendor said, with the tone of someone reciting a description they had given many times. “Passive feature. Responds to emotional resonance in the proximity. Indicates elevated emotional distress via a shift in ambient luminosity—faint blue. Intensity scales with severity. Useful for support classes, healers, anyone who works with the emotional states of others.”

Livia looked at the chair.

Then at the vendor.

“It glows blue when someone’s in distress?”

“Faint blue,” the vendor confirmed. “Non-intrusive. The patient rarely notices. The practitioner has a passive reference point.”

A passive reference point.

For when someone is feeling something they haven’t said yet.

For the gap between what someone shows and what they’re actually carrying.

I have been trying to read that gap with nothing but observation and nine months of practice.

This chair does it automatically.

This is the most useful thing I have ever seen.

She bought the chair.

Nyx, who had been reading the description card on the enchanted rug stall, turned around at the sound of the transaction completing. He looked at the chair. Then at Livia.

“That’s a therapy chair,” he said.

“Yes,” Livia said.

“You bought yourself a therapy chair,” he said.

“I have three hundred and twelve sessions of experience and no passive diagnostic tool,” Livia said. “I have been reading people’s faces and voices and the specific way they hold their shoulders for nine months. This chair will tell me things I might miss.”

Nyx looked at the chair for a moment.

“The blue,” he said. “It shows up when someone’s not okay?”

“When someone is carrying something,” Livia said. “Not always the same as not okay. Sometimes you can be carrying something and still be okay. The chair just—” she thought about how to say it, —“shows me when there’s more there than what’s being said.”

Nyx was quiet for a moment.

“Good,” he said. And went back to the rug stall.

He said good.

The same way Miles says good when he means something larger than the word.

Filed in the section that is getting very full.

They were on the way back when Livia stopped at a stall she had walked past twice without registering.

The sign said: VESSELS — EMOTIONAL INSURANCE & RELATED PRODUCTS. The vendor was a small NPC of indeterminate classification, wearing a vest with more pockets than strictly necessary, arranging small glass bottles on a shelf with the careful attention of someone who took their inventory very seriously.

“Emotional insurance,” Livia said.

The vendor looked up.

“For when you carry too much,” the vendor said. Matter-of-fact, like this was an obvious product category. “You are a support class. You know what it is to hold other people’s feelings while they work through them. The weight accumulates. Insurance provides a buffer.”

“How does it work?”

“Gradually,” the vendor said. “You don’t notice it happening. You just notice, at the end of a difficult day, that you are less depleted than you expected to be.”

Livia looked at the bottles. Small, glass, filled with something that was neither liquid nor solid but moved when the light hit it like both at once.

“How much?”

The vendor named a price that was reasonable enough to be suspicious.

“That seems low,” Livia said.

“It’s not a glamorous product,” the vendor said. “No one wants to admit they need it. Low margin, low volume. But the ones who buy it come back.”

Livia bought one bottle.

Nyx said nothing. But as they walked away from the stall, he said, quietly: “I’ve seen that vendor for three years. Never stopped.”

“Why not?”

A pause.

“Felt like admitting something,” he said.

Felt like admitting something.

Yes.

That is exactly why.

That is why most people who need something don’t buy it.

She did not say this out loud. She filed it instead, in the notepad she had not opened the entire trip, because sometimes the right thing to do with an observation is just carry it for a while.

They were almost back to the guild when Livia realized she had been walking for nearly three hours and had not once thought about sessions, or T.S.’s appointment on Thursday, or the external classification review, or any of the things that usually ran in the background of her attention.

She had just—walked. Looked at things. Bought things she wanted. Let Nyx navigate because he knew the north side better than she did and had strong opinions about which vendors were worth stopping at and expressed these opinions via the specific ghost rogue version of strong opinions, which was a slightly prolonged pause in front of good stalls and a marginally faster pace past bad ones.

It had been, she realized, a good morning.

I bought a lamp.

I bought clothes for the first time because I wanted them.

I bought a chair that will make me better at my job.

I bought something that will help me carry what I carry.

Nyx was there the whole time and I have learned more about him today from watching him navigate a market than from six months of side conversations.

This is what Miles meant.

Find out what you want.

You find out by going somewhere and seeing what catches.

At the guild entrance, Nyx stopped.

“Good trip,” he said. To the door.

“Yes,” Livia agreed.

“The lamp is good,” he said.

“The chair is better,” she said.

Something at the corner of his mouth. Not quite a smile. The precursor to a smile, in the specific Nyx way where the smile happened in his eyes first and his face caught up about three seconds later.

“Yeah,” he said. And went inside.

Yeah.

From Nyx, that is a full sentence.

Filed everywhere.

She set up the lamp first. On the corner of the table she used for session notes, where it would be visible but not demanding. The light it produced at this time of day was exactly as advertised: warm, present, non-intrusive.

Then the chair.

She positioned it across from the two existing chairs, at the angle she had tested when she first started using the room. The diagnostic upholstery sat in its neutral state: no glow, no color, just the warm fabric of something waiting to be useful.

She sat in the other chair and looked at it.

This room has something of mine in it now.

The lamp is mine.

The chair is mine.

I bought them with money I earned.

This is, I think, what people mean when they say they have made a place their own.

I have never made a place my own before.

She wrote in her notepad:

Bought: one lamp (warm, non-intrusive), one set of clothes (white, lavender-grey, mine), one armchair (diagnostic passive, will tell me things I miss), one bottle of emotional insurance (for the accumulation).

Nyx came. Did not explain. Knew the north side. Walked faster past vendors he didn’t like. Said the lamp was good. Said yeah when I said the chair was better.

I think I know what I wanted. I wanted things that were mine. I wanted a room that had something of me in it. I wanted to walk around for three hours and not be anyone’s therapist.

The question was: what do I want?

The answer is: things that are mine, in a place that is mine, with time that is mine.

That seems obvious. It probably is obvious. I had just never been in a position to want it before.

Miles found her in the planning room at some point in the late afternoon, sitting with a mug of something warm and looking at the armchair with the specific expression of someone who had made a decision they were satisfied with.

He stood in the doorway and looked at the lamp. Then the chair.

“You went,” he said.

“Goldmere is good,” she said. “You were right.”

He came in. Sat in the other chair across from the armchair, which put him in the diagnostic proximity range. The armchair remained neutral.

Neutral.

For now.

I am not going to say anything about that.

“What did you find out?” he asked.

She considered the question with the honesty she had been, quietly, applying to more things lately.

“That I wanted things that were mine,” she said. “A room that had something of me in it. Time that wasn’t organized around someone else’s need.”

He looked at the lamp. At the chair.

“Good things to know,” he said.

“You said your first paycheck went on weapons,” she said.

“Yes.”

“What changed? What would you spend it on now?” The question she had asked last night that he had left half-answered.

He was quiet for a moment. The armchair was still neutral. The lamp was warm.

“Time,” he said finally. “I would spend it on time that wasn’t organized around whether the guild needed something.”

He said time.

He said time that wasn’t organized around need.

He and I wanted the same thing.

I am going to look at the lamp.

I am looking at the lamp.

The lamp is very nice.

The armchair produced, briefly, a faint blue.

Livia saw it. Miles did not.

Filed.

Filed in every category simultaneously.

Thursday is tomorrow.

T.S. is at 10am.

The chair is ready.
