[Time]: Late Summer, 1981[Location]: The Umbral Cathedral · The Chapel Flower Field / Selvantara

The chapel at the bottom of the Umbral Cathedral had been grown rather than built, and the valley that held it had never once been asked to choose between wilderness and design.

The sun overhead was one Ovelia had made herself, and it poured a warm, honeyed radiance that pooled in the grass, gilded the water, and lay over everything with the unhurried generosity of a long afternoon. A stream ran along the valley floor, clear over pale stones, chiming as it went. Wildflowers spread to the horizon: blooms of spun glass that rang when the breeze crossed them, petals that cupped light the way a leaf cups dew, tall stalks that leaned toward any passing warmth like things not fully awake. There was no rose anywhere. Ovelia had never had the patience for a flower that carried its own funeral folded inside it.

The viewing terraces had been raised in full baroque grandeur, all scrolled stone and deep colonnades, and among the water and the grass and the fragrant blooms they read as something a civilization had built and then let keep growing: cultivated and overgrown at once, lit through with the optimism of a place that had decided the labor was finished and the living could begin.

Ovelia walked ahead through blossoms that reached her waist, dragging her fingertips across the petals as she went, her black eyes catching colors that did not have names.

"Tell me, Irene." Her voice carried the drowsy cadence of a predator digesting an afternoon. "Do you know how the world is actually constructed? What's the baseline underneath the air in your lungs, the mana in your veins?"

Irene matched her pace, and let herself take a moment before answering, because the answer was long and she intended to deliver all of it.

"A force the scholars call the Source," she said. "The oldest existence in the multiverse, predating good and evil as concepts. It began as a spring at Viltora, the origin point of all realities, and spilled downward into the Abyss to become the River of Sin. At the gates of the lower planes it fractured: the River of the Sin-Abyss brought forth chaos and demons, the River of the Vile-Abyss brought forth evil and devils."

She watched Ovelia's profile for a reaction and found the placid, unreadable stillness of a cat listening to a bird it has already decided not to bother with, so she kept going.

"It also flowed up into the higher planes as the River of Radiance, where it incubated the myriad false gods. In the end it washed into the astral sea and bound itself to the rings of a billion stars, and out of that came the universe, matter, and mana. Which means the mana we cast with is either a degradation of the Source or a refinement of it, and either way, that is the structural reason Witches sit above every other species that has ever looked at the sky."

No single volume in the Inner Sea held that answer whole: it survived in fragments, scattered across centuries of monographs that could not agree with one another, and Irene had assembled them into a structure nobody had built. It was the ceiling of Witch cosmology.

Ovelia stopped walking. She let the vine slide out of her fingers and shook her head, slowly, with the weight of a thousand scriptures being taken off a shelf and dropped in a bin.

"Incorrect."

She turned, and the breath she let out chilled the flowers around her ankles. "The essence of mana, the architecture of life, every atom of this world. All of it is a byproduct."

"A byproduct," Irene repeated.

"The true nature of this universe is a machine." Ovelia's starry black eyes went past Irene, past the arches, into the dark between the stars. "A colossal unfeeling engine that we can neither touch nor perceive, grinding away for epochs at entirely meaningless labor. Its only purpose is to work through some hundreds of billions of numerical permutations. It is calculating that one plus one equals two, that one plus two equals three. The cogs bite. The wheels turn."

She reached out and snapped the stem of a luminescent flower. The petals flared white, and died.

"And in the fraction of a microsecond it takes the engine to conclude that one plus one equals two, it throws off data exhaust. Redundancy." She rolled the dead stem between her thumb and forefinger. "We are the redundancy. Witches, mana, spells, civilization, consciousness, the entire concept of existence. We are the friction on the gears and the waste heat coming off them."

The chiming of the flower field carried on, indifferent. Irene looked at the dead stem between Ovelia's fingers, and then past it, into the dark where Ovelia's eyes had gone.

"Then who sits at the controls?" Irene asked. "And can they be taken?"

Ovelia's ears came forward, and the corner of her mouth went with them. "Nobody, yet." Her voice rose into something imperial. "Witches are the sole and eternal masters of the universe's future. Every objective we hold serves the sublimation of our race, and at the end of it, we climb on top of that automated machine and we assign the labor ourselves. That is our meaning, that is our mandate, and that is the long pursuit at the end of our absurdly long lives."

"And how does one climb it?" Irene asked.

Something older moved through Ovelia's expression. "Not every universe has a sun and a moon. Nor does every debris field of a star river yield exactly one of each. Through a long and excruciating program of experimentation, I isolated the two casting codes that hold across the entire machine. [Moon]. [Sun]."

Every witch learned the week before she learned to read: Moonday, Fireday, Waterday, Woodday, Metalday, Earthday, Sunday, the seven celestial days the spell system ran on. The first and the last were the only two that were not elements, and now Irene understood why.

"Two of the seven are already in your hand," she said. "The other five come down from them?"

Ovelia stepped closer, pleased. "Every one of them, reverse-engineered from those two roots. Seven variables, and that is the whole of the spell system."

If seven roots were the whole of it, every rune ever carved ought to spell them out. Irene had read every runic system the Inner Sea had ever committed to paper, and written one of her own when none of them would do what she wanted, and the roots were in none of them. Not even in Ovelia's, the finest of them all.

"You left them out on purpose," Irene said. Knowledge that turns on whoever holds it was the oldest warning in the craft. "Reading them would do something to us."

Ovelia shrugged. "The source code holds infinite secrets and infinite wisdom, and Witches cannot read a line of it raw. So Cat wraps it up and hands it back as an incantation, and then our kind can safely put it in their mouths."

"Show me," Irene said.

Ovelia opened her hand, and the seven root runes hung above her palm.

Irene read them the way she would have read her own name, and raised one hand, gathered her mana at the tip of her forefinger, and wrote [Sun] on the air.

The rune rose. It climbed past the colonnades and the terraces into the sky of the small world, and high above the valley it became a point of white, and then a weight. The stream leaned toward it and ran a hand's breadth up its bank. Every glass bloom in the field rang at once, and the tall stalks that followed any passing warmth swung round from Ovelia's sun together, like a crowd turning to a door.

Then it caught and burned: a star, with mass enough to pull at the valley and heat that reached Irene's face from far away. The sky Ovelia had made for one sun now held two, and every shadow in the flower field doubled.

Copper came into Irene's mouth and went on coming. She kept her eyes on what she had made.

Ovelia looked at the blood on her chin, then up at the two suns, and smiled: a light thing, almost nothing. When she spoke, it was with the idle irreverence of a cat quoting scripture from the altar she happens to be sleeping on.

"'And God made two great lights,'" she said. "'The greater light to rule the day, and the lesser light to rule the night.'"

She turned and looked off into the distance, and her voice drifted into the narcotic air like something she had not intended to say out loud.

"Every spell is a byproduct of the great machine. Every spell is also a line of code. The one that brings our race closest to the source is the [Wish]. Perhaps, if someone cracked the code of the [Wish] spell, they would have that final power in their hands."

Irene took the blood off her chin with the back of her glove and did not look at it.

"I will do it," Irene said.

She stepped into Ovelia's space, close enough that the distance was no longer an option, and made those fathomless black eyes come back and meet hers.

"Before you do."

With a quiet, unreadable chuckle, Ovelia looked at the three-year-old Witch who had just promised to beat the apex of the world to the end of the world.

She flicked two fingers, and the formula for calculating a spell's root code came apart in the air and reassembled itself inside Irene's skull. Then she drifted off toward the chapel doors.

Irene chose Light: the most rudimentary parlor trick in the arcane arts, the first cantrip in every primer ever printed, and therefore the correct place to put a blade in and start cutting.

Running Light through the formula produced 3,672 permutations, of which 2,150 were structurally compatible. Brute-force exhaustion filtered out the dead code until the raw original equation stood alone. Then the remaining 3,671 rejected codes had to be forced back into that equation, and the friction of the collision produced a naturally encrypted algorithmic key.

That key was the underlying principle of the spell. It was one specific, isolated piece of the cosmic machine's redundancy, held still long enough to be looked at.

She cracked it in a day.

The final variable dropped into place, the loop closed, the key rendered itself, absolute and legible, on the page, and Irene Habsburg started to shake. The chair went over behind her. Her golden eyes had blown wide and were burning with something that had no business being in a face.

She had seen it. She had gotten her fingers under the wallpaper of reality and peeled back a corner and looked directly at the gears.

She tore the space open without bothering about transit protocol and came through into the upper sanctum, and the smell of old paper and starlight closed over her.

Ovelia was standing near the balcony. The distance disappeared, and she went into those arms hard enough to move her, one fist crushing the parchment, every year of aristocratic architecture in her body burned to the ground in the last ten seconds.

"Look. Look, Ovie. I did it—"

Ovelia caught her, steadied her, and looked down at the page. She read it, and she smiled.

Irene had spent a year cataloguing every expression this woman had ever produced, had filed the arrogance and the lethargy and the theatrical cruelty and the seventeen distinct varieties of feline contempt and the joy that had never once arrived without a reason pinned to it, and she had no entry for this. It was a face doing the thing a face does when it is happy, with no supervision on it at all.

Then it was gone, and something moved in the depths of the black eyes, and Irene, who could read this face better than any living creature, could not read it.

Ovelia's lips parted around a word that did not come.

Snap.

The heat hit her first. The perfumed cold of the Umbral Cathedral was gone, and in its place was a late summer evening at full volume: warm stone, frying oil, cheap perfume, the brass shriek of a band two streets over, and the roar of a city that had gotten off work and had absolutely no intention of going home.

The momentum she had carried into Ovelia's arms carried her forward into a wrought-iron streetlamp. She caught it, and hung there, and breathed.

Then she looked up. Neon bled up into a violet sky. Carriages hissed past. Somewhere behind her a woman was laughing at something with her whole body.

She was standing on a corner she had walked a thousand times. Two streets east, the Habsburg financial district. Six streets north, through the plaza and past the fountain, the estate. Selvantara.

Irene channeled her mana to open a rift back. The Umbral Cathedral's doors were built into the concept of darkness itself, which meant there was no place in the universe a door could not be, and she had walked those corridors on autopilot for a year. The shadows in the alley rippled and did not part: inert, flat, ordinary absences of light.

She tried the emergency access points and got nothing. She reached for Allison's administrative channel and got nothing. She engaged the spatial anchors she had quietly seeded in the lower wards, and the coordinates returned nothing at all, because there was no longer anywhere for them to point.

"Thunderer above the crowns that outlasted their kings."

Her voice did not carry over the traffic. She did not need it to.

"Dragon of faith compressed past the point of return."

Somewhere down the block, a drunk applauded.

"She who answers before the question completes itself."

Nobody had ever needed the fourth line; the third had always come true before anyone got there. Irene said it.

"Aerthain."

The sky stayed exactly where it was. The pressure in the street held, the shadows held, the evening went on being an evening, and nobody came down out of it to ask her for what purpose the Lord of Heaven and Thunder had been invoked.

Why? The question came around again with nothing attached to it, and then again, and the thing underneath it flattened out into a blunt-force ache that took her entire nervous system and left no room for anything else.

Did I do something wrong? She ran the last sixty seconds with forensic desperation and found nothing. The calculation was clean. The key was perfect. She smiled. She smiled at me. Why would she smile and then throw me away? What was she going to say?

Irene turned, scanning the rooftops, the awnings, the gutters, the deep places under the eaves where the light gave up. Lantern Cats were as common as weather, and there was not one anywhere.

She was standing in a crowd of hundreds of people on the loudest street in the loudest district in the Inner Sea, and she had never heard a silence like it.

What did you see, when you looked at me? The thought came up out of the floor of her mind like something in a flooded basement. In Aerthain's life there had been no Irene at all. In Shaar's there had been one, and Shaar had moved out. In Solvane's there had been one, and Irene had died first, and left Ovelia a widow in grave-cloth. You have seen every one of them. Did you find a better one? Is this the life where you are spared me? If staying with me is what ruins you—

She crushed it. No. Incorrect. Impossible. Her hand moved before the decision finished, and the River of Time opened under it.

She found the day without effort and watched it run from the morning to the last of the light. What ran was an afternoon she had spent in Selvantara, at her own desk, signing what an heir signs, with the light moving slowly across the floor.

She went through it a second time and a third, looking for the place where the work had been done. A crude erasure leaves a hole, and a hole announces itself: a snarl, a skip, a stretch of water that declines to resolve. She had made those herself, and she knew exactly what her own hands looked like from the inside. The water ran smooth end to end, simply a different day, lying the way the truth lies when it has been left alone.

You told me you were not that petty.

Somewhere between that railing and this streetlamp the household had sat down together and voted the afternoon out of the world. Every one of them had known before Irene's boots touched the cobblestones, and every one of them had let her walk out into the street and find it herself.

The heat went out of her between one breath and the next, and what came up in its place was cold and level and entirely quiet. Her irises stopped shaking. Her pupils came open until the gold was a fine trembling ring and then not even that.

Redundancy. The word arrived with the smell of crushed petals and the warmth of a sun. You went home and did the sorting yourself. You looked at that afternoon, and found it surplus to the calculation, and swept it off the gears.

Was it only the afternoon. Irene went through all of it. The pebble. The roadmap to surpassing Ovelia, handed over in front of Ovelia's own Aspects. The photographs Ovelia had run ahead to demand, a woman who did not allow her own image to exist. The neck, offered without a word, the black hair swept over one shoulder and the pulse under Irene's teeth.

Every one of them had happened. The only place they had happened was inside her own head, and tonight that had stopped being somewhere she could go and check. Her hands closed until the leather of her gloves complained.

Then I will write it back in. It settled into her bones with the quiet domestic certainty of folding laundry.

Irene adjusted the collar of her blouse and brushed the street off the shoulders of her coat. Her palms went down the front of the fabric, and then again, pressing out every crease the displacement had put into her, the way she had once fastened pearl buttons from the collar down in a cold stone hall with a cat watching her do it. She realigned her cuffs and checked the seams of her gloves, and worked until the last physical trace of the last thing Ovelia had ever done to her body was gone, and the silhouette under the streetlamp was immaculate, untouchable, and the Heir of the Habsburg dynasty again.

Then she opened her spatial ring and put the parchment away near the pebble. The envelope from the park was in there too, six inches from her hand, and the river had run clean, and that envelope and the parchment beside it were the last things left in the world still claiming otherwise. She closed the ring without looking. She would sooner hold the chance than spend it and find them blank.

Her fingers closed around the cold weight of her staff, and she pulled it into the world, and the base of it struck the cobblestones with a crack that took the noise out of forty feet of street in every direction. The clarity that arrived was total, and came with a diagnosis.

I have been dull.

When she was a mortal, she had not waited for anything. She had put a blade through every member of her own bloodline and walked up the steps over their bodies and sat down on the throne while it was still warm.

And here, holding more power than her entire birth world had contained, she had spent a year being a good student and a patient heir, filing paperwork, attending the Matriarch, waiting her turn like a girl in a queue. Waiting for a door to open.

Why wait? The Habsburg dynasty had exactly one law and the Matriarch had recited it to her face. The invitation had been sitting there, unaccepted, for a year, because for one year she had not been in a hurry.

She needed the resources, and the authority, and the entire unmitigated weight of an ancient dynasty behind her, because somewhere in the dark there was a door with her name struck off it, and she was going to take it off its hinges and drag the First Seat out through the wreckage by her hair and make her say it. Out loud. To her face.

She had wanted one place in the world that nothing could reach into. She would make the world that place: burn it down to its blackened ribs and forge the slag into a cage with no seam in it and no sky, and bring her down out of that untouchable air into the palm of her hand.

She knew what a hand that tight did to a songbird, and she looked at the cost squarely. Every other Irene had lost her: to a grave, to a house she moved out of, to never arriving at all. This one would keep her.

Let her hate me for it. Hatred still looks at me. There was nothing Irene would not give her inside those walls, except the door.

Anything standing in the way of that cage could burn to ash.
