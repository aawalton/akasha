Something Behind

Since her arrival, Miho had bought her a small breast pump to help with milk extraction.

The milk Miho collected—she sent a sample to an acquaintance first—was later drunk by Miho herself, to Mai’s shock and deep embarrassment. Miho apologized, admitting she simply couldn’t resist the temptation.

She claimed that a fey’s milk had highly beneficial properties, something that was recorded in old legends and confirmed by research only in recent years. Basically, a cocktail of vitamins, much like human breastfeeding, but with a distinctly supernatural twist. Miho even mentioned that her chronic back pain had vanished.

That didn’t make Mai any less flustered.

To see for herself, she tried drinking it too. It tasted sweet, pleasant, and like the first time stirred a strange nostalgia in her chest for reasons she couldn’t explain. And, to her surprise, after drinking it she felt as if she had downed an entire pack of energy drinks.

But as the days passed, Mai began to notice a change in her own body—something she hadn’t anticipated but she it felt growing steadily, quietly, with every day spent in Miho’s home.

At first she assumed it was temporary, maybe just a passing reaction, but the sensation persisted.

After a little more than a week, she made the discovery: she was no longer producing any milk.

It had simply… stopped.

As if something inside her had closed a door for good.

The change was subtle at first, almost invisible, but one glance in the mirror was enough for Mai to sense something dimming inside her, fading into the thick air of the house.

Miho, with sharp perception, noticed almost immediately and took action. Though Mai tried to downplay the issue, Miho promptly scheduled an appointment with a trusted doctor in Shinjuku—a long-time friend accustomed to unusual cases involving feys.

The doctor, well aware of Miho’s peculiar world, greeted them casually.

Mai, on the other hand, felt stiff and uneasy. She knew something was wrong—she just didn’t want to face it. What was happening to her? Something new?

Tests began right away.

The machines hummed softly, while Mai lay still on the examination table. When the images appeared on the screen, they were baffling. There were no signs of a recent pregnancy, no traces of childbirth. The doctor studied the scans carefully, frowning—but in fascination.

According to the images, Mai had never given birth. And though her fey body looked human, it was not fully human at all.

Bone density results revealed something even stranger: she seemed in her early twenties—maybe twenty-one, twenty-three at most. Her osteogenesis was complete, just like a normal adult… but there were irregularities.

The strangest details were some physical anomalies—small, but unmistakable one in radiography images.

The scans of her head showed an unusual cranial structure, her shoulder blades had a faint deformity, as if something had altered their shape long ago. They looked normal from the outside, with a normal back, but the doctor assured her that he had once seen something similar in a fae who had a pair of small bird wings, although Mai did not have wings.

And then there were the moth-like antennae, hidden under her hair, almost invisible to the naked eye. But the scanners did not lie: there they were, clearly outlined. According to the doctor, a good description of what Mai could do with her ability was very similar to a kind of synesthesia, but limited to hearing, sight, and a little bit of smell. The three senses had to be combined by the antennae.

And then came the most unsettling finding, tucked in a thorax section of the scan.

In her heart.

The machine displayed something labeled as a fey core by the doctor—though a very small one.

Even the doctor, with all his experience, seemed genuinely surprised by its size. This wasn’t something one saw every day.

Mai stared at the images in silence, not fully understanding their implications.

Miho, however, watched the screen with a quiet, calculated tension behind her glasses—processing, analyzing, worrying. Mai could sense she was standing on the edge of something Miho did not want to say out loud.

Apparently, the core was typical in feys who possessed abilities, forming around the heart with a crystalline structure that resisted direct study.

Abilities?

Mai had been experimenting with her antennae since her arrival—they worked like a second kind of vision. With her normal eyes she could see a wide range, but with her antennae, her perception expanded. She could see things her eyes couldn’t reach.

Once, while standing on the upper floor of the house, she had let the antennae slip free from her hair and vibrate. The vibration seemed to send out waves that bounced back to her, showing her what was happening several meters away—even below her. Miho walking through her study. The branches tapping against the house. A heightened, echo-like sense of the world. It was even more incredible when she had tried to use it to look at the neighborhood from her room. During the day it was chaotic, but late at night it was much better and she could perceive things more clearly... just a bit.

Strange…fascinating... but exhausting.

Using them too long left her drained, overwhelmed by sensory information she didn’t know how to process and what to do with it.

If that counted as an ability… maybe she really did have one.

But it wasn’t that discovery the doctor discussed with Miho that darkened her expression.

It was something else—something Mai wasn’t being told yet.

The return to Motoyoyogichō was quiet, as if the very air had absorbed all the energy from the day’s events—though that didn’t stop Mai from quietly enjoying the trip. She hadn’t gone out much since arriving, mostly to avoid drawing attention.

The front door closed with a soft creak, and Miho—who had spent the walk home teaching her little things about the neighborhood—finally issued the elephant in the room when they sat in the living. The weight of the conversation she was about to have was clear in her voice, despite her effort to remain calm.

“Mai,” she began, her tone a little lower than usual, “there’s something I need to explain about what happened with your… breasts.”

She sat near her, hands clasped tightly on her knees, as if searching for the right words. “For feys like you, women produce milk before getting pregnant. It’s part of a hormonal process connected to your longevity, because certain hormones take much longer to activate.”

“The doctor told us that already, and you’d mentioned something too.”

“I know. Don’t be alarmed—it isn’t a bad sign. It’s just… different.”

Mai then remembered one of the explanations Miho had given her during their first days together. She had told her about the biology of fey, how their bodies, being so long-lived, took years—some cases decades—to adjust the hormones necessary for a real pregnancy. That was why fey women began to produce milk before conceiving, as a natural mechanism to prepare a body that changed extremely slowly.

Mai looked at her with a mix of confusion and unease, still trying to process everything.

“Is it about why I no longer have milk? What does it mean?”

Miho sighed, her expression softening, though a faint sadness lingered in her eyes.

“In your case, Mai, the changes were… fast. At first, I was scared. Because of your hips, your body—I thought maybe you’d given birth. That maybe you’d had a baby, and something… something separated the two of you before you arrived here. Your figure suggested it because your hips. But the tests ruled that out. I guess wide hips are just genetic.”

Miho hesitated, glancing at Mai’s hands.

“Still, the fact that you produced milk means that, not long before coming here, you must have had a close emotional bond with someone… on the Other Side.”

Mai looked at her, her eyebrows curving downward with a quiet, wounded sadness. “Something like a boyfriend? A husband?”

Miho nodded.

Her words fell into Mai’s chest like a stone dropped into deep water.

A strange ache tightened in her stomach, slowly rising to her throat. She replayed what Miho had said the first day, and a hollow, aching emptiness spread through her.

She knew it.

She couldn’t remember it—but she knew it. Had she left someone behind? What happened before she appeared in that alley?

The question had been on her mind, but she felt terrible for not remembering. That's why she had tried not to think too much and to get used to her new situation, while she decided what to do with her life. She had avoided thinking about it. Because it hurt too much. In that bed where she now had to sleep every night, she felt lonely. And Miho had noticed it.

More than once she had woken up and could feel that she had cried in her sleep. That was probably why the familiar spirits who guarded the house were sometimes in her room to make sure she was okay. But she had tried to hide it so as not to worry Miho. However, she figured it was time to let that go.

And Mai had to face it once and for all.

Something had been left behind. Something good? Something bad?

Someone important?

Someone she had loved?

The thought strangled her, and a knot formed in her chest. Miho had explained everything about the Other Side—or at least everything humans and feys knew—but that didn’t make the situation any less unsettling.

Producing milk was not a strange symptom, nor a sign of recent motherhood, it was just a sign that some part of her, deep down, had responded to an emotional bond. An ancient, biological preparation between feys that could be maintained indefinitely... if it was not interrupted. Means she was with someone in the moment she appeared.

“So… what happens if I can’t remember?” Mai whispered. “What if I left someone behind and I don’t even know it?”

The anguish in her eyes was undeniable. She was trying to stay composed, but uncertainty gnawed at her, threatening to swallow her from the inside.

Miho looked at her with genuine compassion.

“What happens is that memories don’t always define who we are. But what you carry inside… what you are… that doesn’t vanish. Maybe you don’t remember that person, or maybe…” Miho scratched her head, nervous. “Sorry. I’m terrible at this. It’s not like I’m some expert on relationships. I would love to tell you that everything will be fine, but if you don't even know your circumstances, I don't want to lie to you.”

Mai looked genuinely heartbroken, trying her best to understand.

“But you should know something,” Miho continued softly. “What you’ve lived through isn’t lost. Not everything is behind you.”

She paused, as if the words weighed more than she could comfortably hold.

“This is just my experience, but… eventually, things come to light. One way or another. It all comes when it needs to. Not everything is solved right away.”

“But I don’t have my memories.”

“I know, little one. But I think—since you live so long—maybe someday you can recover them.”

Mai stared at her.

“I know it sounds silly,” Miho murmured. “But that’s just how I feel.”

Mai nodded, and Miho wrapped an arm gently around her shoulders to comfort her, though Mai’s thoughts kept spiraling.

“This is the sad truth about feys, Mai. It's a tragedy that thousands have gone through,” Miho said and patted her head.

Miho could be cryptic sometimes—maybe it came from her extrasensory abilities, or her habit of speaking with the dead.

But for Mai, something inside still throbbed with loss, even without a name for it.

Who was that person she may have left behind?

Why did her heart tighten at the thought?

For now, no answers came—only more questions, and the quiet certainty that somewhere, deep in her forgotten self, there was a story waiting for its moment to return.

***

Two weeks had passed since Mai had settled into Miho’s house, and although she had initially enjoyed the quiet and the chance to observe the woman’s work, the feeling of being a freeloader, had begun to seep slowly into her chest.

Miho had given her the room where Mai had first awakened.

It was lovely, and she liked it, but she felt she had to do something for Miho after so much hospitality. Miho had told her she wasn’t the first fey she had helped. In the past, she had taken in a fox-girl and a goblin, both of whom were now fully integrated into the world and working for the government. Miho only hoped they weren’t living the miserable bureaucratic-supernatural hell of special-section office workers.

Mai had tried to help, but she couldn’t do much if Miho didn’t let her take part. The discomfort weighed on her like a tight band around her ribs—a persistent itch she couldn’t scrub away. She couldn’t spend her days simply watching Miho work, reading books, or catching up on the world by watching TV.

She had been kind, patient, even grateful… but something inside her whispered that she needed to do more, that she couldn’t keep living in that strange limbo without offering something in return. She had to find something to do—or the questions circling in her head would only grow louder.

One afternoon, while Miho was organizing some of the strange artifacts in the front, Mai watched in silence. The scent of old books and wood cleaner drifted through the air, but it was the quiet busyness of the house that really drew her attention. Maybe she could help with something—anything—to feel useful. But the shyness that clung to her like a second skin made taking the first step difficult.

Finally, after a long internal struggle, Mai approached Miho, taking a deep breath. With a mix of timidity and determination, she spoke.

“Miho…” she began, not looking directly at her. “I was wondering if… if you might need help. I don’t know much, but… I could help you with your work, if you want.”

Her voice trembled a little, but deep down, Mai knew she couldn’t keep on like this—lurking in the background. She needed to do something more. She needed to feel like she belonged.

“But you already help me. You do the dishes, help with the laundry.”

“That’s not what I mean…”

“What do you want to help with?”

“Anything.”

“Hmm…”

“You still haven’t hired anyone for that position, right? The one posted outside.”

“…You want to work for me?”

Mai nodded and lowered her head, nervous.

Miho looked at her over her glasses, surprised. She scratched her cheek—almost like she had been expecting this question, though she wouldn’t admit it. She thought for a moment, weighing her response. Finally, a warm smile spread across her face, the same smile Mai had seen so many times in the last weeks.

“Are you sure?”

Mai nodded again.

Miho sighed softly and smiled. “All right,” she said, with a hint of playfulness, “you’re hired. Remember you are not a prisoner here. You can go outside. And if someday you find something more interest to do just tell me.”

“I don't want to be a bother to you.”

Miho smiled. “You're not. I helped you because I wanted to. If you want to live here, it's your choice.”

“Can I... stay?”

“As long as you want. This house was getting quiet when there are no customers.”

The simple fact that Miho accepted so readily sent a wave of relief crashing through Mai’s body. It was as if an invisible weight had lifted from her shoulders, replaced by a small spark of happiness. For the first time since she had arrived, Mai smiled—not a forced smile, not a doubtful one, but a genuine, bright smile. At last, she could do something.

“Thank you,” she said, letting out a small nervous laugh, gratitude shimmering in her eyes. “I won’t disappoint you.”

Miho’s smile widened. “I don’t have any doubt about that.” She leaned forward slightly, her clouded eyes shining with a quiet, conspiratorial warmth. “I’ll teach you everything you need to know. But don’t rush… there’s a lot of work here, and it can get a little strange sometimes.”

“I’ll do my best.”

“Not so fast. First we need to sort out some paperwork so the government doesn’t get on our backs.”

Mai frowned and took a small step back. “I-Is that… necessary?”

“Of course. We’d both get in trouble otherwise. The police already reported your arrival to the authorities, but you’re still pretty much illegal here. Feys hide from the public, but for government agencies you need an identity. I suppose they haven’t visited me yet because they know me…” Miho stared out the window with a gloomy sigh. “Or maybe because they know me they’re avoiding it… bastards, why they are scared of me?”

“If it’s necessary, I’ll do it,” Mai replied.

Miho turned back to her and gently patted her head. “Don’t worry. We’ll do it together. That way I can even pay you.”

“But... the stay.”

Miho gestured with her hands as if she were carrying something. “That's one thing, work is another. I'll pay you for your work, but you can stay because I want you to and for as long as you want.”

“T-thank you very much!” she said, bowing.

Miho smiled.

Mai, now filled with a strange mixture of excitement and nerves, nodded. She didn’t know exactly what she was stepping into, but at least it felt like she was moving forward—toward something new.

And for the first time in those weeks, the sensation of being lost, of lacking purpose, began to fade, replaced by something much warmer: a sense of belonging.

“Oh! For the paperwork, you'll need a last name too... think of one.”

Mai's pupils dilated. “Can it...” She took a breath as she felt herself starting to sweat. “Can it be Izumi?”

Mai waited. Seconds passed, but nothing happened. All she could hear was the ticking of some of the old clocks in the house, along her own heartbeat. Finally, she breathed a sigh of relief.

No divine punishment came, no thunder, and it did not dissolve either. Perhaps it was too simple for that to happen.

“Sounds good,” said Miho. Then she narrowed her eyes as she adjusted her glasses. “Is something wrong? I feel like your whole spirit wanted to flee your body.”

“N-No, I'm fine,” Mai smiled nervously and put a hand to her chest, relieved to feel her heart beating. At least she could use her family's last name.
