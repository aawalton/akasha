Half of me wanted to run straight into the hazy, dreamlike throng of Fae. I nearly did, but a few steps forwards- and a glance at the nearest vendor, who appeared to be a very long shadow with far too many hands selling lumps of rock from a bench- sent me pacing sidelong instead.

I trailed along, keeping the enormous wall at one shoulder and eyeing the market with nervous excitement. My steps were slow and I was very aware of Velidan looming behind me as I surveyed each scene in turn.

A nearby tent held two squat, blue-skinned figures with piping voices and armfuls of clay pots. The next vendor was an ivory-skinned, hairless creature touting sacks filled with questionable-smelling fruit. Further on we passed two humpbacked women fussing at each other as a third carefully measured spoonfuls of powder into a bubbling cauldron.

I watched it all breathlessly, hands clasped at my throat and eyes wide. I kept turning, too, half-expecting the figure at my heels to grow impatient or lead me into the crowd, but every glance at Velidan revealed an expression of placid amusement.

We drew closer to the far gap, where the courtyard funneled into a massive interior space. Prowling figures and fabric-strewn tables stretched away into darkness, lit in the front by dangling globes and giggling wisps but impenetrable as it went on. It gave the curious impression that the space inside the ruined castle was endless, though of course that was impossible.

But the thought still sent a little thrill up my spine.

A muffled sob turned my attention to one side. A slender woman with the wings of a dragonfly and skin shimmering like a pearl sat cross-legged in the grass. Bundles of vibrant cloth lay all around her. She was gazing at the stars, eyes glassy and hands stretched out. It looked like she was tracing constellations with her fingers.

“Saebria, Salessa, Serah, Sharay, Shialla, Siffana…” Her silver-spun hair floated as if carried by unseen water. Tears glistened on her cheeks.

I picked my way through the mounds of cloth. “Are you alright?”

Her eyelids fluttered. She seemed to not realize I was there for a moment. Then, with a little gasp, she looked up and smiled. “Oh! M’lord. Mortal. Most welcome.” She rose and swayed, curtsying with such grace my breath caught. A gown of pale petals woven into gossamer traced each delicate curve. Both of her wings fanned back, glinting silver beneath the night sky.

I couldn’t tear my gaze away from the Fae. My mouth worked before my mind and I breathed, “You’re so beautiful.”

She tittered a bell-like laugh, raised both hands high, and twirled. Every inch of her shimmered like starlit water. “Most kind, mortal.”

Heat blossomed in my face. “I’m sorry, I didn’t mean to disturb you. You seemed sad.”

Her smile didn’t fade, but she did look a bit puzzled. Then, after a moment’s thought, she tilted her face up and breathed deep, as if drinking in the moonlight. “Joyful tears, mortal. The faces of my sisters grace the stars this night.”

I turned towards Velidan, a dozen silent questions hovering on my tongue. My eyes fell to a bundle of emerald-green at his feet. It almost looked like…

My teeth sank into my lower lip and I drew a breath, raising the cloth for closer inspection. It was a cape made of shimmering fabric, with a deep hood and sleek pockets lining the inside. The front bore a winding downward pattern of soft, sturdy laces. I eyed it thoughtfully, picturing Teela’s recent delight over her similarly-hued hat and nightgown.

“It suits your lovely eyes, sweet mortal.” Gentle fingers traced my cheekbone, leaving a tingling trail of heat behind. She smiled kindly.

Warmth flushed every inch of my skin. I stammered for a moment, baffled that this dreamlike woman would pay me a compliment when her own beauty was so captivating. “Oh, it’s n-not… er, thank you. I’m… I’m so sorry, but I didn’t bring any money. But I’d love to buy this if I ever come back. How much would it cost?”

Her puzzled expression returned. The Fae’s luminous visage shifted towards Velidan. A few murmured words and the shifting of one of his belted pouches later, I was dazedly walking away with the petal-soft cape clutched between my shaking hands.

Not just idle browsing, then. He’s actually buying me things. Well, he’s buying Teela things. Ashes, I could get used to ‘pleasant.’

A curly-haired, horned young man interrupted my musings. He wore unfamiliar animal skins and smelled of honey, and his wide smile held far too many teeth. He dipped into a deep bow towards Velidan, then beamed at me.

“I’ve a harp strung from dyla feathers.” His voice was rich, every word lilting as if half-in-song. “And a lucky stone. I’ll trade both.” One slender hand tossed a small amber stone high, letting it glint beneath the starlight. He caught it with a wink.

I blinked, then looked down at the fabric in my hands. “O-oh. That’s nice, but… I’m sorry, I don’t think I need a harp. And this is for a friend.”

The handsome Fae’s smile faltered. He looked puzzled, much like the beautiful woman, and cast a questioning look towards Velidan.

“She’s not for sale, satyr.”

I nearly choked.

Brown curls glinted as he dipped another bow towards the figure looming behind me. “Ah. I misunderstood. Apologies, my lord.” Without another word, he trounced into a nearby pavilion.

I stared after him. “He wanted to buy me?”

Soft laughter ruffled the back of my hair. “For a harp and a lucky stone. Quite the price. He must have liked you.”

I turned, heart sinking and skin crawling.

Velidan examined my expression and rolled his eyes. “Not every creature here is ill-intentioned, Brin. Stop looking so frightened.”

“He wanted to buy me!”

Elegant brows rose. “Satyrs are hopeless romantics. He probably wanted to free you from my terrible clutches, spend a few nights serenading you beneath the moonlight, then gather his kin to forge a portal and send you safely home.”

My jaw dropped. My eyes fell to the ground, then to the pavilion the satyr had gone into, then returned to Velidan’s amused face. “What?”

He shrugged. “I imagine you would have had a lovely time.”

I squinted at him. “You’re… you’re making that up.”

“The Night Court is full of bloodthirsty, sadistic creatures, Brin. You’ll see many of them tomorrow. It’s also,” he nodded to the world around us, “full of Fae who can happily coexist with humans.”

I stared at him for a long while, hands clenched around the cape, considering the words and wondering why in the realms he would lie about such a thing yet not fully able to believe that he was telling the truth. Finally, when I gave up on finding an immediate answer, I asked, “What’s a lucky stone?”

He blinked. “I would think the name is self-explanatory. It’s a rock that makes one lucky.”

I spent a moment pondering that, too, then snorted and shook my head in helpless wonder. “Of course it is. Saints. Stars. This is all… this place is just…. I can’t believe I’m… how lucky?”

Velidan cast a sly glance towards the pavilion. “Clearly not as much as he would’ve liked.”

A startled giggle bubbled from my lips. I paced along towards a nearby collection of tables lined by glass bottles and lidded clay pots. A large, lizard-like creature lay snoring beneath one table.

Feeling emboldened by my own mirth and the recent, shockingly pleasant interactions with these strange Fae, I took a deep breath. Then I turned to Velidan and said, “I’d like to talk about the shard.”

His eyes glittered. “Ah. I thought you might. Faer’yll Illyrium,” he purred, holding up a glass jar of shimmering, gold-flecked liquid. “Forged of the soul. A carving out of one’s very self.”

A shiver ran down my spine. “It sounds terrible.”

He replaced the bottle, then examined another. “It’s rarely done. Most Fae don’t have enough strength for such a ritual, and fewer still are willing to pour their power into a tangible object.”

I chewed my lower lip, watching him with wide eyes. Both arms curled tight, hugging Teela’s gift to my chest as my heart hammered against it. “But… someone did.”

Velidan’s lips pressed together. “Someone did.”

“You called him a fool earlier. The… the man who wielded the sword.”

“I did.”

I spent a long moment searching his face. “Why?”

He glanced at me over a stack of sweet-smelling opaque jars, looking if the answer should have been obvious. “Because he died.”

“He sacrificed himself. He fought the Nightmare. He saved countless lives and kept our realm safe, even though it killed him.”

The expression on his face remained the same. Glass tinkled as he lifted a bottle, sniffed its contents, then replaced it. “Yes. And?”

“And that’s not foolish! That’s… saints, noble! It’s heroic, it’s a sacrifice that should be honored!”

He shrugged, drifting towards another table lined with furs. “Honor it, then. But ultimately, a very powerful creature died to save a handful of humans. I can think of nothing so absurd.”

I let the callousness slide past me; I expected nothing less from him. Instead, I forged on with a different question. “You used the word ‘confused’ earlier, too. What did you mean?”

“Ah. You caught that.” A slow, sly smile curled his mouth. “And you looked surprised, but not for long. Which means you and I have come to a similar conclusion.”

I swallowed, pacing with deliberate slowness to the other side of the table and curling both hands together. “The shard is alive.”

“In a way. It’s a fragment of consciousness. A shattered echo of its wielder. Forged of his soul.” He drawled the words slowly, as if tasting each one. The claim hung like smoke in the air as otherworldly figures prowled around us. He thought for a moment, head tilted and gaze heavy, before murmuring, “I first suspected it a few nights ago, after our latest bargain.”

My breath caught. “When I gave it to you, then woke up with it in my hand.” And then I had my dream, but I’m certainly not telling him a thing about that. Not yet, at least.

An unsettling light flickered in Velidan’s eyes. He searched my face, looking thoughtful and hungry and perhaps even a bit angry, and it was all I could do not to step back. “Yes. I believe it chose to leave, just as it did the night we met. I assumed it was a fluke the first time, but the second felt… deliberate. It nearly burned me while you were waking up. I thought perhaps it simply favors humans, but then it didn’t go with the old woman-”

“Resa.”

“-which means there’s something about you it likes.”

My heart slammed out a maddening rhythm. Every word was so bizarre, so impossible, yet I didn’t know how to refute the claims. “Why... why do you say it’s confused?”

He braced both hands on the table, leaning forwards so our eyes were level. “Because, Brin,” the words were so slow and heavy that I found myself leaning in, hanging on each syllable with Teela’s gift clutched to my chest, “it nearly murdered you this morning.”

It took a long moment for the statement to sink in. Fae moved around us, their voices a distant hum, as I echoed the claim over and over. My mind raced, trying to think of when exactly I’d been in mortal danger this morning, and of course the only answer was-

“That was the rune. The shard didn’t hurt me, my magic hurt me.” I shook my head, breaking the magnetic spell. The world refocused around us and I continued over towards the next stall. There was no table in this one, just a collection of raw meat laid out along the grass. A scaly, eyeless figure sat crouched in the shadows. I quickly backed out, straight into a hard chest.

Velidan’s voice came low, just above my ear and far too leading. “And where do you think your magic comes from, Brin?”

I turned, giving him a wary frown. “We talked about this earlier.”

His mouth curled. “We started to. Shall we begin our history lesson now?”

Magic. He’s actually going to tell me about magic. A shiver of excitement ran up my spine and I paced along, towards several tents with long, cluttered tables spaced out before them. Just as before, I said firmly, “Magic comes from the gods.”

He was silent for a while. A quick glance back showed that he seemed to be lost in thought. Then he drew closer and smirked. “Name your gods, mortal. Tell me of them.”

His tone made it feel like I was walking into a trap. But I saw no point in avoiding the conversation, so I recited, “Well, there are three. Each sacrificed themselves long ago, to seal Fae from the mortal realm and protect against their return. The first was Anduin, who guards our days with blessed sunlight.”

Velidan gave a little snarl. “Anduin. Mm. The firstborn son of Andruil and Radarra, lord and lady of the Day Court. A bleeding heart whose parents cast themselves as twin suns to watch over their realm.”

I stared at him. Blinked. Chewed the words over and over, searching for any shred of meaning in them. They were foreign, nonsensical, and… like many other recent realizations, far too big.

“And Anduin thought it noble to follow in his parents’ footsteps.” He sneered. “When the realms were sealed away- by Fae, not by your stylized gods- he disobeyed his father’s decree. Instead of ruling his own Court, Anduin gave himself as a guardian,” the word came out soaked in derision, “for the poor mortals. A distant blaze to sear away any Night Fae who dared hunt you in the day.”

“You’re not… that doesn’t… you can’t… you’re saying the sun’s a Fae?!” I burst out laughing. He didn’t join me. “That’s… that’s mad! That’s insane!”

“Is it? More so than your claim that he’s a god?”

“Well,” I spluttered for a moment, then threw both hands skyward, “yes!”

“Continue, Brin. Name another.”

I paused, mouth open, before letting out another laugh. “Fine. Virtue. The goddess who poured herself out as blessed water, to provide a barrier against monsters who wandered the land.”

He laughed, too, but it was a bitter sound. “Virtue. Ah. All Fae know that tale. She was a princess of the Court of Tides. The first true power to fall and another bleeding heart, and she did not die willingly.”

My smile fell. I didn’t believe anything he said, of course, it was all too mad, but the statement was still intriguing. “You’re saying someone killed her? Who?”

A shadow fell over his features. “Thalessa.”

“What? What?” I searched the space around us, baffled, nearly hoping to find sense on one of the tables. “That’s… you’re trying to tell me one goddess murdered another?”

“They were both Fae. And murdered? No, that makes it sound far too merciful. Too quick. Thalessa feasted on Virtue.” He flashed a sharp smile, taking in my obvious shock. “Shall I list Thalessa’s other names for you? She was given many before the realms were sealed. The Whispering One, She Who Hungers, FaeBane. The most common here is The Writhing Witch.”

“You’re talking about a witchwood! A goddess who-!”

“I’m talking about an ancient, powerful Fae who feasts on her own kind, mortal.” He stepped closer. Broad shoulders and carved features blocked out the night sky.

The proximity sent me stumbling back, but he followed, and I found myself trapped between a table and a tent and looking up at a cold visage. An expression that said, despite the ridiculousness of each claim, he was deadly serious.

“Thalessa.” He practically spat the word. “A mindless witch who knows nothing but hunger, who spreads the seeds of murder through the mortal realm.”

My spine stiffened at that. Half of me begged to turn and flee from the unwanted closeness, but I couldn’t bear the slander against my beloved witchwood grove. “Seeds of murder? Ashes, witchwood is one of the truest, best things in our realm! It protects us! It guards us! Thalessa was a goddess who gave herself to the ground, to flourish throughout the realm and shield humans from your kind!”

“She was a tyrant who spent weeks eating a beloved princess while Fae from a dozen other courts tried in vain to save her. Quite a few were devoured in the attempt.” He sneered down, looming over me as the words sank in. “And now you humans all worship the endless river of Virtue’s blood. The one that flows from Thalessa’s roots. What do you call it?”

I could hardly make my mouth work. I imagined the bright, babbling stream that wound its cheerful way through Fellbrook, then winced at the thought of its water darkened by blood. “The… the Grace. We call it the Grace.”

“Really?” He gave a harsh laugh. “How macabre.”

“It’s… none of this is true! Saints, you’re telling me, what, the gods don’t exist?! They were just Fae?!”

“What’s the difference?”

He might as well have slapped me. I stood stunned, trying to form a dozen answers even as none of them quite made sense.

Velidan leaned down with a wicked grin. “Think about it, mortal. If I were to appear in a human village, impervious to time and blades, healing mortal wounds, imposing my will on lesser minds, and snapping steel with my bare hands, then call myself a god, what human could refute the claim? Who could possibly argue I wasn’t a god?”

“But that’s… that’s different! You’re,” I gestured up and down wildly, indicating the whole of him, “I mean, you’re not the sun! You’re not a river or an ancient witchwood or-”

“You’re an insect.”

I gasped, stunned into momentarily silence by the sudden- and jarringly rude- change in topic. “I… I beg your pardon?”

“It’s a metaphor. Think of yourself, in terms of your own strength and capabilities, as an insect.”

I spent a moment staring up, collecting my wits, then said breathlessly, “I don’t think I like that metaphor.”

He snorted. “You may think of yourself as a butterfly, if that helps.”

“Not really.”

Velidan chuckled as he shifted sideways, sweeping an arm out to the sea of otherworldly creatures. “Most of the Fae you see here are also insects. Some are more akin to vermin, like mice or rats.”

I glanced around the throng, understanding his point but still feeling thoroughly overwhelmed.

“Then there are things like me, or like your foolish hero. Cats, dogs, wolves; predators. We may not mind the insects or the vermin, but if they’re in our way… well.” Smoke and starlight glinted behind his head, framing the flash of a too-sharp smile.

My nose scrunched. “So… based on this analogy, what are Anduin, Virtue, and Thalessa? Bears?”

“Very large bears, but yes. And not just them. The Nightmare, the two suns of the Day Court, many rulers of the other realms… I could give you quite a list.” He regarded me thoughtfully, head tilting to one side. “Has the comparison helped?”

Both hands found my hips. “Well, I understand it, but that doesn’t mean I believe it! You’re telling me that human history is wrong! But who’s to say your version of things is correct? It’s not as though you were there to see Anduin throw himself into the sky or watch while Thalessa, saints, ate Virtue, or… or…” My shoulders sagged and my eyes rounded. I realized that I had no idea how old the Fae standing before me actually was. When my voice came out again, it sounded very small. “Were you?”

He chuckled at my expression. “By the dawn, Brin, I’m not that old. But when it comes to history, I’ll trust the version given by centuries-old Fae over the forgetful, malleable memories of mortals. Ones who only live for…” a crease formed between his brows as he looked me up and down. Then, with an unusually hesitant air, “How long?”

I barely heard the question. My mind was too busy racing, frantically trying to parse through this bizarre conversation. Saints. The version of history given by creatures who have lived for hundreds of years. A version that rewrites theology, belief, divinity, magic…

It was all, once again, simply too big for me to even begin to unravel. If any of it was even true. Nothing he claimed made any sense, none of it could be true, but… why would he lie?

“Brin?”

I felt dizzy. “What?”

He was frowning at me. “How long do humans live?”

“Oh. D-decades.”

The displeased look deepened. He continued to tower over me, clearly waiting for a more thorough answer.

“Sorry. This is just… a lot. Um, sixty or seventy years, maybe? I think that’s the average.” I scrubbed my eyes, then choked out a little huff of laughter. “A bit more or less, I suppose, depending on where you live and how good the nearest cleric is.”

Velidan met my poor attempt at humor with silence. Then, in a voice like ice, “How unfortunate.”

I just shrugged as another helpless little laugh choked its way up. A monster had just told me an alternate version of history, had just casually uprooted everything I knew about the gods, and now pitied me for only having a few more decades to live.

What else could I do but laugh?

“You brought all this up when we talked about magic,” I parsed out once my shoulders stopped shaking. “If we say your version of history is true, what does that mean about runes? Stars, if you really believe the gods are a bunch of Fae, where do you think my magic is coming from?”

“I don’t think, mortal, I know where your magic is coming from.” He cast a hard look around us, eyes glinting as they flicked to each nearby Fae. “And we’ll discuss that later. In private.” Full, red lips twitched, and his tone sank to silk as he cast a glance up towards the shadowy tower. “Perhaps over an actual dinner.”
