The walk to the forest was awesome as expected. I was in full focus mode, soaking in every lesson Bob gave us about the forest’s creatures, and at the same time memorizing the path. I think I had a knack for that kind of thing—I was learning quickly.

When we arrived at the tribe, we got a welcome like no other. A horde of scorpion-surikata kittens came charging right at us. There were more of them this time—and they really wanted to play.

Juliette pulled a clever maneuver to escape the mob of tiny fuzzballs, managing to pull the fruitcakes from the bag. When she held them up, every scorpion-surikata froze. Even the grownups. They all stared in reverent awe at the glorious, sugary creation.

Then, slowly, they approached. Cautiously. Sniffing.

And then… well… table manners clearly weren’t among the scorpion-surikata’s strengths.

The tribe devoured the fruitcakes in a matter of seconds. It was an all-out feeding frenzy. Crumbs flew. Paper got shredded. One of the kittens licked a rock by accident and looked personally betrayed.

While the tribe was distracted by the feast, I stepped out of the cave. There was something I wanted to test. I looked around until I found what I needed: two long, sturdy-looking sticks.

I walked back to Bob, showed him the sticks, and put my hand on him. “Tell bear to practice with stick,” I said.

He didn’t get it.

So I handed one stick to Juliette and we demonstrated simple back-and-forth strikes and defensive moves. “Bear play with stick,” I said again, “become strong.”

I touched Bob, and he showed me that he understood. Now came the hard part: getting the armadillo care bears to understand too.

The tribe leader was present. She seemed to grasp what was happening. With a short screech-like call, she summoned the two care-bear sentries from outside the cave. She was a smart one.

Bob climbed onto one of them, brushing against his fur—beginning his communication. The leader went to work explaining to the other.

I wasn’t planning to really train them—I just wanted to test if the idea had any merit. Could they even learn to use weapons? And if so, how effective would they be?

It was slow at first. The care bears eventually figured out how to hold the sticks, though it wasn’t natural for them. Their claws weren’t exactly ideal for gripping a weapon. But after several tries, they could hold the sticks pretty firmly.

We brought over more sticks. Every time Juliette and I would demonstrate the thrusting motion, they’d try to mimic it. Neither of us knew the first thing about spear combat, but we figured if the care bears could just stab in the right direction, that’d already be terrifying.

They were strong creatures, after all. You wouldn’t want to be on the pointy end of one of their clumsy but forceful jabs.

After an hour or so, I got my answer. Right now, they sucked. But they were getting better. There was no doubt the training was working. With time, they’d become deadly.

I started thinking about the long-term implications. If this worked—and it looked like it would—other tribes would catch on. The entire forest dynamic would shift. This could be the beginning of an arms race among forest creatures.

And what if… what if the forest tribes decided to go further? What if they eventually marched on the towns?

No. That would be suicide. They couldn’t hope to win against human cities.

...Probably.

Before we left, Bob and Alice—yes, I decided to name the tribe leader Alice—asked to talk to us alone.

I put my hand on Bob. He asked when we’d have what we promised: the equipment, the food, the spears.

I did a quick mental calculation.

We needed twelve spears—for me, Juliette, and ten armadillo care bears. A medium-quality spear cost about 10 Sigots (one silver). Full leather armor for the two of us—we had parts already. Shields. Food and other supplies for at least a week. Altogether, we needed about 30 silvers.

We already had 8 saved up. With our recent pay raises, we now earned about 8 silvers a day combined. With food costing 2 silver a day, we could still put aside 6 silver per day. That meant, in just a few days…

“Faster,” I said. “We do it faster now. Still need a few days.”

Bob and Alice understood.

Then Alice stepped forward and gestured for me to touch her back.

She showed me the care bears practicing with sticks, then later with real spears. She liked the training plan. She would keep training them, even without us.

Then she showed me something new—a place in the forest we hadn’t seen. A rival hideout. Different Scorpion-Surikatas and care-bears. It was the white-stripe tribe.

She knew a lot about them—or at least she believed she did. From what she showed me, their tribe was larger, but not by much. Around 30 surikata scorpions, and 40–50 soldiers, mostly care bears. But they had those big cow-deer things, too—and those were a problem. A problem the spears would help solve.

Finally, she showed me something that changed everything.

She showed our tribe launching a surprise attack on the white stripe hideout. Care bears with spears, ambushing them. Some enemies died. The rest fled.

The message was clear.

They weren’t planning to defend their territory. They were planning to attack.

She didn’t show me or Juliette as part of the attack force. That made sense—we were too valuable to risk. It was a smart plan.

But it still made me feel uneasy.

I told everything to Juliette.

“That… makes sense,” she said. “For the tribe to really be safe long-term, the White Stripes need to be driven out. Diplomacy’s not exactly on the table.”

It wasn’t. At least not from our tribe’s perspective. To Bob and Alice, the white-stripes weren’t just enemies—they were monsters. Bob had shown me what they’d done. His dead children. Other tribe members, small and innocent, torn apart. I hated the white-stripes too.

But I also knew—deep down—that this wasn’t the full picture.

It was likely two primitive tribes locked in a cycle of violence. Neither side was innocent.

Still, I was part of one of those tribes now. I cared about them. And that made all the difference.

We would attack.

We would win.

And we would kill enough of the White Stripes to make sure they never retaliated.

The next day, I worked hard at the Clothier shop. I could now make skirts, and damn good ones at that.

I was improving—not magically, but the normal way. And that felt really good. Rewarding.

In another day or two, if I kept this up, I’d hit the next level—maybe I’d earn 5 silvers a day.

Over the next few days, we kept up the routine. We worked hard at our jobs, brought more supplies to the tribe, and finally started buying real spears from the town’s blacksmith. I even managed to hit my daily underwear quota after our forest visits—talk about efficient. I felt competent. Useful. Even proud.

I now knew the way to the tribe hideout without Bob’s help. I remembered which routes were safe, which monster territories to avoid, and what terrain signaled danger. I was good at remembering this stuff. Juliette had a harder time with it, but she was getting there too.

We were also… kind of rich? With both of us promoted, we were bringing in ten silvers a day. With only two needed for food, we had enough to gear up the tribe and even enjoy a drink at the tavern once in a while.

Those tavern nights were definitely dates, but again, I didn’t ask Juliette to confirm.

Then, at the end of that wonderful week—just when everything seemed to be working out—something weird happened.

At almost the same time, Juliette and I started to feel... off.

I could barely finish my underwear quota for the day. I felt like all the energy had been drained from me.

“Probably one of us caught something and infected the other,” I told Juliette after work. “Some dumb fantasy world virus. I’m sure it’s nothing.”

“I don’t know,” she said. “It doesn’t feel like being sick. It feels like… depression. I know the feeling. You feel tired, sad, afraid, right?”

“Well, yeah,” I said. “But that’s not something you usually catch. And we both feel like that.”

“It’s not a virus,” she said quietly. “I think… it’s Bob’s sting. That stuff in our blood. It didn’t just make us love Bob and the tribe—it made us happy. Energetic. That effect is wearing off.”

As soon as she said it, everything clicked.

“Wait,” I said. “So we need another dose? Like… like a drug?”

“This is bad, right?” Juliette asked. But even as she said that, we both knew—we wanted it. Bob was there, snoozing on the pillow. We could fix this in seconds. Just one sting, and we’d feel normal again. Happy again. Ourselves again.

But… what if this time it lasts less than a week? What if it gets weaker each time? Would we end up chasing the high? Becoming addicted? Scorpion-Surikata junkies?

I didn’t say any of that out loud. Not to her.

“We can’t function like this,” I finally said. “The tribe needs us. The war is coming. We need to be at our best.”

“You’re right,” she said. “But this probably isn’t healthy. We should stop… after. When the tribe is safe.”

“Agreed.” I walked over to Bob and gently nudged him awake. “Hey Bob. We’d like a shot, please.”

Bob blinked sleepily, then crawled lazily onto my leg. With a light, familiar sting, he injected the venom into my bloodstream. Then he waddled over to Juliette and did the same.

Then he curled up on the pillow again, as if this were just another boring chore.And just like that, the magic happened.

In minutes, all the sadness, all the tiredness, the worthlessness—it washed away like dirt under a hot shower.

I was back.

Happy. Energetic. Clear-headed.

And most of all, I loved my friend Bob so much.
