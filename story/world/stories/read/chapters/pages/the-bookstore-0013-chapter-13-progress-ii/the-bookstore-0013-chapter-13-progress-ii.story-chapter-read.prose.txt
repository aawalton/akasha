Rick felt a little embarrassed sitting so close to the half-wolfkin.

Not out of prejudice. He would never inflict on others the very pain so often forced upon him. It was simply his usual shyness, the same awkwardness that made him choose the edge of the room whenever possible. So, instead of taking his usual place, he moved to the far right end of the bookstore and settled there with cautious determination.

His patron, the storeowner, had called learning from the Syllable easy.

Rick doubted that the word meant the same thing to both of them.

Still, every day after helping his father at the carpentry shop, he came to the bookstore and threw himself back into the effort. He had even followed Anne’s advice and started copying the words by hand. At first, the exercise had seemed pointless. Worse than pointless. It felt like the extra effort was only making everything harder. Yet he kept doing it because Anne had told him to, and because nothing in his life had ever been done by quitting halfway through.

Eventually, the results became impossible to deny.

He could now recognize most words in a single glance, and the moments when he could not were becoming rarer by the day. The progress was slow, but real.

Now came the next step. The Beastiarium.

Truthfully, he had wanted to read it much sooner. Even if the words had still resisted him, he could have looked at the drawings and tried to understand something from the magical impressions recorded in the pages. Unfortunately, even reading the book inside the bookstore carried a cost, and Rick had already learned that everything here came with a reason for its price. Anne had explained restoration, maintenance, the wearing-down of magical texts. Books with active properties needed to be repaired after being read. The comparison she gave had been easy enough to understand.

It was like drinking from a cup. The cup remained, but something inside it had to be filled again.

So he had waited. He had forced himself to wait until he felt ready.

The fox he had traded to the storeowner would pay for one session with the Beastiarium. Only one. That meant no waste, no panic, and no sloppy reading. He had to make it count.

That morning, barely containing his excitement, Rick had run all the way to the bookstore and asked for the book before even remembering to greet anyone. After apologizing for the rudeness, he drank the miraculous tea almost too quickly and threw himself into study.

Now he sat with the heavy volume open before him, turning the pages as gently as though the book might hurt from rough handling.

His plan was simple.

He looked first for beasts found on this side of the mountains. Since his reading time was limited, he focused on information he could actually use soon. Once he learned how to deal with the local beasts properly, he could begin pushing deeper into the forbidden forest while continuing the search for a safer path through the mountains.

Before the bookstore, he had used the mountains as an excuse.

Not consciously, perhaps, but the truth remained. They were a convenient scapegoat for everything he lacked. The beasts were too few. The routes were bad. The town had failed. All of that was true to some degree, but once he began reading the Beastiarium, a more painful truth emerged.

Even the weaker beasts on this side were often beyond him. Not because they were too strong, but because he had not understood them well enough.

That realization had hurt, but it had also cleared the fog from his mind. Now the road ahead seemed almost simple. Hard, dangerous, and slow—but clear. He only had to walk it prepared.

Whenever he found a familiar beast in the Beastiarium, Rick took up a blank scroll and began recording the most relevant information in his own words. It slowed him down terribly, but he had already tested Anne’s advice enough times to stop doubting it. At first, taking notes had seemed absurd. Why copy information that was already in the book? But the benefit had slowly become obvious. Writing forced him to sort the useful parts, to decide what was important, and in doing so, it made the knowledge feel more like his own.

The last step, before moving to another beast, was always the same.

He experienced the virtual model.

The whole thing remained beyond his understanding, as most spells did. Anne had explained that the book carried an imprinted model of specialist knowledge, one that the reader could experience mentally. The better his understanding of the beast became, the more complete that virtual experience would be. Rick still could not have explained how such a thing worked if someone had put a knife to his throat, but it helped. So he kept using it, and each time it left him a little more certain that the bookstore was worth every coin, beast, and embarrassment it had cost him.

He was making excellent progress when another customer arrived.

Rick did not even need to look up to guess who it might be. It would not take long for the town asset manager to show up and bother him again. In the beginning, Dorgh had treated him with that particular brand of casual disdain reserved for disappointing youths. Thankfully, the old dwarf’s attitude had changed once he realized Rick was actually making an effort. Now, if not respectful exactly, he was at least less eager to look down on him.

This time, however, Dorgh did not seem to notice him at all.

Today was too important for that.

For the last month, the town manager had drilled himself relentlessly on the information inside Kasar’s Compendium of Mystic Brewing, only to be stopped again and again by the lack of proper materials. He had searched through his contacts, written letters, made discreet inquiries, and swallowed more caution than pride. Finally, the day he had been dreaming of arrived. The town courier had brought him an expensive package straight from the province capital—though Dorgh strongly suspected that some of the contents had passed through Riverend first.

Gods forbid the mayor learned that.

Anything that enriched their rival town was effectively banned, or at least treated like treachery, by that deranged elf and his petty war against reality. Dorgh suspected he would be lucky to keep his job if the truth surfaced.

Still, he had the materials now.

After checking the package twice, he had come straight to the bookstore to study the original codex one more time before attempting his first brew. Anne had been right again. The original was incomparably better than the copy. Because it was a codex, he could use its magical models to experience the ingredients and the final brews in a way no ordinary book could offer. It was absurdly useful. Truly, the more Dorgh studied these things, the less he understood how bookmancy had ever been dismissed as obsolete.

“Miss Anne,” he said after exchanging the required greetings and, of course, accepting his tea first. By now that had become part of his ritual visits. “I finally got the materials for my first brew. I want to check the book again before I try.”

“Is that so?” Anne said, her eyes brightening. “Then perhaps you should speak to Ceres later. She has been collecting books for me, which is why she returned so soon. If things continue well, we may build a proper trade route over time...”

Strangely, Dorgh did not pursue the subject.

Normally he would have. Trade interested him too much for silence to be natural. But today his anxiety was stronger than curiosity. He gave only a vague response and made for an empty table with the codex, too eager to lose himself in the work.

It did not take him long to find the recipe he wanted. Iced Mind Relaxing Brew.

It was classified as easy, at least by the standards of mystic brewing, though it demanded precise temperature control and left little room for sloppiness. Fortunately, it required only four ingredients and four steps. By the scale of the craft, that was considered basic.

First came the usual grain mashing. The exact mix could vary according to taste and availability, but winter barley was mandatory.

Then came the truly delicate part. Brewing.

That was where the frost lotus justified its painful cost. By adding the freezing flower while heating the mash, Dorgh could force an unusual balance and achieve true cold-brewing during what should have been a hot stage of preparation. The contradiction itself was part of what allowed the brew to develop its mystic properties.

Fermentation, thankfully, was less demanding in technique and more demanding in patience. Time, the correct vessel, and ice yeast would do most of the work.

The final stage was filtration and storage, which required the steady addition of a cooling aromatic herb. Dorgh had chosen cold rosemary. It was affordable enough not to make him weep and harmonious enough not to ruin the whole thing if his brewing control proved imperfect.

If the temperature was handled properly and the ingredients balanced with care, the brew would strengthen the cold-aligned effects of the materials and make them more easily absorbed by the body. The main expected mystical effect was mental relaxation, but depending on ingredient quality and overall execution, secondary effects could emerge as well—mental recovery, intenser calm, perhaps even a brief strengthening of the mind’s resistance to external interference.

As Dorgh studied the virtual models of the ingredients, one ruthless truth became clear. His understanding of cold essence materials was far too shallow.

The realization pained him enough that, after a minute of internal struggle, he steeled himself and asked Anne for another book. Five minutes and three silver later, he was flipping through the Common Tongue translation of Compendium of Elemental Essences: Cold Edition.

From behind the counter, Anne watched all this with silent satisfaction.

What she loved most about research was exactly this.

One book always led to another.

And then another.
