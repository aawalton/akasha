I didn’t need to wait long for my fingers to brush hard plastic, and I pulled out a 80s-style brickphone. It was larger than should have been possible to store in a pocket that small, but I was too tired and grumpy to care, so I just slapped the flashing, medal-shaped relief on the phone’s back, and tossed it on the bed.

The phone flashed one last time, and blue-gray light enveloped the room in a murky haze. It faltered, and a readout rang through my consciousness.

[>>Cura.exe has detected Domain/Real desynchronization.][>>Please stand by for attempted re-synchronization of positional data.][>>…][>>Insufficient permissions to fully alter pseudophysical reality.][>>Adjusting parameters…][>>Done.]

Pina’s image flickered to life above the bed, as if floating. While her form was rendered in perfect detail, her movements were jittery, frozen one moment before jumping ahead several seconds.

[>>Insufficient bandwidth.][>>Adjusting…][>>Done.]

Blueish light enveloped the room one more time, and Pina vanished, only to reappear in front of the open window. I could only see her upper body, and it took me a second to understand why that perspective felt so familiar. Then it hit me, I’d seen that same view thousands of times on janky Skype calls.

With another deep sigh, I grabbed a chair that sat next to a small writing desk in the corner and dragged it in front of the window. Pina seemed to be back to her old self, fidgeting as she tried to maintain an air of aloof control. If she ever wanted that act to be convincing, she still had a lot to learn.

“Machine, I command you to—” she began as I collapsed into my chair.

Pina blinked, confused, leaned forward, then remembered she should act imperial instead of intrigued.

“I’ve been fighting so we could survive the last two weeks, all while carrying your ‘wire, hexxed carcass around the Witchwilds. What have you done to be oh-so tired?”

Pina glanced at something behind her. I suspected it was Tin and Voni, and that they were asleep, because when her attention returned to me, she ran her hands over her tired face, then through her messy hair as her eyes wandered upward, lost. She was still wearing Voni’s military jacket, at least two sizes too big for her.

Her lips parted, but ultimately, the words wouldn't come. Eventually, her mouth closed, and she rubbed her palm over her face as if to wipe away the tiredness.

I knew exactly how she felt.

“Please tell me you fixed it,” she said, voice not quite quivering.

“So much for being a Saint,” the entitled little shit grumbled.

“Wait, what city?”

I blinked, then I remembered she had no idea what a Domain looked like.

So I spent some time explaining the worlds I’d visited since I awoke. Zephyro’s Domain, sandswept, regal and real. My own Domain, empty, cozy, and dangerous. And now this weird amalgamation of 80s tech that never existed. For someone who hated tech, she asked a lot of questions about digital worlds, and I explained as best I could. As time went on, she leaned forward, her eyes darting over my words with rapt attention. I decided not to call her out on it.

From her questions, I gleaned a bit of information in turn. Enough to begin to piece together some vague idea about how the world worked these days.

It was a mess. Guns still existed, which I already suspected, since we had built them to last. Ammo was scarce, however, which made sense. To my surprise, Pina knew what cars were, and claimed she’d even seen a plane once, which couldn’t be right. We’d never made those work. On the other hand, there were things I knew she should be familiar with since they’d been around before I even arrived on Tobes, but which she claimed to know very little about. Monsters, for example, didn’t seem to exist anymore, except for Ferals. They didn’t fear mind mages anymore, either. Nevertheless, even the idea of a city, with paved streets and houses standing shoulder to shoulder, seemed at the same time exciting and very foreign to her.

“No!” she said, smiling as her mind wandered through the streets of her imagination. “That’s where the Mage Lords live. Anyway, you’re saying they just have these poles standing around with lights on them and—”

She nodded hesitantly.

“In a camp,” she hedged. “We move around.”

Now it was my turn to be curious.

It made no sense. The logistics alone…

Lips pressed to a thin line, Pina looked over her shoulder again. When she turned back, I was sure she was going to tell me to fuck off. Instead, she actually answered the question. In fact, she answered several questions in a row. The whole time, she spoke as though she was desperate for me to say something derisive about their way of life. I made sure to try not to let my confusion, exasperation, and regret slip through the lines. No need to feed her resentment.

In exchange, I learned about what life was like in the remains of the republic. It was very hard to grow anything in the land they now called the Witchwilds, because of the high concentration of Logic. Crops tended to mutate into inedible versions of themselves or turn into tech, often useless because of the concept mismatch. To survive, Pina’s people roamed along a thin stretch of Logic-rich land they called the Path. It apparently ran south from Novus Apex toward Haven-Of-Progress, or east toward Roadmarket. Pina mentioned both, and evaded the question when I asked for clarification.

Either way, it was a dangerous life, under constant threat by not just Feral Havocs, which apparently became more likely if they stayed in one place for too long. The much bigger risk were rival groups trying to steal the tech they’d gathered. Apparently, in their world view, there were two kinds of nomads. Those who found, called Finders, and those who took.

The Takers.

A lot of things made a lot more sense now.

I had managed to keep my thoughts still through her explanations, but when Pina mentioned that it was common practice to send children to scavenge tech from areas crawling with Ferals by exploiting our FoF protocols, I had enough.

The thoughts had written themselves unbidden, a slip of the mind. I wouldn’t take them back, though.

“It’s not that bad!” Pina said, raising her chin in defiance.

“I don’t… “ Pina paused. Her chin sunk again. “I guess, I do, yeah.”

For the first time, it seemed she had realized she was being contrarian for absolutely no reason. I hadn’t thought it possible, but my smug satisfaction tasted of copper. Her eyes wandered somewhere I couldn’t follow. Over her sleeping family, from the way her jaw worked before her hair fell to cover it.

“I don’t get why they want to stay. Voni, especially. They all just want her to do things the way they tell her to and—” She brushed the hair out of her face, revealing a more somber look than I had anticipated. “I guess that’s going to change now. She needs a safe place. Without machines, or Takers, or the Free Flesh. She could become a handmaiden, or something, maybe?”

Voni did not strike me as the kind of person yearning to become a handmaiden, but I saw the glimmer of hope in Pina’s eyes, and couldn’t bring myself to crush it.

“What? Me? No, I want to be a—” she paused. Her cheeks turned red, and for once, it wasn’t from oh-so-righteous anger.

“I want to be a paladin,” she said, as if daring me to do something about it.

Unbidden, I remembered Lorraine of Brightfall.

I wondered if she had anyone to talk to besides me. I wondered if there was someone else in her life who noticed those cracks, wondered if they knew she was ready to hatch.
