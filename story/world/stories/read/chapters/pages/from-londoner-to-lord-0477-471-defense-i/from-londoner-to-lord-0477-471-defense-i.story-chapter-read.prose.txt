Kivamus stopped some distance beyond the manor house door, where the yard was already full of movement. Guards who had been eating or resting were grabbing weapons and running toward the gate. Male guards, female guards, older men, newer recruits, all of them moved with tense faces. Some looked afraid. Most looked focused. Shields were put on the backs of the male guards. Spears were handed to them one by one by the servants helping out. Crossbows were passed from hand to hand to those who were being assigned for them by Hyola who was in charge of the archers in absence of Yufim who was out on a hunting trip.

The servants and maids who were not needed stood farther back, some near the kitchen door, some near the servants' hall. Their faces were pale in the torchlight burning in a few places. No one needed to explain what the horns meant.

Kivamus made himself stand straight.

People were looking at him. He could feel it. A few guards glanced his way as they ran past, and one of the maids looked at him with wide eyes. If he showed fear openly, it would spread faster than anything he could say.

So he kept his face steady.

"Milord," Hudan said sharply, turning back toward him. "You will stay here."

"I should come with you," Kivamus said, knowing that he didn't want to sit idle here, wondering what came next. "You know I can hold my own with a sword and spear now."

"This is not up for discussion," Hudan said at once. "Your safety comes before anything. You are not leaving the manor until this threat is over."

Kivamus opened his mouth, then shut it.

Hudan was right. Kivamus hated that, but he was right.

"The village palisade should hold," Hudan continued. "But if they break a gate, the manor wall is our last defense for you and the children. Stay here."

That made Kivamus feel worse, not better.

If an adzee pack broke through one of the gates, the manor wall would protect him, Lucem, Clarisa, Syrene, and those inside. But most of the villagers were in the longhouse blocks. For a moment he pictured the crowded halls, the bunks, the doors, the people trapped inside while beasts prowled through the roads outside.

Then he reminded himself that they had prepared for this.

Everyone in the longhouse blocks had been instructed multiple times what to do if a horn sounded. Bar both gates from the inner side. Stay inside. Do not come out until the guards give the all-clear.

It would not make the buildings invincible, but the longhouse blocks had their own log walls and gates. They would keep people safer than running through the open village roads in panic. And thankfully, Tiranat was nothing like London. People here did not stay awake deep into the night. Sunset had been only around an hour ago, but most villagers would already be eating, finished with dinner, or preparing to sleep. The children would not be running outside on the roads the way they did before sunset.

Another horn sounded, shorter this time.

From the south again.

Kivamus looked toward the manor gate as more guards rushed in from the village. Only around a third of the full guard force lived inside the manor now. The rest slept in longhouse blocks until the barracks was constructed, and they came streaming through the manor gate in small groups, some still tightening belts or adjusting their leather armor as they ran.

They had been off duty at the time, but everyone knew that a hungry adzee wouldn't discriminate between someone who was on duty right now and someone who was not. This was the home of all guards, and every single one of them would gladly give their life to protect the village.

I did not make Kivamus feel better. He just felt helpless, knowing that if they had better weapons, they wouldn't have to fear people losing their lives even to those dangerous beasts.

"Captain!"

"What do we do?"

"Where are we needed?"

Hudan pointed without hesitation. "You four, north gate. You two, get crossbows from Hyola and spears from the servants, then follow Feroy to the southeast gate. You, stay here and help Kerel secure the manor gate. New recruits will stay inside to protect the manor unless Kerel orders otherwise."

Kerel was already organizing the remaining guards near the manor gate. He had a rough voice and a hard look on his face, and even the new recruits seemed to stand straighter when he spoke. He usually remained in the manor to defend the place while Hudan led the guards against enemies outside. Tonight would be no different.

Tesyb would normally have been with Hudan, but he was out of the village escorting Trevalo to Cinran. He wasn't expected back until tomorrow. Kivamus found himself wishing the young guard was here to help the captain, then pushed the thought away. Wishing for absent men would not help those present.

Duvas had already gone toward the servants' hall with Nurobo, the former fat archer who now served as the main medic. Savomi followed them with the other medics in training, her face tense but controlled. They were going to prepare boiled water, bandages, losuvil powder, and whatever else might be needed if injured guards started coming in.

Kivamus hoped it wouldn't come to that.

But he knew better than to depend on hopes and wishes.

Madam Helga stood nearby with Lucem and Clarisa behind her. Lucem had lost all of his earlier laziness. He was clutching Helga's apron with one hand and staring toward the gate. Clarisa stood half behind Gorsazo, trying to look braver than she felt. Gorsazo had one hand on her shoulder and was speaking quietly to both children.

"It will be alright," the teacher said. "The guards know what to do. We'll stay here with Madam Helga."

Another howl came.

Lucem flinched.

Clarisa pressed closer to Gorsazo.

Syrene stood nearby, but strangely, she did not look as frightened as Kivamus expected. Her face was serious, and her hands were clasped tightly together, but there was no panic in her eyes. She was watching the guards move, watching Hudan give orders, watching the manor shift into defense like she was trying to understand the whole of it. Like she was simply analyzing everything going on.

Maybe it was because of everything Syrene now knew about Earth.

After learning what humans had built there, what weapons existed there, what dangers had been overcome there, perhaps she had begun to see things differently, just like Kivamus did—not as something impossible to survive, but as another problem that could one day be solved by science and technology. In that outlook, her mind might now be closer to his than anyone else's in Tiranat.

Kivamus looked away from her and back toward the gate.

The village was still not safe enough. Not nearly. They had two scorpions mounted now, one on the northeastern watchtower and one on the southwest tower. That was better than before, but it was not enough to cover everything properly. The third and fourth were still being built by Darora and Cedoron. The barracks wall was only half complete. Too many trained guards were away with Trevalo, with Pydaso, at the encampment, or on hunting duties.

And now a full adzee pack might be circling them.

Feroy had told him once that an adzee pack could have half a dozen grown beasts, maybe more if young ones were with them. If the horns had sounded from north and south, then either the pack had split or there was more than one group near the walls.

Neither thought was comforting.

Hudan finished giving orders and started toward the manor gate with several guards behind him.

"Milord," he called over his shoulder, "stay inside the manor!"

"I will," Kivamus said.

Hudan gave a short nod, then ran out through the gate with the others following.

Kivamus watched him go.

The captain's broad back disappeared into the darkening village road, torchlight catching on swords, spearheads, and the metallic edges of shields as the guards followed. Kerel shouted for the manor gate guards to hold their positions until the other guards exited. More horns sounded outside, and somewhere beyond the village wall another adzee howled.

Duvas walked back toward Kivamus a few moments later. His steps were slower than usual, and his lips were moving. Kivamus realized he was praying under his breath.

The majordomo looked up briefly toward the dark sky. "May the Goddess protect us tonight."

Kivamus said nothing.

Gorsazo continued speaking softly to the children. Madam Helga had one arm around Lucem now, and Clarisa stood pressed close to her other side. Syrene remained near the doorway, still watching, still calmer than most.

Kivamus kept his gaze on the manor gate.

There was no guarantee that Tiranat would stand long enough for all the plans in his head to become real. Not with enemies all around them—beasts in the forests, mercenaries expected soon, Torhan still alive, and rapacious nobles like Zoricus waiting for any weakness.

But he could not stop.

He had to do his best for his people.

Early morning came slowly.

Kivamus sat in the manor hall, staring at the table more than anything on it. His eyes burned, and he had to cover another yawn with the back of his hand. Breakfast had already been served and eaten, but the food felt heavy in his stomach. He had barely slept, and from the look of the others, no one else had done much better.

Hudan sat nearby, looking just as tired. The captain had dark circles under his eyes and his shoulders had settled lower than usual. Feroy was not in the hall anymore. He had gone to sleep after breakfast, which was probably the wisest thing any of them had done since dawn. The man had returned from a long trip the previous evening, then spent most of the night at the wall, watching for adzees. By morning, he had looked dead on his feet.

Thankfully, there had been no attack.

That was the only good thing about the night.

After the horns sounded, Kivamus had waited outside the manor house with everyone else, listening to the howls and watching guards run through the gate. No one had been able to settle down properly. The servants had stood close to the walls, whispering. The maids had gathered near Madam Nerida. Gorsazo had stayed with Lucem and Clarisa, speaking to them quietly while the children kept glancing toward the manor gate.

After about half an hour, a guard had returned from the southwest watchtower and reported that two adzees had been spotted on that side. They had stayed near the tree line and had not come closer. Not long after that, another guard came from the northwest tower and reported almost the same thing, except that there had been three beasts on that side.

Three.

That alone had made Kivamus’s mouth go dry.

Adzees were not simple animals. They were wild beasts, yes, but they were smart enough to understand cover and danger. There was no chance they knew the range of scorpions, but they seemed to understand that the forest was safe and the open ground outside the village was not. So they had kept close to the trees.

Even from there, their howls had reached the manor hall clearly.

Later, another guard had come from the southeast watchtower, where Feroy had gone. He reported that at least one adzee had come within range of the scorpion there. A few of the beasts had come close enough to sniff around near another part of the wall, but Feroy had ordered the guards not to shoot unless the animals came much closer or clearly began to attack.

Kivamus understood the reason.

In the light of a crescent moon, hitting a moving beast with greyish fur would already be difficult enough. And if the shot only injured it without killing it—which was far more likely than getting a kill in a single shot—that might give the whole pack a reason to attack the village. Last time, it had taken a great deal of effort to kill just one young adzee. This time there were probably around half a dozen grown ones near the village. At least. Starting a fight in the dark, with only two scorpions mounted and most of the guards unable to see clearly, would have been foolish unless there was no other choice.

So the guards had waited and watched.

Eventually, the beast had wandered back toward the forest.

After a while, Hudan had sent word from the northeastern watchtower that the adzees had retreated into the trees on all sides. He had said an attack that night was unlikely.

But unlikely was not the same as impossible.

So the captain had kept the guards ready for anything. Kivamus had finally told Madam Helga to take the children back inside and put them to sleep. Gorsazo had gone with her. Syrene, Duvas, and Kivamus had remained outside for a long time after that, waiting for more news and hoping none of it would be bad.

That was how the night had passed.

Waiting. Listening. Hoping.

Only when the sky began to brighten above the Arakin mountains in the east had Hudan and Feroy returned to the manor. Feroy said there would be no attack during the day because adzees were night predators. The threat, at least for now, was over.

That had been about an hour ago.

Now it was around seven bells in the morning.

The manor hall was quieter than usual. Syrene had returned to her room after eating. Madam Helga and the maids had gone back to their work, though Kivamus doubted anyone in the manor would be moving with much energy today. Only he, Duvas, and Hudan remained at the table.

Duvas looked down at his cup, then yawned so widely that Hudan glanced at him.

“Sorry,” Duvas muttered. “I am too old for nights like that.”

Hudan snorted. “No one is the right age for nights like that.”

Kivamus rubbed his eyes and leaned back. “How are the guards?”

“Tense,” Hudan said at once. “Nervous too, though most won’t admit it. A few are angry. That is better than panic, but it can still make men careless if they think they can solve fear by shouting at it.”

“That includes women too now,” Duvas said dryly.

Hudan grunted. “Yes, and the female guards are on edge too. Less likely to shout, but just as likely to shoot too early if they are not careful.”

Kivamus nodded slowly.

The guards were one problem. The villagers were another.

“The villagers all heard the howls,” he said. “Even those inside the longhouse blocks. They wouldn't have left for work yet, but I'm not sure if they would even want to.”

“You’re right.” Hudan nodded. “They will be scared to go out today. I would be too, in their place.”

Duvas looked toward him. “You think the workers will refuse to leave the village?”

“Some might,” Hudan said. “Some will go because they are told to. Some will pretend they are not afraid. That does not make it safe.”

He straightened a little, as if forcing himself through his own tiredness.

“I think we should order that no one leaves the village today.”
