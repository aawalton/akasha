I continued to stare into the boy’s eyes as I repeated his words in my head, trying to make sense of them, but I did not. It felt as though I was missing something, like I still didn’t know enough about the world to understand what he was saying. I couldn’t access my memories yet, but something told me that even if I could, it wouldn’t really help. I’d be just as lost as I was already.

“Keep watch of what?” I asked.

The boy flinched. Pain and fear appeared to flit across his face, and he finally looked away from me, his gaze returning to the skies once more.

As soon as his eyes left mine, I mourned the loss. My heart felt hollow and empty without his attention fixed upon me. It was as if something crucial had been stolen from me, and I could not continue to exist without it. I couldn’t breathe, couldn’t live, without whatever it was that had been taken from me, and I needed him to look at me again, but he did not.

“I don’t know,” he admitted after a pause.

I wasn’t sure whether to be reassured or even more scared by his answer. I’d been half-expecting him to say something awful, as if there were some terrifying monster that could fall from space at any moment and kill us all if he looked away for more than a few seconds, but somehow, not knowing made it worse. It made me want to stare up at the sky too, to search for any hint of whatever it was that awaited us there.

But I couldn’t. My eyes refused to move away from the boy seated on the bench beside me, and I found myself filled with the need to know more about him. I wanted to know why he was in the Observatory and where he came from, but the words wouldn’t come out. There was so much I wanted to know about him, but I didn’t know how to coax the questions swirling around my head out.

I’d already asked him some of them, anyway. I’d asked him how long he’d been there and so many other questions, and he hadn’t answered them. Repeating myself would be foolish. It was unnecessary, and I needed to do better.

“Where were you,” I started, the question slipping out the moment I thought of it, “Before you came here, I mean?”

The corner of the boy’s mouth twitched up into a smile, but there was no humour in his expression, and his eyes did not find me again.

“Various places.”

I winced at the vagueness of his answer, immediately feeling the urge to dig deeper, to discover more, and I wasn’t sure why, but a strange, desperate hope fluttered in my heart. I knew I needed him to keep talking and tell me more, and, somewhere in the back of my mind, that scared me. It was distant, though. Easy to ignore and drowned out by the overwhelming desire to hear his voice once more.

“You travelled?” I prompted, unable to resist it. “Did you ever have a home?”

That time, the boy snorted. It was a quiet, almost derisive sound.

“Once upon a time, I did,” he said. “But that was long ago. I was chased from that home, and I did not find another. Not until I arrived here, at least.”

My eyebrows pulled together, and pain tightened around my chest at the thought of whatever the boy had been through. I didn’t know, not really, but I still found myself empathising with him.

“Why?” I could not stop myself from asking. “Why did they chase you away? Didn’t your parents try to stop them?”

He looked young. He must have lived with them at some point, and surely, they would have wanted to protect him from whoever it was that chased him away. My parents would have done so. Or at least, my dad would have. He tried.

Unless the boy didn’t have parents. The thought came to me too slowly. It was too late for me to take the words back, to never have voiced the question that slipped out before I realised how rude it might have sounded, and I stared at the boy, horror growing within me.

“No,” he said, his tone firm and unbothered, but I saw a muscle in his jaw twitch, betraying hidden pain. “They were part of the people who chased me away. I… scared them.”

Confusion washed over me again, and my eyes traced the boy’s face, trying to understand what he had said. I didn’t know him, I tried to remind myself. I had only just met him, so I was not in a good position to judge him, but I could not think of a single reason why anyone would ever be scared of him.

Perhaps I was wrong. He could have been hiding it well. Maybe some unexpected cruelness lurked beneath the surface, and he was just reeling me in, tricking me into trusting him, but that didn’t seem to be the case. There was a gentleness that lingered about him, an air of kindness that I couldn’t completely understand, but I knew in my heart that the boy would not hurt me. He would not hurt anyone.

“Why?” I asked softly.

A sigh slipped through his slightly cracked lips, and his chin started to turn towards me, but his eyes never left the sky.

“I don’t know. I have this… thing,” he began, trailing off and taking a deep breath before continuing to explain. “Knowledge. Divine or unholy knowledge, depending on who you listen to. I’ve always known things I should not, seen things people tried to conceal from me, and I did not learn to hide that until it was too late. They hated me for that. Everyone did.”

“Oh.”

That was all I could say. No other words would come out. My head was spinning, and emotions swirled within me, but I could not place them. They were too messy, too intense. I felt sympathy towards the boy, obviously. I felt bad for him, hated that people had been so unkind to him, but there was something deeper than that. Something else pulled at my heart, making it hard to breathe.

“I was young when they cast me out, too young to survive alone, but I managed. It wasn’t easy. I was scared and lonely, and I didn’t understand,” the boy said. “The rest of my childhood was spent being hunted and feared for this stupid thing I cannot control. I could never stay in one place long, had to run before people realised what I could do, what I was, and I feared that I would never be free, never be safe. Until I found myself here.”

The words rushed out of the boy, seemingly unsaid for far too long, and I was entranced. I couldn’t look away as he spoke, and the more he said, the stronger the ache in my chest became.

I understood it. I understood his tale far too well. It was almost as if I’d heard it before; the story was strangely familiar to me, but I knew I hadn’t. I had never met the boy before in any of my worlds. No one had ever spoken of him, not even in hushed, terrified tones, but I recognised what he had been through nonetheless. His story, his childhood, matched my own.

“Did they come after you?” I asked, the question slipping out. “Did they try to follow you?”

The note of desperation, of fear, in my voice was far too noticeable. There was no way the boy had not heard it, and I looked away from his face. Instead, my eyes scanned the tall walls surrounding the courtyard.

I was just checking to see whether someone would be able to climb them, I told myself. I didn’t want to have anyone sneaking up on me, a dagger clutched in their hand. That was all it was. It wasn’t that I didn’t want to see the boy’s reaction, the awareness of why I had asked, because I knew it would break my heart even more violently than it had been broken before.

But I had already eyed the walls. Before, when I was wondering about how I’d entered the Observatory, I’d assessed them to see whether I could have climbed over, so my gaze did not stay on them long. It flitted towards the sky, anxiously and hesitantly, and I noted that the sun had almost dropped beneath the horizon. The clouds were growing darker, more stormy.

“No. We’re safe here,” he told me. “I guess that’s a small price to pay, really.”

I swallowed down the sudden lump that had lodged itself in my throat and looked at the boy again.

“What is?”

His lips ticked up again.

“Eternal servitude for eternal safety, trapped here alone,” he replied. “Or… I thought that was the case, but now, you’re here.”

Something ignited in my heart. I felt a thousand butterflies take flight as something close to pride or happiness exploded within me. For a moment, I felt truly special. I felt wanted, and I hoped that I, too, could be safe there. I prayed that I would no longer need to run, that I would not have to constantly look over my shoulder in case I was being followed again.

The thought of that felt so wonderful, and the peace that he spoke of, the respite from fear. It filled me with such a longing so strong that I was almost certain it echoed across more than just the reality I was in. I could feel it wrapping around my heart, pulling me in tighter, and I did not resist. I wanted nothing more than to just stay there, where I would never feel fear again, and exist.

But the smile slipped from my lips as I took in the boy’s troubled expression. There was a catch, something more to the boy’s words that he did not quite say. I had missed something.

“Now I’m here,” I repeated cautiously. “What does that mean? Why is that important?”

The question caused the boy to finally look away from the sky again, and I felt my chest tighten as his star-filled gaze found mine once more. I could see the empathy within his eyes, the reluctance to say the words waiting on his lips, and I felt my hands begin to tremble. I laced them together, squeezing my fingers tightly in an attempt to stop them from shaking, but it did little to help.

I still didn’t quite understand, but I could feel it looming. The realisation seemed to hover just above me, waiting to pounce, but I didn’t want it to. It was inevitable, and I was aware of that, but it terrified me. I wished I could say ignorant forever, but I could not.

“I’m sorry,” he said quietly. “I would not wish this upon anyone, especially not you.”

His words made something within me break, and fear flooded my body. I could feel it coursing through me, driven onwards by the knowledge of what was to come, of what my future held.

Tears stung behind my eyes. The urge to sob, to grab the boy’s threadbare shirt and beg him not to do it, thrashed within me. I wanted to plead with him, to make him promise to help me change things, but I knew it would not help. My fate would not be changed. That had already been rewritten.

“So… I’m stuck here now?” I asked.

It was less of a question and more of a statement, though. I knew what the boy would say, and I didn’t truly want his confirmation. I just needed to ask it.

“I’m sorry.”

That was his only response. Nothing more. He did not provide any more details nor agree with my words, but the regret in his voice was confirmation enough, and that scared me.

“What happens—” I started, but my voice broke, and I could not continue. I had to swallow and take a deep breath before I was able to speak once more. “What happens if I try to leave? What if I just walk out?”

The boy sighed.

“Nothing,” he told me. “You cannot. Someone must always be here.”

He’d already said that once, so I should have seen it coming. I should have been expecting that answer, but I wasn’t, and it caused my heart to sink as the hope I’d been feeling moments before drained out of me. How foolish I was to believe I could have been free anywhere. I should have known better. Freedom was not an option for me.

“But what if I try?” I whispered.

I glanced towards the sky, and, out of the corner of my eye, I saw the boy do the same. It was like we were scared that someone would have overheard my question, that they would appear and smite me down, but nothing happened. The only thing that awaited us in the skies above were the thick clouds.

“You’ll find yourself here again,” the boy said, lifting one shoulder in a shrug. “Someone must keep watch, and even if it were possible, why would you? It’s not safe out there. Not for people like us.”

His words sent a shudder through me, and a question pulled at my mind. What would be better? To stay in the Observatory and endlessly watch the skies, like the boy had said, or to risk being out there again?

I truly wasn’t sure. My memories were starting to become clearer. They were starting to reveal themselves to me, and that made me realise that the boy was right. The world beyond the Observatory would always be a danger to me. It always had been. No matter how hard I tried to act normal and hide what I knew, people always figured it out eventually.

It never took long for someone to catch on. Sometimes I slipped up, but most of the time, they just realised that something about me was not right, that I was hiding a secret from them, and then I had to flee.

That was how it went every time, and I was tired. I was exhausted, and I did not want to do that for the rest of my life. I was young still, although I did not feel it. I had spent too much of my time running away, and I yearned for a place where I did not have to. But I did not want to spend my days like the boy, waiting for the skies to change, whatever that meant.

I almost asked the boy. The question reached my lips, almost slipping out, before I caught myself. There was no point in asking him. I doubted he would answer me, and I had time. If what the boy didn’t quite say was correct, if it was truly my turn to stand watch, I’d figure it out. Sooner or later, the answer would reveal itself to me. I had an eternity, after all. An eternity until someone else came along to take my place.

But there was one more question. I had one last question that I needed to ask the boy.

“Why me?”

It was a pitiful question, and it came out quietly. The boy gave no indicator that he had heard me, and for a moment, I feared that he had not heard it and that I would need to repeat myself, but then his eyes filled with tears. He blinked rapidly, seemingly trying to chase them away, but his gaze remained watery.

“I don’t know,” he muttered, his voice not much louder than my own had been. “I’ve asked the same question too many times, but I’ve never found an answer. Maybe you will.”

Something about the way he said that made me assume I would not, though. There was a note of sad acceptance in his voice, as if he knew that I’d torture myself with the question, and never get any closer to understanding.

I looked away, my eyes floating towards the dark clouds ahead before settling on the stone wall before me. I could feel the boy’s gaze still burning into me, but for once, I did not want to meet it. I did not want to see the empathy scattered amongst the stars his eyes held.

“Maybe,” I agreed halfheartedly. “What about you? What will you do now? Will you leave?”

Despite the fear swirling in my heart, I found myself feeling concerned for him. I barely knew him, had met him minutes before, but I knew he would die if he left. The crowd of people who had hunted him down centuries before had long since died, but more would take their place. There would always be some who wished to see him dead, just as they did me.

“Do not fret,” he told me with a warm smile as he began to push himself up from the bench. His movements were slow and stiff. Pain wracked his body, making it impossible for him to straighten fully, but he managed to make it to his feet and turn towards me on unsteady legs. “I’m… I’m sorry this happened to you.”

I felt my lips pull up in a sad smile as I met his gaze once more.

“I’m sorry it happened to you,” I replied, my apology genuine.

His smile widened slightly, and he bent. For a moment, I thought he was falling, that his body had given up on him, but then I realised what he was doing, and my eyes fluttered shut as he pressed his lips to my forehead.

They were dry. I could feel how dehydrated and parched he was, but I still enjoyed it. Even though he was just kissing my forehead, the gesture felt strangely intimate, and I felt myself leaning towards him, not wanting the moment to end, but too soon, he pulled away.

I longed to say something as he straightened once more. I wanted to ask his name, to ask him whether he would be okay, but it was too late. He had already turned away from me, and all I could do was watch as he walked away, his movement slow and shuffling.

He did not make it far. He had only managed a few steps before his knees gave way, and he crumbled to the ground beside a pile of sun-bleached white sticks that must have fallen from a tree somewhere out of sight.

Fear thrashed within me, and the urge to rise from my bench and rush to his side gripped my heart. I needed to go help the boy, to make sure he was alright, but I could not. Instead, my eyes were drawn to the skies, and I could not resist it. I could not look away from the sight above.

The clouds were just as thick as they had been the last time I looked, but I could see something else now. A hint of something peeked through the darkness, and I felt myself leaning forward as I strained to see more. Before my very eyes, the skies lightened.

I could not blink as the clouds cleared from my vision. They were still there. If I focused on them, I could still see them, but they no longer obscured my sight of the infinity that stretched out before me.

My head spun as I gazed at the stars scattered across the sky, realising that I could see them all. Each twinkling light, each dot hanging high above me, seemed to call out for my attention, and I gave it to them freely. A sudden realisation crashed into me, and I did not look away.

I would willingly spend eternity watching over the stars.

Something slammed into me. My body jolted, and I was thrown from the world. Panic exploded in my heart, and I stared at the skies, trying to get one last look at the wonders before my vision turned black.

My body was hit again. Something solid threw itself against my leg, and my heart pounded as I blinked, trying to force my vision to clear again. I had no idea where I was, when I was. How much time had passed since I first sat down on that bench, and was it the same in all worlds? It had felt like moments, but it could have been longer. Years could have passed in the blink of an eye, and I could have missed decades of my life, I realised as I sat there, willing myself to see.

Finally, some things came into focus. I could see Rex lying against my side, still shuffling slightly as he tried to get comfortable, but I could barely make him out. His dark fur made him little more than a shadow in my already dark room.

I did not remember switching my light off, though. Anxiety leapt within me, and my gaze darted towards the ceiling, checking whether it was on. The bright glow burnt my eyes, and I recoiled away, squeezing them shut as pain shot through my head. I pressed my hands to my eyes, rubbing them hard in the hopes that would clear the haziness that was clouding my vision, but it did not work. My room remained dark.

Fear gripped me, and I threw myself forward. Carefully, I tried to climb over Rex, but his black fur blended into the duvet cover below, and I did not see his paw until my hand had already touched it.

“Sorry!” I yelped, despite not having put any weight on him.

He did not look up. I think his eye opened, and he glanced at me in confusion, but I could not see it.

I kept moving, scrambling to my feet and beginning to hurry towards my mirror, but after a few steps, I stumbled to a stop. My eyes flicked towards the window. I could feel the sky crying out for me, and longing seized my heart.

It almost happened. I almost gave in. My feet began to move before I caught myself and shook my head, forcing away all thoughts of the sky and what I might see out there. Clenching my hands into fists, I continued across my room towards my mirror and stared at my reflection.

The clothes I wore were the same I’d put on to take Rex for a walk. I could just about make out the colours, and that soothed my heart a little, but the relief did not stay for long. My gaze found my eyes, and I stared at my reflection as horror spun within me.

My vision was slowly clearing, just as the skies had above the Observatory. The dark, inky blue that had eclipsed my irises was gradually fading away, the shade returning to my usual colour, but that wasn’t the source of my fear. It was the smattering of golden flecks that seemed to linger behind.
