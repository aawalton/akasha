Ela woke to the sound of her own stomach.

It was a familiar sound, a gnawing, rhythmic growl that had become the background music of her life. She curled tighter under the thin, scratchy blanket, trying to hold onto the last shreds of sleep, but the cold seeping up from the dirt floor wouldn't let her.

She opened her eyes. The hut was dark, lit only by the grey, filtering light of dawn creeping through the cracks in the wooden walls. Dust motes danced in the shafts of light, swirling in patterns that reminded her of snow.

She sat up, her movements slow and deliberate. Every bit of energy was precious. She was five years old, but her eyes held the hollow, haunted look of someone who had lived a hundred years in a famine.

She reached under her pillow—a bundle of rags—and pulled out her treasures.

There were two of them.

The first was a small, crudely carved wooden dragon. It was worn smooth from handling, its wings chipped, its head lopsided. It was the only toy she had ever known.

The second was a box.

It was small, made of a dark, polished wood that felt warm to the touch, unlike anything else in this freezing hut. It was ornate, with silver inlays that formed patterns she didn't understand.

Nanny Lue had given it to her. Nanny Lue, with her kind, wrinkled face and her hands that felt like dry paper.

Keep it hidden, little bird, Nanny Lue had whispered, her voice a rattle in her chest. It is your legacy. It is your safety.

Ela traced the symbol on the lid of the box—a silver wolf, howling at a non-existent moon.

Nanny Lue was gone now. The sickness had taken her three months ago. The sickness that came when there wasn't enough food to keep the body warm. Ela remembered begging her to eat the last crust of bread, crying as the old woman pushed it back into Ela’s small hands.

You must grow strong, little bird. You must survive.

Ela clutched the box to her chest. She was alone. The only sound in the hut was the wind whistling through the gaps in the wood.

She stood up, her legs trembling slightly. She had work to do. Berries. Roots. Maybe a beetle if she was lucky.

She walked to the fire pit in the center of the room. It had been cold for days. She scooped up a handful of grey ash.

Hide your hair, little bird, Nanny Lue had said, her eyes fierce. Your hair is moonlight. It is dangerous. Never let them see it.

Ela didn't know why silver hair was dangerous. The other children in the village had brown hair or black hair. Hers shone like the stars. But Nanny Lue had been afraid, and fear was a contagion Ela understood well.

She rubbed the ash into her hair, dulling the shine, turning the silver to a dirty, unremarkable grey. She smeared some on her cheeks for good measure.

She looked like a ghost. A dirty, hungry ghost.

She tucked the wooden dragon into her pocket and hid the box back under the loose floorboard beneath her sleeping mat.

She stepped out into the village.

…

The village of Oakhaven was a skeleton. The houses were slumped and rotting, the streets empty of laughter. The Cinderfall soldiers had come through a week ago, taking the last of the grain tax. They had left nothing.

Ela walked with her head down, trying to make herself small. Invisibility was safety.

Then, a sound tore through the morning silence.

A roar.

It wasn't a beast. It was deeper, louder, a mechanical scream that shook the ground beneath her bare feet.

She looked up.

A shadow fell over the village. A massive, dark shape descended from the clouds, blotting out the sun. It looked like a giant bird made of iron, with six wings that spun and howled.

The villagers scattered, screaming. "Monster! Sky-beast!"

Ela didn't run. She was frozen, her eyes wide. The thing was terrifying, but it was also… beautiful. It was sleek and sharp, like a knife cutting through the sky.

It landed in the field just outside the village, its wheels kicking up a storm of dirt and dead grass. It groaned to a halt, the engines winding down to a high-pitched whine.

The nose of the beast opened. It hinged upward like a jaw, revealing a dark, cavernous throat.

Ela crept closer, hiding behind a rain barrel.

Metal men marched out. They were tall, black, and silent, their single blue eyes glowing in the gloom. They carried crates—heavy, wooden crates that thudded onto the ground.

Then, three figures walked out behind them. They weren't metal. They moved like people, but they were dressed in strange, dark armor that seemed to shift and blur.

They walked away from the crates, heading toward the forest path that led to the capital city. They moved fast, their strides long and purposeful.

Ela watched them. And then, right before they reached the treeline, they vanished.

They didn't walk into the shadows. They just… disappeared. One second, they were there; the next, empty air.

Ela rubbed her eyes. Ghosts?

"Citizens of Oakhaven!"

A voice boomed from the metal beast, amplified by magic. It was a man’s voice, deep and calm.

"We bring supplies from Lord Wight. Food. Medicine. Blankets. Come and receive your share."

The villagers stayed hidden. They knew tricks. They knew traps.

But Ela’s stomach growled again, a painful, cramping twist. Food.

She looked at the crates. The metal men were opening them.

She saw silver packages. She smelled something… incredible.

It smelled like Nanny Lue’s stew, from before the bad times. It smelled like meat and salt and warmth.

Her legs moved before her mind could stop them.

She stepped out from behind the barrel. She walked toward the metal giants.

"Ela! No!" a neighbor hissed from a window. "Come back!"

She ignored them. The smell was pulling her like a hook in her belly.

She reached the crate. A metal man—an automaton—looked down at her. Its blue eye whirred, focusing on her small, ash-covered form.

It didn't strike her. It reached into the crate with a pincer-like hand and pulled out a silver packet.

Another figure walked down the ramp. A person. He wasn't wearing a helmet. He had pointed ears and skin the color of twilight. A Dark Elf.

He knelt in the dirt, bringing himself to her eye level. He held a steaming bowl in his hands.

"Here, little one," he said softly.

Ela stared at the bowl. It was full of thick, brown stew with chunks of real meat. Steam rose from it, curling in the cold air.

She reached out with trembling hands. She took the bowl. It was warm.

She took a bite.

Flavor exploded in her mouth. Salt. Fat. Savory richness. It was the best thing she had ever tasted. It tasted like life.

She ate quickly, shoveling the food into her mouth, not caring about the grease on her chin. She ate until the bowl was clean, licking the spoon.

"More?" the elf asked gently.

She nodded, tears pricking her eyes.

He gave her another bowl. And another.

The villagers, seeing that she wasn't dead, began to creep out. They approached the crates like starving wolves, wary but desperate.

The soldiers began handing out the silver packets. They organized a line.

"One week's rations for every family," the elf announced.

Ela got in line. She clutched her empty bowl to her chest.

When she reached the front, the elf looked down at her. He had a datapad in his hand.

"How many in your household, little one?" he asked.

Ela froze.

She thought of the empty hut. The cold fire pit. The silence where Nanny Lue used to hum.

She looked at the crates. There was so much food.

A lie formed on her lips. Two. My grandmother is sick.

If she said two, she would get double. She could hide it. Save it. She wouldn't have to be hungry for… weeks.

She looked at the elf. He had kind eyes.

She looked at the villagers behind her. Old Miller, who shared his bark with her. The woman with the baby who cried all night.

If she took extra, would there be enough for them? Would someone else’s Nanny go hungry?

Ela swallowed the lie. It tasted bitter, but right.

"Just me," she whispered. "Just Ela."

The elf stopped. He lowered the datapad. He looked at her, really looked at her, seeing past the ash and the rags.

He saw the loneliness. He saw the bravery of a starving child telling the truth.

"I know what it is like," he said quietly, his voice thick with emotion. "To be hungry. To be alone. To think the world has forgotten you."

He reached into a separate crate and pulled out a heavy, woolen blanket. He wrapped it around her shoulders. It was thick and warm.

Then, he handed her a large sack filled with silver packets.

"This is for you," he said. "It's extra. Don't worry. We have enough for everyone. The Lord Commander made sure of it."

Ela stared at the sack. It was heavy. Heavier than she was.

"Why?" she asked, her voice trembling.

The elf smiled. It was a sad smile, but full of promise.

"Because no child should have to be brave about being hungry," he said. "We will be back next week. And the week after. You are not forgotten, Ela."

He stood up and ruffled her ash-covered hair.

"Go home, little one. Eat. Be warm."

…

Ela dragged the sack back to her hut. It was a struggle, but a good struggle.

She closed the door and barred it. She lit the fire pit, using the wrapper from her first meal as kindling. The magical heating element in the packet sparked easily, and soon a small fire was crackling, chasing away the shadows.

She sat on her mat, the heavy blanket wrapped around her. She opened the sack.

Packets of stew. Packets of chicken. Packets of bread that smelled like yeast and honey.

She picked one up. She traced the symbol stamped on the silver foil.

A roaring lion.

She frowned. It looked familiar.

She reached under the floorboard and pulled out her box. The treasure Nanny Lue had given her.

She set the box next to the food packet.

On the box: A silver wolf, howling.On the packet: A silver lion, roaring.

They weren't the same. But the style… the way the silver lines curved, the proud lift of the heads… they felt like brothers.

She remembered Nanny Lue’s words, spoken in the fever dreams of her final days.

The Wolf guards the Lion, little bird. If you ever see the Lion… give him the box. He will know.

Ela looked at the food. She looked at the blanket. She felt the warmth of the fire.

The Lion had fed her. The Lion had sent men of metal and elves of twilight to save her.

"The Ghost of Wight," she whispered to the empty room.

She touched the wooden dragon in her pocket.

"I will find him," she promised Nanny Lue’s ghost. "I will find the Lion Lord. And I will give him his box."

Outside, the roar of engines shook the sky as the metal bird took flight, heading back to the clouds.

Ela wasn't afraid anymore. She had a blanket. She had food. And she had a mission.

For the first time in a long while, she fell asleep not to the sound of her own hunger, but to the crackle of a fire and the full, heavy feeling of hope.
