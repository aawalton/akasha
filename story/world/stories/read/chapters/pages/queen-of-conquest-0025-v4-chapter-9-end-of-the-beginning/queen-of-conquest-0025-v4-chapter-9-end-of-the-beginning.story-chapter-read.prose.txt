The influence of whoever—or whatever—had been manipulating the wolf-beast vanished the instant my Authority cut in.

Interesting. I pulled my gaze from Caleb and peered into the darkness where the strange energy had fled. A large part of me wanted to abandon the caravan entirely, just to hunt it down.

I shifted my weight to my other leg and grunted. No, I couldn’t leave the knights to die just to satisfy my curiosity. These weren’t the hardened veterans I’d fought beside on Ordite. They were fledgling swordsmen, weak despite whatever titles this fragile kingdom had bestowed on them.

“Gareth, Henderson, watch your flank!” Marcus barked, waving frantically toward the rear of the encirclement. “Brianna, Iris, your left! Caleb, fall behind the front line!”

Each order shifted the battle’s flow like chess pieces sliding across a board, easing two dozen knights through an overwhelming tide of monsters. It was as if Marcus saw everything from above, every position, every opening, every danger.

Nearly two hours passed before even his energy began to fray. At least a third of the knights were too injured to stand, let alone fight. I released my Authority again, still thin enough that the knights wouldn’t sense it, but strong enough to drive back anything else creeping toward us.

“This is the final wave!” I called from the top of the carriage, twirling my finger in a circle. “Wrap it up.”

The knights who could still fight gave me nothing but exhausted glares. I grinned and waved. A few groaned in reply. Marcus’ jaw tightened, the corners of his mouth twitching upward in the faintest hint of a tired smile.

The last wave crumbled faster than expected—perhaps from the monsters’ waning morale now that their earlier commander had vanished, or perhaps from Marcus’ constant barrage of orders carving discipline into the green recruits. Either way, the battlefield soon stilled, broken only by ragged breathing and the twitching of cooling corpses.

I dropped from the carriage roof, boots crunching through the ribs and matted fur of a wolf-beast underfoot. “Not bad,” I said, shaking blood off my boots. “Collect as many beast cores as you can in the next half hour, then we continue for Elyndor.” I glanced at the sun through the canopy, thin slivers of gold piercing the green gloom. “We’ve less than a day left. One last push, and you’ll be free of my training.”

A few chuckles answered me, though more than half retained expressions of resolution. Before the knights moved, Marcus rose from the beast corpse he’d been sitting on.

“My lady—”

I cut him off, already knowing what he and the knights wanted to ask. “Consider this a tryout for my personal guard. If you’re interested, speak to Dralos once we return to Elyndor.” I gestured toward the Draconian, who had spent the fight circling the perimeter, watching. Marcus saluted, and the subordinate knights swiftly followed suit.

I kept my sphere of Authority steady. For the entire half hour spent collecting beast cores, not a single beast dared approach.

“I don’t remember Tharnals and Trolls being that strong,” Dralos said as we rode in the lead carriage, watching the knights reform ranks once the time had lapsed.

I flipped my now-full storage ring into the air, catching it flat in my palm. “We were targeted.”

His head snapped toward me. “Targeted?”

“Possibly bad luck,” I said, though I didn’t believe it for a moment. “I sensed something out there. I don’t know Pike Forest’s monsters well, but the one whose energy met mine was… wrong. Mid-tier silver core at the very minimum. It was reaching into the beasts and pumping more energy into them. More than they should have had."

Marcus joined us as the caravan started moving again, wiping blood from his brow as he took position on the outside of my open window. “Apologies, my lady, but I overheard. Do you plan to hunt that creature down?”

I glanced over the battered knights, half covered in cracked armor, all bearing faces so pale and exhausted they looked as if they might collapse on the spot. Then I looked ahead toward the last stretch of black forest looming like a wall of Nothingness. “I might return one day,” I said at last. “But for now, we continue.”

Marcus’ shoulders sagged with relief as Dralos’ furrowed brows relaxed, his frown smoothing into a wry smile.

Did they truly think me reckless? I arched a brow at the two. I hadn’t survived decades of continental wars in Ordite by charging headlong into danger without thought. Still… perhaps I’d been more impulsive since arriving here.

I brushed the thought aside after a moment of reflection.

Though it was true I'd taken more risks, Graedon had been nothing but life-threatening situations from the moment my soul found Lilliana. Boldness had kept me alive. Again and again and again. Without it, I’d be dead. Or worse, shackled for years as some duke’s apprentice.

In this world, risk was sometimes simply part of survival. That was my reality. Always had been. My father had taught me that long ago. One could not conquer without the risk of being conquered.

Still, my need for risk-taking in Graedon had recently diminished as I set the foundations of my power in place. Now I had the luxury of caution. My power, both in core energy and loyal followers, gave me room to breathe. Time to plan. Time to grow.

The past six months since I’d slain the Hydra had proven it. The Scarlet March, though I’d scarcely taken part in it myself, was tearing the corruption from the Alistar Dukedom with ruthless ease. In my territory, no one dared, or could, stand in its way. Once my foundation was stronger than steel, I would push beyond my borders and into the rest of the continent.

Finally.

“My lady?” Marcus asked, snapping me from my thoughts.

“Tell the knights that if we arrive before midnight, I’ll reward them with as much food and drink as they can hold.”

A grin spread across his face. He bowed, turned, and carried the message down the line. A few cheers rose, quickly swallowed by grunts and heavy breathing from the knights pulling the carriages with renewed determination.

I turned to Dralos. “Guard my body,” I ordered. I knew the mindscape within Orpheus’ core ran faster than reality, but I had no idea by how much. In Sealrite, I’d fought Orpheus for decades in my mindscape, only for hours to pass outside. But my Will was abnormal.

My first time in Orpheus’ mindscape hadn’t been witnessed, so I’d never learned how long I’d been gone. The second time, during the Hydra battle, was even less reliable. He’d sped up time deliberately, stripping my memories as payment. I’d considered returning since then, but every time my mind even brushed his core, my soul recoiled like it had touched poisoned fire. I’d told myself I would wait until that unconscious trauma dulled.

It hadn’t.

Still, I was no coward. I would face him again. Hopefully, this time, I wouldn't die again.

Now is as good a time as any, I thought, grimacing. I didn’t want to see him, but no one else could answer even a fraction of the questions gnawing at me.

A dull flash lit my storage ring, and Orpheus’ dense black core appeared in my hand. The knights pulling the carriage shouted as the sudden weight dragged them back.

“Oops,” I muttered, condensing a sliver of heart energy around the core, lifting it into the air and easing their burden.

“Is that… its core?” Dralos whispered, his voice almost reverent. His golden-bronze eyes gleamed with emotions I couldn’t name.

“Yes.”

“How much time will pass?”

I shrugged. “You can tell me when I return.” Those were my last words before I reached out and touched the core.

The instant my fingers brushed the core, the world shattered. Sound imploded into silence. Sight fractured into a thousand shards of blinding light before folding inward, collapsing into a suffocating abyss. My stomach lurched as if the ground had been ripped from beneath me, and I was pitched headlong into a darkness so thick it felt alive—pressing in on me from every side, swallowing breath, thought, and self.

Then, without warning, the darkness burst apart. A spear of white-gold light pierced through my skull, detonating inside my mind with an almost physical force. The mindscape didn’t emerge so much as it slammed into me—every sense flooded at once, too much, too fast. A raw scream tore itself from my throat, unbidden, the sound swallowed by the vast nothingness until it rang hollow in my own ears.

Some part of me wondered why I was experiencing pain when I hadn't before, but the concern was quickly drowned and buried.

Time lost all meaning. I floated in a haze of burning pain until, slowly, the agony ebbed to a dull roar. My vision sharpened in fragments—blurs hardening into shapes, shapes into edges, edges into impossible detail.

Above me, starlight spilled in cascading silver beams, each one so bright it painted the world in ghostlight. There was no moon. Instead, a canopy of constellations stretched endlessly overhead, more vivid than any sky I had ever known. They weren’t random clusters but precise patterns—ancient symbols, intricate diagrams, and beasts I half-recognized from myths whispered in forgotten corners of memory. The stars themselves seemed to breathe, their light shifting and pulsing in time with some unseen rhythm.

The city unfolded around me in quiet majesty. It was not hemmed in by walls, but sprawled open and proud, as if no threat could ever breach its heart. Pale stone and midnight glass rose together in sweeping harmony—towers like spears of light, domes gleaming with soft reflections, colonnades etched in silver script that flowed and re-formed as though alive. The markings shimmered like liquid mercury, shifting into new meanings with every blink.

Some of the structures—if they could be called buildings—floated several meters above the ground, their slow, graceful rotations mirroring the arcs of planets in the night sky. Lamps hung in the air beside the street, each cradling a quill-shaped flame that danced without flicker, casting a warm, steady glow that softened the hard perfection of the city’s geometry.

When I tilted my head back, my breath caught. High above, colossal bridges of hammered gold spanned the distance between the tallest towers. They pulsed with rivers of silver light, the glow crawling through them like veins carrying some titanic lifeblood. Even from here, I could feel the hum of their power vibrating through the air, pressing faintly against my skin.

The ancient rumble of Orpheus’ voice met me with its familiar gruffness. “Back for more, girl?” I flinched at the sharp crunch of his teeth sinking into an apple, the sound dragging up unwelcome memories. The progenitor sat on an old wooden bench, dressed in a ragged brown tunic and tattered black pants, a stark contrast to the opulence surrounding us. Almost as if he and the bench were outside the memory interposed atop it.

"That hurt," I groaned, pressing the side of my head.

Orpheus clicked his tongue. "You've been spending too much time in Mindscapes."

"It's been half a year," I retorted.

Orpheus rolled his eyes. "That is long only to you. Not to your mind. Come back in a year from now, and we will begin your real training."

"No," I stated. "I am here now, and you will answer my questions as promised."

He tsked. “No agreement exists compelling me to answer all your questions. You may ask about the System, mana, or absorbing a core, and I will give you an answer. Other topics…” He shrugged with a lazy, unbothered smirk. “I might.”

“Tell me about the memory you made me live through,” I said. “And where in Ashwash's name are we?"

Orpheus finished his apple, then tossed the core behind him with a casual flick. A long silence stretched between us before he spoke, his voice softer than I’d ever heard it. Almost vulnerable. “That was a very long time ago.” He gave me a thin, knowing smile before turning his gaze skyward, to the stars scattered overhead. “Have you ever wondered what lies beyond this world?” I raised an eyebrow at him, and he chuckled. “Right, right. Different world.” After a pause, he continued. “Like you, I am not from here.”

“No shit.” He shot me a glare, and I shrugged, settling down on the bench beside him.

“I come from the Learned world of Arkon. Not born there,” he amended. “I was born on Idonite. You saw it in my memories. The Sainthood trials took me to Arkon, and I never went home.” He gestured broadly toward the sprawling cityscape. “There, knowledge of the System is as common as information about Ashwash is in your world. It is our god.”

“But what is the System, exactly?”

His shoulders lifted in a shrug. “As I told you before, the System acts like a parasite, feeding off the energy of those connected to it to survive.”

"That is how it behaves," I pressed. "But what is it?"

He shrugged again. “That’s well beyond my understanding.”

“But you’re one of its progenitors,” I said, narrowing my eyes.

“It has thousands like me scattered throughout the System Worlds,” Orpheus said, eyes returning to the stars. "And millions of our first-generation spawn. I believe Graedon refers to them as Beast Kings."

“If there are so many, then why was it so focused on turning me into a progenitor?”

He laughed, a low, rumbling sound. “It doesn’t want you to be just any progenitor. It wants you to be the original Marzana.”

I blinked, realizing with a flush of chagrin that I’d never asked what a Marzana was. “What is a Marzana?” He jabbed a finger at me. I swatted it away. “Answer me, Angellic.”

He sighed. “I do not have the complete answer you wish for. There exist only a few ancient writings in my world that discuss the species. What I can tell you is that the Main System has tried to create this species before with... imperfect results."

“Wait. What? What does that mean?" My head reeled. Imperfect results?

Orpheus rolled his eyes. “The unLearned are hopelessly clueless.” He inhaled deeply. “Remember, the System can grant new species to individuals.” I nodded. "Systems can also create new species outright. Usually, just accepting the System alters your species slightly. Did you not notice that in your Paragons?”

“Primals…” I muttered, recalling the term from my examinations of the Paragons.

“I don’t know what that means, but yes. I’m Angellic — an Angellic Progenitor. Similar, but not quite the same. My descendants, however, are Angellic. They carry a diluted version of the System’s original bloodline.” He waved a hand dismissively. “The Marzana, though, are a rare entity born from the violent convergence of opposing energies. Take you, for example.” He glanced sideways. “If you were a natural Marzana, you would have been born from a powerful clash of Necromantic and Lunar energies merging in a newborn’s soul. Marzana embody the cycle of life and rebirth.”

“The Soul Transference,” I whispered, realization crashing down like a stone wall. “Is that why I could perform Soul Transference? Because I’m a Marzana?”

Orpheus squinted up at the stars. “Not necessarily, but perhaps.”

“Can you move between worlds without Soul Transference?” My eyes took in the strange city around us, my mind spinning with possibilities.

“The System links worlds it controls,” Orpheus said, voice dry and blunt. “Arkon had gates to other places. Fortunately, both Ordite and Graedon are uncontrolled. Even progenitors can’t simply be shipped around. Moving a progenitor into an uncontrolled world costs the System dearly. It’s happened only a handful of times that I know of.”

“The Demon Progenitor,” I said.

“And me,” he replied, smiling without humor.

“Fortunately?” I repeated questioningly, my tone doubtful. It felt rather unfortunate.

He looked at me, dead serious. “Both worlds would be quickly and easily enslaved otherwise. There are very, very old cultivators in some System Worlds who wield power you cannot yet fathom."

I blinked, stunned. “I… what?”

His eyes remained locked on mine as he spoke. “Lilith, you grow stronger with each passing day. When you reach the Diamond Realm, your power will exceed anything you once held on Ordite. But mark my words, the Diamond Realm is only the end of the beginning.” He finally tore his gaze away and looked back to the stars above. “In the future, you may be faced with a powerful threat that convinces you of the need to accept the Main System and become its Marzana Progenitor. It would, after all, grant you great and immediate power. However, remember this, Queen of Rot. Shortcuts are never free. You will forevermore border the edge of True Convergence."

A flood of questions crashed through my mind, too many to voice all at once. Before I could speak, Orpheus continued. “To become a progenitor is to be locked at the peak of Diamond. You may claw forward bit by bit by consuming other progenitors, but you will never cross the threshold into True Convergence.”

“What is True Convergence?” My heart thundered inside my chest like a thousand war drums. My eyes widened, my breath caught in my throat as I struggled to grasp the meaning behind his words.

Orpheus shook his tangled black hair and let out a sharp tut. “You are not ready to learn of that. Reach the Diamond Realm first, and then we will speak.”

Without warning, a snap shattered the air, and pain exploded behind my eyes like a volcano tearing itself apart. The agony consumed me and, in an instant, I was abruptly ejected from Orpheus’ mindscape.
