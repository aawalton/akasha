Chapter 53

“The Lattice distributes resources along optimal pathways. When a node operator redirects flow for localised benefit, the downstream deficit compounds exponentially. The Lattice does not distinguish between incompetence and malice. Both produce the same structural failure.” - Codex of the Long Watch, Watcher commentry

Donna’s leg was a problem Arthur couldn’t solve with his hands.

The compound fracture sat beneath the skin of her left shin like an incorrect sentence he could not rewrite. She was on a cot in Azzopardi’s medical tent, which was a canvas sheet stretched between two poles with a folding table underneath it. She had not made a sound during the carry. That silence was louder than anything she could have said.

Archie had been rotating above the folding table since they arrived, facets brightening each time they passed over Donna’s leg. His geometry tilted toward the tent canvas once, dismissed it, and returned to the injury.

“Archie,” Arthur said. “The leg.”

“Fourteen to eighteen days for passive recovery. Accelerated healing via external Nadi Matrix channelling would reduce the window to two to four days.” The personality dropped a register. “The practitioner at the southern compound has the highest healing throughput I have recorded in a human subject. Three weeks of sustained, high-volume practice. The volume is unusual for voluntary development.”

Nobody spoke. Azzopardi’s runner had gone south forty minutes ago. Forty minutes was too long for a twenty-minute path.

“She’s not coming,” Donna said. Flat. Looking at the tent ceiling.

“She’ll come,” Arthur said.

“Azzopardi sent the runner. The Baron decides whether she goes. Azzopardi made a request, not a demand, and the Baron hasn’t finished calculating what we’re worth yet.”

Arthur didn’t argue. She was probably right.

"Protection economies have been the dominant human organisational model for approximately eleven thousand years," Archie said. "The novelty is not in the model. The novelty is that your species keeps being surprised by it."

They heard voices outside. Maltese, rapid, then a woman’s voice cutting through it in English, “I need to see the injury before I can tell you anything. Move.”

The tent flap opened.

She was shorter than Arthur had initially thought. Hazel eyes that took in the cot, the patient, and the room without moving her head. A fighter followed her in and positioned himself by the tent flap with the practised stillness of a man who had done this escort before.

The woman’s eyes went straight to Donna’s leg. She read the injury as Arthur read cracked foundations. The assessment was running before she had taken a full step inside.

She knelt beside the cot without introducing herself, her hands stopping a centimetre from Donna’s shin. A hesitation so brief it could have been careful positioning.

Donna saw it. Arthur saw Donna see it.

Then the woman’s palms made contact and her face dropped into focus. Her fingers moved along the swelling with the precision of someone reading braille, each millimetre of contact carrying information her hands had been trained to translate.

“Compound fracture, mid-shaft tibia. Twelve millimetres of displacement, fibula intact. The periosteum is torn but the blood supply is holding.” She said it to herself, not to the room.

She looked at Donna. “I need to reduce the fracture before I can heal it. That means pushing the bone ends back into alignment through the swelling, and you’re going to feel it.”

“Do it,” Donna said.

“I wasn’t asking permission. I was giving you a second to brace.”

Her hands moved. Donna’s jaw locked. The tent was silent except for the sound of Donna breathing through her nose in controlled bursts.

The woman looked at Donna with a faint smile, then nodded and said, “Good.”

The violet luminescence was faint at first. A glow beneath the woman’s palms that seeped into Donna’s skin, tracing the injury with a precision that made Steve lean forward. The light followed the fracture line, concentrated where the bone ends met.

“The bone is setting,” the woman said. “Don’t move for twenty minutes. If you stand on this before tomorrow, you’ll undo everything I’ve done and I’ll have to start from scratch, which means twice the Reserve for the same result. So don’t stand on it.”

“I wasn’t planning to dance,” Donna said.

The woman’s mouth moved. It might have been the beginning of a smile. It was gone before it formed.

She worked in silence for several minutes. Under the skin, the swelling was visibly diminishing, the geometry of the shin straightening by degrees as the bone realigned.

“How does it work?” Steve asked, leaning further forward. Now that Donna was stable his curiosity was peeking through again.

The woman didn’t look up. “I put my hands on the injury and the bone knits. That’s the summary.”

“I mean the mechanism. When you make contact, is it a template you’re projecting, or is the Ether reading the existing biology and—”

“I’m not reference material.”

Silence. The guard by the tent flap moved his weight from one foot to the other.

She exhaled. Her hands stayed on Donna’s shin. “I’m sorry. That was uncalled for. What do you want to know?”

Steve matched her volume. “The luminescence stays where you put it. It doesn’t diffuse. That suggests directional channelling. Are you guiding it consciously?”

"Yes. I can feel the bone ends through the swelling. My medical training tells me where they should be. The Ether follows the instruction." She moved her hand position. "A healer without training could accelerate the repair. But they would either burn through too much Ether or knit the bone wrong, because the template would be imprecise. Mine isn't imprecise."

“So the system rewards knowledge. Technical knowledge.”

“I don’t know what that means. I know that when I understand the injury, the healing is faster and cleaner. Draw your own conclusions.”

“The practitioner’s observation is consistent with Nadi Matrix optimisation,” Archie said. “Channelling precision scales with the operator’s conceptual resolution of the target system.”

The woman raised her head and looked at Archie for the first time. She studied him like an instrument that had started offering diagnoses.

“It talks,” she said.

“He talks,” Steve said. “Usually less helpfully than that.”

“My diagnostic output is within normal parameters. I cannot account for the operator’s processing limitations.”

The woman went back to Donna’s leg. “I have three of those at the hospital where I trained. They’re called blood pressure monitors.”

Archie’s geometry brightened a fraction. He did not respond. For a being who had opinions on tent canvas and camp food, the silence was interesting.

Donna made a sound that was almost a laugh, and then winced.

The healing session lasted thirty minutes. When the woman withdrew her hands, her shoulders dropped by a degree that spoke of spent Reserve. She stood and glanced at the guard before turning to leave, a quick check she probably didn’t know she was making. He nodded, and she walked out.

Donna watched her go. “Sarah,” she said. “Azzopardi called her Sarah.”

* * *

Donna closed her eyes. The swelling around her shin was visibly diminishing in real time, but the colour was still wrong, and the line of her jaw had not unlocked.

Arthur sat on the edge of the cot, on the side of her good leg.

“I’m not going to die from a tibia,” Donna said without opening her eyes.

“I know.”

“Then stop sitting like I am.”

Arthur didn’t move. After a moment she found his sleeve with two fingers and held it. She did not open her eyes.

Steve was at the tent flap, watching the clearing. He was running his thumbs over the edges of his blistered palms with the absent rhythm of a man who needed something to do with his hands and had nothing safe to do with them.

“The practitioner’s Ether output was metered,” Archie said. “Each second of contact corresponded to the minimum Reserve expenditure required for the volume of damage present. She did not pour it; it was measured.”

Steve turned. “So she’s efficient. That’s a good thing.”

“That is one possible interpretation of the observation.”

“What’s another?”

“That behavioural signature is what I observe in operators who have been trained to budget. The training is acquired across thousands of repetitions under throughput pressure. It is not a developmental stage that occurs voluntarily.”

Outside, somebody was shouting in Maltese. The sound was urgent.

Three goat-variants had wandered too close to the camp perimeter. Arthur handled two on the shield while Steve dropped the third. The skirmish lasted two minutes. The goats came up the slope from the south, jaws too long, eyes pearled white. Steve’s thermal stream, channelled at lower output than the Mother had needed, burned the third’s chest cavity from the inside. The Reserve cost was minor.

Arthur walked the settlement as the sun dropped. They hadn't seen all of it, and the forced downtime had given him a moment to breathe. Donna mentioning that he was being a mother hen might have factored into the walk as well.

He had expected a survivor camp. Though it was definitely that, it was also a functioning economy.

What seemed like two hundred people lived in improvised shelters along the ridge. A makeshift market sat between two ancient walls. A woman sat behind a stone slab trading dried meat for cores. Two small cores bought a week of food.

Near the eastern edge, a man approached two of the Baron’s fighters with a basket. He counted out five cores and pushed them across. One fighter made a mark on a piece of cardboard. The man received a canvas sack in return and left without speaking. Sanctum zone temple tax. The price of living inside the safe zone the Baron hadn’t built, but controlled because he had arrived with forty armed men, and nobody had told him he couldn’t.

A woman with a boy of maybe eight or nine approached the same fighters. She held out a core. The fighter checked the cores against his cardboard tally and shook his head and said something in Maltese. The woman spoke back, faster, one hand on the boy’s shoulder. The fighter shrugged and turned back to his pouch.

Further along, someone had scratched tallies into the limestone. Rows grouped by fives. Dozens of families. Steve was studying it.

“That’s not a ledger,” Steve said, pointing at the marks. “That’s a company store.”

He saw Arthur’s expression and continued, “Nineteenth century. Mining towns. The company paid you in tokens you could only spend at their shop, at their prices. You could never earn enough to clear what you owed. The debt was the design.”

He traced a row of tallies with one finger. “The mining companies in Appalachia used to print their own currency. Scrip. You could buy anything at the company store except a ticket out of town. Different century. Same arithmetic.”

Arthur looked at the tallies. What he saw was weight concentrated on the weakest members of a structure while the strongest bore less and less. The design would fail. The question was when.

He thought about the queue in the courtyard at Wied iż-Żurrieq. The civilians who had waited longer than the fighters. The teenage girl with the compress against her jaw who had watched two armed men jump her in line and had said nothing because saying something cost more than the wait.

Two miles north, the same arithmetic was operating on a different face. The compound’s queue had been the visible version. The settlement’s tallies were the same equation written in older notation.

“This isn’t the Baron’s invention,” he said.

"No," Steve said. "The Baron is reading something somebody else wrote. The company store is older than the apocalypse. It goes back to the Industrial Revolution and an awful lot of Welsh coal pits, and it's worked the same in every iteration."

Arthur watched a fighter wave a family past the table without checking their tally.

"But the queue is older than the company store." Steve tapped the limestone. "The queue is older than any organised settlement that ever held a scarce resource and decided who got it first. Domesday Book. Sumerian temple grain stores. The first time human beings stood in a line, somebody at the front decided who ate."

“Sumerian grain store is obvious enough, what’s doomsday?” Arthur asked.

“Domesday, not Doomsday. William the Conqueror's tax survey in 1086. The Normans wrote down every field, every plough, every pig in England, so they'd know exactly what to extract. People called it Domesday because there was no appealing what was written in it."

“Somebody at the front decides who eats and who waits long enough that being told to wait stops feeling like an injustice and starts feeling like a fact.” Arthur nodded.

Steve looked at him.

“That’s the design,” Arthur said.

“Yes,” Steve said. “That’s the design.”

Behind them, Archie’s geometry drifted at the edge of the clearing. He had not commented on the debt tallies, the core economy, or the gap between the fighters’ haul and the civilians’ hunger. The silence was conspicuous.
