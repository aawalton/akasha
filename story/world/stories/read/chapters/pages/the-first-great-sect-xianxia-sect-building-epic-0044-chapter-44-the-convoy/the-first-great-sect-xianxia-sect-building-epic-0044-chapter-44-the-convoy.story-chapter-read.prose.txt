Hua was given some truly bewildering news upon her return to the convoy. The kind that made her consider checking her food and drink for poisons. It was unlikely that Liu Xin had managed to poison her—if anything, the mix of a goofy smile, twitching muscles, and his surprisingly common missteps, Hua had poisoned him. It was a good sign. His body was reacting to the concentrated Qi of the pill. Qi tended to move from areas of high density to low density, his unfilled dantian would be naturally inclined to reach for the mass of Qi currently releasing in his gut.

It may still go wrong and he might have a permanently malformed dantian that could not retain any degree of Qi, but Hua had faith. She remembered that boy in the ditch, forgotten in a dank corner of Liaojiangkou, lifeless eyes staring at a starless sky, but with a functioning dantian, and a meridian that shone bright enough for the empty skies. If he could reach Qi Condensation alone, through nothing but talent, then surely Liu Xin could do it with resources and guidance.

And if he couldn’t, he would still be valuable for dealing with mortals for whatever remained of his lifespan. Maybe another forty or fifty years in which Hua would not have to deal with mortals because of him.

Which brought Liao Hua to her current issue. One which Liu Xin could have dealt with if she’d left him behind—possibly to be killed if Weiji’s poisonous glare was indicative of anything—to translate the nonsense mortals tended to speak.

The lesioned mortal, the leader who spoke for the group, was on his knees, forehead pressed to the ground.

“This lord suspects he misheard you. Please, repeat what you have said. He will not consider anything you spoke prior against you.”

The mortal repeated his request. Hua looked at her hands. They were moist from spending time in her riding gloves. If she crushed the mortal’s skull without her gloves, she would have blood on them. But she also didn’t want to ruin her riding boots with mortal blood by stomping his head flat. She could stab him, there was always that option. But it would unfortunately not provide her the visceral satisfaction of killing him with her hands.

Hua sighed. He’d live a while longer.

“You mean to suggest this lord travel days in the wrong direction to meet your convoy because it might be under threat,” she said wearily, trying to reconcile the difficulty she was facing in travelling to one location. “An idea that comes from a suspicion that you cannot confirm. After this lord personally killed twenty brigands just four days ago? Wasting my time is an offence I don’t treat lightly.”

“Lord, I promise you, Kang is a true monster. Please, whatever reason you got for being here, he’s going to oppose you. He’ll defy your rule, defy your clan, defy all that you stand for.”

“And you know what I stand for now?”

“Please,” he begged.

She looked to the Imperial Scribe whose name Hua still could not remember. “What is your opinion on this matter?”

The scribe did not flinch with his entire body. He stopped it before it spread from his arm. “Meeting these villagers would give an accounting of this Kang’s actions before we reach Coaltown. An accounting that will be useful in the case against the Justice should it be necessary.”

To Weiji, she asked the same question. Her cousin said, “Kang’s presence is an insult to your rule. He must be made to submit, one way or the other. I recommend the blade if he has some honour. We can burn him alive if he is little more than a feral animal.”

That viciousness surprised her less than it would have only a month ago. Her other advisors would want to help, Captain Yao for the security threat that Kang would present and Liu Xin because he was painfully mortal and sympathised with them.

The journey would take many miserable days. After three wet hours on the first day, they would be forced to stop, and not because the roads were too poor to travel. By the time the commotion reached Hua, it was already too late.

One of their wagons had its wheel pop off after a vicious pothole the driver failed to avoid and it drifted into another. That created a cascading effect of chaos as donkeys, carts, mortals, and horses all went in different directions in the rain and mud. The news Hua received after order was restored was not hopeful. One needed a wheel replaced and another an axle fixed.

Besides the carpenter they had brought along, the carts were being worked on by two siblings originally from a family of woodworkers who’d found Tempering in their craft, and realised there was greater money in being a soldier under Liao's employ. Weiji explained he’d spent some time making sure they had joined the convoy.

If not for her cousin’s foresight, this already miserable trip wouldn’t have made it out the gates of Liaojiangkou. The other convoys must be in utter chaos without him.

Whilst they waited, Weiji insisted she get her arm checked by Young Ren. Hua vaguely listened to Young Ren’s scathing advice as she looked at the Ranger. The Ranger had been washed, revealing a woman of no small beauty. Sweat beaded on her forehead, cheeks flushed with colour as she slept with fever. There were flecks of blood on her shoulder bandage where an arrow had been pushed through her body. The rise and fall and rise again of her chest was a tantalising sight she struggled to look away from.

Her interest was only natural. The Ranger would have answers about Kang’s danger on her red lips. Waiting for her to awaken was merely a sign of Hua’s impatience for something exciting to happen.

“Once more, I cannot say, my lord,” Young Ren answered tiredly for perhaps the fifth time that day.

Hua eventually left, willingly that time, because she had an important meeting with Captain Yao about matters of grave importance—

“My lord, we can continue this meeting later. I suspect you have more important matters on your mind,” Captain Yao said pointedly.

Hua pulled her gaze from the south where the healer’s cart was. “No, please continue. You were just mentioning the…” The unkind captain did not fill in the blank for her. “It’s like you said. I agree fully with your suggestion.” Still nothing. “And we’ll go along with that plan.”

The captain stood and began rolling up his map. “I will provide Deputy Weiji a report to summarise for my Lord. Please, excuse me.”

On the one hand, that was terribly rude. On the other, she was impressed he had taken a stand for something.

Following the mortal’s directions was difficult when they lacked the ability to cut across fields and ford small lakes that would need to be added to maps. Journeying across marshland formed after a depression became waterlogged by the constant rains was not something Hua would risk, not when her convoy was struggling already.

On the fourth day, they came across the location that mortal assured them would hold their people. Both Hua and Weiji could tell long before they reached it that they would find no one. Hua nearly had all the mortals killed then and there.

If not for finding a yellow marking on the trees, it would have been a grisly end for the mortals. It was a script in no language Hua knew.

“Can you read this?” she asked in the oddly muggy air, offering them a final chance to redeem themselves.

“Yes,” the lesioned mortal said with relief. “Yes, I can.”

“What language is it?”

“Not a real language. At least, not one we can speak. Just directions and distance and some warnings. But I can tell you where the convoy moved to.”

Another detour, taking them further away from the path to her original destination. We might see Coaltown in Spring at this rate. It was a moderately popular path, worn down by frequent use, heading deeper into the forests to the north. Unlike the main road, which whilst damaged in some places let them move quickly along undamaged sections, this path was always slow.

That night she spent at one of the outdoor fires. It was close to the healer’s tent, which Hua did not know, and she most certainly was not stealing glances any time one of Young Ren’s assistants entered or exited the tent.

Weiji played his dizi, his hair taking on a red hue from the crackling fire. He had always enjoyed performing it. Today, though, his choices seemed very deliberate.

“The moon is in the east / And that lovely girl / Is inside my door. She is inside my door / She treads in my footsteps / and hastens away.”

It felt rather like being poked. The healer’s tent was technically to the east. Hua finally pulled her gaze away from the tent, feeling a flush crawl up her neck. There was no end to the amusement on the faces around her. All vassals or relations to the clan. All trustworthy. She still felt painfully exposed.

“Our lord likes the warriors,” one soldier said.

“To the warriors,” another said, raising his cup of wine.

The toast was repeated and they drank. Hua drank her wine well. To her shock, it didn’t feel… cruel. Just humorous. Even Qing looked amused.

The last time Hua had travelled in a group so large, she had broken the Flying Lotus School over her knee. That journey had been filled with close family. It was a quieter trip, Hua performing a role as an honourable Young Mistress, one she had thought natural, but the easy ribbing and loud drinking around a fire… she found it suited her.

“Another,” she called with a grin and her cup was refilled with hot wine.

“Perhaps my lord should slow down.”

“Perhaps my deputy should learn to keep quiet and enjoy himself.”

That roused another cheer and wine flowed freely.

In the morning, Hua was miserable and ready to die. She had drunk far too much, enjoyed the loose nature of the world too much, and forgot to purge her body of alcohol.

She was much like those cheaply made jiangshi in the plays she sometimes snuck into, shambling and awkward, instead of fast and graceful. The servants who brought her hot water to bathe were far too loud and the clang of her spoon against her bowl of rice porridge felt like someone was driving a sword through her skull. Hua looked at her sword and considered it. For a long moment, she truly considered the relief it might bring.

I will not let alcohol defeat me.

“Greeting, my lord!” said Weiji cheerfully the moment she left her tent. Hua winced which only made him smile more. “I hope my lord slept well and had a welcome morning!”

“Please, Cousin Ji, favourite cousin of mine, don’t do this to me.”

“When will I ever have an opportunity to cause you half as much suffering as you cause me!”

Hua winced, clapping her hands over her ears. Which somehow made the pain worse. “What do you want?” she asked miserably. “Just tell me and I’ll do it.”

“I am simply doing as you said and enjoying myself!”

She just barely managed to avoid being sick. That was the only good thing to come out of the morning. Her still-unnamed horse was the only kind creature in existence. Hua leaned her forehead against his neck and just stayed there for a few minutes.

“You’re the only kind thing in my life,” she told her horse who chuffed.

The rhythmic plodding of the horse did not help with her hangover.

Weiji rode close to her and was obnoxious. “What’s wrong, my lord? Did you perhaps have too much merriment last night?”

Even his stupid horse was obnoxious, whinnying loudly just to be evil. Hua was contemplating ordering a halt to the entire convoy and just sleeping the rest of the day. Or maybe just to have Weiji executed.

They were forced to dismount during the final stretch of the journey. The road ended partway into the forest and the path was muddy, filled with stones, and too narrow to trust. Hua did not want to risk another accident.

“Who leads your people?” Liu Xin later asked the lesioned mortal as they journeyed on foot.

The mortal with lesions looked at Liu Xin with a smile. Had he made friends with these mortals already? Or merely pacified them as one would a wild animal?

“Wu Yan took up the job,” he said.

Hua only barely managed to stop from tripping and only because Cousin Weiji in front of her came to a complete stop. She hit her nose against the muscles between his shoulder blades. Her head throbbed like an earthquake, strongest around her nose, spreading outward in rings of agony that eventually engulfed her head. It was an ache unlike any before.

“You are led by a shaman?” Weiji asked.

Hua walked by Weiji with a hiss, nose scrunched, indifferent to the matter of this Shaman Yan. She elbowed him in the side, glad when he wheezed. Her eyes were watering from the pain of hitting his back. Weiji had a deceptively hard spine. And Hua was already in pain.

“Yeah,” the mortal said, unaware of the danger he was in. “Little Yan saw we needed help and stood to lead us.”

“Your shaman, was he the one who petitioned the Mayor?”

“Yes, she was.”

“Well, now this lord knows why the mayor refused to help you,” Hua said slightly nasal. Hua was starting to hate Physical Cultivators. They made her feel delicate. “He would have been hung for treason.”

The mortal shot her a confused look that he passed on to Liu Xin. “I don’t understand.”

“It is a matter of this Province’s history. In the last great war, the Yellow Caps were allied by those who practised Wuyu, those named Shaman.”

“Don’t be seeing the problem.”

“Ignorant mortal,” Hua muttered, blinking away the bright spots in her vision. Her stomach churned and she valiantly kept herself standing.

Liu Xin looked between them and answered for her. “The Yellow Caps stood opposed to the Cultivation Clans of the Amber Sea. There may have been genuine grievances that some of them held, but it was uncovered that the Yellow Caps were funded and organised in part by the Eastern Sanctuary. A foreign force acting against the authority of the Dragon Throne. For that crime, by Imperial mandate, all practitioners of Wuyu were considered traitors of the state and marked for death. Your shaman, if a true practitioner, would be hung. Should be.”

“Not necessarily. The mandate has not been enforced since before the Yongtai Emperor’s reign,” the Magistrate’s scribe said, “but that is largely due to the sheer lack of practitioners still alive. The validity of the mandate would be judged on a case-by-case basis by the lord Governor.” Then, remembering he was surrounded by Liao Cultivators, he added, “or a tribunal of the Great Clans who would know best how to handle them.”

“What that have to do with anything? All sounds like it happened long before any of us or our grandparents were born.”

Liu Xin’s smile strained. “The Lord Liao’s father is the Radiant Lightning Body who stood against the Yellow Caps.”

The lesioned mortal squinted at Hua, nearly tripping over a tree root. “You don’t look much older than me. Guess it must be like the stories say about Cultivators living long lives. You must be what, a hundred years to care about that old war.”

“It is my history,” she hissed, slipping out of formality. It was a miracle she had managed it so long with her head pounding.

“Everyone’s got a history. Old words ain’t worth dying for. History didn’t mean nothing when we had to flee our homes.”

And she neither had the interest or ability to continue that conversation. It took most of her focus to continue through the forest. It was only in the very depths of the forest that they found their refugee convoy.

Hua spotted their sentries long before they noticed her. Civilians in ragged clothing. Underfed. Barely armed. Poorly suited for this work.

The lesioned man brought his hands to his mouth and blew. Birdcall emerged from his cupped hands. From the forest came an answering call. It went back and forth a few times until villagers emerged from the trees.

They were young, Hua noticed. No elders.

There were wary yet excited greetings passed between the mortals. They were so loud, their voices shrill. Hua focused on breathing. Let Weiji and Liu Xin deal with communicating with the mortals as they were led to a more central clearing.

What had been a trickle soon revealed itself to be hundreds of mortals. A true tent city had sprung up in the forest. The clearing wasn’t natural but the work of the villagers, the timber used to erect shelter.

“This might be a bigger problem than we thought,” Weiji said softly, too close to Hua’s ear to be anything but an arrow through the skull. She practically leaned against him and let him lead her wherever they needed to be.

I am never drinking again, Hua thought miserably, closing her eyes. At least Weiji was taller than her. He could draw some attention for a while.

“We moved because we caught signs of a battle,” a mortal said, holding the lesioned man with desperate hope. “I feared you were dead. What happened?”

“Just a little lost. Came across the Liao lord.” He gestured at Hua and her company then. “They promised to help out.”

Had she made that promise? Hua’s head was pounding too badly to remember.

“Who amongst you is the leader?” the Scribe asked. Backed by his panoply of Imperial soldiers, the forgettable man had an air of fearsomeness. Good. It meant Hua wouldn’t need to speak for a while more. “I am the head scribe of Magistrate Shiu of Liaojiangkou. I would hear your case.”

“I think you be looking for me.”

Emerging from the depths of the forest, yellow cut across the sky. Sunlight as fabric smeared across the green canopy. A triple-ringed staff whose jangling rings were louder than an entire convoy. A face painted straight out of the ancient scrolls with a presence that carried a weight to it.

Before Hua stood her ancestral enemy.

A shaman.
