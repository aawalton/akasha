The mud outside the service gates of the Cinderfall Palace was black, slick with grease and sewage. It was the only place in the city that smelled of food—not fresh food, but the sour, cloying scent of fermentation and rot.

Hundreds of people were pressed against the iron bars. They weren't rioting. They didn't have the energy for that. They were waiting.

A young father, his face gaunt and streaked with soot, held his daughter on his shoulders to keep her out of the crush. She was asleep, her small head resting on his grimy hair. He stared at the inner courtyard, where the servants were clearing the preparation tables for the royal feast.

"They have to throw it out soon," a woman next to him whispered. Her lips were cracked and blue. "The wedding starts in an hour. They need the kitchens clear."

"Unless they feed it to the hounds," the man muttered.

"The hounds eat better than us," the woman said. It wasn't a complaint. It was just a fact.

The heavy wooden doors of the kitchen opened. A group of servants emerged, carrying large buckets. The crowd surged forward, a low, desperate moan rising from their throats. It was the sound of animals, not men. They pressed against the cold iron, hands reaching through the bars, begging for the refuse of kings.

The servants didn't dump the buckets. They stopped, looking confused. A horn blew from the upper battlements—not a dinner signal, but an alert.

"Move back!" a guard shouted, striking the bars with his spear butt. "Clear the area! No scraps tonight!"

The moan turned into a wail of despair. The father lowered his daughter, hugging her tight as she woke up crying. No scraps. Not even the garbage.

"Look!" someone shouted, pointing not at the kitchen, but at the massive projection crystals mounted on the palace walls.

The crystals, which had been displaying the golden phoenix crest of the Hegemony, flickered. A harsh screech of static tore through the air, silencing the weeping crowd. The golden light died, plunging the square into darkness.

Then, a new light bloomed.

Deep sapphire blue.

…

High above the cloud layer, the command bridge of The Aegis was a hive of silent, frantic activity. The holographic table was a swarm of blue lights, hundreds of them, moving in formation across the dark map of the continent.

"Time to target: two minutes," Valen reported, his voice tight. "Approaching the drop zones at the terminator line. The sun is just starting to crest the eastern horizon."

I stood at the central console, watching the data stream. Beside me, Nyxia gripped the edge of the table, her knuckles white. She wasn't looking at the map; she was looking at the live feed from the capital square, at the faces of the people pressed against the gates.

"They look like skeletons," she whispered. "My father said it was bad, but... Alarion, they were fighting over buckets."

"We're fixing it," I said, though my own stomach churned at the sight. "Tes, override the broadcast towers. Now."

I glanced over my shoulder. The bridge was tense, but in the corner, a small island of domestic chaos remained.

My mother sat at a small table, sipping tea with a hand that trembled slightly. My father stood behind her, his hand on her shoulder, his eyes locked on the screen.

And on the floor, Lyra was sprawled across a rug. Kaelus, in his thirty-foot form, was somehow curled up small enough to fit next to her, lying on his back like a giant, scaly dog. Lyra was vigorously rubbing his belly, her small hands lost against his cosmic scales.

"Who's a good cosmic terror?" she cooed, scratching a spot right between his front legs. "You are! Yes, you are!"

Kaelus’s leg twitched. A little to the left, he projected into my mind, his voice sounding embarrassingly blissful. Yes. Right there. This is dignified. I am a Prince.

I turned back to the screen. "Drop commenced."

…

In the capital square, the blue light solidified. The image of the roaring silver lion burned onto the screens, crisp and defiant.

Then, the image changed. It wasn't a speech. It wasn't a noble demanding fealty. It was a picture book.

Simple, animated diagrams played on a loop.

Panel One: A silver package falling from the sky under a white canopy.Panel Two: A person picking it up. Tearing the top.Panel Three Pouring water—dirty water, rain water, it didn't matter—into a marked pouch.Panel Four: Steam rising. A hot meal was poured onto a plate. Roast chicken. Vegetables. Bread.

The loop repeated. Again. And again.

For fifteen minutes, while the guards screamed at the mages to cut the power, the starving people watched the instructions. They memorized the steps. They saw the steam in the animation and felt a phantom warmth in their hands.

"It's a trick," the father at the gate whispered, shielding his daughter's eyes from the blue glare. "Cruelty. Mocking us."

"Wait," the woman beside him said. She pointed up. "The sun is rising."

But it wasn't the sun.

The eastern horizon was brightening, the first grey light of dawn bleeding into the sky. And against that pale canvas, thousands of black specks appeared.

They grew larger. Dark, angular shapes that roared with the sound of a thousand thunderstorms. The C-52 Strategic Bombers.

Panic rippled through the crowd. "Air raid!" someone screamed. "They're going to kill us!"

The people scrambled, trying to run, but the square was too packed. They cowered, covering their heads, waiting for the fire.

The bay doors opened.

Thousands of crates tumbled into the void.

For a second, they fell like stones. Then, with a collective snap that sounded like a whip crack across the heavens, the parachutes deployed.

The sky bloomed. Ten thousand white flowers opened in the dawn light, drifting down slowly, gently. It wasn't fire. It wasn't death.

It was food.

…

A crate landed with a heavy thud in the mud right next to the service gate. The impact splattered the father with black slime. He flinched, curling around his daughter.

Silence followed. No explosion. No heat.

He opened one eye.

The crate was wood, sturdy and plain. The lid had popped open on impact. Inside, silver packages glittered in the dawn light.

He stood up, his legs shaking. He walked toward it. The guards were too busy staring at the sky to stop him.

He reached in and grabbed a packet. It was heavy. Solid. He saw the lion crest stamped on the foil. He turned it over. The instructions from the screen were printed on the back.

Water.

He looked at a puddle of rain and muck near his feet. He looked at the packet. Any water, the pictures had implied.

With trembling fingers, he tore the top open. He scooped up a handful of water and poured it into the outer pouch.

Hiss.

The sound was loud in the quiet square. A plume of white steam shot out of the vent. Heat—instant, shocking heat—radiated through the foil, warming his frozen fingers.

"It's hot," he whispered. "It's... magic."

He waited, counting the seconds as the diagram showed. Then he tore the inner pouch open.

The smell hit him. It wasn't the rot of the garbage buckets. It wasn't the metallic tang of ozone.

It was beef. Rich, savory beef stew with carrots and potatoes.

He fell to his knees. He didn't have a bowl. He poured a little onto the clean side of the foil wrapper.

"Eat," he choked out, holding it to his daughter's lips.

She took a bite. Her eyes went wide. She swallowed, then opened her mouth for more like a baby bird.

"It's real!" the father screamed, his voice cracking, tears cutting tracks through the soot on his face. "It's food! It's real food!"

The cry broke the dam. "The Lion! The Lion feeds us!"

The crowd surged. They ignored the guards. They ignored the fear. They tore into the crates, landing all over the city—on rooftops, in alleys, in the royal gardens. People were laughing, weeping, and shoving packets into their pockets.

Strangers helped each other pour water. The square was filled with the hissing of heating elements and the smell of hot food.

…

In the north, where the terrain was too rough for parachutes, the delivery was more direct.

A Kantonov K-225 screamed over a mountain pass, its six engines howling. It banked hard, lining up with a flat stretch of tundra outside a remote mining village.

The massive plane touched down, its landing gear kicking up a storm of snow and ice. It groaned to a halt, a beast of metal resting in the white waste.

The nose hinged upward. A ramp slammed down.

The villagers, huddled in their furs, watched in terror as metal giants marched out. Automata.

But they didn't carry guns. They carried crates.

A figure walked down the ramp behind them. It was a Dark Elf officer, his helmet removed, his silver hair blowing in the wind. He didn't draw a weapon. He held out a loaf of fresh bread.

He walked up to the village elder, who stood shaking with a pickaxe in his hand.

The elf knelt—actually knelt—in the snow, offering the bread with both hands.

"By the order of Lord Wight," the elf said, his voice amplified by his suit. "We bring supplies. Grain. Medicine. Blankets. No payment is required."

The elder lowered the pickaxe. He took the bread. It was still warm from the ship's galley.

"Who is this Lord?" the elder asked, his voice rasping in the cold air. "Is he a merchant?"

The elf stood up, smiling. "No. Just a man who hates to see an empty table."

The K-225 didn't linger. As soon as the cargo was offloaded, it roared back into the sky, leaving a village that would not starve this winter.

…

Back on the bridge, the sun had fully risen on the screens.

I watched the blue dots light up the map behind the red line of my advance. Every dot was a village fed. Every dot was a loyalty shift.

My mother walked over to me. She didn't say anything at first. She just wrapped her arms around me from behind, resting her chin on my shoulder. I could feel the dampness of her tears on my uniform.

"You really are your father's son," she whispered. "But you're my boy, too."

I leaned back into her embrace, the tension finally bleeding out of my shoulders.

"Did it work?" I asked, looking at the tactical display.

Nyxia stepped up to the console. Her eyes were red-rimmed, but she was smiling—a real, unmasked smile.

"The Hegemony garrison in the capital has stopped trying to confiscate the food," she reported. "The soldiers... they're eating it too. They're sharing it with the people. The chain of command is broken, Alarion. You didn't just feed them. You showed them that their King would let them starve, while their 'enemy' would not."

"Good," I said. My voice was thick. "Good."

I looked at Lyra. She had fallen asleep against Kaelus's belly, her thumb in her mouth. The dragon was watching the screen with one eye open, a low purr vibrating in his chest.

Not bad for a bread bomb, he projected sleepily.

I turned to the comms officer.

"Signal the fleet. Return to base and prepare for the next run."

The bridge, usually a place of cold calculation, felt warmer. It wasn't just the machinery. It was the feeling that, for once, we hadn't just won a battle. We had done something right.
