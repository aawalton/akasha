Chapter 115

They rode two days without stopping longer than to change the pace of the horses.

The road from Hoshin ran north through the low country and by the middle of the second day it had begun to climb. The trees thinned to scrub. The scrub thinned to bare rock. On the second afternoon the rain came in and did not stop. It fell steady and cold on the wrapped shape lashed across the trailing horse, and by the time the mountains rose properly around them the wool blanket had soaked through and darkened to almost black, and the cord holding it had swollen and drawn tight against the shape of what it held.

They came up the switchbacks in the last of the light.

The road cut back and forth across the face of the mountain, a shelf of stone no wider than two horses at its narrowest, the drop on the outside falling away into cloud. They rode single file where it narrowed. They did not look down. The rain came harder as they climbed, and the wind came with it now, driving in off the ridges, and by the time they made the last long straight run at the gate they were wet through to the skin and the horses were blowing hard from the climb.

The gate was set into the mountain itself.

It had been cut back into the rock face as a deep arched recess, the iron of the doors hanging in the recess and standing twice the height of a mounted man, and above the doors and to either side of them the mountain went on rising, dark and vertical and blank. The towers of the citadel showed higher up as thin cuts of blacker stone against the darker stone of the peak. From below the eye did not always find the citadel at all. It read as mountain until it began to read as wall, and then the wall did not end where the wall might have ended but went on up into the rock as if the two had never been separate things.

The gate opened for them.

Two guards worked the iron rings on the inside and the doors came in slowly against the weight of them, and the three riders passed under the arch without slowing and without speaking. The guards took one look at the wrapped shape on the trailing horse and did not challenge them. The doors began to close behind them before the tails of the horses had cleared the arch.

Inside the outer wall the lower town began at once.

The streets ran up the slope, cut in tight switchbacks of the same black stone as the mountain, roofed close on either side by houses that had been built against the rock and into it. Torches burned in iron claws at the doors. The rain came down between the houses in narrow sheets. Citizens stood back from the road as the horses came up through it, drawing into doorways, drawing carts against walls, lowering their eyes. No one spoke to them. They did not need to be told what the shape on the trailing horse was. In the citadel a shape lashed across a horse in wool meant one thing only, and it was known.

The riders did not slow through the lower town.

They took the switchbacks at the same hard pace they had held on the road, and the sound of the hooves ran ahead of them up the streets and off the stone walls and up under the eaves and did the work that a herald would have done at a lower gate. By the time they came under the inner gate word had gone ahead of them. The inner guards were already at the rings.

The inner gate opened onto the yard.

The yard was a floor of black stone cut level into the mountain, and roofed only by the rock above it. The mountain came down on three sides in unfinished wall and on the fourth side went up in a long broad stair carved into the rock, wide enough for a marching company, worn shallow in the middle by centuries of feet.

A boy was waiting at the foot of the stair with a wax tablet.

He took one look at the horse and the wrapped shape and turned and went up the stair without speaking, and the three riders swung down and handed the reins to the stablehands who had come up from the yard doors. They lifted the shape off the horse between them and carried it to the foot of the stair and waited.

Word had run ahead of the boy.

Word ran through the citadel the way water ran through stone, along paths worn deep by long use, and by the time the boy came up the last of the stair and into the corridor that ran back into the mountain toward the mage's quarters a runner had already been sent and had overtaken him and had already reached the door and had already delivered the news.

Edrin heard the runner at his door before the runner knocked.

He was at the long table under the working-lamp when the door opened, and he did not turn. He had been working with a small copper vessel over a low blue flame, and he set the vessel aside and lifted the flame down onto the stone beside it and waited until the runner had delivered the official message; then he turned and gave the order.

"Bring him up. The south table. Then leave."

The runner left.

Edrin stood a moment where he was and looked at nothing. Then he crossed to the table and cleared it. He took the two glass vessels that had been standing on it and set them on the shelf. He took the roll of oiled cloth and put it back on the rack. He took the ledger and closed it and set it under the bench. When the table was clear he stood at the foot of it and waited.

He heard the boots on the corridor before he heard them at the door.

The three came in carrying the body between them. They had cut the cords in the yard but had left the blanket wrapped, and they carried it the way men carry something they do not want to feel through the wool. They set it on the stone of the large table as directed, and they stepped back, and they stood there in wait.

Edrin looked at them.

"Unwrap him."

They did. They took the corners of the sodden blanket and drew them back, and the shape of Korr-Va came into the light of the room.

The three drew back a step when they saw him. They had known who they were carrying, and they had seen him in the ash at Hoshin and had lifted him onto the horse themselves, but seeing him again on the pale stone of the table in the clean light of the lamp was not the same as seeing him in the ash. The burn had made his outline strange. The plate had fused in places to what was underneath it. The arms had folded in against the body and the legs had folded up the way heat took them, and the head had turned a little to one side, and where the flesh at the throat had gone the collar of the armor sat down against the collarbone with nothing between.

The three of them stood there.

Edrin waited.

"Out," he said.

They looked at him.

"Out."

They went. The boots on the corridor diminished and then the door at the far end shut and then the room was quiet except for the small hiss of the blue flame he had lifted off the table before the runner had come in.

The lamp lit Korr-Va from above and the mirror lit him from the side, and in the doubled light Edrin saw him clearly for the first time.

He did not approach the table.

He stood at the north bench with his back to the light and studied the body from where he stood. He read it the way he read all things at first. From distance. He took the whole of it in and did not let himself go in yet on any part of it. He looked at the set of the head. He looked at the folding of the arms into the body, which was the way heat took the arms, and he looked at the folding of the legs, which was the way heat took the legs, and he noted the ways in which the folding was consistent and the ways in which it was not.

He walked around the table.

He walked the long way, along the east wall past the shelves, and came up along the south side of the table. He stood there a moment. Then he took from the pocket of his robe the looking glass. It was a small round lens in a brass frame with a folding handle, and he opened the handle and lifted the lens to his eye and leaned in over the body.

He moved the lens across the surface of the burn slowly.

He read the char at the shoulder where the plate had fused. He read the edge of the burn where it met the unburnt places on the underside of the body. He read the collar of the armor where the plate came down against the bone with nothing between. He read the join at the back of the collar, and the place at the hollow above the collarbone where the plate ended, and he read it a long time. He tipped the lens and read it from a different angle. He lifted the lens off his eye and looked at the collar without the lens for a moment, and then lifted the lens again and read it once more.

He straightened.

He folded the handle of the looking glass and put it back in the pocket of his robe.

He stepped back one pace from the table.

He raised his right hand over the body, palm downward, and he spoke three words.

He spoke them quietly. He did not raise his voice to speak them. They were not words in the common tongue and they were not words in the Clawborn tongue and they were not words in any language a man of the empire would have recognised. He had learned them from a book he did not permit anyone else to touch, and he spoke them the way he always spoke them, once each, without emphasis.

The light came out of his palm.

It came slowly at first, a pale line no thicker than a finger, and then it spread the width of the body and became a sheet of light hanging in the air just above the surface of the burn. The light was the colour of cold water in the shadow of a bank. Not blue and not grey but the thing between them. It hung for a moment at the crown of Korr-Va's head, and then it began to move.

It moved down the body from the head toward the feet at the pace a man breathes. It passed over the crown and over the brow and over the face and over the throat and over the collar of the armor. It passed over the chest, and over the arms folded against the chest, and it moved down the length of the body, taking in every fold and every char and every place where the plate had fused, and it did not stop and it did not hurry.

At the feet it paused.

Then it reversed and moved back up.

It came up over the feet and over the legs and up the length of the torso and the arms and the throat and the face and the crown, and it moved a fraction more slowly on the return than it had on the descent, and when it reached the crown again it stopped, and it hung there.

Edrin closed his eyes.

The light left the air above the body and passed toward him and did not stop at his palm. It went up his arm and across his shoulder and into the side of his head, and he felt it enter, and he did not flinch because he had done this a hundred times before, but something in what it brought back with it this time was not what he had expected.

He stumbled back.

His shoulder hit the shelves along the east wall and one of the jars rocked in its place and settled without falling. He put a hand out against the stone to steady himself. He kept his eyes closed for a count of three. His breath was short. His face had gone the colour of the light that had just gone into it.

He opened his eyes.

He opened them wide, and they went to the body on the table and stayed there.

"It wasn't death by fire," he said. "He was murdered."
