Chapter 132

Familiar Faces, Part 3

I’m back at Bokarala Bar, staring up at the winged monkey in faded circus attire. It’s grinning down at me, the once-gold pain of its teeth now a faded nicotine-stain yellow.

“Weird little guy…” I mutter. But I can’t help but think of my friends when I stare at the bar’s sign. The last time I’d stopped by this hole in the wall I was meeting with Veronica. She’d been considering a return to law school, and leaving behind the world of System mechanics and Guild dynamics entirely. Now, she’s made that very decision.

The time before that, it’d been with Veronica and Clyde. We’d just survived a Gate. A close fight that should have killed us and didn’t only because the universe briefly got drunk and sent a last-second assist from a Naked Sasquatch. Who I’m sure thought I was its cousin or something…

I shudder and push inside.

The smell of the dark space is the first thing that slams into me. Old beer, fry grease, wood polish, and something damp. A jukebox mutters something bluesy from the corner, low enough to be ignored but loud enough to make silence impossible. The lights are dim and amber, turning the bottles behind the bar into little rows of alcoholic stained glass.

I glance toward the back. And there he is.

Clyde Richmond lounges near the dart board like he owns that little corner of the bar. One of the first friends I made in the post-System world. And, unlike Veronica, he’s still a Participant in the God Game. I surprise myself by how happy I am to see the guy.

Clyde looks up as I enter.

That’s when I notice he’s different from when I last saw him at the Chase Guild assessment in Columbus. At first glance, he’s still tall with a wire thin frame, all angles and long limbs. But the thinness has changed. There’s lean muscle packed onto that frame now, subtle but obvious if you know what to look for. His shoulders sit stronger under his dark shirt, which he wears with the sleeves pushed up, revealing forearms that now have corded definition and subtle mass. The only word I could use to describe it is athletic.

His hair is cut extremely short now. Gone are the long, near-braided twists he used to wear. The new cut makes his face look older, and more severe.  He has a full beard now too, replacing the thin mustache I remember. And then there’s the scar. It bisects the eyebrow above his left eye, cuts down across the brow, and continues along his cheek in a pale, angry line. Not fresh, but not old either.

I think of Veronica’s scar from our Storm Dragon fight in the Bronze Gate. All my friends and allies have scars or worse, and here I am, I think.

Clyde smiles when he sees me and I push the thought away.

“Hey, man,” he says.

I nod and walk over, taking the second stool near the high-top table he’s lounging at.

“When did you get that?” I ask, gesturing to my own eye.

Clyde touches the scar with two fingers like he forgot it was there.

“This?” he asks.

“No, your beard. Obviously, the face canyon dude. That thing is gnarly!”

He huffs a laugh and leans back against the wall beside our table. “High-level Gate excursion for Chase. Six-person team. Bad intel. What we thought was the Boss Monster was just the appetizer.”

“How bad was the actual Boss?”

“Fucking terrible. Big cat thing. Moved through shadows, and could turn invisible.”

“Sheesh.”

“Yeah. I actually noticed it before the rest of the Party… Just not quickly enough.” He taps the scar again. “It got the jump on me.”

“Did you kill it?”

His smile turns thin. “Eventually. That’s why I’m here, talking to you. Don’t think that thing was going to let us just walk away from that fight.”

A waitress comes by at that moment. My [Perception] noticed her approaching from across the bar. Clyde orders a beer. I order water. The waitress leaves.

Clyde raises an eyebrow. “Water?”

I shrug. “Sometimes it’s nice to pretend drinking a beer is nice, but with our Stats, it’s all just nostalgia.”

Clyde smirks. “Yeah, I can’t remember the last time I was drunk. Apparently there’s some potions that can be added to alcohol that give the same effects to higher-leveled System Users, but they’re pretty pricey. Doesn’t seem worth it.”

Our drinks arrive and I take a sip of my ice water. Clyde downs his beer in a single large gulp. He places his empty glass down and looks at me across the table. He doesn’t waste time.

“I’m in,” he says. “I’ll join your Party.”

The words hit fast enough that my prepared casual opening dies in my throat.

“Oh,” I say. “Er… Great!”

“Conditions,” he adds.

“Of course.”

“Harvest pays my breakup fee to Chase Guild.”

“Okay,” I reply. I’m pretty sure Jerome and Labonte won’t have an issue with that.

“And matches my compensation.”

I blink.

“That’s it?”

Clyde arches one eyebrow. “That’s not cheap. Chase pays top of market.”

“No, I mean—yeah. I figured. Harvest can handle money. Labonte throws money at problems like he’s trying to concuss them.”

“Then I’m in.”

There’s a brief lull. I take another sip of my water.

“Why?” I ask.

“Because you asked.”

“That’s nice, but not what I’m asking.”

“I’m just excited to team up again.”

“Clyde.”

He sighs and looks toward the dart board.

For another second, the bar noise fills the space between us. Glasses clinking. Low conversation. The jukebox groaning through a guitar solo. Someone near the bar laughs too loud, then coughs like the laugh cost them something.

“I want to stay in the Game,” Clyde says.

I nod slowly.

“And I want to do it with Guild backing,” he continues. “Not solo. Not freelancing my way through increasingly powerful Gates. I’ve seen high-leveled Quests, and know that’s just signing up to die. The next phase will only be possible with resources and funding.”

“Okay… What about Chase Guild?”

“Chase is only sending two Parties into Stage Two. I didn’t make the cut.”

That surprises me.

“You didn’t?”

“Nope.”

“You’re strong.”

“I know.”

“Useful.”

“I know.”

“Annoying.”

“I kn—Hey.”

“Kidding.” I flash him a smile. “So why not? Why didn’t they select you?”

His smile has no humor in it. “Political bullshit.”

“Ah,” is all I say. I’m all too familiar with office politics. Dealt with enough of it myself back in NYC. “Bullshit” was a good word for it.

“Chase has favorites,” Clyde says. “Internal factions. People with family connections. People with wealthy sponsors. People who made the right friends early on.”

“And you didn’t.”

“I made results.”

“Rookie mistake.”

“Apparently.”

He leans back. The scar over his eye catches the light.

“I got strong fast. Too fast for some people.” He shrugs. “So, I’m out. Unless I find another Party.”

“And Harvest pays the fee.”

“And Harvest pays the fee.”

I nod.

That part is easy.

“What about Veronica?” I ask before I can stop myself.

Clyde’s eyes flick back to mine.

“She opted out.”

“I know.”

“She’s alive,” he says. “That’s what matters.”

“Yeah.”

Neither of us says more for a second.

Veronica should be here. Not in the Game. Not in danger. But here, at this table, rolling her eyes at both of us, making some dry comment about how men discuss life-or-death decisions like they’re comparing gym memberships.

I miss her. I miss a lot of things lately.

Clyde drums his fingers once on the side of his glass. “Speaking of Veronica… Why are you staying in? Seems like most folks are eager to opt out.”

I stare down at my water, condensation sliding along the outside of the glass.

“I need to help someone close to me,” I say.

Clyde studies me.

He doesn’t ask.

Thank God, I think. I don’t think I can get into the details without getting all choked up. Not now, at least.

He just nods.

“That’s a good reason,” he says.

“It’s a selfish reason.”

“Most good reasons are. And I don’t think risking your life for someone else is selfish. In fact, I spoke to Veronica after all that shit went down here in Cleveland. You saved her, man. You saved everyone. People died, and that could have included you. You’re not selfish, Joe.”

I look up. “What about you?”

Clyde’s face changes. Something opens there – like there’s a door behind his eyes. He fiddles with his empty beer glass.

“I don’t want to give up the opportunity to grow more powerful,” he says.

I wait.

He turns the beer glass slowly on the table.

“Power?” I ask.

“I know what it’s like to be hungry,” he says.

The words are quiet enough that I almost miss them under the bar noise.

“I know what it’s like to be forgotten. To be the kid nobody checks on. To stand in a line for food and know every adult there is pretending they don’t see how scared everyone is. To learn early that the world doesn’t fix suffering. It just steps around it.”

I don’t interrupt.

Clyde looks at the dart board.

“You get power in this world, old world or new, and most people use it to build walls. Buy some distance and a comfortable place to hide. Make sure suffering stays somewhere else. Another neighborhood. Another country. Whatever…” His jaw tightens beneath the beard. “If I win the Game, if I get that Wish or whatever power sits at the end of this meat grinder, I’ll use it to make sure no one goes hungry and forgotten again.”

The words land in my chest like a weight.

“I’ll force there to be a world with far less suffering,” he says.

I think about my own answer again. My reason for going on. Dad. One person. The person who raised me, loved me, helped pull me back together when Sarah broke me and New York spat me out.

I’d burn the System down for him. I don’t regret that. I won’t apologize for it. But sitting across from Clyde, listening to him talk about hunger and forgotten people and forcing the world to be less cruel, I feel my purpose shift slightly. It doesn’t change. Rather, it grows wider. Like I’ve been staring down a rifle scope so long I forgot there was a whole battlefield around the target. Maybe I’m viewing this too narrowly.

Maybe power like a wish demands a bigger answer?

I don’t know. But I admire Clyde’s goal.

I lift my glass of water.

Clyde glances at it.

“To less suffering,” I say.

His mouth twitches.

He lifts his empty glass and clinks it against my glass.

“To less suffering.”

Though we both know that more suffering is exactly what we’ve signed up for. I smile at the irony, and can’t help but think he’s smiling at the same thing.

In the past.

Joe’s Junior Year of High School…

The first time I step into a gym, I am sixteen years old, and absolutely convinced I am about to die of embarrassment before I ever touch a weight.

It’s just the local YMCA.

Still.

It might as well be an arena.

The weight room spreads out in front of me like another country. Machines with padded seats and metal arms sit in rows, each one looking like a medieval torture device sponsored by Gatorade. There are cable stations, benches, racks of dumbbells, plates stacked on pegs, treadmills humming along the wall, and people moving through all of it like they were born knowing where the clips go and how to adjust the seats.

Everyone looks comfortable.

That’s the worst part.

A woman in leggings jogs on a treadmill while watching the mounted TV. An old guy with white hair does curls in front of the mirror with perfect focus. Two college-aged guys laugh near the bench press, changing plates over casual conversation. A man in a cut-off shirt with arms like Thanksgiving hams pulls a handle down toward his chest on some machine I don’t understand.

They all know what they’re doing.

And I know nothing.

I am overweight, wearing basketball shorts that ride up weird, an old Cleveland Browns T-shirt, and sneakers with one lace that keeps coming untied. My face already feels hot, and we haven’t even started.

My dad places a hand on my shoulder, steadying me. His hand is heavy snd warm.

“You good, Joe?”

No. Absolutely not. Absolutely fucking not! This was a mistake. A terrible, awful mistake. I would rather be anywhere else.

“Yeah,” I lie.

Dad gives my shoulder a gentle squeeze, because he knows I’m lying and also knows calling me on it will make me melt into the floor.

He signed us both up two days ago. I had come to him after dinner, throat tight, pretending it wasn’t a big deal. I told him I wanted to try to get into shape. It wasn’t a prepared speech, or anything. Just a mumble in the kitchen while he rinsed plates and Mom packed leftovers.

Dad had turned off the sink, dried his hands, and said, “Okay.”

Just like that. Not finally. Not about time. Not you know, that would be good for you.  Not I love you just the way you are. Not any of the things I was secretly afraid he’d say. Just okay.

Then he signed both of us up.

That was Dad. If either Liv or I wanted to do something, he was all in. If Liv wanted to try soccer, Dad was in the yard setting up cones before dinner. If she wanted to learn guitar, Dad found a used one and watched beginner videos with her even though he had the musical rhythm of a dropped toolbox. If I needed help building some stupid model for school, Dad was at the table with glue on his fingers and instructions upside down.

He didn’t cheer from the sidelines if he could stand beside you. He dove in. One hundred percent. Even when diving in meant walking into a YMCA weight room with his fat, terrified son and pretending this was all totally normal.

I swallow hard. “Maybe this was a bad idea,” I say.

Dad looks at me.

“What was?”

“This.” I gesture vaguely at the entire gym, because I don’t know how to explain that every machine feels like it has personally judged me and found me and my man-tits lacking. “I don’t know. Everyone here knows what they’re doing. I’m just going to look stupid.”

Dad follows my gaze across the room.

For a second, he doesn’t answer.

Then he leans a little closer and lowers his voice, like he’s telling me a secret.

“Joe, nobody here is looking at you.”

I stare at him.

“That’s not true.”

“It is.”

“Dad.”

“No, listen.” He turns me slightly by the shoulder so I’m facing the room again. “That lady on the treadmill? She’s thinking about finishing her run, or just that episode of The Sopranos she’s watching. That guy over there? He’s counting his reps. Those two by the bench? They’re talking about whatever guys that age talk about. Probably girls or video games.”

Despite myself, I almost smile. Dad squeezes my shoulder again.

“Everybody here is here for the same reason,” he says. “To work. To work on themselves. That’s it. Nobody gets points for already knowing everything. You show up, you learn, you try. Same as anything else.”

My throat feels tight. The gym is still terrifying.

But Dad’s hand is still on my shoulder. That helps. A little, but still…

“Okay,” I say.

“Okay,” he says, and grins. “Let’s get to it.”

He has a plan he spent all day working up for us. Dad was an athlete once. Football in high school. Baseball too. He still has stories about two-a-days and bad coaches and the time he slid into second so hard he ripped his pants and had to finish the inning with half the team laughing at his underwear. He hasn’t trained seriously in years, but he remembers enough to write out something simple on a folded piece of paper he takes out of the pocket of his shorts.

“First, we should warm up,” he says.

The warm-up is ten minutes on the treadmill. I walk. Dad walks beside me. He makes conversation about nothing. Dinner. School. Cleveland sports. Whether Liv is still mad because Mom won’t let her dye her hair blue.

My legs feel fine until minute seven, when they decide walking is a personal attack.

By minute ten, I am sweating through my shirt.

Dad pretends not to notice.

We move to the machines.

The first one is leg press. Dad adjusts the seat, reads the little instruction sticker, and puts on almost no weight.

“We’ll start light,” he says. “Learn the movement.”

I sit down, suddenly aware of every inch of my body. My stomach bunching under my shirt. My thighs against the seat. My face in the mirror across the room, red and round and unhappy.

I press.

The platform moves.

Barely.

My legs shake.

Dad nods like I just deadlifted a car.

“There you go. Slow. Control it. Don’t lock your knees.”

I do ten reps.

Maybe eight good ones. Two terrible half-reps.

Then Dad does his set. He moves smoother than me, even though he also keeps it light. He talks through what he’s doing.

We move to the chest press. Then the pulldown machine. Then the rowing bench.

Every new machine comes with a new little panic. Where do my hands go? What if the seat is wrong? What if someone is waiting for me to hurry up? What if I do it badly? What if everyone notices?

I’m struggling through the set of rows when my dad waves a guy over. He’s a ripped guy in cut-off tank top, and he’s fucking huge. His shoulders round and full, arms veined, traps rising from his neck like terrain. His shirt says NO EXCUSES, which feels directed at me personally.

“Excuse me,” Dad says.

My soul leaves my body. The huge guy turns.

“Yeah?”

“Sorry to bother you,” Dad says. “Could you show us how we should be doing this machine? First day here, and I don’t want us doing it wrong.”

I want to run out of the gym. I want to sprint through the lobby, past the front desk, into the parking lot, and keep going until I reach another state.

But the huge guy smiles. The smile is warm and genuine. “Yeah, absolutely.”

He steps over and shows Dad the seat adjustment, then the form for setting up, then the grip, and finally the motion.

“You want it here,” he says. “Chest up. Don’t yank it down with your whole body. Think elbows to your pockets as you pull back.”

Dad nods along. “Elbows to pockets. Got it.”

The guy looks at me. My heart tries to crawl into my throat.

“First time?” he asks.

I nod because words are impossible.

“Good for you, man,” he says. “Keep it up!”

I’m too flustered to say anything, so I just nod.

“Most people never start. Starting’s the hard part.”

I don’t know what to do with that, so I stare at the floor and mumble, “Thanks.”

He sticks around while Dad does a set, offering a couple pointers.

“There you go. Nice.”

Then Dad has me try it.

I sit. Grab the bar. Pull. I try my best to mimic what Dad just did.

When I finish, the guy gives me a little fist bump.

“Nice work.”

Then he walks away.

That’s it.

Dad looks at me. “See?” he says.

I shrug, pretending it doesn’t matter.

But it does. It matters a lot.

By the end of our workout, I am dead.

We walk back to the car, and each step down from the curb feels like an event. Dad unlocks it, and I lower myself into the passenger seat with the careful seriousness of an astronaut returning from orbit.

My legs immediately begin throbbing. My arms are already sore. My chest feels hollow and full at the same time.

Dad gets in, starts the car, and adjusts the vents.

For a minute, we just sit there in the YMCA parking lot.

The sun is setting behind the building, turning the windshield gold. People walk in and out carrying gym bags.

I stare down at my hands. There are small blisters beginning to form from where the bars had rough, gritty texture.

“That was really hard,” I say.

Dad looks over.

“Yeah.”

“I’m already sore.”

“You’ll be more sore tomorrow.”

“Great.”

He smiles.

I don’t. “I don’t know if I can do this,” I admit.

The words come out small. I hate how small they sound.

Dad shifts in his seat so he’s facing me.

“Joey,” he says, “sometimes it takes breaking yourself down and starting at square one.”

I look at him.

He continues, voice steady. “What you want to do isn’t easy. If it was easy, everybody would do it. Getting stronger, getting healthier, changing how you feel in your own skin—that’s a long road. Longer than anybody wants it to be.”

My throat tightens again.

“But you came to me,” he says. “You asked. You walked in there. You did every exercise. You listened. You tried.”

He puts a hand on my shoulder again.

“I’m proud of you,” he says. “Really proud.”

I look away fast. Out the windshield. At the YMCA sign. At the people moving through the doors. At the sky going orange behind the roofline.

Dad squeezes my shoulder. “And I know you won’t give up.”

The thing is, when he says it, I believe him. Not because I suddenly feel strong, or anything. I believe him because Dad sounds so sure. Like he can already see a version of me I can’t. Like the weak, embarrassed, overweight kid in the passenger seat is not the end of the story. Just the first page.

I nod.

“Thanks, Dad.”
