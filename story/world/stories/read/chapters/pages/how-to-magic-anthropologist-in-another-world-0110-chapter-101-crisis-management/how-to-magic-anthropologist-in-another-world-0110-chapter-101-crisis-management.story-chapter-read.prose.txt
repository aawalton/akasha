Tomorrow was opening day.

After weeks of planning, construction, recipe testing, and a dozen other problems I was glad I hadn't had to deal with, since I had delegated most of the day-to-day operations to Louis and Je'e. The pizzeria... or rather The Delightful, since the name had shifted along with everything else after a certain divine incident... was finally ready to welcome its first customers.

I stood in the seating area with Louis, Silnar, and Lera, the four of us inspecting the final piece of the puzzle: the uniforms.

The design was simple. A bright red shirt with a black collar and black lining along the sleeves, paired with black trousers. Over the heart, the restaurant's crest sat stitched in white thread, a constellation rather than a traditional heraldic emblem: a circle of stars arranged into a laurel wreath, with a single larger star anchoring the bottom. Every star was a heptagram, the shape favored by the holy people of Hano rather than Earth's more familiar five-pointed version. The same crest had been embroidered onto the red baseball caps.

Louis kept tugging at the fabric around her legs, visibly uncomfortable.

"I am not sure how I feel about this," she admitted.

"Really? Why?" I asked.

"I'm still not used to wearing trousers." She looked down at herself again. "It feels strange."

Lera, on the other hand, seemed completely comfortable. She was practically built for the uniform, or more accurately, the uniform had been built for her. Years of freelance work had blessed her with an athletic, curvy figure the black trousers emphasized without ever restricting her movement. She bent one knee, then the other, testing the fabric.

"Are trousers Unholy?" she asked.

Louis shook her head immediately. "No. Of course not."

Lera raised an eyebrow. "Not even a little?"

"Well..." Louis hesitated. "They can be, if they're too tight."

"As tight as Silnar's?" The runner girl grinned.

I couldn't help but laugh.

Silnar’s muscular frame was almost bursting out of his uniform. The muscular man looked like a Greek statue, his chest muscles clearly outlined beneath the red fabric. Apparently my earlier conversation with Petra, about keeping the girls' uniforms modest for the sake of Louis's faith, hadn't made it to the men's side of the tailoring.

Silnar glanced down at himself. "What?"

"You look like you're about to explode," Lera said.

"Do I?"

Louis's eyes ran over her fiancé slowly, unmistakably hungry.

"Down, girl," I said. "You can have him next month, after the wedding."

"Lady, help me," Louis muttered. "I don't think I can wait that long."

Lera burst out laughing again.

I smiled. At least the uniforms looked good, and that was the part that actually mattered. A recognizable uniform meant staff the customers could spot at a glance. More than that, it gave the pizzeria an identity.

"All right, gather around," I said, pulling my phone from my bag of holding.

"What for?" Lera asked.

"We need a picture."

"A picture?"

I nodded and flipped the camera around before holding it out in front of us. "Come on. Stand close to me."

Lera shrugged and slid in on my left, Louis took her left, and Silnar settled beside her, rounding out the group.

I raised the phone. "Smile."

Lera grinned. Louis managed something a little stiff. Silnar just looked confused.

"Good enough."

I stretched my arm out a little farther and took the shot.

The screen showed the four of us standing together in matching red and black, caps and all, looking for one frozen second like an actual team.

"Perfect."

"Can I see?" Louis asked.

I turned the phone toward her. Lera's eyes went wide the moment she caught sight of the image.

"What even is this?"

"Dreamer artifact," I lied, and slid the phone back into my bag before anyone could ask a follow-up.

"I was skeptical about the uniforms," Silnar admitted, "but seeing us together like that... it clicked."

"We really do look like a proper team," Lera said.

And, as if the universe had been waiting for exactly that sentence, Calr chose that moment to burst through the back door, out of breath.

"I was checking in with some of my friends," Calr said, still catching his breath, "some of the kids from the street gangs. The usual."

The red-haired descendant of the telepaths gave Louis a sideways glance, her cheeks puffing with disapproval. It slipped my mind more often than it should have that the two of them were, technically, siblings in the eyes of the Holy. Their faith, much like Islam on Earth, treated sharing a wet nurse as a form of kinship, and both of them had passed through the same orphanage as babies.

"Everyone is coming tomorrow," Calr continued, dragging me back from the tangent.

"What do you mean?" Louis asked.

"Everyone even slightly connected to the Holy Faith is going to be here." His expression didn't crack. "Word of the Lady manifesting has spread. Every shop owner, every merchant, every street kid I talked to is planning to show up. And it's not just the Faithful either: Mythic people, Kindred, Dreamers, all of it."

"How many people are you ready to serve?" I asked, turning to the cooks.

"We prepared for a thousand," Louis said.

"At least when it comes to pizza dough, ladyfingers, and silk cream," Silnar added.

"And how many can this shop seat at once?"

"Around fifty."

"That's not nearly enough," Calr said. "Look outside if you don't believe me."

The five of us crossed to the nearest window. Even the street looked busier than usual. Curious passersby kept slowing to stare at the building, trading rumors with whoever stood nearest, while a larger crowd had gathered around the billboard we'd set up beside the shop, the one explaining the menu, introducing the two chefs, and announcing, in large friendly letters, that tomorrow was opening day.

"Are all of those people here because of us?" I asked.

"That's just what's here right now," Calr said. "People have been drifting in and out for a while."

"How did we not notice?"

"The shop's warded with a sound-dampening field," Louis said.

"And I've been using the back entrance since you insisted on closing the front one," Lera added.

Then, to make matters worse, something moved in the sky above us.

A woman in a black, skin-tight bodysuit was dropping straight toward the shop, brown hair whipping in the wind. She wasn't even bothering to hide her approach, landing directly in front of the entrance without slowing, her boots touching down almost silently.

The crowd reacted instantly. Gasps, scattered cheers, a few shouted names.

She raised one hand toward the door. The lock clicked open on its own, and she stepped inside like she owned the place.

Captain Yoka Satori. The rising star of the Freelancer Guild, the youngest person to ever make Captain, and the head of Hano's Information Gathering Department.

Of course. Another A-lister, dropping by on the eve of opening day.

And yes, as I'd learned from the stack of broadsheets living in my bag of holding, freelancers were this world's real celebrities, not bards. If Keressa was Taylor Swift and Marina was Beyoncé, Captain Yoka was something like Sabrina Carpenter, and she'd just kicked our front door open in full view of a crowd that was already staring.

Which meant she'd handed everyone outside a brand new reason to keep staring.

"Alice!" Yoka glared at me the moment she was through the door. "Why is the city on the brink of a riot?"

"Riot?" I gulped. "What riot?"

"Marina had me look into the Lady's manifestation." She crossed her arms. "Every report says the same thing. There's a new holy dessert in town, and everyone wants a taste, just in case eating it bumps up their faith."

"Oh." I glanced at Louis. She looked exactly as horrified as I felt.

"We're ready for a thousand people," she offered. "Maybe two thousand, if we push."

Yoka looked at her for a long moment, then at Silnar. He simply nodded.

"There are half a million Mythic people in this city," Yoka said. "Roughly twenty thousand of them carry the Holy Faithful class. My estimate says at least twelve thousand could show up here tomorrow."

I blinked. "Twelve thousand?"

"Yes."

"Twelve thousand people want to eat tiramisu?"

She pointed toward the entrance. "This is a city hazard, Alice. People get trampled in crowds like this. You can't deny that many people what they want without risking a riot."

I stared at her, horrified, because I understood exactly what she meant. I'd seen enough of Earth to picture it: Black Friday crowds packed shoulder to shoulder, concert stampedes where the crush got so tight people couldn't breathe. A crowd didn't need to be malicious to turn dangerous. It just needed to get too big.

I looked past her, through the windows, at a street already more crowded than I liked. Tomorrow, maybe twelve thousand.

No. Absolutely not. We needed a different plan.

"Silnar, Louis, call in every apprentice you have and start on more ladyfingers."

They nodded and disappeared into the kitchen without a single question.

"Calr, get to the orphanage. Gather every kid over twelve. One bronze an hour if they want to help, and bring in anyone else you trust."

Calr bolted out the door.

"Lera, find Ja'a. Tell her to buy every drop of silk cream and heavy cream she can get her hands on, plus everything else we need for tiramisu."

Lera took off at a sprint.

That left me alone with Yoka.

"We can't let everyone come here," I said. "The street's wide enough, but the double doors are a choke point. if thousands of people funneled through here, someone will get crushed."

Her expression sharpened. "Then what do you suggest?"

"Can we borrow a large public space?"

"Such as?"

"Maybe the park three blocks east? The one with the trees?"

"Alice. That's a cemetery."

"Oh." I really needed to learn this city's landmarks.

"You can have Taka Memorial Square," Yoka said. "it's an open space, used for small festivals. Three blocks north."

"Perfect."

A square meant room, multiple entrances, somewhere a line could form without swallowing the whole street. I could already picture it: a serving area, a queue, tables, a separate entrance and exit. If we were feeding twelve thousand people, we'd have to run it like a festival.

Yoka watched me think it through, then smiled. "Sounds like you've got a plan for this disaster."

"Hopefully."

"I'll hire some freelancers for crowd control and send you the bill."

I opened my mouth to argue, then remembered the alternative.

"Fine. Just keep Sergeant Da'i away from this."

Yoka laughed. "He wouldn't dare show his face."

"Good. I don't need anyone messing this up on purpose."

"Fair enough." Still chuckling, she opened the doors with her telekinesis and shot back into the sky.

I watched her vanish over the rooftops, then summoned my spear.

"I wish I were next to Nina."

The world blurred around me. A moment later, I appeared inside Nina's workshop.

She was bent over a strange wooden contraption, putting the finishing touches on it. When she noticed me, her face lit up with a mix of surprise and pride.

"I finished it!"

She gestured toward the device. At first glance it looked like a megaphone: a wooden cone for a body, narrowing to a handle before flaring out again at the opening.

"It works," Nina announced, and lifted it to her mouth. "Hello, Alice!"

Her voice came out far louder than normal, but rough, distorted, a hiss running under it along with the odd pop and scratch.

I walked over and took a closer look.

"Let me see."

Nina handed it over, practically vibrating with excitement. Inside the housing, just behind the opening, sat the heart of the device: a thin diaphragm in front of a small magnet, copper wire wound around a monster core, the whole thing feeding back into the frame to form the speaker.

Crude, but the principle was sound, and familiar enough that I understood it immediately. Nina had actually solved the amplification problem with Mana.

"You used sky-affinity cores to amplify the electrical signal?"

She nodded enthusiastically. "Exactly! The vibrations move the membrane, the membrane changes the signal, and the core makes it stronger before sending it to the speaker."

"That explains the noise."

"I..." Her excitement faltered slightly. "I haven't figured out how to get rid of that part yet."

"This is very good for a first prototype. Let me test it and see what I can think of."

I lifted the megaphone to my mouth and started singing.

“♫ A B C D E F G ♪”

On the B, a burst of static exploded from the speaker. The F did something similar, lower and longer.

I frowned. "This isn't an electrical problem."

"What?" Nina tilted her head.

I brought the microphone closer. "Peter Piper. Peter Piper. Peter Piper."

The result was almost comical. Every P sent a loud pop through the speaker, swallowing half the word.

"Peter Piper."

Pop. Shhh. Pop, pop.

I lowered the device. Nina's shoulders sagged.

"I thought it would work better."

"Hey." I handed it back. "This is a good first attempt. Really."

"It sounds terrible." She didn't look convinced.

"It sounds terrible because it's hearing more than my voice." I thought about how to explain it. "When I talk, I'm not just making sound waves. I'm also pushing air toward the microphone. P and B especially, they produce a sudden burst of air. F does something similar, just more spread out."

I tapped the front of the device. "Your microphone's picking all of that up too."

"So the air is interfering with the sound?"

"Exactly." I glanced around the workshop. "Do you have anything soft and porous? Sponge, wool, cotton?"

Nina dug through a nearby box and came up with a small bundle. "Cotton?"

"That'll work."

I pulled off a thin layer and laid it over the front of the microphone, careful to leave the diaphragm enough room to breathe.

"Try it again."

Nina lifted the device. "Peter Piper?" Half a question, since she still wasn't entirely sure what the words meant.

The improvement was immediate. Some noise still crept through, but the explosive pops had dropped off sharply, and the words came through clear.

Her eyes went wide. "Peter Piper!" She said it louder this time, and though the noise came back a little, it was nowhere near as bad.

"Wow." She grinned. "This is so much better."

I looked the device over again. "The cotton's blocking the sudden blasts of air before they hit the diaphragm. But there's still another problem."

"The amplification, right?"

"Right." I pulled the cotton off and pointed at the mana core. "You're amplifying the whole signal, aren't you?"

She nodded.

"That's the issue. You're not just amplifying my voice. You're amplifying the wind, the vibration in your fingers, the background noise, every electrical fluctuation in the room."

Understanding crossed her face slowly. "So I need to tell the useful signal apart from the useless one."

"Something like that." I thought for a second. "If you could boost certain frequency ranges while cutting others, the voice would probably come through much cleaner."

She stared at the microphone. "That might be possible."

"You'd need to understand which frequencies make up human speech first."

"I don't know the theory well enough yet."

"I'll help you work through it later." I bumped my shoulder against hers, and she wrapped one wing around me without even thinking about it. I handed the microphone back. "Maybe in the Dreaming. I can show you what electrical signals actually look like."

She nodded eagerly, already eyeing the device with fresh interest.

"But that's going to have to wait. Right now I need your help with something else."

"What happened?"

"We may have underestimated how many people are coming tomorrow."

"How many?"

"Possibly twelve thousand."

Her eyes went wide. "That's a lot of people."

"Yeah." I looked around the workshop. "Have you started on the magical whisks? You said they'd be simple."

She nodded and pulled one off a shelf. "I made a proof of concept, just to check it's possible. Very crude. I wouldn't even call it a prototype."

The thing looked cobbled together from whatever had been lying around: a wooden box that had once shipped a potion, a hole carved through its base, a whisk fitted in with two lubricated metal rings acting as bearings, and finally four small kinetic-affinity cores were wedged into the sides.

I pressed one of the cores and the whisk spun to life instantly.

"Each core pushes the motion toward the core on its left. That's what creates the spin."

It was fast... really fast.

"This is really good."

"No, it's terrible," Nina said. "The box is too big, so it's losing efficiency. Four cores when one would probably do. The whisk itself is too heavy. A smaller whisk with a lighter frame, and only two rings, would work better."

"Good news, then." I grinned. "You get to improve the design ten more times."

She blinked. "Ten more?"

"Yes."

"All right." She was already scanning the workshop, working out how she'd reproduce it.

"Don't make them fancy," I added. "I need them fast."

"I can do fancy and fast." She shot me an offended look.

"Just make them work," I said. "We don't have time for anything else."

"Yeah, yeah. Fine."

"And Nina."

She looked up.

"Thank you."

"No, thank you, my Muse." Her grin was blinding.

I summoned my spear again and reached for the star mana stored inside it.

"I wish I were in the Sunless Reach."

The world blurred a second time.

A moment later I was standing on top of my observatory, beneath the familiar dark sky. I dropped onto my chaise lounge and let out a long breath while my mana slowly refilled.

There was still a mountain of work ahead. Ingredients, workers, tables, chairs, serving stations, crowd control, probably fifty other things I hadn't thought of yet.

Luckily, teleportation could help with that too. Once my mana was full again, I made another wish.

"Let me be somewhere I can rent tables and chairs for a day."
