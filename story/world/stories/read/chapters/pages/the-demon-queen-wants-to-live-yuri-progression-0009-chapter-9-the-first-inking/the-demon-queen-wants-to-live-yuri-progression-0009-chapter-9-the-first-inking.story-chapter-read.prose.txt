"Are you really going to do this?"

Ash stood by the window with her arms folded. Morning light came in through the shutters and covered one side of her face. The Hero had already dressed herself in the clothes Martha had given to us. The pink looked garish to me, though perhaps not as much as it once might have. I stared down at the Darkness Binding across my lap, running my fingers over its surface. I had been doing this since dawn, checking for damage that wasn't there.

"Yes," I answered eventually.

"Just like that?"

"Just like that."

Ash turned from the window to look at me. I looked up and met her gaze. There was a caution in her gaze I had started to recognize now. The kind of caution that came when she was about to say something that she knew would provoke me.

"This is submitting to a power we don't understand. You know that. We know too little of these marks, or what they might do to those who bear them...If I can see it, then surely you can too." She said, confirming my suspicion.

"And?"

The Hero frowned. "Why are you not more concerned? What if these are new chains, and now you're walking into a set of them?"

I set the armor aside and met her gaze. Odd that she now reject power, when she had never once rejected being a Hero. Had never once rejected all of the Gods whose blessings she had taken. Perhaps I was not the only one who felt something uncomfortable and foreign each night.

"You feel it, don't you?" I said. Ash went still, and so I continued, "Your mana is thinning, as is your aura. I can feel it. Each day, there is less of both -your powers are draining." I paused. "If we fought that Divine Beast from the forest now, we would die." Ash said nothing at that. "How long, Hero? A month? Two? Before you cannot even lift that sword of yours?"

Her jaw tightened. She frowned, and her hand drifted toward the blade leaning against the wall. Just for an instant. "That's not-"

"It is." I stood. "I did not claw my way across a thousand years to be outmatched by children who can conjure fire with a snap of their fingers. If this world runs on Ink instead of Classes, then I will have ink." The words could not carry the weight of all the agonizing thought that had gone behind them. I did not like it any more than Ash did. I liked feeling myself diminish even less.

And this 'Ink' might answer some of the questions that gnawed at me. Why was my body different? Where had the System gone? Where were the Gods? My probing questions had revealed that these people knew none of the names I carefully mentioned -names even people in backwaters should recognize. The silence that followed my statement was not quite agreement.

"Then we both go," Ash said quietly. I did not acknowledge the 'both.' But I did not object.

"I want to have a word, dears." Martha caught us at the door. She wiped her hands on her apron and looked from me to Ash. "The Inker who's coming today, his name is Master Aldric. Today ain't the first or the fifth time he's come here. He came first twelve winters ago with a fever that nearly killed him. We nursed him for three weeks. He's not forgotten it since. So the two of you just…take care." She looked between us. "Aldric's a proud man. Don't give him reason to make this difficult."

Ash only nodded at the warning.

We needed no announcement to notice his arrival. The Inker -this Aldric- arrived in the late afternoon and did not come alone. Everyone in the village had been preparing for his arrival for some time. They had all hung lanterns along the dirt path in uneven rows. Others had hung dried flowers from doorways that had none before. The children ran in circles, though they always seemed to do that.

All of the adults were quiet. Even Tom, who could make small talk with a stone wall. There was a reverence to it, I think, and something akin to fear too. I had often seen both directed at me, though the latter far more often.

The Inker was a lean and sharp man, dressed in dark robes threaded with silver. The ten men with him all wore silver armor. Some carried lances, others held spears or bows. A few held swords. One even had a massive hammer. All of them had visible marks in swirling branching patterns displayed on bare arms, nothing like the small dots the villagers had.

The Inker carried with him a black case strapped across his back, and when he walked, the crowd parted without being asked. A holy man, such as existed in this time. Tom and his wife had told me much. They had also insisted we not wear our armor.

He set up at the center of the square. There were many soldiers, and they stood off to the side, two dozen paces away. The Inker opened a black case to reveal a rack of small vials, each filled with ink. Though it seemed to me it was not just ink. The liquid shifted on its own within the glass.

My gaze moved to the Inker's forearms. He had two marks of his own, both intricate, and moving in patterns that formed shapes. One was in the vague shape of brown horns, and the other was an imitation of a red plant of some sort. I had never seen someone with two marks before. Never seen ones that made actual shapes.

It was Tom who led us toward the man. He had told us bluntly that our stories didn't make sense. He had explained the facts of our suspicious situation. Ash had decided to go with something resembling honesty when the time came. The fool.

"Master Inker," Tom said, dipping his head. Ash dipped hers. I did not dip mine. "These two are staying with us. They came out of the forest a few days back and then helped protect my nephew from a wolf pack on the road."

The Inker barely glanced up from his vials. "And?"

"They don't have marks."

Finally, the man looked up. His eyes moved to Ash first, then to me. They lingered on my horns for a long time, and something in his face hardened. "And how old are you?" he asked. The question was directed at both of us, but it was clear he was asking me.

"We are old enough," I answered him.

"She means we're both adults," Ash cut in quickly, stepping slightly in front of me. "We weren't raised in a village like this. And so we didn't have access to this ceremony growing up. It's as simple as that."

The Inker straightened. The silver threading in his robes glowed as he folded his arms. "As simple as that," he repeated. "Two grown women with no Ink at all." His brows furrowed as he eyed us. "And you took them in," he whispered, and this time he looked at Tom.

There was a knife behind that last part. Tom tensed and Ash looked ready to say something. "They earned their place here," Tom said firmly, more firmly than I would have expected. "They aren't suspicious folk."

The Inker all but ignored him as he looked over the merchant's head. "Where are you from? Which province?" Tom stepped back and wilted in on himself.

"We've traveled extensively," Ash said. "We don't claim a single-"

"I didn't ask you." He nodded at me. "I asked you. You tell me first, then the other one can,"

"You are asking the wrong questions," I said. "And I do not answer to you."

Something flickered across his face. Surprise, perhaps. I did not think it was anger. Tom shook like a leaf in the wind.

"Is that so." He took a step closer and one of his marks was glowing faintly, the one in the shape of horns. "Do you know who I am, girl?"

Once, he would have turned to ash at the first word. I had pulled out the tongues of men far grander, for far less. Ash brushed my side. It wasn't just us now. There were other people there, staring at us from a distance. Not far enough not to hear us. That girl was among them.

Damn it.

"A man of some authority," I said finally, my jaw clenched.

"Good. It seems even a savage can understand that much." His posture shifted. The glow on his mark dimmed. "No marks," he said again. "At your age." He circled us, slowly. I had seen beasts circle their prey just like this before they lunged in for the kill. "In what village were you raised that would allow such neglect? Or is it that the Ink was offered, and you refused it?" He let the question hang. The crowd had long gone quiet.

"There are words for those who refuse the Ink," he continued. "Forsaken or heretic." At the last word, some of the soldiers took one step forward. Nobody reached for a weapon. Not yet.

"We submit to it now!" Ash said. She did the last thing I had expected her to do. She bowed her head. The Hero who hadn't bowed to my might, bowed her head in front of this man. The Inker's lip curled and his eyes found mine.

"A village that holds heretics…" he said loudly. "May not last."

My head turned. I stared at that girl again -just at her. The rest might as well not have been there. The Goddess of Love had once demanded I bow before her, when she'd thought I needed her help. I had spat in her face. The Inker I could take, perhaps. The others? Even with the Hero, I did not like my odds. Too much of me had drained since waking up. I had not thought that things would go this way.

I bowed my head like the Hero had done. It felt stiff, as if the slightest bending threatened to break me. My teeth clenched, as did my fists.

"Very well," the Inker said finally, smoothing the front of his robes. "You know how this goes. If the Ink rejects them, the consequence falls on them. And on you."

"The Ink is sacred, you understand," he said, and there was real conviction behind it. "It is not given to those who have spurned it. I will make an exception, just once. For the kindness Hamel once showed me."

I wondered if this had all been a mistake.

Dusk fell over Hamel.

The square was lit by lantern and firelight. The village formed a wide circle around the Inker and his open case. Every soul in Hamel was here. I saw people I never had before. There were mothers with infants on their hips, and old men leaning on walking sticks and children bouncing on their toes. I had not thought Hamel had so many people.

And all of them began to chant. The sound started from the elders and I watched it spread, and before long everyone in the village had joined in. Even the youngest of children, though they were only mouthing the words. And it was in a language I had never heard. The cadence was still familiar. It was prayer. Each word was said with the weight of religious recitation. I had heard it all before. Had been the subject of most of it.

The Inker stood there as the song slowly swelled and even more slowly ebbed. When it faded entirely, he finally spoke. "For the uninitiated," he began, and his gaze swept the crowd, "and for the children who have yet to receive the Ink, I will speak briefly on what is to come."

Briefly. I doubted that. Priests were never brief. All men had chains. I had always wondered if this was theirs.

"There is one rule, and it is the most important. The Ink reveals what lies within you. It does not choose, nor does it create...it finds." He held up one of the vials and the liquid inside shifted like a thing alive. "And what the ink finds determines your Mark. There are five orders of marks." He set the vial down. and then raised one finger. "Somatic." He might as well have said 'dirt.' "They deal with the body. Strength, speed, endurance, and healing. These marks are common. As common as grass and about as remarkable. Most of you will have a Somatic. If you do, learn a trade -there are many- and be grateful."

A few of the adults looked at each other. The Inker raised another finger. "Then we have Primals." He sounded only marginally less bored at this name. "Some of you will have these. These marks deal with the elements. Fire, water, earth, and wind. Others you need not concern yourselves with. You will command one, and only one. Likely not even the whole of it."

The boy who could light the flame then. And Martha.

"Axiom." He raised a third finger. His tone shifted again -it was still not warm, but it carried no mocking. "They create constructs -projection of mana beyond the body. Usually these are in the form of shields, weapons and threads. Some can even form beasts, though that is far rarer." His eyes scanned the crowd.

Slowly, he raised a fourth finger. "We then have Ciphers. Their powers are over the mind and the senses. Illusion, disruption, manipulation of thought and perception." He smiled. "I happen to carry a Cipher." Every word sounded like a boast.

The Inker's posture changed. He straightened and his chin rose. One of his marks -the one shaped like horns- pulsed faintly.

"Sovereign." The Inker scanned the crowd, as if the word alone was enough. As if no explanation he could give would do justice to it. He tried anyway. Priests always did.

"The laws themselves. Of what? Of everything." He whispered, the sound carrying anyway. "One in a thousand. Ten thousand. Perhaps rarer still, nobody knows. My master, His Eminence Archon Valtheris, who leads the clergy of Koralis, bears a Sovereign Mark." There was open pride in his voice now. "He could unmake this village and everything in it without lifting a hand. That is the difference." He scanned the crowd one final time and his eyes were hard.

"None of you will receive a Sovereign Mark. None of you will ever even see one. I say this not to wound your pride but to spare you the delusion. A village like this might see one Axiom in a generation. Two Ciphers in a century. A Sovereign?" He almost smiled, did not say anything more.

Recognition moved through me. These Sovereigns sounded like a power I had commanded. The manipulation of the rules of nature. It was called [Concept Magic] in the old system. It had made me a threat to the Pantheon itself.

The Inker composed himself and shook his head. The glazed look in his eye went away as quickly as it had come. "After you receive your marks, you have to Cultivate them." He waved a hand, as if the topic bored him already. "Your Mark, once given, is small and weak. A dot on the skin, nothing more. We call it a Spark. It will grow only if you force it to. Use your mana as much as you can, though that is not nearly enough. Perhaps some of you can make Essence, that will serve better. But raw and baseless power is not everything."

"A mark requires Insight." He said the word softly, and the sound carried anyway. "With power and Insight, you might reach the Line -the next stage from Spark." He paused, looked at the crowd. Then he picked up one of his instruments and turned it idly in his fingers. "There are more stages in this process. Their names do not matter. There are many. Do try to die with more than a Spark, at least." He looked up. "All of you will receive one mark, that is the way of things. To receive two is rare, a sign of noble blood that...." He shook his head, as if the rest was so pointless it didn't need saying.

He looked at the row of children who waited for him. His expression stayed the same. "Now, let us begin with the Inking."

And with his words there came a pressure. It was faint, a density that the air had not had before. And I recognized it. The pressure was a gaze from the heavens. That meant there was divine power in this ink, or something close enough. This same gaze had made me wage war against the pantheon. It was not focused on me, but it pricked me all the same. Ash's jaw had set tight. She had felt it too.

The Inker started with the three children. They knelt before the Inker one at a time. He dipped a thin instrument into one of the vials. The ink clung to the tip and he pressed it to the first child's forearm. The child's eyes went glassy.

My back straightened. My hand clenched. I knew that look too. I had seen it a thousand times, on a thousand faces. It was the empty stare of one whose soul was being read when the System measured them. Many had called it the look of awakening. The parents all smiled and the crowd murmured warmly.

The child blinked and in the next moment he came back. And there was a single mark on his forearm, so small I almost couldn't see it. It was a blue-green dot.

"Somatic!" the Inker called out, loudly. The boy grinned and ran back to his mother, holding his arm up as if it were a trophy. She swept him into her arms. The crowd let out a cheer.

Then the second child went. She was a girl, short and thin. She knelt and held out her arm steadily. The needle pressed and her eyes went glassy. She came back a moment later with a single mark. Hers was blue.

"It's a Primal!" the Inker called out, and this time the cheering was louder. The girl smiled and rushed back to the crowd straighter than she had left it.

The final child stepped forward. A boy, and one that looked younger than the others. He was small, even smaller than the girl. His mother had to guide him towards the inker. She kissed the top of his head and stepped back into the circle.

The Inker pressed the instrument to the boy's arm and his eyes emptied. This was the moment the self steps aside and something else looks in. I had gone through it once, a millennia ago. I had been ten, I think, when I had awakened to the System.

My parents had been happy, I think. Had I?

The crowd was still smiling. And then I saw something I hadn't seen in the others. It was a tremor, one so faint I almost missed it. It started in his hand and climbed past his wrist and into his forearm. Then someone in the crowd noticed. An old woman stepped back.

The boy's whole arm was shaking now. His back arched and his mouth opened but no sound came out.

It spread through the boy's veins and turned them black. The ink swept up his elbow and moved towards his neck. The boy howled. And the scream was one I had heard before, in halls that no longer existed. My hands clenched.

The boy's mother took one slow step forward. She took another, and then she lunged. Two men caught her, and then there was a third. A forth joined in, though there was no need. The woman fought them. She was not strong. The faint mark on her arm pulsed but it seemed to do little. She fought them anyway.

The Hero took a step forward. I caught her arm, and pulled her back. "Do not do anything," I said.

"He's dying!" Ash hissed, trying to wrench free from my grasp. Her strength was considerable, even now.

"I know he is."

"Then let me do some-"

"You cannot stop this, Ash." I made my voice low enough so only she could hear. "And if you tried to, worse would happen."

Perhaps the Hero could not see it, but it was plain to me. The Inker and his men were watching the crowd for any reaction but the expected one.

The villagers had talked about the Ink 'not taking' in whispers and murmurs. They had spoken of it the way one speaks of a child being slow to walk. Nobody had spoken of this.

"Let me go, Lysanthia." Ash said. Every muscle in her arm was straining against me now.

"No."

The boy was still convulsing, but the Ink had stopped moving. It was even starting to retreat, slowly. And what it would leave behind was just a boy, staring at a sky he would see but never know. His eyes stared at nothing. I had watched the System do this before. Rarely.

The System did not accept everyone it touched. The ones it did not accept rarely died, but they were never whole afterward. Never as they had been before. I had not cared then. I was not sure what I felt now, only that it was not nothing. None of these people had spoken of this as though it could do this.

Finally, the Ink left entirely. It pulled back to the point of entry, leaving nothing behind of what it had done.

And then the men stepped aside, and the mother launched herself towards the boy. She fell beside him. Her hands trembled as they touched his face. She said his name. Said it again. She said it a third time, louder, in a voice that cracked. His name was Marin.

I let go of Ash's arm. She stood stiffly, with her hands balled into fists at her side. The Inker straightened. He cast one last look over the crowd, and then he spoke, "The Ink has judged, and this time, it has found the vessel unworthy." His voice was steady as it carried over the crowd.

There were no cheers this time. The chanting had died some time ago. The Inker's gaze fixed on me, and he smiled. I knew then that he hadn't accepted our Inking as some kind of mercy.

He'd just thought it a cleaner execution.
