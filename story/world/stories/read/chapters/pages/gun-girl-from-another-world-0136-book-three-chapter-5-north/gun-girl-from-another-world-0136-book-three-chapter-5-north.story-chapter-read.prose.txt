Chapter 5

North

Dinner went splendidly. Yorin stayed, too, which made it the second-largest group I had cooked for since coming to Toleste. She wouldn’t admit it, but I suspect even she was lured in by the prospect of Essence Custard. It made for a crowded table, once I assembled a couple extra chairs, but in a good way that made it feel like a proper welcome-home gathering.

I went all-out with the dinner, too. I wanted something distinctly American, to double down on the hominess feel, and to give my friends a new experience, but I struggled to come up with an all-encompassing meal. Any addition of meat would mean a non-essence ingredient, and I hadn’t yet had time to make any cheeses. Oh, I'm going to, it's right up there on my to-do list with figuring out how to make spirits in a timely manner.

And then it came to me in a flash of inspiration so simple that I marveled at its profundity:

When was the last time I’d had a proper breakfast? A real, American Heartland spread? Certainly not since coming here. The standard Furinshaoan breakfast is universally rice porridge with meat. The well-off can add an egg on top if they’re willing to pay for the decadence.

Yeah, technically, my friends were expecting supper, but is there anything more fundamentally American than throwing decorum to the wind in the name of comfort and having breakfast for dinner?

Decision made, I set out on my planned course of action. Buttermilk pancakes were non-negotiable, but I knew I had no access to maple syrup. Solution? Pancake Benedict. Drop a poached egg on those flappies and smother the whole thing with the yolk-and-butter richness of Hollandaise sauce. A bowl of grits, lightly sweetened with sugar and butter, helped offset the thick sauce, and some buttered toast with some of Elara’s jam and a cold glass of milk rounded things off. I wanted to include fresh-squeezed juice, but I worried about too much sweet, especially with the custard and caramel sauce coming in for dessert.

While I was out getting lumber for the new chairs, I got a sample from the cinnamon trees to see if they were ripe yet. So, since I wanted a nice, bitter coffee for a hot after-meal beverage, but knew Essence Arabica would be too overpowering, I decided to make something else I’d once mentioned to Yorin and Ayre.

It wasn’t true Qishr, as I’d left out almost all of the sugar to keep its flavor profile sharp, but the warm cinnamon and spicy ginger still made for a nice coffee replacement.

I think it was a hit, or at least nobody left anything but empty plates.

Elara stayed to make conversation over the tea for a while after the meal was over, but eventually excused herself. I expressed that I didn’t want to kick her out just because I showed up out of the blue, but she assured me, oddly, that it wasn’t unexpected at all, and she’d already been looking forward to spending the night in her own bed again. So, with a promise to be back in the morning, she headed out.

Yorin, too, would obviously be returning to the temple once she leaves, so the only one extra that I had to accommodate was Korrigan. Unfortunately, I had been right about the lack of space, and there was no way to fit another proper bed in here without starting to block other things.

On the upside, I’ve long prioritized our group’s comfort while camping, which means sturdy cots and bamboo pads, with quality pillows and comforter-like sleeping bags. It was a simple matter to pull one out of storage and set it up for the oni child, and now she’s quite asleep, mumbling no doubt about her new favorite sweet, Kyuuga curled up against her chest.

It’s only Yorin and I still up, in fact, sitting at the table in the light of a candle, the rest of the cabin in darkness, as we sip at the last of the Qishr.

“You speak as if you intend to remain in the village for a time.” The pause before the priestess says this indicates a clear change of topic from the gossip we’d been exchanging, and I can’t help but feel like we’ve moved on to the main topic she’s here to discuss.

It’s not like I haven’t been expecting it. Honestly, while I’ve enjoyed the chat, I’ve really just been waiting for this time to come. So I nod without hesitation before I answer. “Now that I have Beacon Teleport, I can return to Lurei at any time, or come back here whenever I feel like it. If I really wanted, I could wake up here in the morning, grab some homemade breakfast, help with a few chores, consult with the contractors, and swing by the guild, all before early morning is over, spend the rest of the day clearing the Crystal Forest dungeon, and be back here for supper and sleeping in my own bed. It’ll save me a ton in hotel fees.”

The flat look that meets my declaration clearly says I'm hero-ing wrong.

“Hey, don't look at me! It’s not my fault that my Hero System is bound and determined to make my life as easy as possible! I'm sorry the Shrine Maiden Hero had to rough it from here to Zazkatoon, uphill both ways in the snow barefoot, but I’m not the Heavenly EssenceTM Complaint Department.”

She lets out a deep sigh, but shakes her head. “I am going to treat all of that as sarcasm. Nevertheless, you misunderstand my response. I was simply thinking that you have a very flippant view of your abilities, unpolished by the understanding everyone else in Toleste has of what is required to cultivate them, yourself. You see financial benefit and convenience for a fungible number of points and some of your abundant mana. You have no comprehension of why that is not the standard of the realm.”

I give a slight pout as I cross my arms. “That’s not not what I said …”

Intelligently, she ignores my petulant double-negative. “It does not matter. You cannot know what you cannot know, and what you cannot know cannot be held against you. It only means that you have a strikingly different perception of the world than everyone around you. I do not wish to reprimand you for that, as it was without doubt a consideration for why you were chosen in the first place.”

That puts a grin on my lips. “You were just momentarily stunned by the reminder?”

“Precisely,” she confirms with a sip of her tea. “Please do not do us both the disservice of making an expression as if that is something to be proud of. If I have any counsel at all to give on the matter, it is to request that you please strive to remember that difference, and seek not to devalue the genuine effort of others.”

I nod to that, it’s a perfectly reasonable request. It’s not like I don’t know there’s a difference just because I’ve never experienced it. I, too, after all, come from a world where growth comes from effort, even if it can’t reach superhuman levels, and I’ve seen the dirty looks Ayre shoots me when I pull a new spell out of thin air. Sure, technically, it’s still taking me effort to get the points in the first place, but I understand that’s not the same as the direct, in-kind grind everyone else has to do. Asking me to remember to have some humility is completely fair.

“This was an unintended segue, however,” Yorin admits. “While you could keep such a schedule, it does not seem as if you intend to do so. I wished to ask after any particular matters you are seeking to accomplish.”

I lean back in my chair, rocking back onto the back two feet as I hold myself from falling by propping my knees against the underside of the table, and I tap my chin as I gather my thoughts.

“Well, there’s the house, of course. The cabin is officially too small, so if we’re going to be coming back here at all, sparingly or not, it’s time to expand.”

Yorin tips her head in agreement. “It is important to have a comfortable home to come back to when one travels often, and you have already spoken of your plans for expanding your team. You also mentioned a workshop, though?”

“Yeah,” I confirm. “I gotta be honest, I need things Furinshao just doesn’t have, in no small part because it doesn’t even have the tools needed to make them. It’s not just for making better hotplates, either. There will be some of that as I get a better understanding of Tolestean engineering, but it’s also things that are going to be directly necessary for us as adventurers constantly going into ever more dangerous dungeons.”

“Such as the Noodle Spitter and young Ayre’s strange, multi-string bow you made?”

But I shake my head. “No, those are baby toys.” I ignore the way her eyes go wide at the suggestion that a semi-automatic crossbow and a bow design that increases performance by half at the same draw weight are so dismissible. “I’m already looking at radical improvements to both that will completely reframe the very concept of weapon operation and performance. And even then, the Noodle Spitter is a passion project and prototyping platform, not something I intend to use to shift the balance of a battle.”

“I am not surprised to hear that is the fate of the Noodle Spitter, but what is it that you have planned for the bow? You have already surpassed Imperial science in that regard. Surely it is enough to increase the draw weight as needed?”

The very suggestion drives me to scowl immediately, my brows furrowed in anger. “Good enough is the excuse of surrender, Yorin! It is the lustful whisper of sloth and lethargy!” I let my chair come back down on all four legs a little harder than necessary as I shove my hands against the tabletop. “Stone spears were good enough! Bone lamellar was good enough! Hide and reed were good enough! We didn’t need steel! We didn’t need Kevlar! We didn’t need denim! Is that the kind of world you would have us forge, Yorin? A world where houses remain built of stacked mud and people wash their hands with caustic ash juice?!”

Yorin holds her hands up to appease me. “Calm, Remmi! I meant your principles no affront. My question was not a suggestion of stagnation, but bewilderment at what possible manner by which the technology could truly be further advanced.”

My scowl retreats to a bit of a self-aware frown as I settle back into my chair again. “Right, yeah, that makes sense. Sorry, I guess that’s a bit of a button for me.”

Yorin gives me that warm, motherly smile. “I understand. Your world is defined by constant advancement and the pursuit of excellence. It is your ancestral answer to the absence of Heavenly Essence, even if you have been unaware of that absence. Without its support and guidance, to slow down is to court death. But Remmi, the bow?”

“It also meant we had to learn about how the world actually operates, instead of somebody having the gall to go, Because magic!” I realize I’m grousing a bit, but I can’t help it. “We didn’t get solutions handed to us just because we were good boys and girls who spent enough time reading the right books.”

Yorin gives another sigh as she tries again to pull me back on topic. “Remmi, there are times appropriate for asking how the bridge supports its burden, and there are times where it is of greater importance to simply cross it as quickly as possible.”

“Counterweights, by the way.”

“Remmi Lee, cease.”

“Right, right, the bow, let's see…”

I lean back again, the odd balancing helping me gather my thoughts like a full-body fidget toy. “Well, how to start … I’m going to take a wild shot in the dark, here, and guess that the Empire doesn’t have a deep, widely-taught understanding of electromagnetics?”

Yorin’s gaze is flat once again, but not in criticism for once. “We understand electricity, and we understand magnetics. I fail to see why you are joining them into a single topic, however.”

“Because they are a single topic,” I insist. “Magnetic poles are how electric current works! You literally can’t have lightning without them!”

“Remmi, you can absolutely have lightning without magnets. Indeed, I have never, in all of my years, seen lightning move between magnets.”

“Alright, Miss Holy Priestess, how do you think magnets work, then?”

It’s her turn to think for a moment. “The contrasting nature of the material either attracts its mate or repels its like.”

“That’s what it does, Yorin. How do you think it does that?”

She holds up a finger, goes to the utensil drawer, and pulls out two spoons. She then comes back to her seat across the table from me, holding them in either hand. “Materials are magnetic because one has something the other lacks, and its very essence recognizes this lack, desiring to return to a whole state. Behold, if I strip from the spoon in my left hand in a way that does not reduce its physical mass, and transfer it to the spoon in my right hand …” After a brief moment, she brings the two spoons near to each other and they snap together immediately.

“... You ionized my silverware, Yorin.”

That puts a rare frown on her lips. “I confess that word did not translate, which is strange. If it truly describes what I did, then the concept of the word should have been connected by the Essence that translates for you.”

I try not to groan as I rub my temple aggressively with the palm of my hand. “No offense, Yorin, but that’s because you don’t understand what you did. You think you moved some intangible essence of one spoon’s being to another, and, sure, in a sense, that is what you did. Ionization is the term that describes exactly what that essence is and what its movement does, but since you don’t actually know it, there’s nothing to translate it into. Tolestean physics always come so close to being accurate that it’s downright painful how they always seem to stop just short of the finish line!”

With remarkable calmness, Yorin disconnects the spoons and sets them down on the table. “Very well, Remmi. Let it not be said that I do not have more to learn. Enlighten me.”

It’s my turn to hold up my finger, and I go digging in my storage space, eventually pulling out the three things I need: An electric stone, a length of wire, and an iron pin. I connect the wire to opposite sides of the stone - I found out early on that they work kind of like button batteries - with the middle wrapped firmly and repeatedly around the pin.

“Do you know what I’m doing?”

Her eyes are narrowed at what I’m doing like she’s trying to assemble a puzzle. “You are electrifying the pin.”

“The same thing that moves electricity through the pin,” I explain as I turn the stone on with a pulse of mana, “is what creates magnetization. It requires an understanding of the very structure of matter, called its atoms. Current flows by shifting the electrons of atoms from one atom to the next. Actually, current flows in the opposite direction of that movement, but that’s an irrelevant nitpick right now. The point being, something that is magnetic is something that has had its electrons shifted out of a balanced alignment. So, if current is the movement of electrons, and a magnetic object is one that has had its electrons moved, not only are they fundamentally connected processes, but one can be used to cause the other.”

“I see,” she nods after a moment. “You are attempting to do the same thing as I did with magical theory by using electricity, to prove your assertion of connection. But how will you prove it is magnetic? Will you make another?”

That puts a grin on my face. “Oh, that’s the fun part! Just let me prep one more little thing while we give the pin time to cook …” And I dig back into my bag for a scrap of paper. I tear a big enough piece for the pin to sit on, set it aside, and put the rest of the paper back.

“That should be long enough.” I turn the stone off again and unwrap the pin … then I place the pin, floating atop the paper, on top of my tea. As the ripples fade, the pin slowly rotates until it comes to a stop.

“... Are you trying to stick it to your tea?” Yorin asks in disbelief.

“Oh, no, the tea’s just to hold it aloft. What’s important is where it’s pointing.”

“Pointing?” she repeats, then looks across the cabin, back to the pin, then back out again, trying to figure out where it might be connected to. “... To the mountains?”

“North,” I say with absolute certainty. “It’s pointing north.”

Her skeptical frown makes a return. “How can you know that?”

“Because that’s the only place it can be pointing. Magnetic north.”

Her eyes widen as she makes the connection. “You made a compass without instilling a Discern Direction spell into an artifact.”

“The technology was critical for oceanic navigation,” I explain. “Before its development, we had to use the stars, which shifted by season and were often lost behind cloud cover.”

She looks between me and my teacup a few more times in disbelief.

“... But what does this have to do with a bow?”
