Traditionally, there were two ways to reach Riverend Town. The first was by road—following the main route from the Province Capital before taking the river road at the first major crossroads. The second was by water, either from the capital or from one of the fishing towns farther down. Traveling by river reduced the risk of bandits, but that safety came at a higher price.

Riverend had risen so quickly for a reason. Its river access made trade easier than in any other borderland town of the Clow Nation. Goods could move up and down the water far faster than they ever could by wagon, saving days and sometimes weeks. The forest nearest Riverend also favored expeditions more than the harsher approaches near Mountainend. Instead of climbing toward rougher terrain, adventurers there could push into a lower forest crossed by the river itself.

A month had passed since the golden trio last made this journey. The road was the same. Their purses were not much fatter than before. Yet they did not feel like the same people.

Each of them had changed in a different way. Cyrus had fought and helped bring down multiple beasts without becoming dead weight. Iris had improved her spellcasting and stood only a step away from her next advancement. Hakon could now treat an open wound in half the time he once needed. None of that made them impressive by Riverend standards, but it was enough to change how they carried themselves.

When they had walked this road before, bandits had ignored them because they looked too poor to be worth robbing.

Now they answered suspicious stares with fierce ones of their own.

Fortunately, the outlaws they passed did not realize the truth—that the three of them were still only copper-tier. Had they known, the road might have become much less peaceful.

Usually, staying close to the road was enough to avoid most beasts. Even so, the trio traveled in formation and watched the brush, the slopes, and the treeline with care. They had learned not to trust statistics with their lives. Unlike Mountainend, the other border towns sometimes suffered attacks from weaker roaming creatures. Those same beasts also served as training targets for low-tier adventurers, which was one of the reasons Riverend seemed so much livelier.

In truth, the trio almost wanted trouble. They finally felt ready to handle a weak copper beast. All they lacked was one foolish enough to attack them.

The Gods, apparently, were in a cooperative mood. Less than a kilometer from the road, Iris noticed movement among the bushes and high grass. A beast was waiting there, watching for an easier target than a full party. It would likely let them pass.

The trio had no intention of allowing that.

After a short discussion, they quickly agreed on a plan. Cyrus volunteered to separate from the group and serve as bait. No one was surprised by that.

They moved together first, stepping off the road and angling toward the beast’s hidden position before splitting apart.

Once they were far enough from the main path, Iris crouched and laid a strip of dry, withered flammable grass in a rough half-circle. Ever since the grey wolf hunt, they had sworn never to travel without some. That choice now proved wise again.

Then Iris and Hakon retreated toward the road, leaving Cyrus standing alone.

The warrior gripped his sword and waited.

Fortunately, he did not have to wait long.

The beast burst from cover with the confidence of a hungry predator certain it could strike before help arrived. A wild dog, lean and vicious-looking, all teeth and speed. Iris and Hakon sprinted toward Cyrus, though both knew they would not reach him before the first exchange.

The dog howled once, almost mockingly, then lunged.

Cyrus met it head-on. He kicked the beast away hard enough to spoil the attack, though not hard enough to avoid a bloody scratch in return. The dog landed, grinned, and prepared to spring again.

Then the grass behind it ignited. The beast jerked around at the sudden flare. Instinct overwhelmed aggression. Fire meant danger, and escape should come first. It tried to bolt past Cyrus, only for another line of fire to erupt in the path ahead.

Before the dog could make a third choice, Cyrus’s sword came down across its back. The blow ended the matter cleanly.

Proud beyond reason, the warrior did not even wait for the fire to die down. He crossed the smoking line himself and dragged the carcass out like he was returning from a heroic campaign rather than a roadside kill.

And so the golden trio marched into Riverend carrying their hunt, not as the same uncertain adventurers who had once left, but as a party that had begun to feel competent.

The effect lasted until they realized no one cared. Riverend remained Riverend.

They passed through familiar streets and noticed that a few new stores had opened since their last visit. The town seemed to grow in ways that defied memory, as if every return only proved how quickly it could swallow yesterday. That strange liveliness still left a bitter taste in them. The place was less intimidating now, but no less unpleasant.

They ignored the usual disdainful looks and continued toward the guild. With three floors and a broad footprint that dwarfed nearly every other building nearby, the adventurers’ guild remained the most important structure in the town and the best place to exchange monster materials for coin.

The trio had hoped to march in and go straight for the bronze advancement test. Instead, they found themselves once more in the copper queue. At least this time they were there to sell loot rather than scrape for miserable work.

Two hours later—or what felt like two days—they finally reached the desk. The attendant was a half-elf girl around their age. The moment she saw the dead wild dog, her bored expression brightened.

“Oh, look at that,” she said. “You three did a number on this one. Dorothy!”

She turned and called into the back.

A dwarf woman arrived with the expression of someone perpetually annoyed at being needed. She was middle-aged, stocky, and carried herself with the sharp practical energy of a person who spent her life evaluating other people’s mistakes.

“Humph,” Dorothy said, crouching by the carcass. “Amateurs. You could have earned two more coins if the fur were intact. Such a weak beast, and you still had to carve across the best side...”

“Dorothy, be nice,” the half-elf attendant said. “For all we know, this was their first one.”

That was meant kindly, but it somehow made the trio feel worse.

“Humph,” Dorothy repeated. “Eight silver is already more than this sloppy job deserves. Next time, pay attention to where the finishing blow lands.”

Eight silver for an unexpected roadside kill was already good fortune.

The trio left the guild in better spirits and a little richer than before. That was certainly a new experience.

They spent the rest of the afternoon buying Anne’s requested materials and a few extra things for Rick. They tried to haggle, out of respect for Anne’s coin if nothing else, but Riverend’s merchants had far more experience in that art than they did. The trio walked away with most of the list completed, somewhat lighter purses, and the growing suspicion that every trader in town had laughed internally at them.

By the time evening approached, they were still short of a few items. So they decided to leave the rest for the next day and look for an inn.

Not long ago, they would have had to rent space in a stable to sleep at all. Now, thanks to Anne’s commission, they could afford two rooms in a tolerable inn. That felt almost luxurious.

Rather than search for a new place, they returned to one they already knew. It was located in one of the poorest and roughest parts of town, but the owner was kind, the meals were hearty, and familiarity in Riverend helped more than comfort did.

As they neared that district, the trio tightened formation instinctively. The streets narrowed. The buildings sagged closer together. Looks grew hungrier. Here, their safety depended less on public order and more on appearing inconvenient to bother. So they kept moving at a steady pace, stayed close, and avoided giving anyone a reason to test them.

Unfortunately, the Gods had apparently decided they had done enough cooperating for one day. Only a few hundred meters from the inn, a scene in a dark alley demanded their attention.

Someone was on the ground being kicked by a group of three.

For a moment, the distance and poor angle made the victim hard to identify. Then the half-elf features became clear, and so did the face.

It was the guild attendant. The nice one.

The trio slowed without meaning to. Then came the real problem.

If they intervened, they risked the task, their supplies, their own safety, and possibly their ability to leave Riverend in one piece. If they ignored it, they would be walking past one of the few people in town who had shown them decency.

No one spoke. None of them needed to. The same question rose in all three minds at once.

What would the bookstore owner tell them to do?
