Inside the hollow of the great tree, Arthur ran a thumb over the cool, stone-like surface of the Mask of Infinite Faces. He'd already rummaged through one of the stolen backpacks, and found a small, polished steel mirror. Now, it was time to see what his prize could really do.

A slow smile touched his lips. He lifted the mask, and as it neared his face, it seemed to melt away. Using his mana as a conduit, it bonded to his skin in a seamless, invisible layer. He held up the small mirror, his reflection staring back at him, unchanged.

He pushed aside the lingering adrenaline, his focus narrowing to a single point. The command surfaced, not as a thought, but as an instinct: Become.

He pictured the face of Hark, the last member of Rowler’s squad to fall: a man with a lean face, a small, faded tattoo of a raven’s feather just below his eye, and unremarkable brown eyes. Mana flowed from his spark, weaving into the mask’s matrix. A strange, tingling pressure spread across his face, like clay being gently molded by unseen hands. His cheekbones sharpened, his nose broadened, and his brow thickened. Even his hair shifted, darkening from its natural brown to Hark's black, the texture turning coarse beneath his fingers. He ran a hand over his new features, the sensation alien and exhilarating. He cleared his throat, and a voice deeper and rougher than his own emerged. "Incredible." The cost of the transformation had been trivial, a mere 200 mana from his pool of 500.

A flicker of cold satisfaction moved through him. He focused again, the image of Rajak, the cruel Guardian from Darvaza, burning in his mind. The transformation was faster this time. The face in the mirror shifted, becoming longer, crueler, the eyes glittering with the same casual sadism Arthur remembered so vividly. He stared at the reflection of his sworn enemy, a visceral hatred coiling in his gut. He realized then that the mask was a powerful tool for infiltration, but one that came with its own set of dangerous limitations. The initial excitement of the power cooled, replaced by the cold, hard logic of his training. A new face was a liability if the man wearing it walked, talked, and fought like someone else entirely. The mask was a beautiful, intricate trap for the unwary.

“A fun little dress-up game,” Drogan’s voice boomed in his head, his rage having subsided into dripping disdain. “Are you planning to scare your enemies to death with ugly faces? Or perhaps you’ll start a traveling theater troupe? ‘The Broken Class Players,’ has a nice ring to it.”

Arthur didn’t ignore him this time. A warrior of Drogan’s stature wouldn’t understand the subtleties of espionage, but a master of strategy could be made to see the value in any tool.

Master Drogan, he projected back, his mental tone calm and respectful. A sword is useless if you can’t get close enough to swing it. This is not a toy. It is a key. It will let me walk through the front gates, find my enemies, and learn their weaknesses. Then, I will use the strength you have taught me to break them.

There was a long silence from the remnant. For a moment, Arthur thought he might have actually rendered the Skullbreaker speechless. Then, a low grumble echoed in his mind. Hmph. A key to open the door before you kick it down. I suppose there’s some merit to that. But don’t mistake cleverness for strength, boy. The breaking part is what matters.

And I will break them, Master, Arthur projected back, his tone unwavering. But a skull is much easier to crush when its owner doesn’t see you coming.

Drogan let out a final, exasperated sigh. Fine. Play your shadow games. Just don’t get so caught up in your disguises that you forget how to fight.

The remnant fell silent after that, leaving Arthur to his thoughts. The conversation was closed. Drogan would never fully approve of his methods, but he had at least accepted them. For now.

He slipped out of the hollow and began to pace at the base of the great tree, his mind a whirlwind of variables. The mask handled the face, the voice, the superficial shell. But what about the rest? The gait, the posture, the subtle tics and habits that were as unique as a fingerprint? What about the way a man held his sword, the specific system skills he favored, the casual slang he used with his comrades? And the most significant hurdle of all: memories. Without the target’s history, he was a hollow man, an imposter who would be exposed with the first question about a shared past.

But these were problems he had solved a hundred times before. Brute force was for men like Drogan. Espionage was an art form, and Arthur was a master. He had a lifetime of experience in becoming other people, of shedding one identity and slipping into another. The mask just made the physical part easier. The rest was tradecraft.

He briefly considered the caravans pulled by the massive bison-like beasts. Hiding among the cargo was a classic infiltration technique, but he quickly dismissed it. The merchants and drivers were never exposed, staying within their covered wagons. He had no way of knowing how many guards were inside, how to navigate the caravan's social structure, or what he would do once he was unceremoniously dumped inside the city walls. There were too many variables, too many chances for a fatal mistake.

With the caravans dismissed as too risky, he turned his focus to the only other viable option: the adventurers. He closed his eyes, sinking into a meditative state. He didn’t reach for mana; he reached for memory. For the past two weeks, he had been a ghost in the woods. While his primary focus had been hunting monsters, he had also taken the time to observe the professional adventurers who operated near the city of Copperpine. He had watched them all, cataloged them, and filed them away. Now, he opened those mental dossiers.

His unremarkable height and build were a strategic advantage he understood from his past life, turning him into a deliberate blank slate. Finding a physical match wouldn’t be the hardest part. The difficulty lay in the details.

He mentally sorted through the adventurer parties he’d observed. His mental checklist was swift and ruthless. The low-tier adventurers who celebrated a single monster core were too provincial; their reaction was a clear sign of their low standing in the city's social hierarchy, making them useless for his purposes. He needed a mid-tier professional. Someone with status but without the suffocating scrutiny that came with nobility.

That left two promising teams. The first was a group of five he’d robbed blind after siccing a few Grubtusks on them. He recalled their faces, their skills, their interactions. They were competent, but predictable. The second was a four-person team he’d only observed from a distance, a more seasoned crew with better gear and a more disciplined formation. Both had members who fit his physical profile. Both were viable.

Arthur opened his eyes, the gloom of the forest feeling less like a sanctuary and more like a staging ground. He now had a solid plan with a list of viable targets. Now, he just needed to find one of them.

He stood, a new, colder purpose settling over him. The Great Hunt may have ended, but his personal hunt was just beginning. He was no longer a hunter of monsters. He was a hunter of identities. And his prey was out there, walking through the forest, completely unaware that his life was about to be stolen.
