CHAPTER 26

“The system gave us thirty-eight Ether for the kill. The system did not distinguish between the man and the fox.”  - The Ether Atlas: Field Notes of Steven Parr, London

There were five of them. Three men and two women with scavenged weapons: a claw hammer, a garden machete, a cricket bat with nails through it, and two kitchen knives. They walked through the gap in the barricade like they owned the block of flats.

Arthur stepped past Geoff. The Bullguard Shield held in front though the crowbar stayed at his belt.

The leader was the bat man. He was the biggest, loudest of the group, holding the nailed cricket bat across his body like a sceptre. The woman to his right held the machete with a confidence that said she'd already used it. The other three looked like backup. Though backup with blades could cause trouble of their own.

"You should leave," Arthur said.

The bat man’s attention snagged on the shield and the faint blue glow on its surface. He smiled at Arthur and gave the bat an idle twirl. He had no doubt discovered that without law, fear made everything free.

“Hand over the grub and there won’t be any bother. Try being clever and we’ll take the fucking lot.”

“Mate, look around you…..there are empty shops all over the place. There’s no need to take from people who’ve got nothing.”

“Nothing? They’ve got tins, medicine, blankets. That’s not fucking nothing.” The bat man’s smile widened. “Plenty worth taking. Isn’t that right, Geoff?”

Geoff said nothing. He'd given up supplies twice already. He hated himself for it, and he'd do it again.

The bat man shifted his weight, waving the bat in front of him. “You think we want to be doing this? There are things out there eating people. This is how you stay alive, lad. Take what you need and keep moving.”

Arthur heard the fear underneath the performance. The man was terrified like everyone else. He'd just decided to terrify others to calm his own nerves.

But any lingering physiological issues didn't matter right now since the woman with the machete was circling right, while one of the others with a kitchen knife was drifting left, towards the area where the mother sat with her sick child.

"Walk away," Arthur said. "Last time I'm saying it."

"Or what? Shield Boy's going to –"

The machete woman moved. She came in fast and direct with a lateral swing aimed at Arthur’s unshielded side. Arthur pivoted, triggering Structural Reinforcement and caught the machete on the Bullguard. The force rebounded up the machete’s blade and the woman’s wrist snapped backward. She screamed.

The bat man came in swinging. Compared with the guardians, this was almost embarrassingly straightforward. Arthur caught the swing, the nails shrieking against the shield's surface. The man overcommitted, his momentum carrying him into Arthur’s reach. Arthur pulled the crowbar free and hit him across the ribs.

A forged steel bar driven by seventeen Strength into a ribcage that was not built for the force. The bat man folded, went down and he didn't get up.

The flanker with the kitchen knife had committed to her angle towards the mother and child. Donna's Reflex Acceleration read the trajectory before the woman finished her first step. The flat of the Conduit-Edge Axe caught her forearm and the knife spun into the dark. Donna followed through with a kick to the knee that dropped her.

The woman with the broken wrist was staggering back with terror on her face. The man with the claw hammer looked at the leader on the ground and ran. The youngest raider, barely twenty, dropped his knife and followed.

Four seconds. It took four seconds to make this party of thugs run in abject panic.

Arthur released the Reinforcement. The paving beneath his feet was cracked in the same radiating pattern as the marble in Temple Church. He looked down at the leader of the group and found that he was not breathing.

He read the failure before it could stop himself. Catastrophic structural collapse of the thoracic cage. Unsurvivable. He’d known the force would be lethal and he’d hit him anyway, because the flanker was moving towards a four-year-old, and the bat man was between Arthur and the space he needed to be in to cover Donna’s approach from the two other goons behind the bat man. So he’d made a decision about a man’s life.

The weight of it arrived slowly, it wasn’t crushing like he had read in books or seen in movies. He knew if the child or Donna or Steve were in danger he would do it again without hesitation. The weight was more like an acknowledgement of the fact that he was willing to do certain things that he would not have thought possible before the world changed.

He found Donna was beside him.

"He was loading the next swing towards Geoff, after he hit your shield," she said. "The bat was already shifting to a horizontal arc. If you hadn't dropped him, Geoff would have got a nailed cricket bat across the temple."

"I know."

"Then stop carrying it like you had a choice."

Arthur looked at her. "You don't carry yours?" he asked.

"I carry all of mine. I just carry them where they don't affect my grip."

She turned to the disarmed flanker who was still clutching her forearm and shoved her towards the exit. "Take your friend with the wrist and go. Come near this camp again and I won't use the flat."

People were huddled around the courtyard looking at them and whispering. Geoff's mouth was working but no sound came out. The woman with the sick child had pulled her daughter close, and she was looking at Arthur with an expression he'd only ever seen directed at firefighters and soldiers.

Then came the notification.

THREAT NEUTRALISED

ETHER ABSORBED: 38

INTEGRATION: 318 / 470

It was the same format as the foxes in the alley. The same format as the rats in the Printworks. The same words, counting with the same un-detached precision.

Archie's geometry brightened with the pulsed spike that accompanied new data. Arthur raised a finger at Geoff and the party moved away from the camp.

"Ether yield from a human host is approximately three point two times the yield from comparable-mass Ether-fauna at equivalent ambient saturation." His voice was flat. "The differential reflects passive Integration accumulation. Humans are efficient receptacles."

Arthur stared at the notification. It hadn't flagged the kill as different. Hadn't distinguished between a mutated monster and a human.

His brain told him he should have more of a reaction to this revelation, but his hands were still steady. That was the part that would keep him up at night, the steady hands. The system counting Ether while his pulse barely shifted.

Steve was standing very still looking at the notification.

"Thirty-eight," he said. It was quiet enough that only Arthur and Donna heard. "From one person. Three times what the rats gave us and every……every survivor with the genetic compatibility is absorbing Ether. Whether they fight or hide. They're all integrating.”

“Correct. Although the rate of progression for those who fight is magnitudes higher.” Archie said.

"What's stopping someone from deciding that killing people is faster than killing monsters?" Steve whispered.

Arthur looked at Archie. The geometry hovered above his left shoulder, invisible to every pair of eyes in the camp.

"Nothing," Archie said.

The reply sat there between the three of them until Steve finally spoke.

"Sixty-two people in this courtyard, Arthur. Sixty-two. None of them fighting but all of them absorbing. If someone with a weapon and no conscience walked in here and –" He stopped. He didn't need to finish.

Donna cut in, “Park it. We deal with what's in front of us."

Arthur could see it in Steve's eyes, fear of a future where every survivor camp, every refugee group, every huddle of unarmed civilians was a reservoir to be drained by anyone strong enough and ruthless enough to do the draining.

They walked back in and Geoff approached. The wariness was gone.

"What do you need?"

People brought things. The offerings coming freely. Food they'd been hoarding. A medical kit. A pair of hiking boots. This came from the instinct that said: these are the people you want on your side.

Geoff came up and asked if they were staying.

"No," Arthur said and his face fell. Arthur knew what Geoff was thinking. Four seconds of violence had shifted the camp's odds substantially.

"I’m sorry. We can't stay," Arthur said. "But I can tell you something worth more than us being here. The creatures, the monsters that you have been seeing, when you kill them, you absorb energy. The blue text tracks it. It is not a hallucination. The more you fight, the stronger you get. Stop avoiding them. Start hunting the small ones. As a group. Together."

"You're telling us to go pick fights with monsters."

"I'm telling you that hiding has a shelf life. The things out there are getting stronger and you need to keep pace." He paused. "Listen, start with the rats. They're fast but they're fragile. Five people with weapons, in daylight and in open ground. Don't chase them into buildings and don't split up. Kill one and you'll feel the difference. Then you'll understand."

Geoff was quiet for a long time. "My daughter's in Lewisham. She's twenty-three, works at a school. I haven't been able to –" He stopped and then started again. “You reckon I can manage Lewisham, if I get stronger?"

"You can get wherever you need to go mate," Arthur said. "But not by hiding behind walls."

Geoff nodded as did several others behind him. Arthur had given them a goal, something to aspire to, a way to stop hiding and start becoming more.

Donna did a last check around the perimeter and walked off nodding to Arthur and Steve. They followed.

Behind them, the camp was changing, people were talking and planning. The weight of the apocalypse that sat on them was redistributing.

They walked, each in their own thoughts. Arthur was thinking about transit platforms and unknown destinations and a dead man who’d been worth thirty-eight Ether. About a flat in Bermondsey with a lease that wouldn’t expire for another eight months, and a coffee mug on the counter he’d forgotten to wash.

The Thames shimmered violet in the fading light. The Embankment waited.

I have been thinking about Archie’s silence for a few hours. He could have said “The system penalises human kills.” He could have said “Candidates who harvest humans are flagged.” He could have said anything that closed this particular door. He said “Nothing” and stopped talking.

The obvious conclusion is that the System permits it.

The System measured a transfer of Ether and reported the amount. We supplied the rest. Threat neutralised sounds like approval. Ether absorbed sounds like reward. The number appeared, went up and used the same language games have trained us to associate with success.

But a measurement is not a judgement. A smoke detector does not penalise arson. A ledger does not object to theft.

The System may not distinguish between murder and self-defence because it has no category for either. It recognises a living receptacle, the end of that life and the movement of stored energy. We cannot expect machinery to provide morality or treat its silence as permission. The category error is ours.

And this only makes it worse. A prohibition built into the System would enforce itself. A human prohibition requires agreement, trust and consequences, three things currently in shorter supply than food.

One of the first boundaries civilisation must rebuild is simple: you cannot kill just because you are strong enough to kill. And the first law of the new world may have to be even simpler: A person is not a resource.

That boundary was maintained by police, courts, prisons and the shared expectation that tomorrow would resemble today. Most of that disappeared in an afternoon. In its place is a blue number hanging in the air.

Every survivor camp is going to need walls to keep the creatures out. It will also need a rule strong enough to keep the people inside from seeing one another as stored Ether. The System will not draw that line for us. We will have to draw it ourselves, then defend it from everyone who finds the arithmetic more persuasive.
