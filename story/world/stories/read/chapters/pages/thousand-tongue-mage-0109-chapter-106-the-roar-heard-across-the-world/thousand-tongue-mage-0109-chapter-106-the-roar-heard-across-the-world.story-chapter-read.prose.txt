The phrase came from the Royal Capital Guard standing beside her in a tongue that didn’t belong to the Divine Capital. It was an old, distant dialect, but she vaguely recognized it as the Runasimi dialect from the far east anyways.

“Kahy shuku balbah,” he whispered.

Hell is here.

Kita didn’t need Zora’s thousand tongues to understand that much. She only needed her eyes to know she was witnessing the opening scene to humanity’s death.

Below the northern earthen wall’s ramparts, the Divine Capital had become a burning hell. Fires crawled along rooftops in uneven lines, bright as fresh wounds, while currents of smoke rolled between streets and courtyards, the sound of panic rising and falling like waves—screams cresting, breaking, then reforming farther away as people ran into each other, ran into dead ends, and ran into the wrong kind of soldiers. Kita watched one of the undead limp through a market street and lift his rifle. A family bolted from a doorway. The undead didn’t hesitate. The shots cracked, the family folded, and the undead kept limping away as if the world had nothing in it worth reacting to.

Suddenly, new sounds threaded in between the sounds of carnage: ropes creaking. Metal hooks biting into stone. Kita didn’t have to turn around where she knelt to feel hundreds of Reunification Army soldiers throwing themselves up over the edge of the wall, faces streaked with mud and eyes bright and burning with adrenaline for finally having made the ascent. Most of them already had their bolt-action rifles chambered and raised, and almost all of them pulled the trigger the moment they hauled themselves over the edge—but then those soldiers saw the Royal Capital Guards, their enemies, staring blindly at the city behind the walls, and their rifles lowered.

Their mouths parted in the same way everyone else’s did, and the air went quiet once more as even the newly arrived Reunification Army realized the same thing at once.

Their civil war had been consumed by something larger, older, and much, much hungrier.

A handful of Regional Lords climbed up after Reunification Army troops. They took one long look at the burning capital—at the undead squads threading through streets and the Giant-Class ants bursting out of the ground like blisters—and then they looked quickly through the men surrounding them.

It didn’t take them long to find Kita kneeling there. It took them even less time to find Zora standing next to her.

“Where are the other lords who went into the Divine Temple with you, Thousand Tongue?” one of them asked, voice hoarse. “What happened here? Where is Lord Baya?”

If Zora heard them speaking to him, he didn’t show it. It was Sora standing behind him—still clutching her notebook to her chest—who slowly shook her head, making the Regional Lords go still.

Then all of them went back to staring at hell again.

… Kahy shuku balbah.

You’re from the empire, too.

Does that phrase mean nothing to you?

Because while the flames of the city were strong, all men were equally captivated by the ferocity with which the Worm Mage slaughtered his way through the carnage.

Below, the boy appeared, vanished, and reappeared over and over with his wormholes tearing through the air. He speared a Giant-Class ant through its head, yanked his diamond bayonet free, and warped to the next one before the first could even finish twitching. He didn’t shout or scream like the living soldiers left down there. He didn’t posture like he was going to do something only to stand completely still. He didn’t look around to see who was staring at him. He simply kept going, sliding from kill to kill with a wave of cold mist trailing after him, and that…

That didn’t make any sense to Kita whatsoever.

She hated how much she envied him for that, because when she looked inside herself, all she found was a long line of moments where she’d failed to stand.

Ten years ago, when her mother and sister had died, she’d been a girl hiding under a carriage with her nails in her mouth, breath trapped in her throat so tightly she’d barely been alive at all. Half a year ago, she’d been a book-smart, battle-useless heiress on the train to Nohock Ik’Balam. Three months ago, she’d been an insignificant soldier in the fight to stop Eria from rampaging across the Grand Ayapacha Military Academy.

A mere ten minutes ago, her father…

Her father.

She clenched her jaw until it hurt.

How do you do it?

Nobody’s pushing you from behind or pulling you from the front, forcing you to go forward.

What do you, a mere soldier, have that I don’t?

Or was it simpler than that?

Maybe he didn’t have anything she lacked. Maybe it was everyone else on the ramparts who had something he didn’t have.

How are you so fearless?

Why can you fight like you have nobody to go home to?

Then someone tapped her shoulder, snapping her out of her spiraling thoughts.

She turned slowly, half-expecting more bad news, but instead saw Ifas holding a folded cape in his hands as if it were something fragile, something that might break if he gripped it too tightly. The deep orange fabric caught the firelight from the burning city below, and when he unfolded it fully before her, she felt her breath hitch.

Stitched into the center of the cape was the honored crest of the Attini Empire, woven in gold: a crown formed of interlocking ants.

“A few days before we froze Eria in the east, she woke up for a bit,” Ifas said casually. “Properly woke up. And the first thing she asked us was where you were.”

Kita’s fingers curled at her side.

“When we told her you weren’t there,” he went on, glancing down at the cape, “she looked… sad. Just for a moment. Then she asked for a sewing needle and some fabric.” He shrugged. “She worked on this whenever she had the strength. Said it was for you. She also told us to make sure you’d get it eventually, but, well, between recruiting soldiers for the civil war and everything that was going on in between, I kinda forgot that Zora had given it to me to hold onto until just now.”

“... Why?” she whispered. “Why… a cape?”

Ifas looked at the cape again.

Then he gave another small, helpless shrug.

“She said the heroes in her storybooks always wore capes. The Worm Mage does, too, with his flower-patterned cape,” he said. “Maybe she figured you’d get some strength out of it.”

Something hot twisted behind her eyes, and her throat trembled as she reached out for the cape, hands shaking despite her effort to still them.

The stitching was immaculate. Careful. Loving. She could almost see Eria bent over the fabric, stubbornly refusing to rest as she begged for just a few more hours of lanternlight to finish her craft.

The Worm Mage… also wore a cape, huh?

She wasn’t the Worm Mage, though. She couldn’t be endlessly fearless like him. She couldn’t walk forward into impossible odds without hesitation, and she wasn’t strong enough to carve an entire path through the city by herself.

… But the First Divine Empress hadn’t fought alone either. She’d gone south to north, gathering as many soldiers as she could, before making the long march south to build the great earthen walls.

What was that you said to me, Zora?

Shared courage is double the courage, and shared fear is half the fear.

If that was true…

If that was really true…

With a proud flourish, she rose to her feet and swung the cape around her shoulders. Her fingers found the clasps by instinct and fastened them to her light pauldrons, one after the other, hands steady even as her legs trembled beneath her. At that exact moment, a wind swept across the capital—hot with smoke and ash—and it caught the cape, snapping it out behind her so it billowed wide and heavy.

The embroidered crest of the Attini Empire rippled like it was alive.

She was still shaking. Fear crawled under her skin, tight and relentless, but she didn’t let it stop her. She marched to the very edge of the wall, drew her twin sawtooth blades, and then sharpened them against each other until the metal screamed—a harsh, tearing sound that cut through the stunned quiet of the ramparts.

Heads turned. Soldiers—Reunification Army and Royal Capital Guard alike—found their eyes drawn to her back as she lifted one blade and pointed it south, towards the distant southern earthen walls.

“Look south!” she roared. “That wall is the last breath of the Attini Empire! If it breaks, this city dies, and if it dies, the land dies after it! There will be no retreat, no second capital, and no tomorrow to argue over who was right!”

She took another step forward, one foot over the edge of the wall.

“Right here—right now—we will decide what humanity is worth!” she continued. “We alone decide whether we are the bugs who are stamped upon, or the boot that does stomping! The Attini Empire will not be held by prayers or hesitation! It will be held by blood, teeth, and by our hands that refuse to let go!”

And her blade trembled with the force of everything she was forcing into it.

“To all men still capable of wielding a blade: descend!” she screamed. “We shall flood the streets, carve a path through fire and chitin, and assist the Worm Mage in reaching Regalia!”

With that, she slashed the back of her palm without hesitation. Crimson blood welled, and from it spilled her army ants—hundred and thousands of them pouring over her arm, across her shoulders, swarming her cape until it writhed and thickened into a living cape of chitin and raw ferocity.

She threw her head back and bellowed, voice cracking stone and courage alike.

“FOR THE ATTINI EMPIRE!”

Then she ran—and leapt—hurling herself from the hundred-metre wall into smoke, flame, and screaming wind, diving straight into the burning capital to follow Enki into battle.

Her fear went with her.

But it was no longer hers alone, for she had faith the Attini Empire would share it with her.

Kita Salaqa vanished over the edge of the wall like a banner torn free from its pole, and for a breath, the ramparts were nothing but watching—Royal Capital Guards with soot-streaked cheeks and Reunification Army men with mud on their lashes, all of them staring down at the Heiress’ living cape of army ants.

Zora heard it all.

He heard the wind snap her cape outward as she fell. He heard the sudden skittering legs of her army ants as she whipped her cape down, making it thicken beneath her like a living net. He heard her land on a street with no bones cracking, no ugly thud. The writhing cushion of army ants beneath her took the impact for her, and then she surged forward, snatching her cape off the ground and slinging it over her shoulders in one swift motion.

He heard her blades.

Twin sawtooth edges screamed as they bit into the first Giant-Class ant that tried to climb a burning cart. Kita’s feet hit the street and never stopped moving. She carved a path through smoke and mandibles and giant legs, her cape whipping behind her like a war-standard made alive. Every slash was answered by a wet crunch. Every bug she came across was answered by a decapitation. She fought as though she were trying to cut her own grief out of the world… but the men on the ramparts still weren’t following her.

He heard a Royal Capital Guard nearby whisper to another, “She’s going to the southern wall,” as if saying it aloud might make it less true.

He heard a Reunification soldier answer, too quietly, “Then she’s already dead.”

Regalia. Calamity. Greater Insect God. Now they were all staring at Kita the same way they were staring at Enki, because they still didn’t believe Regalia could be stopped.

They didn’t believe the same way he didn’t, until he saw Enki.

… So he thumped his staff and cleared his throat, making every man around him flinch and turn.

“In the Records of Yawa Qhapaq’s Codex,” he said, “it was said that the First Divine Empress was not born from gold. She was a carpenter’s daughter, born from sawdust and starvation, who then built an empire with her own two hands. Her name was Tika Kuntur-Ñusta.”

He spoke with all the strength he had, and let the wind carry his words down the entire length of the wall. He let it roll through the ranks. He let it wake the men who were trying to sleep while standing.

“In the Records, it was said that she had two daughters!” he continued. “The first daughter—wild as a storm-bird, unruly as a young queen in a world that wanted her obedient—was exiled, cast out, and refused from her own household. The second daughter, then, inherited everything the first daughter was supposed to inherit. Her name was Tika Anta-Ñusta, and she was the Second Divine Empress.”

He angled his staff towards the burning city as he continued reciting names.

“After her came her daughter, Tika Anka-Ñusta, who became the Third Divine Empress, and when she perished, her younger sister, Tika Qori-Ñusta became the Fourth Divine Empress!” he continued. “Tika Chaska-Ñusta inherited the throne after the Fourth Divine Empress’ untimely death, and as we knew it, she has been ruling the Attini Empire for the past ten years as the Fifth Divine Empress, but now she, too, has perished!”

A ripple went across the wall—denial trying to lift its head and failing.

“The Fifth Divine Empress was taken from you!” he shouted. “Taken by Regalia long, long ago! With the death of Tika Chaska-Ñusta, the bloodline of the second daughter is finished! Cut clean! There are no more daughters of the divine bloodline to inherit the throne!”

He paused just long enough for every soldier’s mind to reach the same dead end.

Then he glanced around, head lilting.

“…Or is that really true?” he said, reaching under his cloak and retrieving a small notebook: the one he’d had Sora find and read for him out of pure curiosity. “For in the Records of Tarpuy Sumaq’s Codex, it was said that the first daughter of the Divine Empress did not vanish into nothing. Exile does not erase blood. It only hides it, and blood always remembers where it came from.”

He turned his head towards the sound of Kita’s blades below.

“The first daughter of the First Divine Empress was named Tika Amaru-Ñusta, and after she was exiled, she married into the family of Inti Sara-Q’umer, a small Outer Regional Lord in the Nohoch Region!” he shouted, throwing the records over his shoulders and letting a Royal Capital Guard catch it. A dozen heads immediately crowded around the man as everyone tried to read it. “She took the Inti Household name, and her sole daughter was Inti T’ika-Q’umer, who later married into the family of Tayta Killa-Wayra! Tayta Orpi-Inti was then her sole daughter, who later married into the family of Salaqa Baya-Xibal! The Salaqa Lord then had two daughters, only one of whom survived an assassination attempt on their lives ten years ago!”

Then he shouted her full name, and it came out like a coronation forced through fire.

“Salaqa Kita-Xibal is the daughter of Tayta Orpi-Inti, who was the daughter of Inti T’ika-Q’umer, who was the daughter of Tika Amaru-Ñusta, the first daughter of the First Divine Empress!” he bellowed. “Kita Salaqa is the lost blood of the First Divine Empress, and by right of succession, by right of blood, now that your throne has no one else to sit upon it…”

He paused.

“…Kita Salaqa is your rightful Divine Empress,” he whispered. “So what are all of you standing here for?”

As the Royal Capital Guards passed the notebook amongst themselves, whispers began spreading like wildfire. Heads snapped towards him. Spines straightened without permission. Men who’d been staring at death like it was inevitable suddenly looked like they’d been insulted.

He fanned the flames.

“Kita Salaqa—your Sixth Divine Empress—has charged into the fray!” he roared, and his calm was gone now, replaced by the kind of fury that made cowards feel ashamed of their lungs. “Will you falter now? Will you watch her bleed alone? Will you let your Divine Empress die once again because you were too afraid to move your feet? Is this all the fire the Attini Empire can muster?”

He slammed his staff into the stone beneath him, and from the tip of his staff, a dozen fireworks tore into the sky like spears of light—hot, savage arcs that split the smoke above the walls and burst open in violent blossoms. They exploded overhead in all directions, and though he couldn’t see them, he just knew they illuminated all fearful faces, turning every man on the ramparts into a silhouette carved from flame.

Zora held his staff to the sky, growling through his teeth.

“This is my fire, and mine alone!” he roared. “What about yours, Attini Empire?”

For a heartbeat, nobody said anything.

Then the northern earthen walls erupted.

Not one voice. Tens of thousands, soon to be hundreds of thousands, split with a roar that started in the throat and ended in the bones. Soldiers picked up their rifles and remembered they were made to be held. Blades came free from their sheaths with unruly hisses that sounded like beasts baring its teeth. The mighty cannons on the wall—still warm from firing at their fellow humans—began to turn, pivoting inward towards the true enemy inside the walls. Ropes were thrown down. Mushroom scaffoldings were shoved over the edge. Men began descending in waves, and as they moved, the chant rose with them, swelling until it stopped sounding like a chant and began sounding like a storm.

“FOR THE ATTINI EMPIRE!”

Zora smiled as he heard the empire wake up.

For even if not all of them believed in his words—or in the bloodline he claimed some girl they didn’t know still carried—what they saw was a single heiress willing to descend into hell for the empire’s sake, and what they wanted to believe was that they could win.

All they’d needed was just a little push forward, and his tongue was more silver than any other tongue in the world.

Today, as the Attini Empire stood united for the first time in a decade, they gave a roar that could be heard across the world.
