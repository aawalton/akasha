Vir stepped back, his hand instinctively moving to his katar’s hilt. “You’re one of them.”

The words came out flat and hollow, though not as an accusation. He hadn’t quite had the time to process that yet. Rather, it was just a statement of fact. Which only made it all the worse.

Ashani didn’t even flinch. “I am,” she said, meeting his gaze, and Vir couldn’t decide what appalled him more. That she was the enemy, or that she seemed so unapologetic about it.

“Since when?” Vir asked through gritted teeth, though he already knew. “The beginning? Since we met?”

“Since long before you were born,” Ashani said gently. “I became aware of it shortly after the Fall.”

Vir’s mind reeled. Every conversation, every moment of vulnerability, every time he’d confided in her... had it all truly been a lie? She’d been there when he’d shared his grief over Rudvik’s passing, when he’d struggled with becoming the Akh Nara, when he’d leaned on her a million times. She’d guided him and supported him, and she was someone he’d been proud and honored to call a friend.

And she’d been working for the enemy the entire time.

“Vir,” Maiya said quietly, squeezing his arm. He hadn’t even realized he was shaking.

“Did you ever wonder,” Ashani asked, her voice as gentle and placid as ever, “why I sought to remain close by your side at all times?”

Because you were carrying out their agenda, Vir thought bitterly. Because I was your mission.

But Maiya’s grip tightened on his arm, and her words made Vir’s thoughts grind to a halt. “What Janak said earlier,” she said. “Remember? About how there’s nothing they can do in your presence?”

Vir blinked, the words not quite connecting. “What?”

“It was only ever a hunch of mine,” Maiya said, sounding more confident as she spoke. “But after becoming the Blessed Chosen, I was something akin to an agent of theirs, right? Against my will, without even knowing it.”

“That wasn’t your fault,” Vir said immediately, the very thought of their enemy coopting someone so precious to him without her permission filling him with anger. “You couldn’t have stopped that.”

“Right. But whenever I was near you, the burden eased.” Maiya turned to face him fully. “I thought it was because I sought comfort in you. Because you made me feel safe. But now, after what Janak said, I have to wonder.”

There was conviction in her eyes when she turned to Ashani. “What I felt was real, wasn’t it? That relief was tangible.”

“It was,” Ashani said with a nod, and to their shock, actually knelt before them. “You have my sincerest apologies for not saying anything sooner, both of you. It would have compromised the plan and doomed us all. You cannot know how I wished to confide in you so.”

“The plan.” Maiya’s eyes widened, and she grabbed Vir’s shoulder. “Vir, don’t you get it? She’s not an agent of theirs. Well, she is, but she’s also an agent of Janak’s. Aren’t you?” she asked, addressing Ashani directly. “You’ve been working with him all along. You came to Vir for the same reason I found relief near him. Because their influence doesn’t work around him.”

“You were always so perceptive, Maiya,” Ashani said, and her smile was sad but genuine. She was still kneeling and didn’t make any move to rise.

Vir’s mind was racing with a thousand thoughts, too preoccupied to be flustered by the fact that an Imperium goddess had knelt before him. “Why?” he asked finally. “Why doesn’t it work?”

“Because you were made to be that way,” Janak said, his voice carrying the weight of millennia. “To blind them. To be a weapon against them. The weapon.”

“Made?” Vir turned to the ancient god. “By whom?”

Janak’s smile was weary. “Why, by me, of course. Or more accurately, you were made by my predecessor and enabled by her efforts and mine through the millennia, shepherding the Akh Nara to his destiny. I could have done none of it without Ashani’s help, so I ask you to forgive her. As difficult as it has been for you, your plight is incomparable to what Ashani has endured all this time, bearing this secret alone.”

Vir struggled to process the ramifications of Janak’s words, and when he turned back to Ashani, his voice came out rough and hoarse. “Then you meeting me, coming with me on this journey… helping me become the Akh Nara, your hesitance to become my divine patron…”

“Every action I have taken has been in pursuit of helping Janak with his plan. I admit this freely,” Ashani said, looking up at him. There was no apology in her voice now, just an honest statement of fact. “But know that the loneliness I felt in my time alone was real. That our friendship is real.”

“You expect me to believe that’s even possible?” Vir asked. “After admitting to all of this?”

Ashani’s smile turned sad. “Can two things not be true?”

Vir opened his mouth to respond, to argue, to demand more answers, but Janak spoke before he could form the words.

“Alas, I’m afraid her sacrifice may very well have been in vain,” the ancient god said, and it was as though his weariness dyed every syllable as he spoke. “They won this war long ago.”

“Is there no hope?” Ashani asked, glancing back at Janak.

“There is always hope,” Janak said with a wry smile. “Even if it is but a fool’s hope.”

“I don’t understand,” Vir said, finally motioning for Ashani to rise, which she did, but not before giving him a concerned nod.

Janak walked slowly around the perimeter of the domed chamber, his eyes cast upon some place far away. “Every decision made is an inflection point. One that allows them to go back in time and create another realm where that decision was made differently. Months, years, even centuries in the past. This lets them predict our realm’s future by observing the outcomes in countless parallel timelines... though there are limits to their power.”

He turned to face them. “Think of them less as an intelligent enemy and more as a force of nature. They examine all possible tapestries of reality, and when any of those threads threatens them, they simply… reweave. Create a new present where that threat never existed.”

“But they can’t do that with me,” Vir said slowly, understanding dawning. “That’s why they couldn’t revert the doomed universe to their whim.”

“Correct,” Janak confirmed, and Vir breathed just a bit more easily in the knowledge that his life hadn’t been a lie. “They cannot accurately recreate you in those alternate timelines. Your presence meddles with their ability to rewrite reality and makes it impossible for them to predict your actions or anything you touch.”

“You said I was made this way,” Vir pressed. “But how? Why?”

“We’ve both journeyed far and sacrificed much to be here,” Maiya added. “Please, we deserve an explanation.”

Janak was quiet for a long moment, his gaze on some far-off place. “The original Janak, of which I am a copy, had but one wish. To save his daughter. Siya was dying of an incurable illness, and the Imperium’s best physicians were powerless to stop it. So he did what any father would when faced with the death of his child.”

The ancient god’s hands clenched. “He devoted his life to save hers. With the full might of the Imperium resources, he created a machine capable of exploring probability. Of examining alternate timelines where she lived. Hoping that the cure that allowed her to in those might be brought back to ours.”

“And he found one?” Maiya asked.

“He found several,” Janak said. “Hundreds of timelines where Siya lived, though in every one, she’d not fallen sick at all. There was no cure to be learned from because it was never needed.”

“Why?” Maiya asked, squeezing Vir’s arm unconsciously. “Why was she healthy in those timelines but not in ours?”

Janak shook his head. “We will never know, for in delving into these possibilities, my predecessor unwittingly intruded on the domain of a force so terrifying, it should never have been crossed. Those who wove the tapestry of reality itself.”

Vir felt a chill run down his spine, but it wasn’t the revelation of the Fateweavers that caused it.

Predecessor.

It was as valid as any word to refer to the other Janak, and yet…

“His actions angered them,” Janak continued. “And so they rewrote the future of our world. From one in which he found the cure that saved his daughter, to one in which his machinery failed catastrophically at the precisely wrong moment due to an imperceptible defect in its construction. The event you know as the Fall. The moment they broke the world. The moment the very girl he’d been trying to save ceased to be as she was.”

The silence that followed was heavy and suffocating, and Vir couldn’t begin to imagine what that Janak must have felt in that moment.

“But he refused to die,” Janak said. Something like pride crept into his voice, and Vir had to wonder just how much of the original Janak’s identity his copy retained. “As far as I can piece together, the explosion did not kill him immediately. In that moment when he glimpsed the timelines where Siya lived, it seems he saw something else as well. The secret of their power.”

“What secret?” Vir asked, wondering if that was the real reason the Fateweavers had retaliated so violently.

“That they’re not gods,” Janak said simply. “That they’re more akin to parasites than anything. They survive and propagate their power by feeding on crystallized moments of Fate. In their case, the moment of a being’s death. They embed fragments of their power into the souls of their victims, crystallizing the moment of their death and turning what was only a possibility into a certainty.”

“Their agents,” Vir muttered. “That’s how they create their agents.”

“I have the moment of my death carved into my soul,” Ashani confirmed. “As does every agent they’ve ever created. It’s what binds us to them. No matter our actions, no matter how much we think we operate out of our own free will, we invariably do their bidding.”

Janak nodded. “The more valuable the agent, the more of their power they embed within, the more certain the moment of death, and the tighter their control, though the victims themselves will never realize it. They may live their entire lives not knowing their Fate was preordained.”

“Then the nightmares I’ve been having,” Vir said, causing Maiya to tighten her grip on his arm.

“Their interference,” Janak said. “An attempt to undo my predecessor’s work.”

“Your predecessor?” Vir asked, eyes widening. “Then that means…”

“Indeed, it is as you guess,” Janak said. “In his final moments, dying from complications of the explosion that destroyed everything he loved, he discovered the way to carry his work into the future. By using Fate itself to combat those who control it. By embedding a shard of Fate into his soul. Not of his death, but something else. Something they could never expect.”

“What?” Vir asked.

“His daughter’s life,” Janak said quietly. “A timeline where she lived. He crystallized that possibility and bound it to his soul. And when he died, his soul lived on, carrying with it his memories and his will.” He stared Vir in the eyes. “Carried onto the next incarnation of the Akh Nara, and eventually, to you.”

Vir’s mind reeled. “The first Akh Nara was Janak.”

He’d always wondered who the seventh Akh Nara was, and how the concept had come to be. Now, he knew, but the answer left him with only more questions.

“You are a blind spot in their vision,” Janak said. “A thread in the tapestry they cannot see or control. A counterbalancing force of Fate.”

“He failed, though,” Vir said. “The memories didn’t transfer. They were lost with each passing incarnation. I didn’t even know what I was until Cirayus told me.”

“A tragic flaw that my predecessor had no chance to fix. A flaw our enemy has exploited to bring ruin to our plan. Yet Janak was no fool. In his dying moments, he siphoned the very same power they use to manipulate Fate and disseminated it into the world at large. In the form of something new. Something that had never existed before. Something that operated on a principle beyond reason. ”

“The Chakras,” Vir said, realizing where Janak was going with this. “It’s the reason Imperium magic is so vulnerable to it, isn’t it? Because it uses their power?”

“Indeed,” Janak said. “In creating them, he hoped to give the mortals who lived in the world that came after a way to fight back, though his true aim was to create mindscapes so secure that not even they could penetrate them. A space where he could pass down knowledge and messages to his successors.”

Vir looked at Janak, at Ashani, at the impossible architecture surrounding them. At the ruins of a civilization that had transcended mortal bounds and been struck down for it.

“And still, he failed,” he said. “I only got those messages a few years ago. By then, our universe was already under siege, and I would never have gotten here unless I broke through the safeguards he’d erected to prevent anyone from embarking on our journey early.”

Janak’s expression darkened, as did Ashani’s. Vir didn’t know Janak nearly as well as Ashani—or at the very least, he’d spent more time with her—and she rarely ever showed such a grave expression.

“Those safeguards,” Janak said slowly, “were in place for a reason. Because while I am pleased to see you here, I’m afraid your journey may very well have been for naught.”

“I don’t understand,” Vir replied, frowning. “You’re telling me that without obtaining all the Ultimate Arts, there’s no way to fight back?”

“We have been fighting since the beginning,” Ashani said softly. “But our enemy ensured a critical art was lost centuries ago. A battle we lost.”

“Have you never wondered at their ability to rewrite reality?” Janak asked with a small smile. “Have you not ever felt how familiar it is to something you know?”

Vir’s eyes widened in understanding. “Reality Inversion. The Iksana Lost Art, said to be able to alter events in the past, rewriting reality.”

It was true, the art was shockingly similar to the power the Fateweavers wielded.

“It is no coincidence,” Janak replied. “That art, more than any other, borrows heavily from knowledge of their power.”

“Which is why they ensured its existence was erased long ago,” Ashani completed.

Vir had wondered why Saunak lacked the blueprints to the Lost Arts. He’d assumed it was because it simply happened before his time, but now…

“Does that mean you lack the inscription as well?” Vir asked, already knowing the answer.

“Despite my best efforts to ensure copies survived, my ability to influence the world is limited. Not unlike our foe, ironically. I can only act through proxies, and only when I believe they will not notice. Nor could Ashani leave this realm without your assistance. That was no lie. She has remained trapped here for millennia.”

“I see,” Vir said through gritted teeth. “But none of this tells me what the Akh Nara is supposed to do. Maiya and I came here hoping for a solution against the Ash Tears. Is that my purpose?”

He prayed it wasn’t. For if it was, then all might be lost.

What Vir didn’t expect, however, was for Janak to bark a laugh.

“Hardly anything so minor.”

Minor? Vir thought. His universe was dying. With every passing hour, more perished from prana poisoning and from Ash Beasts flooding through unstable Ash Tears. Half died in the process, but the other half wreaked havoc until the Asuras arrived.

“No, Akh Nara, as I said, you are, and always have been, a weapon. A weapon to erase my predecessor’s gravest mistake. To excise the blight that is our enemy. To make our broken world whole once again.

“A weapon created, above all, to save my daughter, that she might live in a world uncorrupted by Ash.”

The old man extended his hand. “Come. It is time you witnessed your destiny.”
