CHAPTER 143

Grief kept private changes nothing. Grief that is shared, named and counted becomes intelligence, and intelligence is the only weapon a house without soldiers has. — Codex of the Long Watch, Operational Directive

When they reached the blue door Sarah pushed it open without bothering to knock.

“Mamá?”

Eleni came through from the kitchen with a tea towel in one hand. She stopped when she saw them and crossed herself. Sarah crossed the room, and her mother caught her by the face, kissed both cheeks and pulled her into an embrace. Yiayia appeared behind her and to Arthur she looked smaller and frailer than he had remembered.

“Panagía mou. You are safe. You came back.”

Sarah held her mother as her eyes searched the house. The children were in the shade beneath the stairs. The older one sat drawing careful lines with charcoal on a piece of red roof tile while the younger slept on a folded blanket with one fist closed against a cheek. Sarah saw them and a part of her relaxed. Both were safe.

“Mamá. Where are Petros and Maria?”

Before Eleni could answer, a chair scraped inside the kitchen and Stavros came out. He looked as though he had not slept for days. His shirt was blackened from the forge, and soot had been rubbed away in two clean streaks where he had dragged his hands down his face.

“Stavros?” Arthur said.

“I came for Petros,” the smith said. “Milos is gone.”

Sarah looked back at her mother. “Tell me.”

Eleni took her by the wrist and pulled her into the kitchen as if the walls could make the account safer. Everyone followed. Spyros came last and closed the courtyard door.

Yiayia righted a chair and sat with her hands placed on the head of her walking stick. Eleni remained standing.

“They came before the sun was fully up,” she said. “Six soldiers and one officer. They had Petros and Maria’s name on a list.”

“What list?”

“Essential work, they said. People with skill the city needs.”

“They knew about Maria’s leatherworking,” Donna said confidently. “That would fall under essential work for a new regime.”

Eleni nodded. “They took the machine too and gave me a piece of paper.”

“And Petros?”

“They knew everything,” Eleni said, her voice sharpening. “His yard. The harbour. The people he knows. They said something about Kastella and the cultists.”

Arthur felt Sarah go still beside him and remembered the Council room. General Mavros at the table, making a note after Arthur had told them Petros made the Kastella operation possible.

Eleni opened a disordered drawer and took out a folded document.

“I asked where they were taking my son. The officer gave me this as though it was a favour.”

Sarah read the document.

The page named Petros Vassilou and Maria Vassilou as civilian experts. Both had been taken under emergency essential-service authority and assigned to the Acropolis garrison. Petros was listed for harbour logistics and repairs. Maria for protective equipment and leatherwork. At the bottom of the page sat the military seal Arthur had seen at the forge weeks before. There was no other Council signature anywhere.

“They took them together?” Sarah asked.

“Together,” Eleni nodded. “Maria would not leave the children until I came down. The soldiers waited, one was Sefano’s boy from the mill. They did not touch the little ones.”

Eleni balled her hands on the table and shut her eyes.

“Petros kept telling everyone it was all right. Po po po, your brother. Six men in the courtyard and he tells me not to make a drama,” she said, her voice faltering for the first time.

“He would,” Sarah whispered.

“He told the children they were going to work on the hill and would be home soon,” Eleni said, looking towards the courtyard as if Petros were still standing there with an easy smile. “Then they searched his room, your room and the roof cistern.”

“They were looking for us,” Donna said.

“Yes,” Eleni replied. “The officer asked whether the Englishman’s shield was here or any other weapons. He said they had been ordered to make sure.”

“Make sure of what?” Steve asked.

“That we had left,” Donna said. “They knew we were gone; they were just not sure for how long and what we had taken with us.”

“They knew you went to Aegina,” Eleni said, nodding at Donna. “They asked when the boat was due back.”

The table sat in silence for a long while before Stavros slid a piece of paper across the table.

“They took Milos too,” the smith said.

“I came here yesterday because Petros knows every quartermaster and dock clerk. I thought he might know where they had put Milos.” Stavros looked at Eleni. “Petros was gone too.”

His son’s notice was almost identical. Essential-service authority. Drafted for the Acropolis garrison. Metalwork and fortification maintenance.

“They came to the forge at the same hour,” Stavros said. “Four men and a lieutenant. They took Milos for a new forge up the hill and they gave me a new list of items to make.”

His mouth twisted without humour.

“Milos told me to keep the fire hot,” he said, shaking his head.

Spyros slid his own requisition paper across the table.

“They took your boat?” Stavros asked, reading the paper.

“They chained her. Till they make up their mind, if she fishes or fights.”

“Was anyone hurt? When they took people?” Sarah asked.

“No,” Eleni said, looking at Stavros, who shook his head as well.

“What happened after they took Petros?” Arthur asked.

“I went to the civil council office,” Eleni said. “The clerk there told me civilian orders were suspended. So I asked for Antoniou and nobody will tell me where he is. They said Antoniou was not receiving petitions.”

“Who authorised this?” Arthur asked, looking across the room.

“Mavros,” Eleni said.

“And the Council went along with conscription?” Steve asked.

Yiayia spoke up for the first time. “There is no Council anymore.”

“What does that mean?” The words were out of Arthur's mouth before he realised what it actually meant.

“It’s a coup. The military wing of the Council, which is effectively Mavros, has taken over. That explains the need to control and contain useful people,” Donna said.

“I went to the lower gate on the hill. There were families there. They do not allow a crowd to form. Two at a time, ask your question, receive no answer, move on.”

“Did you see Milos?” Sarah asked.

“No. But I saw a list on the table when the guard checked his name. Two columns full of names. Thirty names, maybe more. Petros. Maria. Milos. Three men from the harbour I know. A cooper from Drapetsona. Two smiths from Athens. A woman who keeps the garrison stores.” He rubbed the heel of his hand against the table. “Useful people.”

“Were they all inside? These people, on the list,” Donna asked.

“The guard said everyone named under the order was quartered in the garrison.”

“Do they have armed escorts inside?” she asked.

Stavros shrugged. “Don’t know. He said they work by day, then sleep inside. Too many guards all over the hill.”

“They took more people from the harbour this morning,” Spyros said and everyone turned to look at him.

“Yorgos from the grain quay. The brothers who keep the west crane. Andreas the sailmaker’s nephew. Two men from the lighter crews.”

“And the rest of the Council did nothing? I know our interactions with Papadakis and Antoniou weren’t exactly friendly, but they don't seem like the kind of people to just give up,” Steve asked.

“They can’t do anything. What happened to the city happened to them first,” Eleni said.

“The woman who works at the church came here after they took Petros,” Yiayia said. “She came because she had nowhere else to go. Soldiers entered the Metropolitan’s house in the same morning. Nikephoros was taken away under guard and up the hill.”

Eleni crossed herself.

“She saw it?” Steve asked.

“Her sister works in the kitchen. She was there.”

“And what about Papadakis?” Arthur asked.

This time it was Stavros who answered.

“I met a man from the museum at the gate below the Acropolis. He was asking after her and the soldiers told him academic administration was suspended.”

“Dissolved more like,” Donna said.

“He said her neighbours saw her being led away by soldiers, same night. Then after an hour more soldiers entered her house and took away books.”

“And Antoniou’s fate was the same?” Sarah asked.

Both Eleni and Stavros nodded.

“Same night, soldiers walked him up the hill. Now there's a general and a hill, and everyone who used to run something is behind the same wall as your brother," Eleni said to Sarah.

Nobody spoke. The older of Maria's children had stopped scribbling on the tile and she sat very still, her eyes going from face to face round the table.

"Smiths. Boatwrights. Harbour hands. Leather workers. That's not who you round up to make a point during a coup," Donna said, turning an empty cup on the table. "It's a workforce. Mavros is building himself a garrison staff."

“The three Council members were Mavros taking power; the civilians are about him keeping it,” Arthur added.

“This was meticulously planned. He had been fortifying the Acropolis for weeks,” Donna said, nodding at Arthur. “The Council was taken within hours of each other and while the city slept, then important civilians were taken the next morning. New checkpoints at strategic locations to limit movement. Mavros is not an amateur.”

“The city woke up to find that every person who could give a competing order was already gone. Cincinnatus without the part where he gives the power back," Steve said.

"How many soldiers does he have?" Donna asked, sitting up. She was moving from grief to logistics.

"Many," Stavros said. "I do not count soldiers."

“Enough to hold the harbour and the hill. I know the men on the water. He has enough to hold the city," Spyros said, joining the conversation.

“So Mavros has the harbour, the roads and the Acropolis and he’s taken transport, craftsmen and anyone with useful local networks,” Donna said.

“Not everyone. Some soldiers still call it Council service. Others say the military has emergency authority. Most only care which post they have been given,” Stavros corrected.

“That’s deliberate. You don’t need every soldier to support a coup or even understand it. You only need them to obey their commanding officer and orders,” Donna said. “We will go through the events again, starting at the beginning.”

Eleni placed a plate of biscuits in front of them before setting a briki on the flame. Then they assembled the city in pieces.

“They took our names at the harbour and had moved the names to the next checkpoint before we got there. They knew the boat, the berth and the house. Then they took the boat so we couldn’t leave by the same route,” Donna said. “They’re mapping who moves through the streets. And closing movement behind the people they want to keep.”

“He was ready before we sailed. This wasn’t planned after we left; the four of us leaving was the window,” Arthur said.

The kitchen was quiet but they could hear carts moving outside. Hammers banged and men cursed somewhere beyond the courtyard. Bread was still being sold, fish was still being cleaned, and children were still drawing on broken tiles.

No Council order could challenge the military register. No civil signature could free Spyros’s boat. No archbishop could call the parishes against it. No academic wing could preserve a competing account of what had happened.

The machinery of the city continued because it only took one hand to steer it.

“They recorded us at three posts. We should assume that Mavros already knows we’re back,” Donna agreed.

“Good because we are going to the Acropolis. I’ll see what he has to say when I paralyse him from the neck down. I spent half my time with MSF dealing with petty tyrants; I’m not doing that again,” Sarah said.

Eleni turned to her. “No.”

“He has my brother.”

“And your sister. And Stavros’s son. And dozens of others.” Eleni pressed her palm over Sarah’s closed fist. “So you will not run up the hill angry and give him my daughter too.”

Sarah met her eyes. “I’m not leaving them there.”

“I did not ask you to.”

Donna gave Eleni a small nod.

“If Mavros wanted us arrested, the harbour was the easiest place to do it,” Steve said, putting Antoniou’s now powerless passage paper back into his coat pocket.

“He wants us to come to him and establish the power structure early,” Donna said.

“Or he wants to see whether we will,” Arthur said, shouldering the field pack.

“Then we go to the Acropolis and we find out which,” Donna said.
