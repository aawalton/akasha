That evening, when the Stormcrows Band gathered around our usual table in the tavern, I cleared my throat loudly to get everyone's attention.

“There’s someone I want to show you,” I announced proudly.

From my bag, I pulled out a small wooden cage filled with furry, flapping alien moths and set it on the table.

“Moths?” Juliette made a disgusted face and pushed her chair back. “That’s what you wanted to show us?”

“Not quite,” I said, grinning. It was time for my grand demonstration.

I lowered my hand into my bag, where they couldn’t see, and popped the cork off the small bottle that held the carbon monoxide gas. It wouldn’t leak too fast on its own. Then I took two steps back and began casting my magic. Now I could whisper the strange syllables instead of shouting—it sounded more like a mysterious spell and less like I was having a stroke.

“Look at the moths,” I instructed.

They all looked.

I shifted my sphere of influence with the CO gas from the bottle to the cage. After about ten seconds, the moths stopped flapping altogether.

“What happened? Why’d they stop flapping?” Charlotte asked.

Santiago reached in through the mesh and poke one. It fell limp. “They’re dead,” he said flatly.

“Yes!” I said, beaming. “Death magic!”

Everyone shifted uncomfortably.

Charlotte paled a little. Juliette looked at me strangely. Nayeem looked disturbed and started murmuring a quiet Muslim prayer.

“I mean, it’s cool, I guess,” said Santiago. But he didn’t smile. At all.

That was... not the reaction I was expecting.

“It’s not cool!” Samar snapped. “Those alien moths were cute! And you killed them just to show off your stupid magic. That was mean!”

“S–sorry,” I muttered. Honestly, I’d considered doing the demo with one of the small horned rats. If I had, Samar would never have forgiven me.

“It’s just… really disturbing to see,” said Juliette. “It looks like you’re sucking the soul right out of them.”

“Exactly!” I said, still proud. “That’s the point—it’s supposed to look like that. But it’s not really so. Do you guys know what’s actually going on?”

“I can guess,” said Hyun-woo. He didn’t look nearly as disturbed as the rest. “You’ve been working on that gas magic lately, so I’d say… you made carbon monoxide, contained it, and used your gas magic to move it into the moth cage.”

“Bingo!” I said, pointing at him. “10,000 points to Hyun-woo!”

Damn, that Hyun-woo was a sharp one. I’d thought it would take them longer to figure out. I pulled out the now-empty bottle from my bag and held it up. Everyone instinctively leaned back.

“It’s… uh… empty now,” I said. “There was only a small amount, and it’s dispersed in the room already.”

“So you’re a poison gas wizard now?” asked Samar, still angry.

“I prefer the term carbon monoxide mage,” I said with dignity. “But yes. The idea is that our enemies will think I’ve discovered some kind of soul-sucking forbidden magic art.”

“Actually,” said Hyun-woo, “that’s really smart. Good job.”

“Thanks!” I said, happy someone appreciated the effort I put in.

“That’s still horrifying,” Charlotte muttered. “I don’t see how invisible poison gas is less disturbing than soul-sucking magic.”

“Because he used science,” Santiago explain, then glanced at me apologetically. “I think it’s cool,” he added quietly.

“Will it be useful in combat?” asked Juliette.

“Oh, not at all!” I smiled. “Not yet, anyway. I need to keep practicing—increase the sphere of influence to at least… err… half a meter, make it last a full minute, increase the movement speed and range. Then maybe I can start thinking about supporting maneuvers in a real battle. Maybe… in a few months.”

“Well, that’s still good,” Juliette said. “Could give us an edge. Might make a difference someday.”

“Yeah, I hope so,” I said. “I’ve put a lot of effort into this.”

“…”

No one was saying anything.

I didn’t like it. It felt like everyone was silently trying to decide whether I was creepy or not.

“Hey Santiago,” I said, trying to change the subject, “how’s the archery going?”

“Good, actually,” he said. “Still not close to hunting level—turns out it’s unbelievably hard to hit a small target from a distance. But Lori’s teaching me. She’s actually an amazing archer. Did you know that?”

I did not.

“How’s that work? The teaching, I mean?” I asked.

“She demonstrates, and I try to mimic her. Then she says weird poetic things about posture or spirit or breath, and I try to guess what she means. Not a great teacher. But she’s cute.”

“Hey!” Charlotte said, mock-offended. “You don’t plan to replace me with an NPC, do you?”

“No way. Not until you’re at least thirty,” Santiago teased, and she punched his shoulder playfully.

It was nice that someone else was bonding with Lori. Maybe she’d start standing really close to Santiago and gifting him dead animal parts. That’d probably get him in trouble with his girlfriend—which would be hilarious.

The next day, I went to the forester’s cabin after the market run as usual. I handed Vel’nari her gear and got the day’s silver. But just as I was about to leave, something strange happened.

Elrik suddenly blurted out:

“My Lori’s bound for town with a pack full of herbs and pelts. It’s a fair road, but these days… not a safe one. If you’re heading that way, traveler, she could use a steady blade at her side.”

NPCs always caught me off guard with their weirdness.

What was this? A new rewardless quest? Why now? And what was all that nonsense about town not being safe?

I didn’t have patrol duty today, but I’d planned to put a few hours in at the clothier shop. Bah. I guess I couldn’t really refuse Elrik. They were key to our goblin trade operation, Santiago’s employers, and I even used their hunting shed for my gas magic practice. I owed them a lot.

“Sure,” I said. Then I looked over at Lori. “When are you heading out?”

No answer.

She just walked out of the cabin with her herb bag, and I had to hurry after her. Right…

She walked close to me, with a bright smile, looking even happier than usual.

I didn’t actually remember Lori ever going into town to sell stuff before, but maybe I’d just never noticed.

“Birdsong tells more than words, if you know how to listen,” she said cheerfully.

This statement didn’t make sense at all. Was that a conversation starter?

“That’s nice,” I said, smiling. “Do you like… birds?”

“…”

“If you hear three crows together, turn back. It’s bad luck.”

“I’ll try to remember that,” I said. It was nonsense, but her cheerful mood was infectious. I was actually enjoying our comical nonsense conversation.

“I’ve a song my mother taught me,” she said. “Father says I should share it with kind travelers. Want me to sing it?”

“Uh… sure.”

And then she actually started singing:

“♪ Hush, hear the whisper, the forest takes breath,Wind through the branches, no shadow of death. ♪♪ Spirit of greenwood, so gentle, so kind,Guide every traveler, guard every mind. ♪”

Lori was…

A disaster of a singer.

I wasn’t Mozart myself, but her singing was monotone. No rhythm. Totally off-key.

And yet, she was so happy doing it. She kept going.

“♪ River runs silver, the moon’s gentle light,Owls keep their vigil through velvet of night. ♪♪ Spirit of stillness, O keep us from harm,Cradle our footsteps in leaf and in charm. ♪”

She then repeated the whole thing. I couldn’t help it—I started laughing.

We kept walking and ‘talking’ until we reached the market district.

“Lori, the herbalist is over there,” I said, pointing. “You missed it. And the apothecary’s the other way.”

But she didn’t stop. Just kept walking.

I had no idea where she was going, but I followed anyway.

Eventually, we left the shopping area entirely, and then she suddenly stopped. I nearly bumped into her.

“What? Why here?” I asked, looking around. The only building nearby was “The Severed Tongue,” one of the town’s three restaurants. Creepy names were kind of a theme here.

Lori walked right in and sat at a table.

Huh?

Had she suddenly decided she was super hungry and must eat before selling her herbs?

I remembered that NPCs didn’t usually eat much. But then again, Lori’s weirdness had no limits. If she turned into a frog and started flying, I wouldn’t even be surprised. Well... maybe a little.

So I sat beside her.

A friendly NPC waiter/owner/chef came over with a menu.

“Evening, traveler. Hungry, are you? We’ve hot stew on the fire.”

Ten options on the menu. I picked two.

“I’ll go with the cabbage and carrot stew, and some oatcakes with butter,” I said. “Lori, what do you want?”

“…”

Right. She couldn’t actually order.

“She’ll have the same.”

The owner left us alone, and Lori filled the silence.

“I have stories the hunters tell by the fire. Want to hear them?”

“Sure, I’d love to hear a story.”

So she told me. Short, weird, poetic tales.

One was about a pool so still it reflects not the sky above, but dreams yet to come.

Another about a flower that only opens during thunder, releasing a scent that silences all creatures.

And a third about a bird woven from starlight that circles the forest once every hundred years.

They didn’t make sense. But like her singing, it was oddly charming watching her be so happy and animated.

When the food arrived, she ate five bites of her oatcake and didn’t touch the stew.

Then came the check.

Five silver.

FIVE.

That was a day’s wage.

I glanced at Lori. She smiled.

Of course I had to pay.

Still… the stew was good. I’d come back here with the others sometime.

Afterward, Lori stood and started walking again. I assumed she was finally going to sell her herbs.

Nope.

We passed the market again. No sign she’d even considered stopping.

Then something truly strange happened.

She took my hand in hers. And just kept walking.

And at that moment, the realization hit me like a lightning bolt.

All of this... was actually a date.

I blushed furiously.

Elrik had helped Lori set this up. She had coaxed me into going on a date with her.

Even after I said nothing Lori did could surprise me, I was dead wrong. I never expected a clever, orchestrated ambush date from an NPC.

We walked hand-in-hand the whole way back to the cabin.

The next day was a patrol day.

Once again, I was paired with Nayeem and Duchess.

As usual, Nayeem came alive in the forest. His eyes sparkled, he asked a ton of questions, and nodded thoughtfully whenever I explained something. He genuinely loved this stuff.

And also as usual, Duchess never stopped complaining.

At her thousandth complaint, I finally snapped. “Could you please just shut the hell—”

“Quiet!” she squeaked suddenly, cutting me off. “Shutup. Don’t move.”

Yeah, this happened from time to time. Usually a false alarm—We’d stand frozen like idiots for minutes, and it turned out to be nothing. Super annoying.

But this time wasn’t one of those times.

“It’s them, Chuu!” Duchess whispered, voice tight. “The Pissbucket-Smearlords!”

“Farhad’s group?”

“Yes,” she squeaked. “There are… a lot of them. But they’re not moving. I think… I think they’re setting up camp.”

“That makes sense,” said Nayeem, crouching. “It’ll be dark soon. They’re a large group, probably can’t travel easily at night. They must be planning to attack tomorrow.”

“Good,” I said. We’d been preparing for this after all.

“It’s about fucking time.”
