Three days passed in a blur of temporal peace for the warring sects.

The icon catastrophe had done too much damage to both sides. They had lost people—too many people. But the good news was that the Origin Plane of Storm had finally come to a standstill. Not expanding. Not shrinking.

It wasn't the balance they'd sought. But it was a break, and after everything that had happened, they all needed one.

So for the last three days, the smoking crater that had once been the battlefield lay quiet—a graveyard of bubbling blood and broken bodies, abyssal essence still rising into the air like smoke from a massive bonfire.

Aodhán could see it from his mansion back in the sect. Everyone could. It hung at the edge of the horizon like a backdrop of death and sorrow, fitting perfectly with the atmosphere within the sect—gray-cast skies mirroring the emotions of every storm cultivator still alive.

There was much to be thankful for, Aodhán knew. But there was more—so much more—to mourn.

And in the last three days, that was all they had done.

Mourn.

They mourned the thousands of Mythics who had lost their lives in three weeks of war. They mourned the two Calamities who had given their lives for theirs. But most of all, they mourned the two specials they had lost.

Mirelle. And Seamus—whose body remained unrecovered despite the desperate searching of Mythics across the battlefield. Not a shred of tissue. Not a toenail. Not a bone. Not even a wisp of his energy signature.

The catastrophe had destroyed it all—erasing Seamus from existence as completely as if he had never been part of it.

As if he had never lived.

Aodhán closed his eyes, pushing away the grief that tried to well up within him.

Every war had losses. Those losses were anticipated. Expected, even. But anticipation couldn't numb pain. It couldn't cure grief. It couldn't dry tears. And most of all, it couldn't sidestep the recurring shock that came with losing a friend—the way the mind kept reaching for someone who was no longer there, kept needing reminders, kept expecting that if he turned a corner now, Seamus would be leaning against the wall with that smirk on his face, announcing that this had all been an elaborate prank.

But Aodhán knew he was only still in shock. He'd dealt with grief before. He knew the stages—even if they felt entirely different this time.

Sighing, he stepped out of his room and climbed to the roof, inhaling deeply as he escaped the cloying stillness of his mansion. Crixis was already up there, alongside Su Tianan and Kairn, Seamus's younger brother.

"Good evening, special disciple Aodhán," they greeted as he arrived. Then, reading his expression—or perhaps just his silence—they began to leave.

He didn't stop them. He'd been holding himself together for three days, forcing a brave front, and he couldn't sustain that with Kairn's tear-stained eyes in his line of sight.

A cool evening breeze moved through his hair. Aodhán took a slow, shuddering breath, steadying himself.

Their allies had remained with them in the aftermath. The Wind Sect, the Water Sect, the Lightning Sect, the Shadow Sect, the Silence Sect—and finally the Space Sect, whose two transmigrants had taken up residence within the special sect itself.

His gaze drifted to the newest building—only a stone's throw from his mansion—and he grimaced, unsure how to feel about their presence.

Part of him resented them. Blamed them for all that had happened. For the icon catastrophe. For Seamus.

Another part knew it wasn't their fault. They had barely participated in the war itself. If there was anyone to blame, it was the icon-happy Space Calamity who had pulled the first trigger.

A hearing had been held with the High Elders yesterday, but no concrete decision had been reached. Aodhán didn't blame them for that either. The Space Sect was an ally—one powerful enough to supply two Calamities, two transmigrants, and tens of thousands of Mythics. Not an ally they could afford to lose, especially when none of their other allies had made so bold a commitment to the war.

And the Space Sect had saved them. All four of them. Without that first icon manifestation, perhaps they would all be dead—all four specials, Elder Ghaltris, every elder on that viewing deck.

Perhaps the Space Calamity had been right to act. Perhaps there truly had been no other way.

But try as he might, Aodhán couldn't squash the resentment. It filled his chest like tar, churning and twisting every time he remembered.

Which was every time.

It wasn't all bad news, though.

Darien was still alive, unconscious but stable. The healers believed he would recover fully within a few weeks.

That was a good thing.

It was the only good thing that had happened in three days.

Exhaling, he pulled up his status screen.

There wasn’t much change in his screen save his tier which had shot up by four in the last two weeks. It wasn’t a drastic increase, but it was something. One more tier and he would be halfway toward his goal of leaving—of returning home to his family.

The thought saddened him, digging up emotions he didn’t feel quite capable of handling. Not yet.

Inhaling deeply, he pushed the emotions aside and closed the screen, muttering to himself.

Be strong. You can do this.

His gaze strayed to the opposite mansion once more, core sense picking up the cores of the two transmigrants within, and he wondered what to do with this feeling in his chest. For several minutes, he stayed there, simply watching until finally, a new core entered into his range.

He shifted his gaze to the approaching figure in the distance.

Nymera.

And despite himself—despite everything—his lips tugged into a small smile.

Unlike him, Nymera had let herself drown in her grief for the first two days, crying without restraint. She had crumbled, and it had clearly worked to ease the weight of her grief.

Her eyes were still red, her expression still somber, yet she seemed lighter. Brighter. Gray to his own black.

He nodded silently in greeting as she arrived on his roof and simply turned his attention back to the opposite building. Nymera came to stand beside him, her frost-white hair billowing in a breeze that existed only for her.

His hands itched to touch it.

He kept them entwined behind his back.

For several seconds, neither of them said anything. But it wasn't a terse silence. It was comfortable. Over the past three-four weeks since he'd first met Nymera, they had slowly become close, each one in awe of the other's abilities.

They had become friends of a sort.

They were becoming something more.

And though there was much to say, the silence was still comfortable.

Until Nymera spoke.

"We should visit them," she said quietly, her gaze fixed on the opposite building. "We are not being good hosts."

Aodhán stiffened, an instinctive aversion welling up at the thought of speaking to those he considered—

"We are mourning," he said, shaking his head.

"Yes, we are." Nymera agreed with a heavy sigh. "But it's been three days. We are not being good hosts."

“We don’t have to be good hosts to them.” he snapped, then sighed, his voice sobering. “We don’t have to be good hosts to them. They don’t deserve it.”

Nymera glanced at him, silver-black irises piercing yet holding no judgement as she spoke.

“They are not the cause of what happened, Aodhán.”

"No." He glared at the house. "But their sect is."

A beat of silence stretched between them then, only this time, it wasn’t comfortable. It was terse.

For nearly a minute, Nymera regarded him without uttering a word, then she glanced back at the building.

"War is never fair, Aodhán," she said softly, as though afraid of her own words. "We rage now that it has begun to hurt us. But we mustn't forget that we chose this. We could have avoided this. We chose war."

A wave of crushing guilt crashed over him, and Aodhán felt his voice fracture before he could stop it.

"You can't blame me for this."

“I’m not,” Nymera replied evenly, her gaze still fixed on the mansion. “I’m not blaming anyone. I’m simply saying we chose this. Just like I chose to kill the Kael Varros and Volgrim Kaizen. If you resent the Space Sect this much for potentially causing Seamus’s death. What do you think the blood and earth sect think of us after I killed their transmigrants?”

Aodhán sighed. “It’s not the same.”

Nymera nodded slowly. “Maybe it isn’t. But this is a war, Aodhán. Death and loss is bound to happen. These things are bound to hurt.”

“I know that.”

“Do you?” Nymera raised an eyebrow, her tone still gentle. “Because if you do, you’ll realize that we are alive today because of the Space Sect. That sooner rather than later, we’ll have to dive into a new battlefield with these transmigrants and we barely know them. We need to start building camaraderie.”

Aodhán scowled, feeling a headache form behind his eyes. She was right. It was poor strategy to enter a battlefield with people whose abilities and instincts you had never tested, never understood.

But he already knew how powerful the Space transmigrants were—he could sense it in the blazing intensity of their cores, in the strength of their bloodlines. But that wasn't what Nymera was talking about. She was talking about trust. About the kind of camaraderie that kept people alive when strategy fell apart and everything came down to whether the person beside you would move when it mattered.

He hesitated all the same, unable to bring his heart and his logic to the same page.

"Take Sophran," he said after a moment. "He's the people person. He'll know how to handle this."

Nymera raised an eyebrow. "Sophran is busy with his pendant. The elders gave it to him two days ago. I'd rather not interrupt his meditation."

Aodhán blinked. He hadn't known. He'd assumed Sophran had locked himself away with his grief, the same as everyone else.

"Ashscar, then."

Nymera smiled sadly. "He's preparing for a path evolution. And if you were a good leader, you would know that."

"I'm not the leader." Aodhán scowled at her.

"Then why do you walk the path of authority?" she asked softly then shook her head. "The storm isn't just clouds and lightning Aodhán. It's the people too. And you've neglected them in your grief."

Those words hit him like a hollow punch, and Aodhán let out a heavy, weary sigh. The tension left his muscles. His shoulders sagged, and for the umpteenth time, his gaze strayed to the opposite house where he could sense the two space transmigrants training.

He glared at their blazing cores for a long, agonizing moment before finally agreeing. "Fine. Let's go pay them a visit."

Nymera smiled widely. "I knew you'd come around. Come on, let's go with cake. Mirelle makes these cute little ones that—”

Her words died on her lips as she remembered Mirelle was no more. Pain flashed across her eyes, tears pooled, but before any could fall, she wiped them off. "We should probably get them something else."

Aodhán nodded without a word, summoned a storm platform, and held out his hand.

"Come on. We'll think of something."

Nymera took his hand. Her palm was warm and soft against his, and Aodhán held it carefully, as though it were something worth keeping.

It seemed his mind wasn't the only one that needed constant reminders.

***

Ten minutes later, Aodhán stood at the entrance of the Space transmigrants mansion, a beautiful cake on one hand and a bottle of wine on the other. Nymera stood beside him, a small smile on her face as she watched him quietly wrestle his own unease.

“You can do this,” she whispered. “Being a leader entails making difficult decisions.”

“Save it, Mera,” Aodhán snapped quietly. “This is hard enough without your philosophical commentary. Just knock.”

Nymera chuckled, then knocked, the metal handle tapping thrice against the wooden door.

Aodhán tensed, but his muscles had barely even finished contracting before a blazing core arrived at the entrance and then—hinges creaked.

The door opened.

And Aodhán had to force himself not to scowl as a beautiful blonde girl peeked out, a hint of surprise on her face. He extended the cake and wine to her before he could destroy them with his fist and forced a smile.

“Good evening. I’m Aodhán Brystion. This is Nymera Vaelthorn. We welcome you to the Storm Sect.”

The girl's expression shifted through confusion and surprise in quick succession. Then a gruff voice came from somewhere within.

"Meredith, who is it?"

Aodhán went still, his eyebrows crunching at the familiarity of that voice. The particular weight of it— He would have recognized it anywhere. Had heard it every morning for years, calling him to meditation before the sun came up, steady and unhurried as the mountains themselves.

His eyes widened as the second core approached.

The door opened wider.

And Aodhán felt a bolt of pure shock race through his entire body as his gaze locked with the man standing in the doorway. Broader than he remembered. Older, perhaps, or simply more settled into himself. But unmistakable.

"Elder Gyatso," he breathed.

Elder Gyatso was no less stunned. For a moment, he simply stared—the composed, unflappable monk Aodhán had known since childhood rendered completely speechless by the sight of him. Then, slowly, he exhaled.

"Aodhán Ashoka." His voice was quieter than Aodhán remembered. Weightier. "What in the world are you doing here?"

Aodhán's smile broke across his face before he could contain it—wide and bright and entirely involuntary, the first real smile in three days.

"I could ask you the same thing, Elder Gyatso." He shook his head in disbelief. "We are both very far from home."
