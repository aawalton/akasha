A brooding aura hovered over Aodhán’s room as High-Elder Zarxan dismissed his tea tray and finally began to speak.

“Az’marthon Ranok was my master… and my friend—though I doubt he ever saw me as the latter.” He smiled faintly. “To him, I was always young Zarxan. Always willful.”

He leaned back, eyes drifting somewhere far beyond the walls.“I remember he used to tell me, ‘The storm doesn’t want to be controlled, Zarxan. It wants to be unleashed. Let it gather, and let it go. But I never understood him then. How could I? I was barely at the thirtieth tier, while he was already a Mythic.”

Zarxan’s lips twitched. “His power was undeniable, and I chased after him like a puppy desperate to catch his stride. He had just emerged from the rage that consumed him after his parents’ death, and for a time… he was doing good. I might even say he was happy.”

His tone darkened. “But everything changed when he entered the Calamity class and became a general.”

He drew a slow breath. “Life lost its meaning to him after that. I didn’t understand it back then, but I do now. I can feel it—the creeping detachment, the sense of being too much for this world. Divine.” His gaze hardened. “At the time, I thought he was simply drunk on power, and I wanted that for myself.”

A sad smile curved his lips as he looked at Aodhán. “I begged him to leave me his legacy. I was ready to abandon my own path to chase his shadow. But he refused me every time. He despised the very idea—or so I believed.” Grief flickered in his eyes. “Until recently. When I stumbled upon your will imprint and recognized his essence signature.”

He laughed softly, though there was nothing joyful in it, his runic irises flaring as he fixed Aodhán with his gaze. “Turns out, he didn’t hate the idea at all. He just didn’t think I was worthy.”

A tense silence followed before Zarxan sighed again. “I fear he was right. I wasn’t worthy. You, on the other hand…” He studied Aodhán with something between envy and admiration. “I’ve seen you fight. I’ve seen your Icon, your Aspect, and your Familiar. Wherever Az’marthon is now, I’m certain he couldn’t have been prouder to have chosen you.”

Aodhán met his gaze cautiously before nodding. “Thank you, High Elder Al’ruhn. But I highly doubt Az’marthon chose me. It’s more accurate to say I stumbled upon his legacy.”

“Oh, you little child.” Zarxan chuckled. “What do you know of fate? To an ascendant, it’s practically orchestration. No matter the circumstances in which you gained this legacy, I am a hundred percent certain it was arranged.”

“By fate?” Aodhán frowned in confusion.

“By Az’marthon.” Zarxan replied. “I don’t know how, but I’m sure of it. When a person ascends, they are given a choice to either leave a legacy in AEFLYM or choose a world outside the collective—"

“The collective?” Aodhán whispered, the word ringing in his memory. This wasn’t the first or second time he was hearing that word, and if he understood perfectly what it meant, then the hierarchy of power in the universe was much wider than he’d thought. Perhaps, even more than he could imagine.

Zarxan, however, didn’t explain what the word meant and simply smiled. “You’ll find out all you need to know in due time. No need to rush. Now, where were we?”

“Choosing a world outside the collective.”

“Right.” Zarxan nodded. “Tell me, Aodhán, what do you think happens when an ascendant leaves their legacy in a world outside the collective?”

This answer was obvious, and with a grimace, Aodhán answered. “They create a transmigrant.”

“And a disaster.” Zarxan nodded. “From the little we know and have deduced from the accounts of several transmigrants, we know that the lives of each one have been marked by disaster.” His expression sobered, and he asked. “Tell me, Aodhán, just how much do you remember of your childhood?”

A memory of fire and lightning flashed across Aodhán’s mind in that instant, and he grimaced. “Very little. My… my parents died in a fire. The last thing I remember is the smell of smoke and ozone in the air as I ran from a burning house—" he paused, and his eyes widened with a possibility he’d never considered before. He looked up at Zarxan, and in a whisper too low for anyone else to have heard, he asked. “Did Az’marthon kill my family?”

“Doubtful,” Zarxan shook his head. But then, with a sigh, he added. “Though I cannot say for certain that he wasn’t present. Perhaps he arrived too late. Perhaps he tried to save them in a world without magic and failed. Or perhaps he could only watch and choose. I don’t know, but I’m certain he didn’t kill your parents.”

“How are you so certain?” Aodhán asked. “You just said he created a disaster.”

“Because if he had, you wouldn’t have found his legacy at all. Agents of the collective would have destroyed it. And trust me, they would have found it.”

Aodhán frowned, still feeling a bit conflicted, though he was certainly very relieved to hear that Az’marthon hadn’t killed his parents. He wasn’t quite sure what he would have done if he’d found out that his patron had been the cause of his parent’s death.

The fact that Az’marthon could have been there, though, was very troubling, and Aodhán’s memory of lightning and the scent of ozone didn’t help either. But now that he thought about it, the idea that someone had been watching, guiding, and protecting him during his time in the forest made a whole lot of sense.

He had only been six years old—alone in a forest even adults would have hesitated to traverse. And somehow, he hadn’t gone hungry. He hadn’t been cold. He hadn’t been attacked by beasts. He had survived the forest for nearly a week alone as a six-year-old child.

It made so much sense that something or someone had been watching over him. Only now, he knew it was the god he had thought.

Zarxan stared at him with a sympathetic expression, letting him process his emotions before asking. “Tell me what happened next.”

Aodhán did, and by the time he was done, even he couldn’t deny how convenient it all sounded. Surviving the forest, saved by unknown travelers, and somehow stumbling into a cave with Az’marthon’s legacy simply waiting for him.

He hadn’t even needed to fight for it like Daruk had. He had simply taken it. As if it were his. As if it had been left for him to find.

Aodhán shook his head, amazed and disturbed at the same time. It was clear now that what he had considered a lucky encounter was anything but.

“Az’marthon ascended four hundred years ago,” Aodhán said slowly, still trying to make sense of it all. “But the fire that killed my parents happened only eleven years back. That creates a gap of almost three hundred and ninety years. How is that possible?”

Zarxan chuckled—not out of humor, but with the tired disbelief of someone who had long abandoned the comfort of simple explanations. “Aodhán… you are thinking linearly.”

He lifted one hand, and the storm responded instinctively. Lightning flickered along his wrist like serpents eager to bite through reality. Space around his fingertips bent visibly, and though it wasn’t an attack, Aodhán felt his heart stutter and grow cold in fear.

But Zarxan wasn’t looking at him. His runic gaze was fixed on the warping space as he spoke. “Do you think worlds sit side by side like stones in a stream?”

He glanced at Aodhán then and shook his head. “I have never gone out of this world, but I believe there are vast distances of space between each world, and that’s even among the collective. When an ascendant leaves AEFLYM in pursuit of a world outside the collective, there’s no doubt they’ll have to cross a gulf so vast space has no distance and time no meaning.”

In the time Az’marthon traveled to your world, hundreds of years must have passed. And when you found his legacy and were transmigrated back, crossing such vast distances, even with the aid of the legacy, a few hundred years must have passed too.”

Aodhán felt himself grow colder as he put it all together. “And when I eventually arrived, four hundred years had passed.”

“Exactly,” Zarxan chuckled. “If we were to count the passing time, then you’re certainly much older than you look. All transmigrants are, hence your profound spirits. Though age is not the only contributor to that.”

Silence settled between them once again as Aodhán processed all that he had just heard. No doubt, he would confront Az’marthon about all this when he next saw him—the age, the disaster, everything.

This conversation, however, had opened his eyes to the workings of fate, and he was realizing now that nearly all his life, he’d been carried by Fate, stumbling blindly through it all.

His arrival at the Warren woods had been no mistake. His acceptance and consequent actions in the 5th academy had been no mistakes either. Every action he’d taken since his arrival in AEFLYM, everyone he’d met. All of it had led him here—to Gordon. To this conversation.

The question now was why? Why was he here? What role did he have to play?

He glanced up at Zarxan in the moment and asked. “You and the other High Elders have spoken about me. What did you discuss?”

Zarxan paused in that moment, his aura grew slightly heavy, and he sighed. “I fear it’s not good news, Aodhán.”

Of course, it wasn’t.

Aodhán felt a lump rise in his throat, but he pushed through it. “Tell me.”

The air crackled faintly, the scent of ozone grew thicker, and in a rumble of what seemed to be distress, Zarxan spoke. “When I came to save you from Lutia, I wasn’t thinking about the sect. I was curious, and I wanted to satisfy that curiosity. After I did, however, I realized I couldn’t leave you to die in Lutia. But bringing you to Gordon also had its own challenges. I decided it was the better option.”

Aodhán felt his stomach curdle. “Does it lead to war or death?”

Zarxan hesitated, and Aodhán felt his stomach sink. “Oh no, I should have known not to trust you.”

“Listen to me,” Zarxan rumbled, his gaze intent, and despite his fear and sense of betrayal, Aodhán froze. “I chose between certain death and potential death. There was only one good option.”

Aodhán closed his eyes in frustration. He didn’t care that there hadn’t been any good options. He was tired of war and death. He’d been looking forward to an interesting, yet normal time in the sect. He’d been looking to wind down from the whole Abyssos saga. But of course, Fate had other plans.

Exhaling deeply, he opened his eyes and gestured resignedly. “What’s the war about this time? Century-old grudge? Millennia?”

“No,” Zarxan replied quietly, understanding Aodhán’s frustration. “It’s about balance. You see, here in Gordon, transmigrants are prized and celebrated, not just because of their power, but because of the effect their presence has on the Origin planes. The more transmigrants a sect has, the more powerful they become, and with your addition to the sect, the Storm Sect will become the only sect in Gordon to have four transmigrants. The other sects won’t just watch that happen.”

Aodhán opened his mouth and shut it. He had so many questions. But one took priority. “What does that mean for me?”

“It means the other sect will want to restore a balance.”

“They’ll hunt me.”

Zarxan sighed. “They’ll hunt you, but this time you won’t be alone like you were in Lutia. You’ll have the entire sect protecting you. You know why? Because we stand to gain so much by keeping you alive. If you cannot trust our good intentions, I need you to trust our selfishness.

Aodhán stared at Zarxan for a long moment, and when he couldn’t take any longer, he pushed himself to his feet and began pacing. What options did he have? Even if Zarxan had told him this back in Lutia, he would have eventually agreed. Just like Zarxan said, potential death is a great deal better than certain death.

But another war was just… tiring.

Did the continents of this world do nothing but fight? Why is every continent fighting one war or the other?

Exhaling again, he massaged his head, feeling a headache come on.

“Is there no way I can hide my identity?” he asked a moment later. “Disguise myself perhaps. I don’t mind being an outer sect disciple at all.”

Zarxan shook his head. “You can deceive the world, Aodhán, but you can’t deceive the origin plane. As long as you’re with us, the origin plane will grow more powerful. The other sects will notice, and you will be discovered, because let’s face it, you’re terrible at laying low. You tried to keep a low profile in Lutia. How well did that work for you?”

Aodhán glared at Zarxan, unable to deny the truth in his words. Still…a war was something he wasn’t just ready for.

However, after a few minutes, he had to come to the conclusion that it was inevitable. He had fled trouble in Lutia only to spark a war here. And this time he didn’t even have to do anything. His presence alone was enough.

Frustrated, he eventually sat down and said. “My goal is the 100th tier. Once I reach the Mythic class, I’m leaving.”

Zarxan smiled sadly. “The storm sect is not just a sect, Aodhán. It’s a family, and you’ll see that very soon. But I respect your decision. If by the hundredth tier, you still want to leave, then you’re free to do so. No one will stand in your way.”

Aodhán nodded. “Then it’s settled then.”

Zarxan let out a deep breath. “I’m sorry, Aodhán. But I want you to know that as much as this benefits us, it wasn’t my motive for saving you. That, I did because you are my master’s legacy.”

Aodhán’s expression softened. “I still can’t thank you enough for that.”

Zarxan smiled. “You don’t have to. We are family. And I know wars can be… dirty. But perhaps that’s exactly what you need to push yourself to the hundredth tier. I know it’s what the sect needs. Perhaps, it’ll ruin us. Perhaps it’ll make us. But regardless of what happens, this is an ANVIL for us all to forge ourselves. For you to forge yourself into something stronger. There is no backing down in the world of cultivation. Only advancement. And what better way to advance than through war and bloodshed?”

Aodhán wasn't sure he agreed.

But if Fate had brought him here to spark an era shift, who was he to disagree?

His jaw tightened. "Okay, but I have one request."

Zarxan tilted his head slightly. "What is your request?"

Aodhán raised a hand to his head and closed his eyes, reaching for that hidden recess behind the Veil of Shrouded Mind where the memories of his life on Earth had been buried.

The veil had sealed away the emotions tied to those memories, dulling his grief, burying his love, and leaving his past feeling distant—as though it belonged to someone else.

His eyes opened, and with a determined gaze, he said. "There is a veil in my mind. The Veil of Shrouded Mind. It seals the emotions tied to my memories of Earth."

He swallowed. "It has kept me alive when I needed it. It protected me from those who sought to end me. But I don't want to live like that anymore. I don't need to anymore. If my past is shaping my future this precisely, then I have no right to keep hiding from it."

His gaze sharpened, becoming resolute.

"I want to remember everything as myself. I want to feel every emotion as I should." He glanced up at Zarxan, meeting the Calamity's runic gaze unflinchingly.

"I want you to deactivate it."

Aldric didn’t think so.
