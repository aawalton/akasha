The elevator groaned as it rose, chains creaking under the strain of the carriage-sized platform climbing steadily up the colossal mushroom’s stalk.

Kita stood beside the glass wall, gloved hands resting lightly against the rail as she watched Camatoz Ik’Balam unfold beneath her. Her father had always told her it was the greatest of the eastern capitals, but words were small currency for what it really was.

The fungi forest was endless, its stalks the width of towers, their caps blotting out the dusk sky in great, ribbed parasols. Homes and streets and buildings were built between the mushroom trunks, most upper floors linked by bridges and lattices of rope, iron, and hardened chitin. Lanterns—hundreds of them, thousands of them—were strung between them and made the city glow like an entire constellation had fallen and taken root.

From above, it looked less like a city and more like some sacred chamber hollowed out of the world, and for a moment, Kita allowed herself to think: if the empire wasn’t dying, it could’ve been this everywhere.

A forest of light, a people unbroken.

Instead, she hadn’t seen a capital city as peaceful as this for the past two months. Even Salaqa Ik’Balam was a city at war these days.

“... Sketches like these draw themselves,” Sora said behind her, and Kita immediately heard the scratch of pen against paper as the Spinneret started putting the sight down in her journal. “But still, one must hurry before memory cheapens the color. You’d be surprised how quickly beauty fades when it’s only carried in the mind.”

Machi’s reflection shifted faintly in the glass. She stood close to Kita, steady as always, but her look of concern was anything but steady.

“Are you well, my lady?” Machi whispered.

Kita drew in a breath and returned the smallest of smiles.

“Well enough. Perhaps only… a touch anxious.” She allowed herself the honesty. “It has been long since I last saw them. Long enough that I wonder what has changed… and what has not.”

Machi dipped her head, and the elevator jolted as it reached the highest level. The doors shuddered open with a hiss of spore-damp air, releasing them into a wide and regal hallway. Its stone floors gleamed and its walls were traced with veins of amber fungi, but though this was supposed to be the Regional Lord’s home, it was empty now. Quiet, save for the muffled hum of the city far below.

Ifas, irreverent as always, strode out first and gestured. “This way, this way,” he said with a grin, as though he were showing them to a tavern instead of the heart of power in the east. He stopped before the first door on the left and swung it open with little ceremony.

The chamber through the door was intimate rather than grand, but no less striking. It was a decently sized living room with a wide pane of glass stretched across one wall, offering a full sweep of the fungi city’s sprawl below. Velvet carpets softened the floor, and low tables bore trays of nuts, fruits, and cured meat.

Kita immediately found Enki sitting cross-legged in front of the window, rifle laid neatly across his lap and his posture rigid.

Cold as ever. Still as ever. The Worm Mage looked as though he might’ve been carved into the room rather than seated in it, but the Thousand Tongue—Zora was already eating. He sat by the sofa in the middle of the room, his staff set beside him, one hand reaching for another morsel of nuts from the platter before him.

He didn’t look at them when they entered, but Ifas quickly waved them to the sofa opposite, urging them to take a seat.

Kita obeyed, smoothing her dress before lowering herself into the cushions. Machi, ever her shadow, stood directly behind her, but Sora dropped into the cushions beside her and leaned forward, grin already wide at the snacks.

“Don’t mind if I do,” she said, reaching across without hesitation.

Ifas vanished briefly only to return with more trays, setting them down with the air of a host rather than a driver. Kita ignored him for the most part. Her eyes were on Zora.

“... Let me be direct,” she said at last, her tone firmer and more noble than she’d thought it might be. “It was no simple thing for me to come here. Sneaking out of the heavily monitored Salaqa Region, through Royal Capital Guard patrols, and through forests and checkpoints… it was a two week long ride for me. My father, Lord Baya, isn’t exactly pleased with the two of you vanishing after the ruckus in the academy, but…”

She steadied her voice.

“He understands why the two of you had to disappear. As do I. That’s why I’ve been sent here.”

Then she reached for the books and notes Machi drew from beneath her dress, laying them across the table in precise order. She leaned forward now, feeling her heart quicken but not allowing her voice to betray it.

“I know you and Enki haven’t been idle here in the east,” she said, pushing forward the first book on the table. “You know of the civil war. You know of the battles. I’ll speak plainly then: in a month, the Reunification Army will launch a full-frontal assault on the Divine Capital.”

That made Sora pause mid-bite, though she said nothing, only scribbled with her other hand.

Kita pressed on. “Thirty thousand troops drawn from the western and central Outer Regions will throw themselves against the walls of the Divine Capital. Our objective is not to kill the Divine Empress, as rumor would have it. We’ll simply fight our way into the Divine Temple and expose Decima for what she is: a Magicicada Witch who has wrapped the entire empire in her coil for ten long years. If we can take her head and show her real face to the people, it’ll be easier for the empire to forgive the Empress for her weakness.”

Sora whipped her head at Kita. “Hm? So you’re not going to throw her off her throne?”

“No. She will remain there, though bound by heavy concessions and reforms led by the Outer Regional Lords,” Kita said curtly. “There must always be a Divine Empress in the Divine Temple to lead the Noble-Blood Households. One strong leader to command the empire. It’ll take us too long to find a new empress by tracing back the noble bloodline, so in an ideal world, father and the other Regional Lords would be pulling the current empress’ strings after we knock down Decima.”

Then Kita turned back to Zora. “So. If the west and center can field thirty thousand, what of the east? How many soldiers can the east provide? Rumors claim you and Enki have ten thousand soldiers marching under your unseen hands. If we combine them, we’ll have forty thousand—”

“Forty thousand against a hundred and fifty,” Ifas said, setting down another tray full of snacks between them. “Even with land to retreat to, and even with the weapons factories on your side, you’re still storming a fortified capital city with triple your numbers. It’ll be suicide to attempt a full-frontal attack.”

“We know. That’s why the assault is not meant to succeed.” Kita swiped the books and notes about the assault aside, tapping on a small piece of yellow paper instead. “The assault is just a diversion. While the Divine Capital’s Royal Capital Guards swarm the walls to defend, we’ll send a small elite force into the Divine Temple and assassinate Decima. That’s why I’m really here. Father wants the Thousand Tongue and the Worm Mage to join that force, and he also asks for the east’s strength to make the full-frontal assault look more convincing.”

Zora still didn’t speak.

Now that Kita had finished saying everything she was supposed to say—her father’s plans, the coming assault, and the numbers she’d rehearsed in her head until they weighed on her tongue like lead—his silence pressed on her harder than any rebuttal would have.

Her throat tightened. She swallowed, realizing she’d been waiting—almost desperately—for some acknowledgment, some measured phrase from the famed Thousand Tongue.

So when Zora finally pushed the half-empty platter aside and rose with unhurried grace, her heart skipped a beat.

“Follow me,” he said.

Kita blinked. Her brow creased in a small frown. “What for?”

He turned and headed for a door in the back, his staff making soft thumps against the floor.

“You should see Eria.”

Kita shot up so quickly the cushions sighed, and Sora was on her feet a heartbeat after, notebook already half-lifted as if even grief might require citation.

The two of them immediately followed Zora through the door at the back with Machi trailing closely behind—and Kita was nothing but surprised to see what resided behind the door.

It was a small, dark, and windowless room, wet-breathed with cold, but there was only one thing of note: the block of ice in the centre, standing taller than a man and twice as broad. It breathed fog from hairline fractures, glowing an unnerving blue from within as though a small winter had been hammered into a single prism.

Kita’s eyes quickly found the face suspended in the ice, and everything inside her clenched.

Eria’s eyes were shut. Her dark hair, fanned and silvered by frozen vapor, haloed her brows. The curve of her cheek was still intact and impossibly young, while the lower half of her body—stuck in a giant bug the last time Kita saw—was still there as well.

Kita’s first step towards the block of ice was pulled by something in the chest—habit or hope, she couldn’t tell—but the sheer cold wafting away from it stopped her by the second step.It bit through her gloves, her skirt, and her spine. Her breath caught and showed itself. She simply couldn’t believe there was a material this cold in the world.

Behind her, Machi let go a quiet exhale.

“... So you did it,” she whispered. “You actually cut her out.”

Zora inclined his head at Kita, and she gave him a small, grave nod. Everyone in the empire knew about Vantari’s underground laboratory now, and she’d been filled in about the extra details from Machi—who was the last person to see Zora and Enki—but despite being there and engaging in the fighting that day, she still didn’t know what, exactly, had been done to Eria.

So Zora filled her in now.

“This is the only way to keep Eria safe,” he said. “Even free of her giant ‘grafting bug’ body—the name of the bug she’s been infused with the essence of—the essence itself is still inside her, eroding her humanity. She’ll continue to be plagued by urges to consume bug meat and human flesh alike, so the moment we managed to cut her out, Enki sealed her in a block of ice using his cryogenic mist.”

“Hibernation,” Sora muttered, tucking a finger under her chin. “The long sleep that cheats the ledger of days.”

“She won’t age in the ice,” Zora continued. “Nor will she need to breathe, eat, or drink. Her urges won’t get to her, either, so all Enki has to do is cool down the ice every week or so to keep her from waking up.”

Kita’s throat finally worked.

“Is it really… safe?” she said. “To… to freeze her like this, I mean.”

Zora tipped his head the other way, craning his ear at her. “As safe as our world permits,” he said. “The grafting bug essence in her body is very strong. She won’t die in the ice, so we can keep her eternally fourteen years old until either two things happen: one, research progresses to a point where humanity can figure out a way to cure the Afflicted, or two, we manage to make a grafting bug system so it can keep her essence from tearing her apart.”

Kita nodded once and stepped closer, inch by careful inch, until the ache of cold became a thing with teeth and dignity demanded she not yield to it.

She laid her palm to the surface of the ice. Pain immediately flared bright and simple—and then her skin numbed, and her heart, which had spent three months pretending it was stone, remembered it was only a heart.

“... I never knew,” she whispered. “All those years in the academy… all those nights sharing bunks, sharing stories… and I never knew you were bearing this.”

Her vision blurred. She bowed her head against the ice, breathing raggedly as guilt knifed through her.

The last thing she said to me was…

‘Help me.’

And she didn’t.

She did nothing but watch.

Her tears fell freely now, dripping onto the frost. Her hands trembled against the surface, and she let her head sink lower, shoulders shaking as weeks and months of buried grief bled out at once.

“... You and I should talk,” Zora said softly, directing his words at Sora.

So while Machi moved closer to Kita, steadying a hand against her shoulder, Zora turned and left the chamber with the Spinneret.

It didn’t take long for Kita’s muffled sobs to fill the chamber, stark against the hush of the hissing frost.
