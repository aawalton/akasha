Interlude

Solace

Vampires only find freedom in the night, but so too their hunters away from the public eye.

Prowling a hallway, Asmara cleaned the red off her silver épée. There wasn’t anywhere to wash her face in a castle of empty rooms, but it was fine. She was used to the tackiness of dried blood. Her coat, however, would have to be burned later, perhaps along with the bodies.

She didn’t feel anything. A long time ago, revenge fueled her, but she could tell you now, it was an unsatisfying road. Now, this was just something she had to do so others didn’t have to live through the same hell.

It was tiring. Most people went about their lives unaware of the true nature of the world they lived in. Every day, children entered the churn of human life, growing up into fodder for nobles’ private armies or livestock for hidden monsters. The Order hunted both, waging a nightly war to contain them and keep the ordinary people unaware, all so these fodder children would somehow—and for whatever reason she couldn’t comprehend—grow up smiling.

It got warmer as she proceeded down a hallway, and lingering in the air was the smell of wood smoke. At the end was a double door guarded by two suits of armor. They weren’t the kind she trusted to stand still.

Leaving behind a pile of folded greaves and cracked chest plates, she entered a rich man’s study. A fireplace on the left dyed the walls yellow. There were two couches huddled around it, a stained glass window on the far side, and a floor-to-ceiling library on the right. There was a tower of books and a rolling staircase right up against a vacant shelf. They must have been rearranging when I’d arrived.

A rustling sound had her drawing her sword—​but only halfway. It had come from a crib by the fireplace. A vampire child? They were mythical in rarity, but a monster was a monster; just like earlier, no matter if they’d begged, it didn’t bother her. They usually did that. They’d kill you and everyone around you once you’d let your guard down—the reason why she worked alone now.

She unsheathed her sword and approached the crib. This is for you, Amity.

She stopped the moment she saw inside. This can’t be. Inside was a chubby-faced baby—​still sleeping—​swaddled in a white cloth, the vampires’ crest haphazardly embroidered onto it.

It’s human.

Based on the golden ring beside it, it was a boy. There was a shaker toy and a ball beside him, but why him? The golden ring—why make him the family’s heir?

His human parents had undoubtedly either abandoned him or died. Those vampires earlier must have killed them. As it usually went, they’d raise this baby as livestock, but to care for it this much…

No, they didn’t do that. This must have just been some strange houseplay the couple had for entertainment. Even worse, perhaps they had wanted to make him a thrall. Vampires had difficulty entering carefully-warded areas, but if they had human thralls, it would be trivial. Well, that wouldn’t be happening anymore.

She quietly sheathed her sword, making sure not to disturb his sleep. If her fate was anything to go by, this child’s would be the same: the Order would raise him into a hunter and dye the colors of his life in black and red. She didn’t think it was wrong—to give someone power to balance their tragedy—but looking at the child now, still asleep and naively believing in his safety, she knew that this would be one of his last moments of reprieve.

She stepped away. Her mission wasn’t done yet. The ones she’d slain seemed intelligent enough to use words, hence they might have also left records here. Lists of other vampires’ names, clan meeting locations, and feeding grounds would no doubt aid future hunts, and only after finding as much of those as she could would she take the baby and leave.

There was a writing desk before the window, so she started there. Index cards were blotched with ink, an inkwell having toppled over them. That mistress had a fountain pen. This was one more point towards them being intelligent and literate; finding meaningful records was almost a certainty. Too bad the cards were unreadable now, but perhaps they had been cataloging the new books.

Pulling out the drawer under the desk, she found a set of journals, but one was thicker than the rest. She placed it on the desk and opened it to the first page.

Tabular writing. A ledger. She didn’t think she’d strike gold so soon.

She scanned its items, left-to-right, top-to-bottom, finding entries as “goat’s milk,” “oatmeal,” and a “bolt o’ cloth.”

Hm? She squinted and bent down. Had she read it wrong? The list went longer, yet all were equally mundane. Is this a cypher? No, the prices all matched what she knew of the town’s market.

By Gothe, it’s a true grocery list.

Did vampires truly keep grocery lists? The bolt of cloth, she could still understand—​their fashion had to come from somewhere—​but did they enjoy oatmeal cooked in milk? No, they only needed blood. Was it an artifact of the time when they were still human? That was assuming, of course, vampires emerged from humans; the Order’s own scholars weren’t clear on the matter.

The rest of the ledger went much the same: more housekeeping expenses and associated receipts sewn onto the pages. They’d even accounted the names of hired gardeners and commuting maids to keep this place maintained—names Asmara had become familiar with over the past month’s investigation. It was awkward for her to have taken out their top client, but they would be better off for it.

Some carpenters were listed beside their addresses and consulting fees. She’d seen no renovations in the castle. Were these actually feeding grounds? By Gothe, were the ledger entries fakes encoding something else?

She doubted the journals would solve the code, but she at least wanted a clue that they were code.

Stacking the leather journals on the desk, it irked her that they weren’t labeled, but at least they were distinct: one was missing a spine and its cover was flaking, but the other one, likely the newest, still retained its sheen.

She stayed careful opening the newest one. Thankfully, the pages didn’t crumble, and the ink was still crisp and legible—beautiful.

“David’s cousin turned. We were too late to release him, and he had mutilated nineteen of the townsfolk before we could. When I remember him, having lost all reason, I fear for how that might be mine and David’s fate, even if I know how such a thing shant come to pass now.”

Asmara checked the header. Two months ago. Recent, but why hadn’t she heard about it?

“After burning the bodies, we set about finding those who had seen and helped throughout the affair …”

She was probably talking about the townsfolk.

“… and ushered them, each and every one, man, woman, and child, to the square, that one with Esmeralda’s statue. I still yearn for her songs.”

Asmara had passed by said statue many times. Of course, she’d asked the locals about its lore. It was a monument for the town’s savior—been there for two hundred years now.

“As we commenced, a babe’s cry and its nurse’s hushes, foremost among the herd, stole our attention. The other folk chided them, but it is not as if David and I thought it worth of any note; that is, until I remembered how infants wouldn’t withstand forgetting spells. It would be rude of us to kill by accident in such a manner.

“Hencely, I approached the nurse. She was clearly a nurse; she and the babe shared no features, but I learned, anyway, from the nurse that its parents had been among the deceased of the rampage. It had no relatives, and the nurse lived two streets-wise from the couple, barely a neighbor, yet acquainted by happenstance and landed suddenly with the task of caring for a not-her-own child.

“Perhaps it was selfishness that I did so, but I could not conceive with David, so I had him take it under our wing posthaste. The buffoon looked at it like an unwanted pet then, but look at him now; it seems to have stolen David from me, the thieving cat!”

She had to put the journal down. Even if old-school, the things written within were all too human. Isn’t this all a trick?

The other journal looked older. She cracked it open, and the first page was written in three different inks: a faded, hard-to-read light gray (1), a faded but clearer brown (2), and a dark gray like squid’s ink (3). They were answering each other.

1: “We hað lost ðe louve of our subjekts. Ðe clan ist forsook; ðe madness hast wynned.”

2: “Not stille.”

3: “Not yet.”

She read and understood these words—that they were a glimpse into the lore of these monsters—and yet, it must be a trick, she thought, it’s only a trick.

But in the following pages, there were no tricks.

“In our tryals,” the second ancestor had writ, “arose one patterne. For wye the main familie still survives; for wye our branches do rot; we have come upon magicks anew, by which I dub ‘Caritas.’

“For long, we have pruned those who showeth signs of seeking blood, of which we all crave even a drop, and because of which any and all who showeth no more satisfyed with one cup as with one barrel shalt be burnt upon a silverie cross. Yet, perchance, I and my wyf, in a bid of bitter desperation, deviseth a methode of escape.

“The patriarch, the matriarch, and their progeny, by blood or adoption, shalt each extract and consume at least a drop, or to a wine’s sip, from each of his or her familie. One shalt experience a warmth arise from one’s chest for but a brief moment; this warmth is the magick deemed ‘Caritas.’ Once one senses this, rejoice! For thou shalt suffer ye fear of the madness no longer!

“Howsoever, such a breakthrough is not so worthwhile to the branched families. Some succeed, yet others do not. By nature of our curiosities, and so too self-preservingness, else the branched familie heads revolt that onlie I and mine be allowed to live in bliss, I belabored to discover that which either blesses the main familie or, perhaps, curses the branched, such that this methode begifts them as well.

“I hath never imagined the cause to be so simple and poetickally cliché ;  it is truely befuddling. We once believeth how blood was the way to assuage the blood-madness, yet I have now come to believe, upon my progenitors’ ashes, that blood is but a mere channel, a connective mark, for the true blood which sustains the families now: love. Yes, love, but in becoming a magick of giving lyfe, it becomes different: Caritas.

“In this sense, the madness befalling our kin is a twisted lovesickness, as we all each know what is pure love by instinct, yet some among our kin stray from toiling for it, believing the hedonism of vampiric power is enough to forgo it entirely; the madness befalls them anyway.

“The proof, of course, is writ in later pages, all-though I fear many in the branched families will take offense, and more, to resist bearing this tired truth.”

As befuddled as the ancestor had said, Asmara flipped to those pages without delay, and she found descriptions of cases—studies of the dynamics of several branch families.

In one, it was a family of seven. The siblings had an ordinary relationship, but their parents had despised each other and themselves to the point of neglecting their children. Later, the parents turned, and the children survived. The second ancestor had taken them in, and after twenty years, he had found no changes in them.

In another, it was a family of five with three daughters: one from the husband’s first wife, and the two from the current wife. It seemed the current wife held no real feelings for her husband nor her daughters, and so did her daughters take after their mother, self-centered in every way. Later, only the father and the odd daughter survived; by the second ancestor’s intervention, the others had to be burned shortly after they’d turned.

In yet another, it was the most heretical case: a human man marrying the lady of a distant branch family. Her particular family had all long been burned, leaving her its sole heir and survivor. As a result, there had been no one to report her taboo, and she had gotten away with her marriage for many decades until the man’s natural death. It had all already transpired before her discovery by the second ancestor, and he tacitly allowed her secret to fade into history. There was nothing mentioned of the lady’s children with the man, but there was also an odd journal entry twenty pages earlier, where the ancestor had welcomed several promising young servants. The chronologies aligned.

Asmara couldn’t keep reading. The world spun, and she dragged out the chair to sit before she might collapse. Somewhere deep within, she had already begun to accept the journals’ words as truth, but she didn’t want to acknowledge it, not even in her mind, and it became a buzzing sound that wouldn’t settle. Already, it was a struggle to breathe.

Her gaze flittered to the newer journal. For whatever reason, her hand quested across the desk and reached it. She opened it, and she felt like a serial killer as she searched it for a name. Christine. Well, Asmara had killed her fair share of regular people before—​just people who were in the wrong place at the wrong time, serving the wrong person for mundane reasons like stable employment. That was usually how it went, and she made no ritual out of remembering their names as pride-trophies whenever that happened. Why’d she even bother to look for the name?

She looked at the crib, and she took note of the ice chest and pitcher of water beside it. Goat’s milk, undiluted, would cause an infant various illnesses, but a wet nurse was still leagues better. Oh, didn’t the carpenters say they were going to make a servant’s quarters for a rich lad?

Asmara opened the journal, flipping until the last unblank page. “Methinks we will be naming him Silvester,” Christine wrote. “Fit for being our only weakness, I suppose. I do wonder if this was how Sofia felt—”

She closed the newer journal and opened the older one. Human adoption has happened before. Even knowing that coexistence has happened in the past, there was just something not right about having an infant in a vampire’s care. There must have been a catch—an experiment gone wrong, any sign of a dear cost to cure vampiric bloodthirst, or even just a minor temptation to drink from a defenseless blood bag.

But she knew that wasn’t why she searched for the name “Sofia.” Each time she invoked the name in her mind, it also invoked familiarity. It invoked an old lullaby, a calming vertigo, and voices whom she couldn’t name.

She stopped at the page which mentioned “Sofia,” authored by the third and last ancestor, David. Already, there was a sinking feeling in her chest; already, she knew that once she’d seen these things, she wouldn’t be allowed to look away. Some poisons, however, were addicting.

“Silvester, my son, we have brought thou into a world which cannot accept thine existence. Should thou be reading this, then we have been greeted by the ferryman, and thou have inherited our estate.”

Asmara was the cause. She hammered that into her head.

“Thy true father’s and mother’s names are John Mason and Lilette Mason, née Allard. By our own failure, they met an unkind demise long before thou could walk. As a pittance of fate, Christine and I have since raised thou as our own, yet, my son, by being thy new protectors, we have bound thee to the cruelties of hunting and being hunted.

“One day, even we shall perish”—she hammered that even deeper—“and thou will once again be alone, perhaps under the persecution of the humans, who shall suspect and justify thee a thrall, or the clan, who shall regard and justify thee an inferior aberrant. Do not seek their approval, my son, and do not seek to change their ways, for thou art not in control of them, who cannot even see the immorality of discompassionate truth, sometimes more damaging than a compassionate lie, the kind which I cannot tell.

“It shall be a lonesome road. I am sorry. Christine and I can only give you the pittance of knowing thou art not a singular existence.

“Christine’s sister, Sofia”—that name again—“escaped with a human babe many years ago, persecuted by her family for committing taboo. Christine and I, knowing the truth that saw my father conspired against and burned on a silver cross, aided her in her disappearance. By now, her child must be a baker or songstress somewhere, and if she had loved that child the same as we to thee, then thou art the same.

“Thou art drunken of our estate, hence thou art have the power of our Caritas. Make it thy power to resist the persecution of this world, and if thou find persons worth enough to live and die for, grant to them thine own Caritas, such that thou, we, and all our ancestors, shall live forever. We are always with thee, son, in life and in death.”

She gently closed the cover. Rationality was the defense of the mind. Her perception narrowed into a purely objective tunnel.

Inheritance of “estate”—if a human takes even a drop of vampiric blood, it opens a magical channel. The Order had always known this was how thralls were made, but now she knew, through Caritas, it could also give the human vampiric powers without becoming a thrall at all.

Not all of them were thralls. Vampire hunters were the strongest of humanity’s strong.

Amity had a soft spot towards vampires who begged, and she’d told Asmara her mother’s name.

What have I done?

She couldn’t feel the chair under her anymore, nor the floor beneath her feet. For what reason had the past thirty years gone by? There was no revenge, no past, and no sense of north and south. There wasn’t even anyone to blame: not the vampires, whose normal human problems were punished with vampiric consequences; not the Order, whose members believed it was as simple as monster and monster-killer; and not even herself, who followed mother duck because there was nothing else to do.

She had glimpsed the cycle. Her comrades’ convictions and the monsters’ predation were all founded on half-truths and eternal problems, never to be solved, each giving life to the other—and it was all so isolating that she was the only one who saw this.

A cry from the crib nabbed her from her reverie. She got up and hurried to the baby’s side, and when they locked eyes, they were each afraid of the other, and she was afraid of herself.

I am your monster, she thought, and I…am sorry.

‘Sorry’ wasn’t enough for her. She poured water and goat’s milk into a porcelain dish and cut her palm over it, dripping blood, watching it suffuse but careful not to add too much. Carefully, she scooped up the infant, and afraid of killing him, teased his lips with the dish, waiting until he stopped crying and clearly wanted to drink.

They would be hunted. Before she left, she took their records and any maps; she buried the bodies in the courtyard and burned her coat and uniform. Among the children’s storybooks beside the staircase, she took two thin ones, light enough for travel.

She walked out of that castle with Silvester slung in front, cocooned in warmth with her. After buying a carriage ticket and frozen breast milk at the market, she thought of becoming a baker after all, although she had never made bread before, and most far-fetched of all, cared for a child.

“Are you sure?” Enthusia asked.

“I robbed something I’d never received,” Solace said, “so give me their hunger. I want to know the magic that fed them.”
