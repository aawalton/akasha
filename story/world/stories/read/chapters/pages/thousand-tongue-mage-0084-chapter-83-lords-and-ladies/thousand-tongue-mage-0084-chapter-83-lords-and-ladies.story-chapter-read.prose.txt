Sunlight warmed the left side of Zora’s face again.

It seeped through the high slotted windows above the arena’s waiting room—those same angled vents he’d counted out last week during the semifinals—and he felt the heat spreading in familiar patterns across the floor. Of course, he wasn’t paying much attention to it. He continued writing in his journal to Amadeus Academy, recounting his loss to the heiress in great, perhaps excessive detail.

His kids always had patience for his self-deprecating humour.

The crowd outside, by contrast, had no patience at all.

Boots thundered on the upper decks. Students shouted names in gusty pulses that crested and dipped like waves in a foreign sea. The cheering had started half an hour ago and hadn’t let up since, though now, it’d sharpened into something more feverish. Anticipatory. Hungry.

Zora merely adjusted his posture on the bench and kept writing.

Beside him, though, Eria kept hovering around the stretching Kita, worried like a mother before sending her daughter off to war.

“You’re stretching too hard,” she squeaked. “What if you’re already limber enough? Maybe it’s better to be… slightly tense?”

Kita exhaled softly through her nose. “That’s not how ligaments work, Eria.”

“But… I just mean he’s not like the others, right? Eryn never even looked winded in any of the rounds—”

Kita chuckled and reached over, ruffling Eria’s hair. “You’re more worried than I am.”

Eria let out a scandalised squeak, but Zora let a smile curl at the corner of his mouth and dipped his quill once more. “Can you blame her?” he said. “The crowd is screaming your name like it’s a prayer. Even I’m beginning to worry for you.”

Kita made a quiet noise, caught somewhere between a laugh and a sigh.

“You too, Mister Alvay?” she asked. “But… aren’t you hoping Eryn wins? The two of you entered this tournament together, did you not?”

He stopped writing.

Then he folded his notebook closed and turned his head towards her.

“There’s an old saying in the eastern Halwajji tongue,” he said, tone even. “Ven aturra kelna drassa, en sereth arkal raven: ‘A stone unburied grows no moss, but even the sky-bound eagle needs to bathe in mud.’”

Kita blinked.

“Vague as ever,” she said plainly. “What does that mean?”

“I wonder.”

Outside, the announcers began bellowing from the mushroom speakers.

“The final match of the Annual Grand Ayapacha Tournament is beginning!”

The stadium roared like a furnace. Zora felt the sound pressure crawling up the walls.

“And this time,” the announcers continued, “our final match breaks from tradition! There are no arena walls! The entire campus—yes, the entire campus—has been approved as a battlefield! Artists and commentators are stationed all across the rooftops, hallways, terraces, and courtyards to bring you live updates should the battle leaves the pit!”

Another wave of thunder from the crowd. Vendors hawking rice skewers and storm-salted eggs tried shouting over them, and failed.

Zora adjusted the collar of his uniform as he felt Kita adjusting her grip on her twin sawtooth blades.

“... I’m off.”

Then she walked past him and Eria towards the entrance of the fighting pit, clenching her jaw like she’d never clenched it before.

Zora gave her a little smiling wave as she passed by.

“Have fun out there.”

The dormitory halls were finally quiet.

Ifas’s footsteps echoed soft against the polished stone as he pulled his cart of fresh ingredients behind him, wood creaking, glass jars clinking against each other. It was a blessing, really, that he was able to pull his cart straight down the hall like this. After all, the entire student body had vanished toward the grand arena for the finals, so the usual voices and footfalls were swallowed by the spectacle of nobles trading blades and blood on a campus-wide battlefield.

And that means the kitchens are empty.

He hummed to himself a half-forgotten lullaby from the marshward coast. A good, slow rhythm. Now, he didn’t need signboards to find his way to the kitchen—he’d long since mapped out the dormitory corridors in his head already, counted the doors, the turns, and the number of steps between the last carpet fray and the kitchen threshold—so he made his seventy-second turn, seventy-third, and bumped the seventy-fourth door on his right open with a lazy kick.

The faculty kitchen greeted him with a rush of stale heat and the sharp bite of cardamom.

It was even more beautiful than he thought. It was massive, open, and tall-ceilinged, with latticework windows that let in mid-morning sun like a sieve. Dozens of stoves lined the curved outer wall, spaced evenly between hanging spice racks and coldstone sinks. Polished brass fixtures caught the light in soft glints. Every cutting station had its own whetstone drawer, and the smell—fresh oils, old herbs, lingering garlic—hung in the air like a familiar cloak.

Unfortunately, someone already beat him to it.

One of the rear stoves was already lit. A lady stood with her back turned to him, sleeves rolled, ladle in hand. Ifas recognised her posture even before the scent of her broth reached him.

He pulled his cart towards the adjacent stove with a grin.

“And here I thought I’d be the first scavenger to pick the bones today.”

Machi didn’t turn to address him. “Once the match ends,” she said, “my lady will return, and she will be hungry.”

Ifas began unpacking his goods from the cart: rice, smoked chilkroot, a capped jar of fishbone stock. “I thought you’d be up in the stands with the others, though. Wouldn’t she want her most loyal steward to cheer from the balcony?”

Machi tapped her ladle twice against the pot’s rim as she continued stirring her stew. “My lady does not need more noise. She will need a proper meal after her battle.”

He laughed softly and bent to check the firebox beneath his chosen stove. “You really don’t trust the cafeteria to feed her well?”

“I have reviewed their offerings.”

“And?”

“They are insufficient.”

Ifas chuckled and struck a match. “We’re quite similar, then. I’m also cooking for my masters, even if Master Eryn isn’t going to be hungry.”

The flames took quickly. He filled his pot, let it settle, then began preparing the chilkroot with small, neat cuts.

The silence stretched between them Like two swordsmen cleaning their blades without speaking.

“How about we make a deal?” he added casually. “I’ll cook for Master Alvay, Master Eryn, and Miss Eria, while you’ll cook for your lady?”

Machi’s ladle moved without pause. “I had intended to cook for them all to begin with. It is the first time my lady has seen me in a while.”

“Well, that just makes me unnecessary, then. I don’t want that. I’ll make the appetisers, and you—”

The first explosion rang out in the far distance.

A deep, booming concussive sound shook the high windows. Some of the copper pans hanging on the walls jingled in reaction. Ifas straightened up, wiping his hands on a cloth, and through the massive windows in front of the two of them, they both looked up to see a column of smoke curling up from the distant arena.

The fighting pit wall had ruptured.

A moment later, the mushroom speakers across the campus flared to life.

“... And they are moving! Kita Salaqa and Eryn Salaqa have left the fighting pit!”

Another blast rang out closer this time. Distant shouts followed, barely audible. Something rumbled near the southern quad.

Ifas leaned against the counter, watching the far sky through the window as more trails of smoke curled like ink into the morning air.

“They’re swinging hard,” he remarked.

“They always do,” Machi replied.

The next blast cracked even louder, even closer. Stone shrapnel flew skyward beyond the kitchen windows, arcing like birds too eager to die. Smoke curled in thin strands across the distant rooftops.

Both Ifas and Machi stood still for a while, watching in silence.

Then, without turning, Machi spoke.

“What are you cooking?”

“Salted congee,” Ifas said, tapping his ladle gently on the pot’s rim. “Fishbone and chilkroot.”

A pause.

“That’s a Mori Masif dish,” she said idly. “No driver under the employ of the Salaqa Household has been past the empire’s borders in over five years.”

Ifas smiled without looking up. He patted the inside of his coat once, right over the hidden pocket. “I got my letter from the Salaqa Lord himself, who gave me full clearance to ferry the Thousand Tongue around in secret. Would you like to see it?”

Machi stirred her pot with a steady rhythm. “No need,” she said curtly. “I am sure you have it.”

He let out a small sigh and leaned on the counter beside his stove.

“Well, you’re an odd one too,” he said. “That broth of yours with bloodfruit, marrow, and smoked pearleaf… that’s Rampaging Hinterland cooking. Deep winter type. It’s hard to make unless you’ve spent time out there, so you must be well-travelled, too.”

She said nothing as she reached for a handful of cracked barley and dropped it in with careful precision.

Ifas grinned at her sidelong.

“So, where’s home for you then, Miss Steward?”

“...”

Machi didn’t answer.

So Ifas only chuckled softly, let the question evaporate, and began humming again—low, tuneless, and faintly bitter.

In silence, they returned to their separate stoves while the battle raged on outside.

Before long, the explosions outside the waiting room quieted.

The rhythm of them changed. They were less frequent and less frantic, like distant aftershocks from a firework finale. He could feel it in the way the stone floor stopped humming underfoot, and he could hear how the crowd outside wasn’t screaming anymore. In fact, they weren’t making any noise at all.

Eria fidgeted beside him, her breaths catching in her throat as if she was trying not to ask a hundred questions at once.

But Zora simply sat with his hands folded over his journal.

Silence.

Then—

The mushroom speakers around the campus erupted into a chaos of voices, layered and echoing, a half-dozen announcers shouting over one another like a parliament of birds. The crowd broke into a rolling roar so loud it rattled the beams above the waiting room. Footsteps pounded the upper decks. Cheers, gasps, screeches—students and nobles and staff all shouted in outrage and disbelief.

Zora smiled faintly, tilting his face upward as if watching the ceiling sway.

“She did her best,” he murmured. “But this tournament was rigged the moment he decided to enter.”

Boots approached from the entrance of the fighting pit to his left. The steps were measured. Slightly uneven. One leg was dragging a touch.

Then a hiss of cold mist vented as the tournament’s one and only victor limped into the waiting room.

“... Well,” Zora said, turning his head with a soft smile. “You look like a boy who just fought a monster. Was the little heiress that difficult?”

Enki ignored him at first. The boy simply walked past, trailing a faint scent of smoke and metal—like cooled steel and bitter resin—but then he mumbled something under his breath, so soft even Eria tilted her head as though she couldn’t hear him.

But Zora heard him.

“I had to use my wormholes to evade that final strike,” Enki mumbled.

And he didn’t sound pleased about it.

Zora grinned and thumped a thumb against his own chest. “If I hadn’t lost to her in the semifinals, do you think I would’ve given you a run for your money?”

Without missing a beat, Enki answered, “I have no money.”

With that, Enki passed the two of them and headed out of the waiting room through the exit on their right.

“… And so, the winner of this year’s Grand Ayapacha Tournament is Eryn Salaqa!” one of the announcers screamed outside, and the final volley of boos and outraged applause actually hurt Zora’s eardrums a little. “The award ceremony will take place in one week’s time, in the Empress’ Hall!”

One week.

One more week, and we’ll either find Vantari, or this would’ve been a complete waste of our time.

Zora leaned back, lacing his fingers together as he focused on Eria nibbling her nails.

One more week, and I’ll know just what she’s supposed to be.
