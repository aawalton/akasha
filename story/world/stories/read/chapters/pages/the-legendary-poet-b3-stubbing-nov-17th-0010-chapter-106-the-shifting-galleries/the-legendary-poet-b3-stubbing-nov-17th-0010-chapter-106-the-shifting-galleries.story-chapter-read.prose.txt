Darkness swallowed the fall.

It took the wind first. Then direction. Then the field above them, which folded into a shrinking shard of painted green and vanished like a page crumpled in a fist.

The System finally arrived.

[Raid Dungeon Entered: The Shifting Galleries]

The notification flashed in front of all ten players.

For Kage, the clean block letters held for half a second. Silver filigree rooted through the edges. The words softened, bled, and rewrote themselves stroke by stroke in thin black calligraphy.

[Archival Instance Entered: The Second Stanza - Draft 4 (Abandoned)]

The altered text carried a texture. Wet ink. Dry vellum. Dust caught between old pages.

Great, Kage thought. The raid came with special treatment.

The void below brightened.

An island of polished black glass rushed up to meet them. It floated in endless dark, smooth and circular and wide enough for a small plaza. The surface reflected the falling party in distorted silver smears. A pressure rose under Kage’s boots a heartbeat before impact, the same way an elevator caught itself at the last floor.

He landed in a low crouch, one palm on the obsidian, Mumyo already in his hand.

Around him, the rest of the party sorted themselves out with varying degrees of dignity. Finn landed awkwardly and grabbed Ren's arm to stop from pitching sideways. Jax hit the obsidian like he'd dropped from a ladder, planted one knee, and came up with his sword already half-drawn. Zherdev belly-flopped with a noise like a man falling off a dock.

"I planned that."

He pushed himself upright. The stained glass pane was gone. Gone the way quest items disappeared when their purpose was spent. His gauntlet was empty, the Curator's Invitation consumed somewhere between the vestibule and the fall.

For a few seconds, nobody spoke again.

The place earned the silence.

The floor under his boots was obsidian, polished to a finish that caught light it had no right catching. The light had no source. It simply existed, distributed and soft, emanating from somewhere architecturally impossible, as though the void itself had decided this particular platform deserved illumination.

The platform wasn't large. Maybe forty meters across, roughly circular, a stage floating in absolute nothingness. The nothingness extended in every direction, down and up and laterally, until it ceased to be a concept and became fact.

Through that void, things drifted.

Frames.

Massive gilded picture frames the size of buildings, some of them larger, coasting through the black on their own slow and private paths. Some held paintings so vivid they read as windows. One frame, maybe two hundred meters to the left and descending slowly, contained a storm. The lightning inside it fired in absolute silence. The clouds moved. Rain fell within the frame's interior, striking nothing, falling into its own contained depth. Another frame held a city burning. The fire shifted inside its borders, something alive and terrible, and yet the whole spectacle drifted through the void with the unhurried weight of a cloud passing over a field.

And above.

Colossal shapes filled what passed for sky. Crumpled balls of parchment, huge as moons, caught the sourceless light on every wrinkled surface and cast long shadows down into the void below. Some of them turned, very slowly, with the lazy rotation of celestial bodies. Each crumple in the paper held its own shadows. Each shadow contained an abyss.

Finn's hawk settled on his shoulder and went completely still.

Somewhere far away, something made a dry, patient sound.

Like a page turning in an empty library.

Finn tipped his head back and forgot to be scared for a second.

“Oh,” he said.

The word came out small and honest.

Even Zara’s face loosened.

Lily stepped close to the island’s rim and looked out over the drifting frames. Their light painted itself over her armor and broke along the silver trim. “It’s beautiful.”

“Yeah,” Jax said, almost against his will.

Vami gave a short laugh under his breath. “Now this is more like it.”

Zherdev slowly rose to his feet. The cape behind him streamed royal purple and somehow orange across the black mirror floor. For once, even he kept his voice low.

“Bro,” he whispered. “I… I bought this?”

Kage stood.

The reflection that rose with him looked wrong. Too long. Too sharp at the edges. The black script from the threshold still wound over his hand and wrist in drifting lines, as though the air above his skin had accepted handwriting and refused to let it go. He closed his fingers once. The letters tightened, then loosened.

He looked up at the parchment moons again.

Beautiful, he thought, and the word annoyed him on contact.

It still fit.

That annoyed him more.

Jax broke the spell first.

“All right.” He pointed at Kage with the hand that wasn’t on his expensive sword. “Before we walk into whatever nightmare museum this is, we’re talking about the voice.”

Silence shifted toward Kage in a single motion.

Finn nodded immediately. “Yeah. Uh. That. It called you Master.”

Zara folded her arms. “In case the phrasing slipped past anyone, the dungeon recognized him before it recognized the raid.”

Lily looked at Kage, careful and direct. “Do you know why?”

Zherdev raised both hands. “I would like to state for the record that this is the coolest possible outcome and also maybe the worst one.”

Julie’s gaze settled on Kage’s floating script, then lifted to his face.

Ren said nothing. His attention held steady in that quiet way disciplined people had. He watched like he was waiting for the first real strike in a duel.

Vami smiled.

Kage exhaled through his nose.

“There’s an easy answer,” he said. “I obviously haven’t been here before. The game launched like, not even a week ago. This raid’s probably one of the first attempts worldwide. So unless I am a planted dev, it’s the class.”

Nobody argued with that.

The logic landed cleanly. The problem stayed exactly where it was.

Jax stared at him. “So you do have a legendary class.”

“The red door, the voice from the sky, and the part where the threshold went insane probably already answered that,” Kage said.

“Still nice to hear it out loud,” Zara replied.

Kage looked over the floating dark one more time, buying half a second. The secret had already died outside the door. No point acting possessive over the corpse.

“When I got the class,” he said, “the system called it a legacy of the First Maker.”

The words hung in the void.

Even Zherdev shut up for a full second.

Lily blinked. “The First Maker?”

“That’s all I got,” Kage said. “A title and a headache. So my best guess is this place has something to do with that.”

Zara’s eyes narrowed. “And your class name?”

He glanced at her.

“Architect of Verse.”

Zherdev actually clutched his chest. “That is so disgustingly cool.”

Julie looked at Vami without turning her head. “You dragged me into a raid with a man named Architect of Verse and decided the glowing berserker prince was the weirdest person here.”

Vami spread his hands. “I make efficient decisions.”

“You sure do,” Julie said.

Finn looked from Kage to the parchment moons, then back again. “Wait. So the dungeon called you Master because your class belongs to whoever made... all this?”

“Maybe,” Kage said.

“That’s a bad maybe,” Jax muttered.

“A useful bad maybe,” Zara corrected.

Ren finally spoke.

His voice came out calm and even. “Legacy class.”

Kage gave a small nod.

Ren’s gaze drifted to the drifting frames, then to Kage’s hand again. He filed the information away without another visible reaction.

Vami took a step closer.

“What were the conditions?”

Kage looked at him.

“For your class,” Vami said. “Mine took work. A lot of work. The requirements were vicious, the route was worse, and the final part was... creative.” He paused, then added with complete honesty, “I cheesed the ending.”

Julie closed her eyes for a moment like she’d heard this confession before.

Kage almost smiled.

“Respect,” Zherdev whispered.

Vami ignored him. “So what opened yours?”

Kage rolled one shoulder.

“I wish I knew that.”

“You must have a theory,” Zara said.

“Several.” He watched a distant frame drift past with a blizzard trapped inside it. Snow swirled forever around the same leaning pine. “Most of them are bad.”

“Those are the fun ones,” Vami said.

Kage let the thought settle into words.

“I built full offense at the start. Damage, speed. I left Artistry at the floor because I figured it was a dead stat for anything that paid. Then I got pulled into a chain that clearly expected an actual artisan. Somewhere in there, something went sideways.”

Finn blinked. “Sideways how?”

“I think I forced a questline through a door it wasn’t built to open,” Kage said. “It wanted somebody with real Artistry. I showed up with base scraps, a sword, and the kind of bad judgment that keeps hospitals in business.”

Zherdev nodded like this confirmed something sacred. “That sounds like you, yeah.”

Kage kept going.

“Maybe I bugged it. Maybe the game liked the contradiction. Maybe Morpheus saw a cheap joke and signed off on it.” He lifted his marked hand a little. “Then it turned into this.”

Vami’s grin widened.

“So you brute-forced an artist class.”

“Basically.”

“That’s amazing.”

“It was a stupid investment,” Kage said, then added, "at first."

“Those are also the fun ones,” Vami replied.

Julie studied Kage in silence. She wore the expression of someone rearranging a diagnosis after new test results came in.

Ren stayed still.

Lily looked thoughtful. Zara looked vindicated. Jax looked irritated that the explanation somehow made the situation both clearer and worse.

Then Zara spoke again.

“For someone who spent launch day answering every question with one word, you’ve become much more talkative.”

Kage looked at her.

Finn nodded at once. “Yeah. In the mines you were kind of scary.”

“That is underselling it,” Jax said.

Lily smiled faintly. “You do seem different.”

He said nothing.

She continued anyway, gently.

“Your gaze lingers on things now. Scenery. People. You actually react.” Her eyes flicked past him to the drifting frames and back. “I like this version of you more.”

Jax grunted. “He used to walk around like the world owed him time.”

“Facts,” Zherdev said instantly. “Before this? Bro. I would send him entire panic essays, and I’d get back one "ok" and maybe a grunt. He acted like breathing cost money and I was wasting his oxygen.”

Finn made a helpless little gesture. “It's… you’re still intense. Just... less like you’re about to disappear into a wall.”

Valdrias shifted his shield onto his shoulder. “You carry yourself easier.”

That one surprised Kage enough that he actually looked at him.

Valdrias offered a small shrug. “What? I have eyes.”

Vami folded his arms and watched with open interest, his expression sharpening in that way it did whenever the conversation stopped being social and started revealing structure.

Kage stood there with all of them looking at him and had no useful answer.

Was I really…

The question came fast and heavy.

He searched for a clean explanation and found a mess.

Money helped. That part was obvious. The immediate debt had stopped trying to bite his throat out every morning. His mother had time now. Six months. Breathing room. A future far enough away to plan for. He still needed more. A lot more. The cure sat out on the horizon with a price tag big enough to make a saint rob a bank. But the pressure had changed shape.

Before, every wasted minute had teeth.

It bit when he talked too long. It bit when he looked at scenery. It bit when he let a conversation become human. It bit whenever something inside him tried to stop and feel anything except urgency.

Keep moving. Keep earning. Keep her alive.

That order had lived under everything.

Now the command still existed. He could feel it, dull and heavy in the center of his chest, like an old bruise pressed by weather. It still spoke. It just had less leverage. The old twisting pain that used to force his hand had loosened. The feeling remained. The power had drained out of it.

He’d noticed it at Songstone.

He’d noticed it in Greywater, standing in front of Lyra’s paintings longer than justified.

He’d noticed it with NPCs. In the way he had handed Lily the skill book. Even with Zherdev, which was frankly incriminating.

He kept doing things that the old version of him would have cut off at the knees.

And the strangest part was that some of those things felt right.

Not efficient or safe. Right.

The answer sat somewhere between his class, his relief, his exhaustion, and whatever crack had opened in him when survival stopped screaming loud enough to drown everything else.

No clean label fit.

Kage looked at Lily first, then Zara, then the others.

“I...” The word caught on the way out.

That alone bought him ten full points of social damage.

He finished anyway. “I don’t know.”

Nobody interrupted.

The page-turning sound in the distance moved once through the dark.

The honesty of it landed somewhere in the space between him and the rest of them, and he watched it land, and it stayed there, and nobody filled it with something easy.

A beat.

He straightened.

“Okay. We didn’t come here to review my personality,” he said. “Focus on the raid.”

Zara accepted the pivot immediately. Bless her damaged little spreadsheet soul.

Nobody pushed. Julie gave a small nod that acknowledged both things simultaneously, what he had said and what he hadn't, and turned back to the environment. Vami was already looking outward. Ren moved to the platform's edge and looked down into the void, measuring the drop the way someone thought in terms of falling and what came after it.

The party spread across the obsidian island and took inventory of the situation.

The frames drifted in their own orbits. The crumpled parchment moons turned overhead, slow and immense. The sourceless light held steady.

And then the geometry decided it had been patient long enough.

A staircase emerged from the edge of the platform twenty meters to their right. Stone steps, functional and ordinary, ascending at a reasonable angle toward a second platform higher up. For approximately four seconds it looked like a beginning.

Then the stairs flattened. Each step rotated horizontal, smooth and deliberate, until the staircase had become a ramp. Zherdev stepped onto the first reformed stone out of reflex and immediately lost his footing. Finn caught his arm.

"Uhh, thanks," Zherdev said.

"Don't do that," Finn said.

Across the platform, a bridge of text materialized. Letters of a blackened, archaic script arranged themselves into planks and railings, an entire structure of pure language suspended between the obsidian island and a secondary platform to the north. It held for three seconds. Then the letters redistributed, rearranging into different words, and two of the planks dissolved mid-span as the letters forming "floor" became "ceiling" and relocated accordingly.

Vami stepped to the edge of the obsidian bridge and peered down into the dark below it with open delight.

“I love this place.”

“You say that about every location that looks like it could kill us for stepping wrong,” Julie said.

“Because those are the good ones.”

There were no fixed hallways. No fixed anything. The geometry rewrote itself on a cycle Kage couldn't calculate from one observation. Every surface carried the implicit asterisk of temporary. The crumpled parchment overhead cast shifting shadows as the moons turned, and each new shadow produced a new configuration on the platforms below. A ramp visible only when the light hit it from the left. A text-bridge assembling itself in the shade and dissolving when the shadow passed.

Kage watched one of the massive frames drift past at platform-level, maybe fifteen meters out, containing what looked like a half-rendered shoreline. The paint on its rightmost third was absent. Bare canvas, white and waiting.

Abandoned, the designation had said. Draft 4.

Everything in this place had been left unfinished.

Mumyo purred in his palm.

Ten people on a floating island. No fixed paths. Constantly revising architecture. Frames drifting through the void that might lead somewhere or might simply be scenery from a thought that never completed itself.

Kage looked at the text-bridge as it assembled a new configuration, at the reformed stairs deciding again between ramp and staircase, at the secondary platforms orbiting at different heights with no obvious connections between them.

The question was straightforward enough.

In an infinite void full of floating frames and restructuring language, where exactly was the beginning?
