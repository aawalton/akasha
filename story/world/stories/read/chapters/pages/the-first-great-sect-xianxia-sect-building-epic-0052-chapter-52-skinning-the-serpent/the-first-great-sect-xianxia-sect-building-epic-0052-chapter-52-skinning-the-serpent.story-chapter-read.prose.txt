Liao Hua, the new Lord Liao, had been born to the preeminent Cultivation clan in the Amber Sea and from the moment she could make a fist, she had held a blade. Before she knew the letters for her name, she knew the Trigrams and their meanings. The history of her clan, their victories and their rise, those were her bedtime stories.

She could trace her descent to heroes because her ancestors were great warriors. To fight, to kill, to stain her hands red and murder until no enemy remained were the aspects of her legacy she had inherited most certainly.

And so it was with the legacy of lightning in her blood that she led a charge of cavalry towards a bandit camp. The camp was burning, bandits funnelled out in a great panic. Her spy had done his job well; such disarray his doing and Hua’s will—a tool worthy of silver and accolades should he escape the chaos unscathed. If he should fall, she would avenge him.

What did the bandits see? With fire at their backs and smoke in their eyes, could they see more than just blurry shapes crossing the plain. Hear thunderous hooves and shaking earth over the snap and hiss of flame, orders shouted in chaos and chaos in every scream of a dying bandit. Smell the impending spray of blood over thick smoke and that fear sweat of panic.

Faced with thundering horses and a fire at their back, the bandits chose to form lines. Tried to form them. Missing weapons, lacking armour, roused from slumber, the bandits showed great discipline in doing so. Scattered, confused, without leaders to marshal them, the lines were weak things, more pockets of temporary order amidst the smoke and fire than truly solid lines.

Hua arrived first and with her blade held in her-off hand, she leaned to the side, balancing through stirrups with Cultivator’s innate balance, and swung her blade. The bandit, wide-eyed, fear-eyed, could barely bring up his knife in response. Not that it would help.

The rising edge of Hua’s sword carved through skin and bone, flesh and jaw. Steel gave the bandit a new face, one of sheared bone and deep red. Hua righted herself and continued onward. Her lovely horse, always so placid and calm, did not so much as flinch from the blood. Charged forward, momentum toppling over the next hapless bandits; one, two, three, four, five plus five more knocked down bandits as the full weight of her cavalry shattered lines, scattered bandits, breaking through with contemptuous ease.

Hua tugged on the reins and the horse turned in a long arc. A squad of riders broke off and followed her whilst Captain Yao and the main force went in an arc opposite hers. They would trace spiral that turned in on itself, the main force becoming a hammer whilst Hua’s force would become the anvil against which the bandits would break themselves.

Disorganised, trapped in a sudden churn of death and violence and fire, they could do nothing to fight back. Captain Yao’s plan was simple, it was elegant, and it gave Hua the chance to have some fun.

Hua leapt off her horse and slammed into a hasty defensive formation, barrelling through with her momentum. She did not think as she swung her blade, finding flesh somehow. Blood sprayed, hot and tacky. It was a childhood friend, this violence, and it greeted her with bodies to slaughter.

Her cousin landed near her. Landed on a bandit to be specific, knees first, a crushing force with his bulk. Brushed his sword along the bandit’s throat and rose, arcing his sword to strike anyone too slow to back away. Sometimes, she forgot just how strong he was, surprised when he sent three bandits flying back like they were nothing.

Hers had been a force of their strongest in Body Tempering. Not a perfect hammer and anvil—they just lacked the numbers or geography for such a tactic, but such tactics didn’t tend to consider the force multiplication that occurred with Body Tempering.

Hua alone could hold up twenty, maybe thirty of these soldiers. With Weiji beside her, they were a wall unto themselves. Supported by ten other soldiers in armour, who had trained for years to wage battle just like this, they were a mountain for the bandits to smash themselves against.

Together they would hold the bandits along one direction whilst Captain Yao and the bulk of the cavalry would hold the other direction. The third was held by their camp, burning, smouldering, fire spreading against winter’s best efforts to smother it. It left only one direction to flee, one that would soon be plugged by the refugees and the imperial contingent.

Quickly, she fell into a state of battle calm. Her Qi flowing easily through her body, her body moving through instinct, needing no instruction thrust her blade the moment she found a vulnerable throat.

The bandit was dead before he knew it.

Hua drew her blade back as she walked past the bandit. Brought it down and claimed another life. Steel biting through leather, through fat and skin and bone. Faster, she forced herself to move. Steel whistling, her blade little more than a smear of silver and crimson.

There came a point where Cultivation allowed one to be efficient in a different way. Hua was simply faster, could go around blades and spears, pick out killing blows without needing to fight a protracted battle. And anyone stronger, strong enough to perhaps slow her down, she kicked towards her cousin who had the strength to just rip their clubs and halberds from their hands, and turn them against their allies.

Her cousin was making an admirable showing—

She turned on the spot and felt more than she saw the spear that tried to run her through. She spun low to the ground, avoiding the spear. Her sword came down and gave a bandit a smiling gut, gut as tongue lolling out soon after.

—with each kill he made. What a liar. Acting like ten kills would be difficult for him. When he was prepared and had time to steel himself, Weiji moved in a manner befitting their Clan. He struck with the ferocity of lightning, breaking through ranks like a thunderclap.

The bandit she had gutted fell to his knees, holding his exposed cavity, as though he could stop them from spilling out. It felt a fair trade for each of the Jolly Rangers.

Hua found her new sword served her well. It did not so much as threaten to break as it bit into a collarbone and did not complain as she met steel with steel, pushing back a saber, stepping into her opponent’s guard, and gracelessly shoving the blade in his gut once, twice, thrice, hot, tacky blood spilling over her hand.

Hua pushed her blade deeper, pushed him back, and let gravity remove him from her sword.

Hua spent a moment to glance across the field of battle. Captain Yao was doing an excellent job of leading their main contingent. They were cutting through bandits, using the advantage of horseback and better preparation to counter any disadvantage in numbers.

It was as the bandits finally realised this was a losing battle, and they could only escape if they wished to survive, that the third and final part of their plan came to fruition.

A wave of refugees crashed upon the scattered bandits, led by a central core of twenty Imperial soldiers, one of who was a Cultivator of Qi Condensation. The refugees were armed in a mishmash of borrowed spears and swords, unarmoured and scared, but numbers had a quality all of their own. And spears were spears.

Hua had been an anvil, Captain Yao a hammer that struck again and again, and imperial-led-civilans would pull the noose the bandits didn’t even know had been thrown around their necks.

The battle had been decided before it began.

***

It was somewhere around her thirtieth kill that she found her target.

Hua hadn’t kept a true count, she was guessing purely on the amount of blood covering her. Her dark leathers dyed burgundy, cobbled pieces of armour tinted red, painted in splatters and swirls of viscera; bone fragments matted her hair, gave her a crown of teeth and femur blending in with her silver strands.

Filthy, dirty work, this butchering was. Grandmother had warned Hua she did not understand the smell of a battlefield: hot blood thick in the nose, shit heavy as the dead voided themselves and the cloying bright stink of piss as the living marked their fear; sweat beneath that, and far beneath that, the indifference of churned mud; frigid winds providing a moment of relief before smoke swept through, sizzling meat from those too slow to escape the camp. All of it in her mouth and nose and throat. Death and the dying, covering her thoroughly.

Nothing could be done about it except to win and leave. Victory was her only escape and Hua carried it on her blade’s edge which thrummed with Qi, strengthened, reinforced, eager to kill. With every death, she came closer to a future away from this battlefield.

As she moved, the world seemed to move for her. She always found the right place to step, the best moment to strike. Like the world was keeping count to the beat of her heart, waiting for the moment she struck. Always finding a bandit just looking away, a bandit just distracted. Conveniences adding up, luck always in her favour.

Teeth barred, Qi flowing, Hua struck, sweeping her sword through leather and flesh. Blood whetting her blade. Hua truly, truly missed the simplicity of her older battles. The nobility of single combat. Not this thing where it became impersonal and made her little more than a bored butcher.

“Didn’t you say you would slaughter the gods for me?” Qing interrupted, mocking her.

Hua flinched back from her vitriol.

An arrow flew by her.

The arrowhead struck a bandit through the neck, blood spraying. He clutched at the shaft uselessly. In the riotous din, she had not been able to hear the distinctive whistle. Hua reached out and plucked the arrow from the bandit’s neck.

It took her a moment to find her target. The winds fell just right, cut through curtains of smoke to reveal an archer. There, still in the burning camp, thinking he could hide and strike her down. He should have fled, should have counted on more than mere distance to protect him. Hua drew her arm back, took three steps forward, and on the fourth, she threw the bloody arrow back.

The arrow struck clean through his chest, possibly the shoulder, possibly the heart, Hua couldn’t tell. He fell, maybe dead. Dead as the bandit sneaking up on her who had his face kicked in by her cousin.

In the corner of her vision, she saw the Shaman dancing. Hua stepped forward in time with her. Felt a surge of irritation as she landed on a suspiciously solid piece of ground while her opponent slid in the mud.

That should be thirty, she thought, stabbing her opponent through the heart.

A flare of Qi heralded his arrival. It was the greatest amount of Qi after Hua, after Weiji. Stars burning bright as a mountain of a man emerging from the flames of the camp. He wielded a wolf tooth cudgel as easily as Hua wielded her blade. A great club, tall as Hua was, made of solid timber and banded in metal with sharp spikes.

Where he swung, Hua’s soldiers, those in Body Tempering, were flung back. It caused a shift, a moment of rallying amongst the bandits.

With a face freshly burned, and recently shaved, Kang was not what she expected. Not the matter of his build, which spoke of power, or the soot-stained white robes, but just the lack of presence for someone who had so thoroughly terrified the Western Reaches.

Kang leapt with a Cultivator’s strength and landed amidst the Imperials. Hua didn’t care for them. But she did care for those they led, the refugees who had picked up spear and saber against their oppressors, if only for a debt owed.

The lesioned mortal, who was something like Shaman Yan’s second, was unable to withstand the great shaking of Kang’s landing and toppled onto his back. With one great swing, Kang unseated the captain of the imperial contingent and sent his armoured bulk flying into a row of his soldiers. And with another swing, he struck the lesioned mortal who was trying to rise.

His chest split open by the force of the descending club. His torso pulped in an instant. A fast death, at least.

A scream, a shout, a stutter as the leader of the refugees screamed. Shaman Yan, standing with them, her dancing ending.

Hua crossed the distance and shoved Wu Yan away, raising her blade and blocking the full weight of a club with one arm. It rocked her, force rippling through steel and flesh and bone. It hurt, Hua could not pretend otherwise.

I need to stop using my main arm until it heals fully, she thought with a snarl. Her sleeve was turning red. Old wounds reopened. New wounds formed.

He was strong. That could not be denied. A true physical cultivator.

Much as Hua wished it, she was not one. Qi rose, seeping from her meridians to her bones and flesh.

“Dance!” she ordered the rising shaman, “and make yourself useful.”

Hua dodged and slipped beneath Kang’s blow. With a tensing of her muscles, and a push off the ground, she slammed into him shoulder-first. Forced to use her leading arm, it jostled her injury again. But it pushed her enemy back.

Kang caught himself on three points, cudgel still held defensively. Strength and agility. Hua would barely have managed to keep hold her sword, let alone a club as long as she was tall, if someone slammed into her with that much force. A physical Cultivator of the second star, maybe even the third. Absurdly powerful for a backwater like this. But nowhere as powerful as Hua had hoped for.

Even mortals can kill a Cultivator if you bring enough of them, Hua thought, disappointed, in Kang, in the Jolly Rangers.

“You don’t seem very much like a serpent,” she said, dodging a swing of his club once more. “You’re using a wolf club. What, is there a Wolf bandit who stole the title first? That why you had to call yourself a serpent?”

“Why don’t you taste this and find out?”

Hua was not stupid as to miss the gleam on his spikes. The cudgel had been coated in poison. Which… how many people could even survive a glancing blow from that thing that he felt the need to poison it as well? It seemed excessive at best.

Even though she was on a battlefield and someone could kill her with a loosed arrow, Hua still felt compelled to point it out since it spoke of incompetency.

“Shut the fuck up.”

“I think not. I find myself very curious.”

“Your curiosity can follow you to the grave.”

The club came down in an overhead swing.

Her heart steadied and she was pervaded by a deep calm. Hua stepped to the side of it. She didn’t trust her sword not to break under the earth-shattering force of that strike. It wasn’t something she wanted to do, replacing the blade. The last time she went without a blade, she nearly challenged an elder to a death match. Hua wasn’t keen on repeating the experience.

Quickly, it became a matter of blocking his wide swings. He was powerful, yes, but nowhere near fast enough to make use of it against her. And that led to her boredom. There, a moment’s hesitation. Hua slipped past his guard and her blade bit into his wrist, cutting deep enough to reveal bone.

He yelled, dropping his spiked cudgel. Skittering back, grabbing at a curved knife strapped to his waist.

The fight, what little of it remained, was of little interest to Hua. His Qi had an uncertain quality to it and his footwork was poor. This is what felled the Rangers?

“How long ago did you unlock your last meridian?”

“Last night when your mother was riding on my—”

Hua stabbed him in the shoulder and pulled the blade out before he could even react to the pain. “Don’t speak of my mother.”

Kang squeezed his shoulder, teeth showing past curled lips. “She was a whore and danced on my—”

She punched him in the throat. It was foolish but she leapt on him and brought him down. Hua smashed the pommel of her blade against his face over and over again.

Mortals didn’t get this privilege. They were squishy, died easily, and she didn’t enjoy getting heir blood on her. They got to die quick for annoying her. But a Cultivator? Oh, she could take her time.

It made her feel better. Boness snapping beneath her pommel, first, and then her fist as she dropped the blade. The squelch of flesh. The cloying smell of viscera and blood. Bone splintering, grey matter splattering.

“Hua, stop. You don’t have to do this. This isn’t what a hero would do.”

She ignored Qing. Ignored everything else. Only the pounding of her knuckles was worth paying attention to.

***

Finally, after too long a time, her bloodlust settled. She raised her head, breathing hard, breathing blood and guts and smoke. The battle was drawing to a close. Her forces had won. Now all that remained was the slaughter.

“I think he’s very dead,” Weiji said, snapping a bandit’s neck. He liked doing that. “Punching his head to a paste probably did it. And if not that, ripping out his heart probably managed it.”

“Would you let anyone get away with insulting your mother?”

Weiji’s eyes widened, and he stepped back, nearly tripping on a smear of blood. “Ah, that would explain it. And no, I’d find a way to kill even you if you insulted my mother.”

Hua gave him a smile, blood-splattered, teeth glinting, golden eyes burning. “I imagine you would. I couldn’t stand anyone who accepted such disrespect. Now, let’s make sure the rest of these bandits are dead.”

“Please stop smiling like that,” Weiji said.

It would take an hour more to make their way through the entirety of the battlefield. An hour more to kill stragglers, check for those faking their deaths, and explore the burning camp for any stragglers.

“Liu Xin, you did well. Weiji didn’t believe you were capable of it.” She gave her cousin a sickly-sweet smile. “Weiji, don’t you have an apology to make.”

“Of course,” he said through gritted teeth. “Forgive me for mistakenly applying the concepts of nobility and honour to a Jurchen. I forgot they were inherently treacherous creatures.”

Hua shook her head and threw her sword. It shot past Liu Xin and cut through a bandit who had been playing dead, making space with the arrow in his shoulder. The same bandit who had nearly landed a clean shot on her with an arrow.

“I need to start confirming that I’ve killed a person,” she told her cousin.

“Bad habit not to,” he agreed, smirking at Liu Xin.

Liu Xin, sadly, was shivering too much to give an answer. Hua left him to it and ripped her sword out of the man’s chest. Later, in the midst of searching the camp, she found Captain Yao who had engineered this slaughter.

“My dear captain, am I mistaken or have you broken through to the next stage of Qi Condensation?”

“You would be correct, my Lord,” he said, his dour resolution lifted by an undercurrent of pride, a hint of ambition. It was a good look on him. A worthy look for someone with two stars burning in their soul.

Hua wasn’t bitter at all that he’d advanced after so easy a battle.

“Then let me be the first to offer my congratulations. The reward for your service is yet greater service. This lord has a need for heads. Preferably unattached to their bodies.”

Kang had inspired her. Why couldn’t she make a monument to her victories as well?

And she had promised to bring back some heads for Xue Meili. Hua was just making good on her agreement.
