Chapter 89

The road was empty in both directions when Rayan stepped out of the brush, and that was the only reason he stepped out at all. His boots were ruined. He had walked through a creek somewhere south of the compound and never quite dried out. Now the leather rubbed at his heels with every stride. He kept walking anyway, because stopping felt worse than blisters.

He talked to himself when he was alone. His mother used to swat the back of his head for it, and it had still never gone away. Now there was no one to swat him and so he muttered as he walked, complaining to the trees about his boots, about the smoke smell that would not come out of his shirt, about how stupid it was to be alive when all the others were not.

He should be dead. Literally everyone he had been living with all these years was dead. Riley's army came in and killed everyone but him. He was sure she would burn the whole compound to the ground. This was routine, it was revenge. He had been walking for a while, long enough to seriously consider his recent life choices. He remembered when he had joined the raiders. Jobs were hard to come by and for those with limited options robbing people was the only option. It was unfortunate he was really bad at it. He remembered back to when he had first started.

The raiders had been going to kill him almost from the first week. He had known their faces and their numbers and the way the loot moved through the compound, and they had no use for a boy who could not fight or rob. Cooking was his only option and he was bad at that too.

He laughed under his breath, while walking. He had been a terrible cook. Genuinely, historically bad. The first stew he made had separated into a grey film on top and a sludge at the bottom, with a layer of greasy water in between, and the men had thrown the bowls at him. Two of the bowls had been wood and one had been pewter and the pewter one had split his eyebrow open and left a scar. The next night they had locked him in the broom closet because the bread had been raw in the middle and burned on the crust, and Old Mott had said if he had to chew through one more loaf made of charcoal and dough he would feed Rayan to his own oven.

He had cried in the closet. He was not ashamed of that now. He had been thirteen and he had thought he was going to die there, smothered between the brooms and a barrel of lye soap, and he had cried until he ran out of tears and then he had sat in the dark and waited.

What had saved him was a cookbook.

The raiders had come back from a market town three days later with a wagon of furniture and a chest of clothes and, almost as an afterthought, a stack of books from some merchant's house. They had been about to burn the books for kindling. Rayan had asked, in the smallest voice he had, if he could look at them first. Bart had laughed at him and tossed the whole stack at his feet and said to keep one and burn the rest.

He had picked the cookbook because it had a chicken burned into the leather cover. He had read that book until the pages came loose from the binding. He had read it by candlelight and by firelight and once, memorably, by the light of a burning shed that the men had set alight after a job. He had learned what a roux was. He had learned why salt went in early and pepper went in late. He had learned that the secret to a stew was not the meat at all. He had taught himself, over weeks and then months, to cook.

By the end he was good. The men stopped throwing bowls. They started waiting outside the kitchen door when they smelled what was coming. Old Mott once told him the lamb shank had been the best thing he had ever eaten, and then immediately pretended he had not said it.

He had found something. That was the part he could not stop turning over now, walking on these blistered feet. He had found a thing he was good at, and the men who had been about to kill him had instead come to love him for it, in their own ugly way, and now they were all dead and burned and the kitchen was a hole in the ground, and he was on a road in boots that did not fit.

He wanted, with a sudden ferocity that almost stopped his feet, to be back in that kitchen. To be standing over a pot of broth with the bones rolling slow at the bottom and Mott yelling for him to hurry up. To have all of this be a story he was telling someone later, instead of the thing that was happening to him now.

He heard the cart before he saw it.

The sound came from behind him, the slow clop of hooves and the creak of wood on a packed road, and he stepped to the verge and turned. A wagon was coming from a distance. One driver, broad in the shoulders, a crossbow propped near his knee where his hand could find it.

Rayan thought, very fast, about running into the trees, and decided against it. Running was what guilty men did. He was a cook. He was an unemployed cook on a road, and that was all he was, and he repeated it in his head the way the cookbook had taught him to repeat the order of operations for a sauce.

He raised his hand.

The driver pulled the horses down to a slow walk twenty paces out and looked him over from boots to hairline before he spoke.

"You're a long way from anything, friend."

"I know it," Rayan said. He tried to sound tired, which was not difficult. "I've been walking since dawn."

"From where?"

"Back that way." He gestured vaguely. "I got turned around in the trees a day ago and came out on the road and just kept on it."

The driver studied him another moment and then nodded once and tipped his head toward the bench. "Climb up. I'm going to Stonehaven. You can ride if you don't smell worse than my horses."

"I might."

"Then sit downwind."

Rayan climbed up. The bench was hard and warm from the sun. He set his hands on his knees so they would not shake.

"Garron," the driver said, slapping the reins.

"Rayan."

The wagon rolled on.

For a while Garron did not say anything else, and Rayan was grateful for it. He watched the road unspool ahead of them and tried to slow his breathing. He tried to remember what an unemployed cook in his situation would actually be thinking about, and could not, because all he could think about was the smell of the longhouse burning.

"Hunting party, you said?"

"I didn't, but yes." Rayan made himself look at Garron. "I was with a hunting party. Three others. We got separated when a stag broke north and I went after it like a fool."

"You a hunter?"

"No." He almost smiled. "This was my first time. I really don't want to hunt. But a good cook tries to source his own ingredients when he can. I wanted to see how the animal dies. How it gets dressed out. What the men do with the parts I don't usually see."

Garron made a noise that could have been agreement or could have been nothing. He did not look at Rayan when he spoke again.

"Cook, you said."

"For hire. Mostly."

"Mostly."

"I had a place at an inn south of here for a year. Before that I cooked for a household that lost its money. Before that I was an apprentice." It came out smoother than he expected. He was surprised by himself.

"Hm." Garron flicked the reins. "You any good?"

"Yes."

"Just, yes?"

"I'd say yes. The men I cooked for would have said yes. The men I cooked badly for at the start would have said something different."

Garron's mouth moved at one corner. It was not quite a smile. "Tell me about a stew, then."

"What kind?"

"Any kind. The kind any farmwife in Rivermark could make. I want to hear you talk about it."

Rayan understood, with a small clean drop in his stomach, that this was a test. He took a breath through his nose and let it out and put his hands flat on his thighs.

"All right," he said. "A common stew. Mutton or beef, it doesn't matter much. Onion, carrot, whatever root you've got, a little barley if you have it, salt, pepper, a bay leaf if you're lucky. Most people will tell you the secret is the meat. They're wrong."

"Are they."

"They are. The meat matters, but it's not what makes the stew. Most cooks throw in the cubed meat and a pot of water and call the liquid broth, and what they end up with is brown water with chunks in it. That's not broth. That's an excuse for broth."

"Go on."

"Real broth comes from the bones. You take the bones with a little meat still on them, and you crack the long ones if you can, because that's where the marrow lives, and you put them in cold water and you bring it up slow. Not a hard boil. A slow simmer for hours. Four at the least. Six is better. The marrow comes out into the water, and the connective tissue breaks down, and what you get at the end is something thick on the spoon. You can feel it when you stir. That's the difference between a cheap stew and one a man will remember. It's the bones. It's always the bones."

Garron drove for a few paces without answering.

"Skim the top?" he said.

"Constantly. Every quarter hour for the first hour, and then every half hour after that. The scum on top is what makes a broth taste muddy. You skim it off and you throw it out. Some cooks try to stir it back in. They shouldn't."

"Salt early or late?"

"Early in the bones. Late in the vegetables. Salt pulls flavor out of the bones over a long simmer. It does the opposite to a carrot."

Garron grunted and made a small adjustment to the reins that did not, as far as Rayan could tell, need making. Some of the tightness went out of the air between them.

"You are a cook," Garron said.

"I told you I was."

"Men tell me a lot of things on this road."

They rode on. The trees opened out into a stretch of low pasture and then closed back in. Rayan watched the road and tried not to think about how loud his heart had been a moment ago.

"You're lucky I came along when I did," Garron said. "These parts aren't safe these days. Raiding has been on the rise."

Rayan smiled. He felt it stretch his face wrong and tried to fix it and could not, so he turned a little toward the trees and spoke to the side of Garron's head.

"I hear there's something worse than raiders out here."

"Nonsense."

Garron said it the way an older man says nonsense to a younger one, dismissive and almost fond, the word doing the work of ending the conversation. Rayan let it. He watched the verge of the road slide past, and his hands stayed flat on his thighs, and a long minute went by with only the sound of hooves between them.

"Like what?" Garron said.

There it was.

"I heard there's an army out here that wields a fire that's not natural."

"Every army has fire."

"Not like this fire." Rayan kept his voice low, conversational, the way men talk about ghost stories on a long drive. "This one burns hotter than it should. You can't put it out. Water doesn't touch it. It sticks to wood, to cloth, to skin, and it eats whatever it touches until there's nothing left to eat. It's of the magics. I'd swear it on my mother."

Garron side-eyed him and shook his head, but he did not say nonsense again, he just grunted.

"They say the one who leads them is a witch," Rayan said. "Cold-hearted. Iron-willed. The kind of woman who could stand against the Clawborn themselves and not blink. There must be something in her, some old magic she carries, because by every account she's got barely any soldiers and she's already burned a compound to the ground. With a small force she could undo a whole army. That's what I heard."

He stopped talking. The cart rolled on.

Garron laughed.

It was a big sound, the laugh of a man who has been on the road a long time and met a lot of fools, and it rolled out of him and filled the space between the trees and rang back from them.

"My boy," he said, wiping at the corner of his eye with the back of his wrist, "thank you. Thank you. I'll take that one to the tavern next visit. The cold-hearted witch and her unquenchable fire. They'll buy me drinks for a week on that one alone."

Rayan did not look at him. He kept his eyes ahead, on the long line of the road, and his mouth did what it could to hold a small embarrassed smile.

The laugh went on a moment longer than it needed to. It began to thin and Garron's shoulders, which had been moving with it, slowed. The sound trailed off into something that was almost still a laugh but not quite, the way a fire keeps making noise after the flame is gone.

He glanced sideways at Rayan. Rayan did not turn.

Garron looked back at the road. His hands on the reins were not loose anymore. He clicked his tongue at the lead horse for no reason, and the cart kept rolling, and neither of them said anything for a long time after that.
