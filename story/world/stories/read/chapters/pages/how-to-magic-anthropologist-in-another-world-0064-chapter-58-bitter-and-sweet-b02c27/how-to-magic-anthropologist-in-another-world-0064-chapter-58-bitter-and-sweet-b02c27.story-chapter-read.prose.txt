The world folded in on itself with a familiar pull as star mana transported me back to the sanctified camp.

“Hey, Katar, catch,” I called as I threw the silver sword.

The black-haired boy caught it by the hilt with perfect dexterity.

He examined it carefully, then nodded at me.

I hesitated for a second, then spoke.

“Can you stab me?” I asked.

I opened my mouth to justify the request, but that didn’t seem necessary.

Before I could blink, the sword was already veering toward my side. Katar didn’t hesitate at all. It seems like the boy didn’t need much encouragement to stab an ally.

I tried to jump to the side by reflex, but I could tell it was too late. The sword was definitely going to skewer one of my kidneys.

Then, in the next instant, Katar was no longer in front of me.

Shingo’s back filled my vision instead.

“Nice teleport,” said Katar. “I thought I had you for sure.”

“You could have killed her,” said Calr, shock clear in his voice.

“She did ask,” the swordsman shrugged. “Plus, Vena is here. She would have gotten to her before she bled out.”

“What was that for?” asked Raik, looking slightly annoyed at what just happened.

“I needed to test a conditional wish for teleportation,” I said, my voice still slightly shaking. “I guess it worked.”

I gave them a small smile.

“That was my idea, don’t blame the psycho boy for following instructions.” I said then I let out a long sigh.

“Anyway, I am heading back to Hano. I will be back near dawn.”

I gripped my spear and wished myself back to the Sunless Reach.

“Don’t forget to bring cake,” I could hear Ja’a shout as I disappeared.

After a few minutes to calm down, recharge my star mana, and reset my conditional wishes, I closed my eyes and wished once again.

I appeared in a room that had gotten even more cluttered than before.

Nina’s workshop.

I barely had time to register where I was before something warm slammed into me.

Wings wrapped around my shoulders and back, folding in close and firm.

“Alice, you are safe!” Nina said, her voice tight and shaking.

I laughed softly and hugged back as best I could, my arms awkward against the curve of gray pigeon wings. She held me for a moment longer than necessary, wings curved protectively around me, feathers brushing my hair and spine.

“I’m fine,” I said.

She pulled back just enough to look me over, eyes sharp and searching. “You are not hurt, are you?”

“You worry too much,” I replied.

That earned me a huff and another brief squeeze before she finally let go.

“Okay, you have the rest of the day off,” Nina shouted suddenly, turning toward the far end of the workshop. “All of you. Out.”

Three young men and one girl froze mid-task, hands still on tools.

“But, mistress,” one of the boys started.

“No buts,” Nina snapped. “Go eat, sleep, or do something stupid that does not involve glass lenses for once.”

They scattered immediately, tools clattering as they fled with visible relief. I noticed all four had bags under their eyes.

“Mistress, huh?” I teased.

“Don’t even get me started,” Nina scowled. “Je’e was supposed to send me journeymen, yet they still act like apprentices.”

She shut the door behind them and turned back to me, wings folding neatly against her back.

“Now,” she said, her voice softer, “tell me everything.”

I exhaled and leaned against a worktable, careful not to knock anything over. “Where do I even start?”

“Start from the start, duh,” she said, sticking out her tongue.

I considered it for a moment.

“I met a goddess,” I said.

Nina blinked once. “You what now?”

“Damada showed up in her mountain village conclave.”

“You know mythic gods rarely manifest outside their domain, right?” Nina said, sitting down on a stool and motioning for me to do the same.

“Yeah,” I nodded. “It was amazing. Her aura was so vast. Caring and indifferent at the same time.”

“And were you anywhere involved in the summoning of a goddess?” Nina asked, narrowing her eyes at me.

“No, I had nothing to do with it,” I vehemently denied. I explained the story in detail, how a priest was trying to sacrifice a rival to add a blessing to the land and increase fire root production, and how the goddess showed up to stop him.

“Well, I might have accidentally caused the spike in demand for fire root with my pizza business. I might also have suggested to a child that he pray directly to the goddess, asking for her help to save his mother. But other than that, I was barely involved.”

“Of course you did,” Nina said, smiling knowingly.

“I killed a troll,” I said quickly, changing the topic before Nina could start pinning the goddess’s arrival on me.

Her wings twitched. “You killed a troll.”

“Two, actually. The first one was with help from my team,” I said quickly. “The second one was mostly alone. I teleported behind it and was like, nothing personal, kid, then BAM, lightning spear to the heart.”

“And then,” I continued, my voice lowering, “we went into the Undead Dead Zone.”

That made her go still.

“Why in the Endless Nightmare would you ever do that?” she said.

“Meh, it isn’t too bad,” I shrugged. “Apparently, Vena’s holy power works really well with the undead.”

I told her everything then. Ectamel, skeletons, sanctified ground, ghosts, and the banshee. She was particularly interested in the harvesting machine we found and made me promise to let her examine it once we were back.

Nina listened without interrupting, her eyes never leaving my face.

“You should not be doing this,” she said finally. “You are too smart to die fighting monsters. You should stay here with me and be an inventor.”

“I can’t,” I said. “I really enjoy seeing the world. I can’t be cooped up in a workshop all day.”

She sighed, but nodded.

“Come,” she said, standing. “I am eager to show you what I have done in your absence.”

She led me behind the work desk, stacked with lenses and covered in dense sketches and notes.

She stopped at a padded case and opened it with obvious pride.

“Improved spyglasses,” she said. “Several iterations.”

She lifted one out and handed it to me. The body was slimmer than the prototype, and the focus ring smoother.

I raised it to my eye and adjusted the focus.

The clarity made my breath catch.

“This is amazing,” I said. “Much better than the first one you gave me.”

“I know,” Nina said smugly. “It has some minor enchantments, and the glass lenses were treated with water, sky, and light mana.”

“Is this what you are going to sell to the army?” I asked.

“Nah, it’s too costly,” she said, shaking her head. “That was just me trying to push the concept as far as I could take it.”

She placed her hand on top of mine as I held the spyglass.

“You can keep this one,” she winked. “A thank you for all your help.”

I hesitated. “This is too much. Can I at least pay for the cost of the materials?”

“Don’t worry about it,” she said. “After you introduced me to Je’e, I don’t need to worry about money anymore.”

“Now if only she could get me more competent assistants.”

I laughed. “If you find fault with everyone she sent you, chances are you are the problem. You are the common denominator, after all.”

“You are probably right,” Nina said. “I am an inventor. I don’t have the patience for teaching.”

Her wings fluffed slightly.

“There is something else,” she said, lowering her voice.

She led me to another workbench covered in heavy glass frames with thick lenses.

I frowned. “Those look uncomfortable.”

“They are,” she admitted. “Prototype.”

She lifted the device. Too bulky to be regular spectacles, the frame was reinforced to excess, and two white monster cores jutted from the sides, awkward and unfinished.

“I am trying to see without light,” she said. “Or rather, see a different light.”

“After you explained the prism thing and how light was made of different components, I figured, why not try to see those invisible lights?”

“Infrared or ultraviolet,” I nodded slowly.

She tilted her head. “Are those your words for them?”

“Yes. Infrared are low-frequency, heat-adjacent wavelengths,” I said. "Ultraviolet are more  dangerous, high energy waves, so you should be careful with them."

Her eyes lit up. “Yes. Heat-adjacent. That is the one I am tracking.”

She explained it in her own terms, describing how the monster cores had a light affinity from a creature that lived in extreme darkness and emitted something steady and unseen. She had created a type of glass that bent that light back into visibility.

I nodded as the pieces clicked together. “The glass is catching emissions from the cores and translating them.”

She stared at me. “You understood that far too fast.”

“I had a head start,” I said.

She laughed, delighted.

We talked some more about light waves and frequency, trading words and metaphors until the conversation naturally died down. I stood up, stretching, ready to leave.

Her expression shifted.

“You could visit more,” she said. “You can teleport whenever you like. Come at least at night so we can dream together again.”

I winced.

“I thought about it,” I admitted. “But leaving my team felt wrong. What if they’re attacked while I’m gone?”

Her wings folded tighter against her back. “Then on your next stay in an inn.”

“I will do that,” I said.

“Promise?”

“Promise.”

We hugged again, quieter this time, lingering.

“Come back soon,” she murmured.

“I will.”

The next jump brought me somewhere even warmer, filled with the familiar smell of tomato, yeast, and wood smoke.

For a moment, I just stood there and breathed it in.

The kitchen was large, far larger than what the temple orphanage ever had, with multiple stone stoves lining the walls and enough space for at least ten cooks to work without getting in each other’s way. Hanging racks held pans and ladles, sacks of flour were stacked neatly in one corner, and jars of herbs and oils lined the shelves. The place felt new, a place yet to be tested by a hungry crowd during rush hours.

Louis looked up from one of the stoves as I appeared.

“Alice,” she said, eyes widening.

Then she smiled, bright and unguarded, and wiped her hands on her apron before stepping closer.

“Did you eat?” she asked immediately. “I have some pizza almost ready.”

I laughed. I hadn’t realized I was starving.

“Yes, please,” I said. “I would kill for some of your pizza.”

She grinned. “Let me fix something for you.”

She turned and pushed open the door leading into the restaurant’s seating area. Beyond it, rows of sturdy wooden tables filled the room, still empty, the floor freshly swept. A boy I recognized from the temple orphanage was sweeping along the edge with a broom.

“Seth,” Louis called. “Tell Tan Je’e that Alice is here. She should be in the bank finishing some transactions.”

The boy froze, looking at me, eyes wide. “How did she… the door is locked?”

I gave him a peace sign, not elaborating on my teleportation power.

He let out a petulant sigh and abandoned the broom, unlocked the door, and took off running.

Louis returned to the counter and started working the dough, her movements practiced and confident. She kneaded, turned, and stretched it while we talked, never once needing to look down.

“So,” I said, glancing around, “new shop?”

“Yes,” she said, unable to keep the excitement out of her voice. “We are not officially open yet, but everything is almost ready for our first place. If it works, we already have two more locations planned.”

“That fast?” I asked.

She nodded eagerly. “Je’e says timing matters. She has dropped hints with bards and known gossips, so people are really curious right now.”

“Where are we, exactly?”

“Guild district,” she said. “Right between the merchants’ guild and the carpenters’ guild.”

I smiled. “That’s a good spot.”

“We are also planning one near the freelancer guild,” she continued, “but we want to see the general reaction first. And I need time to train new cooks.”

I watched her for a moment, then gestured at the size of the kitchen. “So you do all the cooking here? Isn’t this a bit large for one person?”

She hesitated, then blushed.

“No. Silnar is helping me,” she admitted. “I needed someone I could trust, so he decided to join me.”

I raised an eyebrow.

She avoided my eyes and focused very hard on spreading sauce.

“We might even convert their old restaurant into another branch of the franchise,” she added quickly.

Her blush deepened. “His mother keeps hinting that she can’t wait to retire and focus on helping with grandbabies.”

I chuckled. “I see your relationship has evolved to the next level.”

Louis nodded, smiling despite herself. “Yeah. We might get married by the end of the year.”

She said it simply, as if the world were natural.

“Congratulations,” I said, and meant it. Then I gave her a look. “So how far have you gotten with him?”

“Alice! I am still a faithful,” Louis reprimanded.

“Oh come on, you can tell me,” I teased.

“We have kissed a few times,” she admitted, blushing. “I know it’s wrong, but I couldn’t help it.”

“There is nothing wrong with love,” I reassured her. “You haven’t lost your class, have you? That means you are fine.”

“I know, but the more I do it, the more I want to do more,” Louis blurted out. She realized what she had said, blushed even further, then turned back to the oven and busied herself with it.

I couldn’t help but chuckle.

“How are Vena and Calr?” she asked, changing the subject.

“They’re doing great,” I said. “Stronger than ever.”

Before she could ask more, footsteps approached.

Tan Je’e emerged with a scroll tucked under one arm, her expression shifting into a smile the moment she spotted me.

“Alice,” she said. “What do you think? Is your investment paying off?”

I looked around the kitchen, the spotless counters, the organized ingredients, and the confidence in Louis’s movements.

“I’m impressed,” I said. “Things are moving faster than I expected.”

Je’e smiled, satisfied. “Good. And how is my sister?”

I met her eyes. “The same as ever.”

That seemed to reassure her more than anything else I could have said.

Louis slid a fresh pizza into the oven, the scent growing richer by the second.

“Want one too?” she asked Je’e.

“Dear Louis, you are going to make me fat,” Je’e said, her nose twitching at the smell of cheesy goodness. She sighed. “Yes, please, but make it small.”

“Hey, you would never believe what happened,” I said.

“I met a goddess,” I grinned. “Damada. And it all started when Je’e sent an envoy to ask for more fire root. From there, everything spiraled.”

I retold the story in detail. Je’e frowned more and more as she heard about the priest’s actions, while Louis quietly started working on Je’e’s pizza. By the time I reached the end, Louis slid a fresh pizza onto a wooden plate in front of me, along with a tall glass of lemonade. The sharp citrus smell mixed with melted cheese and herbs, and whatever I had been about to say vanished from my mind.

“And then the goddess just disappeared back into her tree,” I finished lamely, before starting to inhale the pizza.

Je’e frowned. “I swore I sent that envoy so they would plant extra fields. Not for whatever that turned into.”

“Yeah,” I said between bites. “The priest got a little too eager at the idea of profit.”

Je’e pinched the bridge of her nose. “I’ll send them an apology gift. Maybe a shipment of fertilizer.”

“Please do.”

I stopped talking entirely and focused on eating.

By the time I leaned back, satisfied, something clicked.

“Did you get the coffee cherries as I asked?” I asked, looking at Je’e.

“Yes,” the merchant replied, starting on her own pizza. “They’re here.”

I grinned and turned to Louis. “Help me, and I’ll show you a drink that will revolutionize Hano culturally.”

That got her attention immediately.

The cherries were stored in a thick sack near the flour stores. I hefted it onto the counter and opened it. Inside were dried coffee cherries, wrinkled and dark, faintly sweet-smelling.

“There’s about ten kilograms here,” I said. “The fruit itself is mostly useless. You can make tea from it if you want, but the real treasure is inside.”

I split one open with a knife and popped the seed free. “These.”

Louis leaned closer. “They don’t look like much.”

“They never do at first.”

I handed her a bowl. “Help me extract them. Seth, you too.”

The boy needed no further encouragement. Soon, the counter was covered in split cherries, sticky fingers, and growing piles of pale green coffee beans.

Je’e, notably, did not help. I hadn’t expected her to. With her refined posture and merchant habits, the idea of her wrestling dried fruit with a knife was almost funny. I had seen her home, full of servants. I doubted she could manage it even if she wanted to.

After a few minutes, Je’e frowned at the slow pace. “There has to be a faster way.”

“There is,” Louis said.

I watched her activate her Soulbook. Knife’s Edge manifested as invisible blades, slicing through the fruit cleanly and precisely. Once she got the hang of it, her speed increased dramatically.

Soon, we had several handfuls of green coffee beans sitting in a bowl.

“Now,” I said, rubbing my hands together, “comes the important part. Roasting.”

I pulled out my computer and opened an encyclopedia article, scrolling through diagrams and text showing the stages of roasting.

Louis barely reacted. She had seen the device before, back when we were quarantined together after I nearly died from Red-Death.

Je’e, however, stared.

“What is that?”

“A dreamer artifact from my homeland,” I said smoothly.

She narrowed her eyes. “I thought you were mythic.”

“What a surprise,” I replied flatly. “The girl with mysterious knowledge has more secrets. Don’t ask questions. I’ll explain when I’m ready.”

Inside, I winced. I really sucked at being a mysterious isekai protagonist.

Je’e snorted. “Fine. Keep your secrets.”

We spread the beans evenly in a heavy stone pan and set it over low heat. I guided Louis carefully.

“Keep them moving,” I said. “You don’t want hot spots.”

The beans slowly shifted color, the smell changing from grassy to nutty, then to richer, sharper tones.

“Listen,” I said. “You’ll hear a crack.”

A faint popping sound echoed from the pan.

Louis gasped. “That’s it.”

“First crack,” I said. “After this, it’s all about timing. Too light and it’s sour. Too dark and it tastes like charcoal.”

When they reached a deep brown, I had her pull the pan off the heat and pour the beans into a metal tray. We spread them out and let them cool naturally, stirring to release the heat.

“You’re supposed to let them rest for at least twelve hours before grinding,” I said. “Ideally, a full day.”

I looked at the beans, then at the eager faces around me.

“But I am not waiting for that.”

Using a stone grinder, I had Louis crush them into a coarse powder. The aroma bloomed instantly, rich and intoxicating.

The smell filled the kitchen.

Everyone reacted to the smell. I even took a deep breath, taking it in.

I boiled water in a kettle, then brewed the coffee simply by pouring hot water over the grounds and straining it through cheesecloth.

I poured the dark liquid into three small cups and one oversized mug. I slid the cups to Louis, Je’e, and Seth.

I took a deep gulp from the mug. After six months without coffee, the effect hit immediately. I let out a groan that was almost sexual.

Louis took a cautious sip. She frowned, then took another, larger one, letting it linger before swallowing.

“I can see it,” she said slowly. “This would work well with pastries. Especially in the morning.”

Je’e was next. Her eyes widened.

“Oh,” she breathed. “That’s dangerous. I can feel the rush from just a sip. This could break the tea business.”

I smiled at the merchant woman’s distant, ambitious look.

Seth took a sip, then immediately spat it back into the sink.

“It’s bitter,” he complained. “Why are you acting like it tastes good?”

“Add milk and honey,” I said calmly. “This is not really a child’s drink.”

“I am not a child,” the thirteen-year-old child protested.

He still reached for the small jug of milk and the jar of honey. He hesitated, then poured carefully, stirring with intense focus. When he took another sip, his eyes widened.

“Oh,” he said slowly. “That’s actually good.”

Louis laughed softly. “You just don’t like bitter things.”

“It’s not bitter anymore,” Seth said defensively, taking another drink.

Je’e watched the exchange with interest, her merchant instincts already working. “So,” she said, tapping a finger against her cup, “let’s talk about how this becomes a business.”

That was my cue.

“First,” I said, “you cannot do everything here.”

Louis looked up from the counter. “What do you mean?”

“Coffee roasting should be its own operation,” I explained. “Different staff, and especially a different space. The smell alone will overwhelm the kitchen, and you don’t want cooks distracted by timing beans while baking bread or prepping sauces.”

Je’e nodded slowly. “A separate roasting house.”

“Exactly,” I said. “The space would need ventilation, consistent heat, and controlled batches. Roasting is where quality coffee lives or dies.”

“And grinding?” Louis asked.

“That can be done closer to serving,” I said. “The fresher, the better. You can grind enough for a day or two, but not more.”

Je’e’s eyes gleamed. “So specialization in roasting increases consistency. Consistency builds trust.”

I pointed at her. “You’re getting it.”

Louis sipped her coffee again, thoughtful. “And how do we serve it?”

“There are options,” I said. “You can serve it black, for people who like strong flavors. Black with sugar, which will probably be the most popular. Milk added for smoothness. Or cream if people like it heavier. Or half and half for balance.”

“Half and half?” Louis asked.

“Milk and cream mixed,” I said. “Rich, but not overwhelming.”

“And this?” she gestured at the cup Seth was guarding like a treasure.

“That’s closer to a latte,” I said. “More milk than coffee. It’s very approachable, good for people new to the taste, or children.”

Je’e was already nodding. “Multiple preparations for the same base product.”

“And pastries,” I added. “Coffee and desserts belong together.”

Louis’s eyes lit up instantly.

“I was thinking the same thing,” she said. “Sweet breads, honey cakes, maybe something soaked in syrup.”

“Tiramisu,” I said.

She blinked. “What is that?”

“A layered dessert,” I explained. “Coffee-soaked biscuits, cream, cocoa. I’ll teach you. And Silnar too. I remember that boy has a real knack for desserts.”

Louis smiled fondly. “He does. He’s patient with sweets.”

Je’e leaned back, thoughtful. “We could serve coffee only in the mornings at first, to create demand. Once I can secure more coffee beans and start a roasting operation, we can think about expanding.”

“Good idea,” I said. “Make it feel special. Not something people can get anywhere.”

“Well, they can’t,” Louis beamed.

“And the price will be higher too,” Je’e added. “The fruit was expensive, and that was when the only use for it was a watered-down tea.”

“Obviously,” I said. “This is a stimulant. People will pay for it, even just for the clarity and energy effect.”

Seth raised his cup. “Can I have more?”

Louis laughed and refilled it, making it mostly milk without even asking.

I refilled my mug again and leaned back, enjoying coffee for the first time in months.

I stretched, feeling relaxed, and checked the timer before I would be teleported back into the Sunless Reach.

Only one hour left.

“Hey, Louis, could you make me nine pizzas to go, please? I think I want to treat my team tonight.”

“Of course,” she said immediately, standing up and getting to work.

“I’ll be back in an hour. I need to buy some supplies.”

“It will be done by the time you come back,” Louis said.

“Thank you.”

“Oh, one last thing,” I added as I reached the exit door. “There is a freelancer girl named Lera who would make a perfect delivery girl.”

“A freelancer?” Je’e asked.

“She’s pretty, so she would be a good face for the franchise. She’s fast, strong enough to protect herself from harassment, and best of all, she’s struggling to find good work now that Yon dismantled the Misfits.”

Je’e considered that for a moment, then nodded. “Alright. I’ll check her out.”

I left to buy some crossbow bolts. As I walked toward the market, I noticed a pastry stall selling sweets and cakes. I remembered Ja’a asking for some.

Why not indulge tonight?
