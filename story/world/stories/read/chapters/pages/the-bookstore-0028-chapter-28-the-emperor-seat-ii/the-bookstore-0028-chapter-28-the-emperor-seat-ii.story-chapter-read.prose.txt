Asha entered the hall with the grace expected of an elven noble and the attention of someone stepping into enemy ground.

The room was large enough to hold several hundred guests without feeling crowded, yet only a small gathering waited at the far end. Even so, the place did not look empty. Servants lined both sides of the carpeted approach in careful rows, each holding trays of drinks, folded napkins, or small polished dishes. Their stillness filled the space almost as much as people would have.

At the far end, a cluster of elven dignitaries and Clow nobles stood in conversation. From a distance, the exchange looked warm enough. Up close, Asha knew it would be the usual performance—measured voices, empty pleasantries, and the sort of smiling caution people adopted when every word might later be used against them.

The Clows were easy to identify. Their hair and eyes came in shades of purple so varied that they almost seemed designed to remind everyone of the family’s nature. Somehow, through bloodline, craft, or whatever strange old bargains had empower them, their features had taken on the color of soul essence itself. Asha forced her thoughts into safer paths the moment the idea formed. It was rare, but not impossible, that one of them might notice more than they ought.

Fortunately, Clow protocol did not demand that visiting nobles arrive before their hosts. That spared Ailen and Asha one humiliation, at least.

They crossed the hall together and bowed first to the Clows. Asha bent lower than her mother. She had no desire to test how much disrespect the Seat tolerated.

“Greetings, Minister,” Ailen said.

“Countess. Lady Asha.” The old man returned the courtesy smoothly.

He had sharp eyes. Perhaps, too sharp. They settled on Asha the moment she rose, resting just long enough to make her wish she had bowed lower still.

From there, mother and daughter turned toward the elven delegation and bowed again, this time with greater deference. Ailen’s bow, however, was so slight that it could almost have been mistaken for a nod.

Asha very nearly laughed.

The other elves did not.

“Countess,” said a silver-haired elf with pale green eyes and a voice polished into insult.

His tone made the word sound like a reminder rather than a title. He had been forced, before the Clows, to begin with the proper courtesy due to Ailen’s standing. In return, he chose to stress that standing as little as possible.

Ailen answered without the slightest change in expression.

“Ambassador.”

The single word landed just as neatly. Asha could not tell whether the exchange was partly for the Clows’ benefit, but the dislike was real enough.

An older elf woman behind the ambassador abruptly ignored all formal etiquette and stepped forward.

“Ailen, as always, you look lovely,” she said. Then she turned to Asha with warmer interest. “Come here, child. Let Aunt Aynar see you properly. You have grown even taller.”

The interruption broke the tension by sheer refusal to respect it. Asha was grateful for that, though not for the reason Aynar would have preferred.

The minister, thankfully, gave no sign of caring either way. The younger Clows near him—a boy and a girl, both carrying that same unsettling family coloring in lighter forms—seemed more amused than offended.

Then the servants finally moved. Trays glided forward. Cups were offered. Conversation loosened by half a degree. Asha seized the distraction immediately, accepted a drink she did not want, and used the shift in attention to slip out of Aynar’s immediate reach before the older woman could begin asking affectionate, dangerous questions.

After that first unpleasantness, the gathering settled into something outwardly smooth. Ailen spoke with both the Clows and the elves, but only about light matters. Weather, roads, provincial arrangements, seasonal changes, old mutual acquaintances who could be discussed without consequence. Asha did her part for a while, answered when addressed, avoided saying anything memorable, and then escaped toward the balcony the first moment she thought she could do so without attracting notice.

The air outside was cooler. She stepped out and breathed it in slowly, grateful for the silence and the brief illusion of distance. The balcony overlooked ordered grounds, carefully maintained and therefore somehow less comforting than wild growth would have been. Even here, in open air, the Seat felt controlled.

Asha rested one hand on the railing and let herself relax for a single moment.

Then she sensed movement behind her.

By the time she turned, the minister was already approaching.

He held a glass of red sparkling liquor in one hand and wore a composed expression that made direct observation feel unwise. Asha did not need to see his face to see the danger. She guessed it from the smoothness of his pace, the lack of urgency, and the complete assurance that she would still be here when he reached her.

Only then did she realize her mistake. The moment when she might have escaped this sort of attention had passed long ago. She had mistaken temporary freedom from conversation for safety, and now she would pay for it. She did not need to look directly at him to know why he had come. He wanted her alone to measure the extent of her gift without the inconvenience of witnesses interfering.

Suddenly, Asha missed the weight of her mother’s presence. Ailen Treefehl might be a hostage in all but name, but only a fool would confuse that with powerlessness. Even the Clows would not push her openly without reason. Royal blood still mattered in this place, however twisted its use had become. Asha, by contrast, had drifted away from that shield of her own accord.

Now she stood alone.

The minister came closer. Each step pulled her heart tighter.

Despite herself, Asha looked properly at him. He wore a dark noble vest adorned with fine silver-colored chains, likely naueri, and matching trousers cut without any trace of excess. His hair was dark with clear purple threaded through it, while his eyes carried a paler version of the same shade. Age marked him, though less by weakness than by refinement. On one hand he wore several purple crystal rings, four or five at least, all tools that stabilized and strengthened craft in practiced hands.

“You have grown well in the last year, Lady Asha,” he said. “Quite beautiful.”

Asha tightened her grip on the railing to stop herself from stepping back.

“Thank you for the compliment, Minister.”

She kept the reply short on purpose. Fewer words meant fewer openings.

“It is only the truth.” His voice was mild. Pleasant, even. “Your eyes, especially. Brighter and greener.”

Asha’s mind clouded. The shift was so sudden that for a moment she did not understand what was happening. Then she felt it—his voice, his craft, the pressure sliding through the edges of her mind with terrifying ease. The rings glimmered faintly. So did the naueri chainwork on his clothes. His eyes brightened just enough to make denial pointless.

“I wonder,” he said softly, “how far you can reach. What can you feel?”

Her vision blurred. Her soul spread outward before she had chosen to let it.

Down through stone.Through floor and wall.Into the ground below.Toward roots, trees, cultivated beds, and distant growth.

“I feel...” The answer left her mouth without permission. “The gardens... the trees... the roots running deeper below... the cultivated fields... the crops being cared for... the wild green around the Seat...”

She was reaching and speaking and could not stop either.

“Can you go farther?” the minister asked. “Past the woods. Into Elvyhn?”

The command pressed deeper. Asha strained without meaning to. Her power stretched outward in desperate unwilling obedience, searching farther than she would ever have dared to reveal on her own.

Then something snapped.

“What—?”

She came back to herself confused, breathless, and trembling. Panic followed a heartbeat later.

Her legs felt unsteady. Shame hit even harder. She had laid herself open to him. Not willingly, but that would matter very little if the Clows chose to act on what they had learned. She looked around with the pointless instinct of a trapped creature searching for an exit that did not exist.

The minister only watched her.

“You need not panic, girl,” he said. “If your power cannot reach the elves, then you are no threat.”

No threat.

Asha hated how much humiliation fit inside those two words.

He took another sip of his drink before continuing.

“Still, you should have someone help you understand the extent of your gift.” He turned his head slightly. “Yana.”

“Yes, uncle.”

Asha jerked toward the new voice. A girl with light purple hair stepped forward from a place Asha had not noticed before. That frightened her almost as much as the minister’s compulsion had. She had been there all along, hidden by angle, craft, or both.

The minister addressed the newcomer without even looking back at Asha.

“You will accompany Lady Asha and the Countess back to their border town. A stay in the frontier may do you some good. While there, I am sure Lady Asha will appreciate assistance in better understanding her gift.”

Then he left.

Just like that.

As though he had rearranged a few pieces on a game board and found the result satisfactory.

Asha barely managed to lift her eyes to the young Clow. Yana gave her a small nod that was, unexpectedly, not hostile. Then she too turned and departed, leaving Asha alone with the aftermath.

The young elf gripped the railing harder. Her will had been violated. Her craft had been laid bare. More than anything, she now understood something she had only known abstractly before. This was how the Clows ruled. Not only through armies, law, or noble authority, but through violation made to look like conversation, through fear sharpened into obedience, through power so intimate that resistance was pointless.

History made more sense now.

Why kingdoms had bent.Why clans had submitted.Why whole races had chosen compromise over open defiance.

Asha closed her eyes. What had she done?

It would almost have been easier if the minister had declared her a threat and imprisoned her. That would have been direct. Instead, she had brought something far worse down upon them.

A Clow.

To Mountainend.

To her parents.To her father’s plans.To the town.To the bookstore.

The thought of Anne’s store being dragged under the notice of that family turned Asha cold. The bookstore had little to do with the Clows, yet that would not protect it. Once an overseer from the Seat stepped into Mountainend, nothing nearby would remain comfortably outside her reach. Everyone who mattered to Asha might suffer for this, and it would be because she had failed to protect even the one thing she should have understood—herself.

When she finally returned inside, she felt hollow.

Ailen was still engaged in conversation with the minister’s wife and did not notice her at first. Only when Asha drew near enough for the change in her face to become impossible to miss did her mother turn fully toward her.

Ailen’s expression turned serious. Without waiting for anyone else to mark the shift, she moved to her daughter’s side.

“Mother,” Asha said, her voice breaking despite all her effort. “I... I made a mistake. A terrible mistake...”
