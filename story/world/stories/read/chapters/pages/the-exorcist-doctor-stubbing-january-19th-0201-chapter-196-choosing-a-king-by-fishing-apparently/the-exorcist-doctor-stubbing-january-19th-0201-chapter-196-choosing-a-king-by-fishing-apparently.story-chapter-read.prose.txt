The day after they’d pieced together the thoroughly unpleasant likelihood that the Breeder’s laboratory was sitting somewhere beneath the northern sea, Gael and Maeve returned to the Drowned Parish alone. The place was already loud enough on an ordinary day to make the dead reconsider resurrection, but today the noise had doubled. Perhaps even tripled. The long central canal that ran through the cathedral was choked with skiffs, narrow fishing boats, bait barges, and old wooden things that technically floated and therefore—by Wraithpier standards—deserved legal recognition as ships. Fishmonger men shouted from the overhead rope bridges hawking their wares, fishmonger women shouted from the upper floor balconies hawking their wares, and more fishmonger children ran beneath everyone carrying hooks large enough to gut entire leviathans.

The decorations around the building were new, and that was what caught his attention first.

Lengths of black-and-red sailcloth had been hung between the cathedral’s cracked columns, while great painted fish made from paper, bone, and thin sheets of tin twisted overhead on wires, their jaws full of little white candles. Old nets had been stretched across broken stained-glass windows and decorated with bells, teeth, oyster shells, hooks, and scraps of colored ribbons. Even the enormous drowned Saint statues lining the cathedral walls had been dressed for the occasion, some given wreaths of fishbone around their necks, others equipped with fishing rods that looked suspiciously stolen from the Saint’s Hands. Were they Saint’s Hands fishing rods? He couldn’t be sure.

While he slowed a little to admire the decorations, Maeve also stared around, eyes wide. “I don’t think this place looked as lively the last time we were here.”

“Hah. No.” Gael tilted his mask towards the nearest stone Saint, who’d acquired three hooks through one nostril and a dead eel around her shoulders. “And unless the locals have recently developed a religious doctrine centered around humiliating statuary, I suspect we’ve wandered into preparations for… something.”

The crowd thickened as they pushed toward the central stretch of the Parish. Gael kept one hand loosely around Maeve’ for whenever the foot traffic became particularly bad, and they passed stalls selling braided lines thicker than their thumb, reinforced reels with hand-cranks meant for three people, and harpoon heads blackened in forge oil, barrels of chum, and cages of live bait. A row of old women sat beneath a hanging sign advertising blessed fishing knots while loudly insulting anyone who tied them wrong, and on the opposite side of the canal, three men were testing a new chain net by throwing one of their friends into it.

Gael didn’t think he’d ever get used to the supreme chaos of the northern ward, but with any luck, he wouldn’t be here for much longer. Before long, they finally reached the centermost stretch of the cathedral, and he spotted the Fishermen almost immediately. Five of them sat behind the second-floor railings exactly where he remembered them, and they were the exact five he’d spoken to three months ago: anglerfish hat man, eel hat man, fishing hook hat man, jellyfish hat man, and salt cube hat man.

Currently, their hats were low over their brows and their fishing lines were cast down into the black canal water below. They were also drinking heavily. One Fisherman had both boots up on the railing while another leaned over the canal and squinted at the water. A third was jingling a handful of coins, taunting the other two about it. Gael caught fragments of their conversation as he approached.

“Tellin’ ye, it’ll be Old Glassjaw that breaks first. Third bell, maybe fourth if he’s gone lazy.”

“Old Glassjaw ain’t been in this canal since spring, ye blind corpse. That’s Hookback below us. I seen the tail.”

“You seen yer own reflection and got frightened!”

“Five thousand Marks says I’m right.”

“Ten says yer mother still regrets feeding ye.”

The others erupted into hoarse laughter, so before all of them could take another swig of their alcohol, Gael stopped before them, cupped both hands around his mouth, and shouted up.

“Oi! Grave fishermen! Down here!”

Five enormous hats slowly tilted down at him, and as they stared at him, the silence lasted long enough that he briefly wondered if they’d forgotten him entirely. It was a distinct possibility. These were men who had nothing but fish on their minds, after all.

But then the salt-hatted Fisherman leaned forward, seemingly narrowing his eyes.

“... Ah!” he shouted and pointed down at him. “It’s them strange southerners!”

Another Fishermen squinted at them before slapping his knee. “Ah! The Myrmur lot!”

“The Raven with the giant ugly ship!” another added.

Gael felt an immediate and irrational sting of pride. “My ship’s magnificent.”

“It’s ugly!” all five of them shouted.

“It cost three hundred thousand Marks!”

“That makes it expensive and ugly!”

Gael made a note to insult their boats later, but the salt-hatted Fisherman leaned even further over the railing, giving all of them a bright grin.

“Heart ye lot’ve been doin’ well with the Myrmurs. Yer… Saint’s Hands pulled near half the northern lakes clean of them, from what I hear. That true?”

Another Fisherman shifted eagerly. “And is it true ye caught a pistol shrimp? Heard the bastard could fire through hull planks!”

“Could fire through Maeve too,” Gael said. “Nearly did, actually.”

The nearest Fisherman whistled through his teeth. “And how’d it fight on the line? Was it a feisty one?”

And as the Fishermen began bombarding him with questions about fishing details—what weight most Myrmurs were, did Myrmur Hosts tire faster after a prolonged reel, were lobster Hosts harder to reel than eel Hosts, did lake Myrmurs prefer blood chum or live bait, so on and so forth—Gael started answering everything with increasing enthusiasm as well. Surprisingly, they were much warmer and friendlier now than the last time they’d met. They’d treated him as a foreign nuisance three months ago, but now it seemed as though he’d crossed some invisible threshold. Maybe it was because they knew the Saint’s Hands had been reeling up Myrmurs by the dozens, or maybe it was because they knew he’d spent months on Wraithpier waters instead of merely appearing for a day and ordering people around.

Whatever it was, he was also having fun sharing fishing stories with them, and the next ten minutes passed by in a blur. He told them about the pistol shrimp dragging one of their lines so violently that two Saint’s Hands had gone overboard. He described the hookleg glutton that wrapped itself around a barge anchor and forced them to cut half the rigging loose. He exaggerated the size of at least three catches by ten percent because that seemed culturally expected. The Fishermen reciprocated. One claimed he’d once wrestled a twelve-meter river eel beneath a collapsed pier for four hours before discovering he’d hooked the wrong end of the eel. Another described being swallowed by a leviathan and cutting his way out through the gills, and it was mostly bragging, bragging, and more bragging—not that Gael minded it, of course. He liked showing off just as much as the next Bharnish drunk.

But when it became increasingly obvious that the rest of his gang were bored out of their minds—and that Maeve had long since stopped listening to them talk to stare at the decorations around the cathedral instead—he made a show of tipping his head at his surroundings.

“Come to think of it, boys, what’s happening to this ward exactly?” he said, changing the topic swiftly. “What’s with all the dressing up?”

The Fishermen looked around. The jellyfish-hatted man scratched beneath his hat. “What, the decoratin’?”

“Yeah. There’s some sort of… festival going on?”

The salt-hatted man grinned at him from ear to ear. “It’s the Leviathan’s Wake.”

Maeve looked up, her interest renewed. “Leviathan’s Wake?”

“Aye. Happens twice a year.” The anglerfish-hatted man leaned his forearms over the railing. “Every six months, we get too many big fish driftin’ into the ward. Those really big ones are leviathans. They come in from the open sea, follow the trenches, and settle under canals, docks, flooded ruins, deep basins—whatever takes their fancy. They mostly behave, but the problem ain’t that they don’t attack. Problem’s they still eat. The big leviathans all sit under the lakes and canals for months and eat everything that swims past.”

The fishing hook-hatted man added, “Them leviathans make normal fish scarce. If they eat all the smaller fish, all fish prices go up, smaller fish move elsewhere, and the whole ward starts losin’ catch.”

“So twice a year we clear ‘em out proper!” the eel-hatted man cheered. “The whole ward shuts down for a single day, and everyone sails out to lure ‘em up, rile ‘em up, chase ‘em into the open waters, and kill what needs killin’! And, and, and—whoever gets the largest kill during the festival receives the blessings of the drowned Saintess! Means ye’re the king of Wraithpier for the next six months until the next festival!”

Maeve, who’d been standing mostly quietly beside Gael, looked up sharply. Gael felt the same thing happen inside his own head: a small click of an idea shifting into place.

“... And lemme guess,” he started, smiling up at the Fishermen, “you lot are the winners every festival.”

All five Fishermen laughed so loudly that several people on the lower walkways turned to look.

“‘Course we do!” one of them shouted.

“Who else would?” another boasted.

“The crown’s been ours for thirty-eight Leviathan Wakes!” another cheered. “And this festival’ll be no different! This year, we’ll be huntin’ Saint Mercy’s Widow! They say it’s the newest and most exciting leviathan to have swam into our waters!”

The descriptions of this ‘Saint Mercy’s Widow’ leviathan became less and less useful as the Fishermen started rambling about it. One Fisherman swore it resembled a ribbon serpent crossed with a gravejaw ray, but another insisted its head was shaped more like a deepwater sturgeon. They agreed on several points, though: it was mostly black along the spine, silver underneath, and it was some sort of eel-like leviathan that was super, duper cool. But now that they were going on another tangent about how beautiful the leviathan they were going to hunt this festival supposedly was, they were no longer paying any attention to him.

So he let them ramble on by themselves and turned to walk away, humming as he leaned his weight against his cane.

“Just to double-confirm:” he called over his shoulder, “whoever gets the biggest catch of the festival becomes the king of Wraithpier for the next six months, right?”

“Yep!” all five of them hollered.

“And when’s the festival day?”

“In three days!”

“Great. We’ll see you then.”

Gael waved them his farewells and grabbed Maeve’s hand, pulling her away from the rowdy Fishermen as they launched into their own ramblings again.

The Fishermen had been very clear during their previous encounter that they didn’t really care about Gael’s war against the Myrmurs. They liked to fish, and protecting Wraithpier’s waters from the Myrmurs was only second to that interest. Even if they may like him and the Saint’s Hands a little bit more now for how many Myrmurs they’ve been able to reel up over the past three months, they most likely had no interest in sailing north for them, nor for helping them locate a monster laboratory beneath the sea.

So he could just ask them for their help again—or he could turn to Maeve and give her an excited grin instead.

“How many ships do we actually have under our command?” he asked.
