As dawn broke, a man born with shoulders broad the strength of an ox was lashed to a cart. Each step he took was powerful and carried with it the reassurance that nothing in his path would block him. Certainly, whatever animals might have scurried across the dirt path feared his thunderous footfalls.

“Can you get off already?” asked Liao Weiji, currently the beast of burden.

Hua found this a deeply amusing sight, sitting at the front of the cart that her cousin towed. “You used to carry me when I was younger,” Hua called, knees drawn up to her chest, resting her head upon her arms balanced by knobby knees.

“That was a decade ago, before you were a Cultivator. Now get off.”

“I am not a significant increase to your difficulty in dragging this cart.”

“Your entire existence increases the difficulty of my life,” he shot back, glaring over his shoulders bundled with thick ropes over them. Sweat beaded the back of his reddened neck. They were making good pace, Hua thought, fast as a horse could manage carrying a cart loaded with six corpses.

It was yet cold enough that the half dozen corpses in the cart bed had not bloated and released their pungent gasses. She would still be washing herself off thoroughly when they returned. But for now, if she had to take a slow route anyway, she could get a free ride.

“Why are you carrying those?” Weiji asked when they stopped for a break. Dawn chased them. Hua thought they might stay ahead of it. Hoped they would before the bodies warmed.

Hua held her bundle of bloodied leathers and hemp clothes away from him. She had stripped it from the bandit who had an unfortunate case of crushed head. “It’s a gift.”

“I don’t want to know but I’m afraid not knowing will make my life more difficult.”

“Would knowing make your life less difficult?”

Weiji opened his mouth. Closed it. Hummed in thought. “With you, I struggle to know.”

“In a strange way, this is a good thing,” Hua added, yawning, gesturing at the cart filled with corpses.

“Please, do not enlighten me on your thought process. Under no circumstances do I want to be exposed to your madness.”

Her cousin needed to stop whining all the time. “Consider what the cart represents.”

“Dead Cultivators and my constant suffering?”

“It represents someone strong enough to kill a band of six Cultivators,” she said pleasantly, enjoying how his face fell. “Which means someone strong enough to possibly give me a fun fight. Weiji, I might not die of boredom on this trip.”

“Every second I spend with you causes me spiritual pain and permanent karmic harm. A Cultivator that strong will kill you. Fighting that many with a numbers disadvantage is no small feat.”

“Come on, the Ranger in our camp is barely in Qi Condensation. Even if everyone else was twice as strong, that adds up to one person slightly stronger than you.”

“I don’t think you understand how arithmetic works. Let us assume it wasn’t the hundred bandits who did most of the work, which I’m hoping for, but given that your beloved Ranger was taken out first it seems likely.”

“I have no interest in her.”

Weiji continued past her interruption. “Now, that means Kang fought six Cultivators alone. A group who knew each other and likely fought well. That synergy is a force multiplier. Even if they were all at one star of Cultivation, that’s someone at six stars, minimum, possibly more.”

Hua’s smile widened. “Which might mean someone stronger than me. Weiji, do you know how rare that is?”

“I am painfully aware that you are a prodigy who exists to make my life more difficult.”

“A good fight is coming. This is beautiful. Those refugees are the best thing to happen to me in a month.”

“This month being the one where you nearly had two Qi deviations and nearly destroyed your arm?”

“It barely hurts anymore.”

That was a lie. She had just learned to ignore the dull throbbing pain that occasionally flared up.

“Hua—”

“Come on, let’s go before the bodies start smelling.”

This was going to be a great time. Surely, everyone else would agree with her.

***

“About two hundred were gathered from what I could see,” she told Captain Yao when they returned.

You could hear the silence in the command tent as everyone registered that. It was a full meeting once more: Hua, eager and bubbly; Weiji, nervous and twitchy; Liu Xin, showing admirable calm; the good Captain Yao who knew only dourness and mild despair; the forgettable scribe and his less-forgettable guard captain.

Now that Hua thought about it, two hundred bandits was a dangerous number of bandits. Perhaps the Jolly Ranger hadn’t been entirely incompetent. Hua might have to… not apologise, she wasn’t doing that, but perhaps be less cruel to a woman so vulnerable and in need of aid.

“My Lord, four-to-one odds are not completely untenable but I would caution against engaging in that fight,” said Captain Yao, breaking the silence. His voice was deep, strong, and not at all pleasant like the Jolly Ranger’s voice, even as she was in the aftermath of torture and dehydration.

“Between Weiji and I, that’s at least fifty you won’t have to worry about.”

Her cousin glanced at her for a moment then turned to look at the Captain. “Please ignore our lord’s assumptions on my ability to convert a valley to a red sea and assume the odds will remain constant.”

Hua nearly rolled her eyes. She’d seen how quickly he’d cut through the bandits that had ambushed them. He was just letting his nervousness get in between him and his talents. She said as much.

“This is not the time for flattery.”

“Flattery requires falsehood and exaggeration to exist.”

Weiji blinked. Then he turned a worrying shade of red. No Liao child was meant to have that much colour. Beyond their green eyes, they were mocked as living ghosts for a reason. Then she registered her words, the sincerity in her tone.

I must be tired if I’m being nice, she thought, eyes stinging. Embarrassed, equally, to have said something so kind without following it up with an insult.

Captain Yao coughed aggressively. “My lord, perhaps we could return to the matter of a battle not in our favour.”

“Battles are rarely in our favour and yet Liao always emerges victorious.”

Assuming one ignored every loss they took before her grandmother joined the clan and changed their fortunes with the force of her cultivation. One marriage capable of sending a clan into ascendancy. Hua would continue that legacy by defeating this Kang.

The imperial scribe who Hua still couldn’t remember, spoke, “I can’t command my soldiers to walk to their deaths because of this plan.”

Hua felt a deepening annoyance. Why was she allowing a mortal into her—Captain Yao’s—tent and why was she letting him speak?

“Firstly, we’ll be attacking under cover of darkness with the element of surprise. Secondly, we have cavalry, they do not. Thirdly, we have enough young mortals who can pick up a spear and kill people.”

“You think the mortals who fled will be willing to join in this fight?” Weiji asked. “The mortals who blame us for everything that’s gone wrong in their lives. Those mortals.”

“I won’t give them much choice. If they want my protection, they will fight for it.”

“Untrained fighters are a liability,” said Captain Yao.

Hua waved that argument away. “Not important. Besides, we won’t be fighting the full complement of bandits.”

“This lowly captain hesitates to ask but why is that the case?”

“Liu Xin, do you think you can sneak into their camp, kill some of their captains, and start a fire?”

“It would not be the first war camp I have snuck into,” he said to the astonished silence, with the conviction of a man who knew he had hitched his fate to a leopard that was now sniffing his face.

Hua patted the bandit’s clothes she had filched. The bundle had drawn curious looks when she entered. “You’ll fit right in.”

Liu Xin smiled tightly. It made him look older, made the grey at his temples look appropriate. “As my lord wishes.”

“No! There’s a limit to madness and this is the limit.” Weiji’s fury was both blinding and delectable. “You want to hinge this entire plan on an untested and untrustworthy savage with no verifiable skills in stealth?”

“Yes.”

“I forbid it.”

He looked and sounded like his grandfather, the Elder Shenyu, in this moment. And Hua could not abide that.

Hua sensed the potential shift occurring. Weiji was older, well-respected, even-tempered. She was capricious, and mercurial, and young. Defying her like this, like she was a child playing at a role, was a dangerous thing that would destabilise her rule.

“What exactly do you forbid?” lord Liao asked, the brilliance of six stars opening themselves and inflicting the weight of the storm upon the gathering.

Weiji flinched. Captain Yao flinched. They all flinched at the reminder.

Hua had been named lord Liao despite her youth, despite being born a second child and a girl besides. A spiritualist who studied the war arts unlike every former Lord Liao who had been a physical Cultivator. She was an anomaly, an abomination, but power was forgiveness, and power was her right to rule.

“This Lord awaits your answer, Liao Weiji, son of Lady Sing.”

He bowed as she knew he would. If nothing else, she trusted he wouldn’t usurp her. “Nothing, my Lord. This Liao Weiji was overexuberant in his caution.”

“I have seen Liu Xin’s combat skills. There are few Cultivators who move as quietly as he does. He may not yet touch Qi, but he will be invaluable in this role. Do any wish to lay a challenge to my order.”

No one spoke up against the thick ozone and bright sparks. No one was quite so suicidal. She let her meridians settle and let her sparks fade.

“My Lord,” began the tentative Captain Yao, who paused as the tempest of her glare focused on him. “Could I have a map drawn of the terrain?” Hua graced him one. “And might I offer some additional amendments to this plan?”

“You may.”

Captain Yao told them. He marked out the map in charcoal as he spoke. Confident as he laid out lines of battle, marking out a map of future slaughter as he went along.

“It will be a simple plan. The better to avoid confusion in the dark. We haven’t trained as much for a night battle as I would like, and some of my soldiers are fresh recruits, but we can make this work. I know we can.”

“There’s a certain elegance in brutal simplicity,” Hua said, smiling with all her teeth. “I rather like this plan, Captain Yao. Let us put an end to this matter of bandits.”

“It will done, my lord.”

***

Hua made her way to the healing tent, through the camp that was rousing in the early light and being directed by Captain Yao and his seconds—who Hua would endeavour to know if she didn’t have Weiji to handle any annoying little things like that—but was accosted before she could reach it.

“Hear you’re making plans for us.”

“Did throwing bones help you divine that?” Hua asked, genuinely curious, turning about to face the shaman who had a quiet presence.

Wu Yan shot her an unimpressed look. “Your brother told me.”

“He is this lord’s cousin.”

“Act like siblings and it don’t make no difference to me, not when you’re making us do your job for you.”

Hua was tempted to enter informal registers and fling vitriol at the shaman. She was easy to dislike and distrust. Hua owed her, and she’d pay back that debt, even if parts of that meant biting down her vitriol and learning to cooperate.

“Can it be done? Can enough of your people get organised and help.”

“Asking us to die, you are. Got no reason to run face first into a line of spears.”

“I don’t need a grand army. Your and yours will be there to cause chaos, not to hold a line—I wouldn’t expect it of untrained mortals. Imagine you have a powerful Cultivator from one direction, cavalry charging you in another, and a whole line of spears aimed at your back. That’s what will happen to the bandits.”

“Me and mine die first if your plan don’t work.”

“Yes,” Hua agreed. “But it will for the simple fact that it will take a god to kill me. And Wu Yan, isn’t this worth a little risk? A chance to go home without worrying your homes will be pillaged. Do you know how many bodies we found of people who refused to leave their homes? Be half as brave as them and your refugees will have homes again.”

“Never would have needed bravery if you did your job earlier.” She spat at the floor, a degree of rudeness that nearly had Hua ripping her head off. It was only the debt and her iron control holding back her lightning. “Curse on your house. We’ll see this through, but not for you, never for you. Got nothing to do with loyalty to you.”

“You may clean up after yourself. I did not expect civility and yet I am still surprised by your savagery.”

Then Hua walked past her, furious, humiliated, disgusted, and entered the tent. Young Ren, who had likely heard that confrontation, looked up at her and decided this would be a good time to take a break. He knew her well.

The Ranger was asleep. A restless sleep, eyes twitching rapidly beneath her lids. Sweat beading on her forehead. Fever underneath it all, that sickly yellow smell. Hua sat. Laid a hand on her shoulder. Willed her to be at ease. Thought alone was not enough. With the tiniest of efforts, Hua allowed her Qi to seep past her barrier and roll over the Ranger. Not a lot, not much at all, a hint, a touch, delicate as silk strands, softer than wool, as sure as static running over your skin. Powerful lightning that the Ranger might remember in her sleep, the snap and crackle of a lord promising to avenge her brethren.

She did this long enough for the Ranger to calm. Hua couldn’t help but stare. Took in her messy black hair, matted on her forehead, and wiped her brow with a wet cloth. The Ranger made it easy to ignore Qing who was sitting on the Ranger’s other side, saying nothing, simply judging Hua for the very act of existing.

Finally, when the Ranger was at peace, Hua let herself relax, settling into her chair. Hardwood carved without thought for comfort, no one could have fallen asleep on it. Maybe that was why Hua fell asleep where she sat; she enjoyed making impossibilities real.

A cherry tree blossomed in her mind. Tall and broad, ancient roots grown gnarled and deep, leaves pink like the early hints of sunrise, promising another day, another future, spring pink, living pink, hopeful and delightful pink. Leaves shivering on the breeze, a noose swishing like a pendulum. Hua saw her in a noose, swaying endlessly, back and forth, left and right. Qing who became the Ranger, a lie becoming another lie.

She reached out and found herself trapped in the dirt. Trapped in darkness, surrounded by tall walls that were impossible to climb. She climbed anyway. Clawed at stone and lies as her nails shattered and her fingers bled. Kept going as her mouth filled with dirt that crawled, writhed, undulated for it was maggots filling her mouth, consuming her from the inside as she died and she died and she kept dying without ever reaching Qing. But Hua couldn’t stop. Wouldn’t. No matter the pain, she needed to keep climbing, keep rising, and if she reached out, maybe, just maybe, she could feel Qing again.

Someone grabbed her.

That was not Qing’s hand.

Hua’s eyes snapped open.

It was just the Ranger. Hua blinked. She had fallen asleep. By the quality of the light entering the tent, it was late afternoon. There was a blanket around her shoulders.

“You looked like you were having nightmares,” the Ranger said kindly, softening her weary rasp into something pleasant. “Your brother brought that blanket.”

“Cousin,” she corrected tiredly.

“Family is family, the details don’t matter.”

“Blood matters.”

The Ranger struggled to shake her head, wincing. Conveyed her disagreement with her eyes instead. “It doesn’t matter one bit. It’s who you choose and choosing blood is a good option, but not the only one. I… You Clan folks have such soft hands.” Hua looked down where their hands were intertwined. “Never thought I’d meet a man with hands softer than mine.”

She felt no urge to pull away. Squeezed, instead, to feel that connection. In another time, she might have been disgusted by the Ranger’s missing fingers, bandages speckled with red. But here, now, she enjoyed the strength that was found in each callous. Brushed her thumb over the scar along had thumb, old and silvery.

Hua huffed. “Your flesh becomes stronger than any hilt, too tough for callouses to form.”

“Longshot Tong told me the same. He was… our leader, the best of us. He taught me how to use a bow and how to swing a sword. Taught me to hunt and track. Taught me all I ever needed to know.”

Hua could not begin to guess which Ranger that was even though she had been intimately acquainted with their guts. Had shoved intestines back into abdomens and sealed them shut with thread. Knew their hearts but not their names.

“They tell me you found my friends strung up on trees,” the Ranger said. “Just like me.”

“Yes,” Hua said, unwilling to lie. Unable to in the face of the Ranger’s bravery. Seven of them had descended into a valley and fought a battle Hua refused to join without ten times her number. It was admirable. Foolish, but admirable.

The Ranger’s eyes fell shut and she squeezed Hua’s hand with surprising strength. Hua laid her other hand over it.

“Keep him close, that cousin of yours. Family and friends vanish if you look away from them.”

I know, Hua thought. Saying it felt worthless. Grief would remain, a ghost that would not leave.

“Be stronger than I was,” the Ranger added, furious, grieving. “Don’t let anyone or anything take your loved ones.”

“This lord will kill the bandits,” she promised the Jolly Ranger because revenge was a decent gift. “He will bring you their heads back. This, I promise you, as my name is Liao and lightning is my blood.”

The Ranger nodded. For a while, they sat like that, just two people with a shared grief that needn’t be spoken.

“Xue Meili.”

“Hm?”

“That is my name. Use it when you return.”

Beauty and grace, her name meant. Hua knew the Ranger had the former, but there would be time to learn if she had the latter. Hua offered her a violent smile, one with teeth and savagery.

“I will return with Kang’s blood on my blade. Sleep well and dream of my return.”

***

That night, a battalion of Liao soldiers threaded their way through a thick forest under the cover of dark. It was that time that Hua had never been able to square as being late at night or the very early hours of the morning.

Horses plodded down dark paths carefully and quietly, trained well to expect battle. Divested of any blue and white, their saddle blankets were motley greys, browns, and greens.

Frigid winds wound their way through her robes. She felt underdressed without a full set of armour. Instead, she had leathers thrown over her robes, shoulder guards and a pleated and armoured skirt from the soldier’s spare that managed to fit her.

Hua gazed at the bandit camp down the valley. Protected by river and hills, with a crossing of three li to reach the camp, it was a good place to hole up. Were the scouts not dead with slit throats before they knew what was happening, Hua and her men would have been in for some trouble. But now, they had the option to simply wait, to watch the distant movements of the bandits. Captain Yao had made adjustments to the order of battle on seeing the terrain.

“That barbarian got himself killed by now,” Weiji muttered, irritable, though with an undercurrent of humour. If it were true, her cousin would never let her live it down.

Weiji was currently her least favourite person. It was meant to be his job to make sure she had everything she needed on this trip. He’d considered spare swords but not armour. Truly worthless, her cousin. And sure, maybe she didn’t have a full set of armour sized for her, and there wasn’t time to get one made, but she would still blame him.

“Just give him a few more minutes.”

Their plan relied on chaos, speed, and aggression. If Hua squinted, she could see the group of civilians on the far side of the forest, opposite Hua’s group, armed with spears gifted from Liao reserves, though she could not spot the Imperial soldiers who would be leading them.

Fires lit up amongst the camp, following a trail that could only be noticed at such a remove, when the tableau could be viewed in the fullness of its chaotic pattern: the immediate silence from the dead sentries who could not give orders, those few who were barking confused orders, the many screaming in fear as their tents caught on fire, and the yawning emptiness that would precede a charge.

“Weiji, I hope you know that I expect you to get at least twenty kills.”

He said nothing but in his expression, she read a litany of curses.

Hua rose in her saddle and withdrew her sword, pointing it forward. Toward the coming battle. Toward the field of slaughter.

“Charge!”

Their horses thundered down the plain.

The sound of it carried across the valley. Hua felt the harsh jolt of each stride through her body. Ahead of her were new targets, spilling out of their camp in bewilderment and fear. Failing to understand the fire behind them and the cavalry charge ahead.

It was horror plain and simple. Awe, perhaps, at the great kingfisher resplendent on powder blue banners, those same banners that had defined the Amber Sea and protected its borders against invaders. Banners that heralded legends and heroes, martyrs and saints.

The Age of Heroes had died, its legends were long gone, and there would be neither martyr nor saint found after this battle. When all the titles and lies were removed, all that remained were the killers.

For Ranger Xue Meili, Hua would be the best of killers.

Liao Hua, the Lord Weilong, the great talent of a generation, swung her blade low and claimed her first life of the day.
