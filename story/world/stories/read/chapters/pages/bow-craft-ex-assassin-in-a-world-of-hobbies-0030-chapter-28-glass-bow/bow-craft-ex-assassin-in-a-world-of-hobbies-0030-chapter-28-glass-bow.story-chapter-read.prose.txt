Chapter 28: Glass Bow

It’s creaking.

A tillering stick was a simple tool that simulated an archer’s arm. It was about one-and-a-half meters (five feet) long, consisting of a thick, straight piece of wood. On one end was a wooden fork so a bow could be mounted by its handle. Along the length of the tillering stick’s body were notches cut at one-inch intervals; the bowyer would draw the bowstring down and let it catch on increasingly farther notches, hoping to the nearest god the creaking sounds weren’t a prelude to breaking.

When drawn, the whole thing looked like a crossbow. Fun Fact: the body of a crossbow is also called a tiller. Fun Fact #2: for bowyers, the shape made it feel like they were crucifying their babies. Fun Fact #3: tillering had to be done a couple dozen times, drawing out the raw anxiety of the experience.

But if they didn’t do this, they wouldn’t be able to step away from the bow and examine its shape from a distance. If its curve at full draw were to be crooked, then its limbs would fight against each other during real use, flying forward with mismatched timing. Only on the final tiller — when its shape was as balanced as it could be — would it become a crafted bow.

For now, Craft wasn’t done, hence he suffered. His bow had twice the limbs, so he suffered twice more than he deserved. Every time he leaned the tillering stick against the front of the house, mounted the bow, and drew the bowstring down, it creaked twice as loud. Not just him, but even Lei-rei, Dane, and Otto, watching from the side, imagined each tiny wooden grinding noise to become a sudden crack!… But it would never come — or so you’d think!… But no, it really didn’t.

On the other hand, Craft had the benefit of diluting some of that anticipation with the sheer focus of being absorbed in the work. The rest of them shared in the anxiety of a father waiting outside the delivery room, unable to do anything other than feel useless and be at the mercy of the sounds of creaking (the wood was crying; make it stop).

It was precisely because they were spectators, however, that they noticed something he didn’t.

“Huh?” Dane blurted. Lei-rei looked at him.

“What?”

“Is it just me or is it glowing a bit?” Dane pointed at the bow.

Craft had been taking it on and off the tillering stick for a while now, ferrying it back to the bench for a light shave to adjust the awkward spots in the wood. Certain sections of a limb might be too stiff and not curve at all, and especially towards the tips, this introduced inefficiencies to be shaved away. Do it too much, however, and you would cause that section to bend too much, then you’d have to shave away the opposite limb to match the new curve.

These tiny adjustments needed plenty of focus, leading to Craft not noticing the glittering air around him.

“Oh, I didn’t notice,” Lei-rei said.

“Huh? How? We’re both watching, aren’t we?”

“I…suppose I was too nervous about the bow.”

Otto hummed wisely, catching their attention. “Maximum emotion.”

Dane looked at him funny. “What?…”

“I see.” Lei-rei nodded.

He looked at her. “You got that?”

“If the target of a Hobby is a physical object, sometimes magical effects can be imbued in them with enough passion.” She shook her head. “No one has managed to pin down the exact conditions, though.”

Dane paused. “For real?”

“It happened to me once.”

He paused again. He shook his head. “Never to me. I wonder why.”

Lei-rei eyed him. She had a hunch as to his question, but she wouldn’t say — not with that forlorn expression on his face.

— Crack.

They snapped towards the bow like spooked cavemen. Craft noticed them and looked up from the work.

“Oh, no, I just stepped on a twig.”

They took a deep breath — and waited two hours and another tray of cookies. They didn’t know when it would come out.

At least, not until they heard the first cry — “Yeeeees!” — of a fully-grown man.

Dane let out a shout of surprise, while Lei-rei accidentally crushed the cookie in her hand. Otto approached Craft and gave him a slap on the back, letting a sense of recognition flow between fellow craftsmen.

“I’m impressed you can make something out of that glasswood stuff,” Dane said. “I bet those pranksters from Monstera didn’t imagine this.”

Craft looked at him. “Pranksters?”

“Oh, hah! Yeah. They thought plantin’ a forest of this stuff would be funny. Wasn’t so funny after Solace beat ’em to a pulp, though.”

Noted: Don’t commit eco-harassment in the Big Madame’s territory.

Dane snapped his fingers and pointed at him. “Oh yeah, y’got any spares, buddy? I’m low on bow stock, and I’m pretty willing to buy ’em off of ya.”

Craft thought about the Bow Shed. It’d be so convenient to cash in on everything inside, but he hadn’t figured out how to open the thing. Then again, was it something he should be opening? He’d rather ask Lei-rei away from other ears. “I’ll think about it,” he said.

“Just let me know, alright?”

Craft nodded, facing the bow again — and sighed with a slight smile. While everyone gave him another round of compliments, he removed the bow from the tillering stick. Other than the initial exhilaration of completing such a difficult project, he felt…nothing. A part of him wished the euphoria would have lasted, but he hadn’t come out here to feel that. He’d come out here because Lei-rei had requested it, and he genuinely wanted to show her something he had made.

Already, there was someone itching to get her hands on it. Standing behind him was a presence much like a dutiful soldier’s, and he turned around to find Lei-rei standing at attention with a cookie in her mouth.

He waited for her to finish it. Crunch — she drew it in with her teeth — crunch, crunch. The raw eye contact of the moment went unmentioned between them, but he very much understood she was eager to try it out.

“I mean,” Craft started, “if you’ve got an arrow” — she stabbed one into the dirt — “alright.”

She saluted him. Playing along, Craft saluted back with a smile. He hadn’t wasted his time at all.

He lowered his hand and presented the bow to her. Behold: made from wood as fragile as dinner plates, scraps of leather, murder-cable, and bowstring imbued with awkwardness; sporting a humble fifteen-pound draw weight at a thirty-inch draw length, was his very first bow in this world.

Lei-rei stepped closer, reaching out — but she couldn’t bring herself to hold it. Not yet. She admired it like it was an ancient heirloom weapon, as if previous owners’ reverence of it had built up into a mass of spiritual energy in its fibers, and touching it would burn her fingers.

Anyway, fingers could regenerate. She grabbed it with the zealousness of a samurai wishing for death, and the moment she touched it, energy thrummed through her fingers.

“Synergy?” Craft blurted out. He recognized it this time. Well, the bowstring’s from her, after all. He was still holding onto it, and he could see the same energy flowing into his hands. He thought he’d get electrocuted by it somehow, but it felt warm.

Looking at Lei-rei, he expected her confirmation, but he was confused to see her jaw hanging loose as she stared at the bow, a tear sliding down her cheek.

Despair, loneliness, and grief — she felt these things through the bow, and they were not at all unfamiliar to her. He hides these feelings, she realized. She didn’t know whether to mention it, however, and she gently pulled the bow closer to herself, away from Craft’s hands.

“Are you okay?” he asked.

She wiped her eyes. Are you? — she’d rather say, but it wouldn’t have made sense to him. “I’ll shoot it now. Make sure you take a good look.”

He stepped aside. There was something else meant behind her words, he was sure, but this was the sort of thing to quietly oblige. “Have at it.”

She nocked an arrow and pointed skyward, drawing slowly, and the four limbs arched together in a way more complex than its actual mechanism should have implied. That glitter of Synergy started to form around her again — and also around Craft. This time, he also cried.

Just as Lei-rei felt the feelings put into the bow, every inch of the arrow drawn closer to her face also drew Craft into the very act. Emptiness, dullness, and the uncaring march of time — he felt these things swirling around the air, being sucked into the arrow. They were all familiar colors to him, and staring at the tail of the arrow, his eyes followed it while it was being drawn — until it rested beside Lei-rei’s cheek, and his gaze met her eye.

They only shared a glance, and that was all it took. They realized this Synergy had taken their emotions and laid it out for both of them to feel, and now, they were replete with fear of what the other might think of it — then next, saddled with relief as they realized none of this was completely new.

One of Synergy’s requisites was empathy. This connection would not have formed from nothing.

Security was a relaxing thing, and it relaxed Lei-rei’s fingers — loosing the arrow. A cool gust swept in from behind, and with it broke the Synergy between them. They missed it almost immediately.

They distracted themselves with watching the trail of shimmering light falling from the arc of the arrow’s path. Though the bow had been weak, the arrow flew farther and farther away, disobeying the rules of how an arrow should behave. It even became a second sun dispelling the shadows of the forest. The fragments that fell from it were like wishing stars themselves, but it was all just for a moment. It went dark, and the only light remained in memory.

Craft and Lei-rei glanced at each other.

“Lei-rei.”

“Yes?”

“I can’t see your face.” Staring at a second sun may have been a bright idea, but it wasn’t a good one.

She chuckled. “What a coincidence.”

Having experienced being so close then made to step away, they each realized their place in the whole. Even if they couldn’t see each other’s faces, they didn’t need to to know what the other was feeling.

“Well,” he said, “that sure…brightened…my day.”

Lei-rei side-eyed him and shook her head. “I’d punch you.”

“Punching is the way,” Otto said.

“Y’all dumb for staring at that.” Dane had covered his eyes early. Now, his neck was still craned up, seeing off the dwindling arrow’s flight.

Craft squared up with Lei-rei and bounced on his feet. “Well? Come at me!”

“Er, y’all” —

Lei-rei was flustered. “I can’t see properly!”

“Come on! We’re both visually impaired!”

“Y’all” —

“At least you only see a circle! Mine’s weirdly shaped!” She’d been holding the bow, after all.

“Hey!” Dane really forced it. “For real, we got a problem!”

Dane’s shout spooked them, and they followed his finger to the sky.

Craft squinted. The small light of the arrow had begun to slow down. Wait, is that really a problem? “Is it actually” —

“It should be close to lower orbit,” Lei-rei answered before he could ask. “Just a bit more, and it’ll leave.”

Just as she’d said that, the light stayed still — and began to fall.

“Well, shit. What now?” Dane said. He started walking backwards. Any glowy thing coming from the sky was a guaranteed disaster! “What now!”

“Calm down. It’s just an arrow,” Lei-rei said.

Craft was of the same opinion. “Here I thought Amatoria would have its first space program.” He chuckled and looked down at his feet, maybe a little disappointed that his first bow hadn’t done something amazing, but not too much. Shooting for the moon only to land among the grass was how reality worked most of the time anyway. What did he expect?

“But the darned thing’s glowing more! ”

Craft looked up and saw the light had turned blue. Actually, he’s right, huh?

There was something flickering in the corner of his vision, and he checked. It was the shadow of a tree, and it was getting sharper.

Meanwhile, the bow in Lei-rei’s hand started to glow and shake. Remaining levelheaded, she tried to let go of it, but when she realized her fingers were either glued or paralyzed — hard to tell in the moment — she tried shaking it off. It was stuck.

The bow glowed brighter, and she started to glow as well. Her aura was getting sucked into the bow — and the bow’s aura was being sucked up into the sky.

Veteran assassins panicked productively when they were about to die. She crouched and put the bow on the ground, stomping on it, but to no avail. She drew her dagger and tried to cut her hand off, but there was a repulsive force perfectly parrying her attacks.

“This” — she froze — “this isn’t good.”

She became as panicked as a child who had discovered the dangers of using superglue without gloves. “Craft!” she shouted, but he couldn’t tear his eyes away. The cool blue shooting star had become heaven’s wrathful finger, its roar like a jet engine’s.

“Craft!” she shouted again. This time, he noticed.

Before the arrow fell into the forest, he tackled her to the ground.

There was a flash — then the shockwave followed.

It drummed at their chests and shook the ground. The trees around them turned bare in an instant, their branches stripped away and turned into spears that impaled the front of their house, the back of the wolves’ den, and the ground all around them.

It was done in just a moment, and the place was left looking like a graveyard of spears.

“Whoa!” Dane shouted from afar. There was the sound of things falling over in the back of his carriage again. He’d apparently taken refuge in it, and apparently, that canvas was capable of deflecting projectiles.

This takes me back, Craft thought. At some point, the CAZ had traded air support for space support. Once practical teleport tech had been developed, it turned out that tungsten rods dropped from lower orbit were more cost-effective than tactical nukes.

As it turned out, the archery of another world was even more cost-effective than that. I hope that didn’t hit anyone, though.

“Excuse me,” Lei-rei said…but her voice had been strangely muffled. Craft opened his eyes and looked down at the head of black hair being generous with her patience. Ah.

He released his hold on her, and she pulled her head away. Fortunately, both were too stoic to feel any romantic development from this context-appropriate post-shockwave position they were in. Before they could get off of each other and apologize like normal people, however, there was an unfortunate crack — but how quaint, it sounded just like glass.

They both looked down at the bow sandwiched between them. Long cracks fractalized up and down its body, and with their every shift, bits and pieces of it fell away.

Lei-rei froze like a statue. “M-my apologies” —

“No, it’s okay.”

She looked up at him, and he nodded to assure her. He did feel a little vexed that it had broken after one shot, but after leaving a tactical nuclear mess like that? A one-shot that went down in history was an overall win in his book.

She seemed unconvinced, however. “Are you sure?”

He shook his head. “It did its job a little too well” — he noticed something blinking on her wrist — “wait, what’s that?”

She looked down at it. Her eyes shot wide. “Nightshade!” she shouted.

Her voice rang in Craft’s ears, and she went and shoved him off with all her might — all of it. He flew off and hit the ground, coming to a skipping stop some fifty meters down the road. Even if there was barely any pain, it still left him disoriented.

He pushed himself up just in time to see Lei-rei cocking her whole body like a trigger, her hair turning every color of the rainbow.

Well, been a while since I’ve seen that.

She blasted off into the forest. It occured to him just then that that was the direction of the temple.
