Chapter 50

"Protect the asset. Maintain the asset. Replace the asset when it fails. Do not confuse maintenance with kindness. The schedule does not accommodate sentiment." - Codex of the Long Watch, Operational Directive

The western track dropped from the plateau in switchbacks through dry scrub and prickly pear. Two of Azzopardi's men walked ahead, setting a pace that suggested they'd made this trip before. The heat concentrated in the bowl of the cove below, the cliffs reflecting it inward, and by the third switchback Arthur could feel sweat running between his shoulder blades. The shield was a kiln on his back.

The compound came into view as they descended. Three long buildings arranged around a courtyard. Converted fishing warehouses, the stone walls pale and solid. Arthur read the modification history involuntarily. Original dry-stone construction, reinforced with concrete lintels and steel angle-brackets. The courtyard entrance had been narrowed to a single defensible gap fitted with a timber gate. Good work. Someone with a practical mind had turned a fish-packing facility into a stronghold inside three weeks.

Steve was looking at the cove beyond the compound. Blue water, stone jetties, and a slipway with the remains of a boathouse. "Wied iż-Żurrieq," he said. "This was a fishing village. The Blue Grotto tourist boats launched from here."

"Tourist boats aren't launching anymore," Donna said.

The gate was open and two fighters stood beside it, carrying weapons with the practised grip of daily use. One had a boat hook resharpened to a gleaming point. The other carried a machete, obviously Ether-transmuted.

"Mela, wait, we get the Baron," one of the guards said, and walked inside.

Inside, the courtyard had a sense of purpose. Crates were being sorted on one side and equipment cleaned on the other, while a woman repaired netting Arthur doubted was being used for fish. Along the eastern wall, a queue.

Arthur counted fifteen, maybe twenty people, sitting on the ground or leaning against walls. Some were injured, many with bandaged limbs, a man with his arm in a sling improvised from curtain fabric, a teenage girl with a compress held against her jaw. Others looked exhausted rather than hurt. Civilians, from their clothing. The fighters dressed differently.

Archie had been watching the queue since they entered.

"The fighters do not wait," he said. "The civilians seem to be waiting longer. Nobody objects." A pause. "I find this arrangement familiar."

Arthur touched Steve's arm in a small gesture that meant later.

As Arthur watched, a door opened at the front of the queue. Two of the Baron's fighters walked out, one flexing his shoulder with the careful motion of someone testing a recently healed joint. They passed the queue without acknowledgement. The next person in line stood. A fighter at the doorway put a hand on his chest. Said something in Maltese. The man stopped.

The fighter pointed at two other fighters further back in the courtyard. They were carrying fresh cuts across their forearms. They walked past the man with the sling and through the door. The man sat back down. He'd been waiting long enough that the patch of shade he'd chosen had moved a foot from where he'd started.

Donna's eyes were moving across the courtyard in the systematic sweep Arthur had learned to recognise. She was building a tactical picture, counting fighters and weapons, mapping the exits.

Steve was looking at the civilians. At how they sat and the distances they kept from the fighters. He was reading the social structure the way he read a burial mound. The order showed in negative space, in the gaps between people, in who moved and who waited.

After about fifteen minutes of waiting a man emerged from the central building. He was maybe in his mid-forties and solidly built and moving without hurry. Dark eyes, dark hair greying at the temples. He was wearing a clean linen shirt with the sleeves rolled to the elbows. The left sleeve neatly folded. The right pulled higher, revealing a forearm that didn't match.

Arthur's brain catalogued the structural differences between the two. The right forearm was thicker by about thirty percent. The skin was taut across tissue of a different density, a ridge of hardened material running along the outer ulna. The muscle moved unnaturally when the man flexed his hand. Something had added structural capacity to this limb without redesigning the connections. Reinforcement without integration with a maintenance cost the owner might not yet understand.

The Baron saw Arthur looking. He held the arm up and turned it slowly. Displaying it.

"You've seen the others," he said. His tone made it a statement.

"Azzopardi told us," Steve said. "The cores. What your people are doing with them."

The Baron looked at Steve and shrugged. "Azzopardi disapproves. He is a careful man. Careful men survive, but they don't advance." He flexed the forearm. The ridge of hardened tissue caught the light. "Mid-grade core. I chose the forearm because I wanted a stronger grip and a harder strike. Intent matters, my friends. If you focus on what you want, the change follows the focus."

"And the joint?" Arthur said. He was looking at the wrist. "The bone density has changed but the connective tissue hasn't caught up. The load through that wrist is off."

For a moment the warmth stepped back. Underneath, the Baron's face acknowledged that he had just been read by someone who understood structures better than he did.

"Everything has a cost. The question is whether the return exceeds the investment."

"Does it?" Donna asked.

"Stronger grip. Harder bone. The arm heals faster than the other one now." He smiled. "The investment pays for itself."

"What about the pain?" Steve said.

The Baron's smile didn't waver. "What pain, my friend?"

Archie's geometry brightened a fraction. "His physiological baseline indicates chronic pain management in the affected limb." The geometry returned to standard rotation.

"Noel Zammit," the Baron said, turning the smile back to full professional warmth. "They call me the Baron. Come, my friends. Sit."

He led them to a table in the courtyard's shade. Real chairs. A tablecloth. A jug of water with actual ice.

Steve sat down, then looked at the jug. "Is that…"

"Block ice from the fish market freezer. Packed in sawdust, deep under the rock. Perhaps another week of this luxury."

"Right." Steve picked up his glass. "I wasn't… never mind. Thank you."

The courtyard around them continued its business. The queue along the eastern wall didn't shorten.

The Baron had chosen the chairs that put his guests' backs to the courtyard and his own face toward the gate. Donna had clocked this attempt to keep the party guessing at what was happening behind them and keeping their back exposed.

"You came out of a cliff," the Baron said, pouring water. "My patrol saw you climb from the coastal path. Nobody lives down there. Nobody goes down there. So either you arrived by sea, which I would have noticed, or you came from inside the rock." He set the jug down. "Which means you have a way of getting to this island that doesn't involve a boat."

Arthur let the silence do the work.

"Mela," the Baron said. He smiled. The smile was professional warmth serving a function. "I'll go first. I have forty fighters. Levels six through eight, most of them. We control the southern coast from here to Marsaxlokk. The temples are inside Captain Azzopardi's perimeter, but everything between the temples and the sea is mine. If you want to move across this island, you move through my territory."

He drank his water and waited.

"We're following a network of sites," Arthur said. "The temples. The monuments. There's infrastructure underneath them, connecting them. We need to access the temple complex to continue."

"Infrastructure." The Baron's eyebrows rose. "So there IS a system. My people have been fighting in the zone around those temples for two weeks. They come back stronger. The creatures come back stranger. I assumed cause and effect." He leaned forward. "But you're telling me there is an architecture to this."

"There's an architecture," Arthur said. An engineer confirming what a smuggler had intuited.

"Level ten. All three of you?"

"Eleven. All three," Arthur corrected him with a smile.

The Baron absorbed the number. Arthur could see him doing the arithmetic. His best fighter was Level eight, and three Level 11s had walked into his compound and sat down at his table. The Baron had already catalogued the power differential. It was visible in his face.

"Issa," he said. "What do you need from me?"

"Free movement. Access to the temple and the surrounding area. No interference from your patrols."

"And in return?"

"Information. What we learn about the system, we share with you."

The Baron considered this for a moment. "Information." He turned the word over like a coin, examining both sides. "Information is valuable, my friend. But you're asking for territory. Territory is worth more."

"The information will be worth more than the territory," Steve said. "Trust me on that."

The Baron looked at Steve for the first time with real attention, measuring something. Then he nodded.

"We have an understanding, then. You move freely and you share what you find. We will discuss further once I see what your information is worth." He spread his hands. "Reasonable, mela?"

The door along the eastern wall opened.

The two fighters who'd jumped the queue emerged, one rolling his shoulder, the other pressing his palm against the wall as if testing whether his hand still worked. Behind them, framed in the doorway for a moment before stepping back inside was a woman.

She had dark hair pulled back over scrubs laundered to grey. She stood in the doorway and looked across the courtyard at the Baron and waited.

The Baron gave a small nod. It was barely a motion. A permission granted so casually it could have been a reflex and the woman turned back inside. She motioned at the next person in the queue and he rose and followed her through the door.

The transaction had taken five seconds and Arthur filed it behind the negotiation. The Baron was still talking about territorial boundaries and patrol schedules.

Donna kept it at the front of her mind, where she kept active threats. She'd seen the look. The woman's eyes had found the Baron before she moved, asking for permission. It was the body language of places where initiative cost more than obedience.

The Baron saw Donna looking. “Ah, that is the most important member of this community,” he continued with a smile. “The Doctor, she can heal with Ether and we have an arrangement that benefits all of the island.”

Donna looked back at the Baron and gave a tight nod.

The Baron offered to host them at the compound. Offering them rooms, supplies and proximity to the coast. Donna declined for them before he had finished the offer.

"The captain's camp is closer to the temple," she said. "We'll need to be up there early."

It was true, but it wasn't the real reason. The Baron accepted it with a nod that said he knew it wasn't the reason and didn't mind, because a guest who chose the military camp over his compound was a guest who'd revealed a preference. Preferences were data, and data was leverage.

They left with an agreement that amounted to tolerance. Arthur shook the Baron's hand, the left one. Donna waited until they were two switchbacks above the compound and comfortably out of earshot.

"The doctor," she said.

Arthur waited for her to go on.

"She looked at him before she moved. Before she treated the next patient, she looked at him. For permission." Donna's voice was flat. The voice she used for threat briefings.

"He called it an arrangement. He said it benefits everyone. People say that about arrangements that benefit them."

"How sure are you?" Steve asked quietly.

"I'm sure."

Arthur didn't answer for a moment. He was thinking about the queue. The fighters who walked past the injured man. The woman in the doorway who waited for a nod that should never have been necessary.

"We'll deal with it," he said.

"Yes," Donna said. "We will."
