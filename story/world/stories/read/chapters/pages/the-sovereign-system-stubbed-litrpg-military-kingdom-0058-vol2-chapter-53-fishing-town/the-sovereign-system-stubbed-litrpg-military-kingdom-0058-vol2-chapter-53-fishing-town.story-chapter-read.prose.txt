A few hours later, Luke dispatched scouts on horseback to survey the path running along the Wu River. Thanks to the enemy soldiers they had slain the night before, they now had ten horses at their disposal. However, aside from the open grasslands to graze on, there was no additional feed for the animals.

Two carts were repurposed to carry the wounded—those too injured to walk—while the rest of the army marched on foot. Luke ordered the now-armed soldiers to form a protective outer ring around the formation, spacing them evenly to guard against any potential ambush.

Fortunately, the scouts returned not long after, bearing good news. They had located a medium town nestled by the river, built along the widest stretch of the Wu. The settlement’s defenses were minimal—hardly more than a token wall—but what caught Luke’s attention was the unmistakable sign of fish farming in the area.

He exhaled a quiet breath of relief. Depending on the size of the farms, it might be enough to sustain them until they reached Ralis.

“I hope you like seafood,” Luke said, a rare smile tugging at his lips.

“As long as there’s ale to wash it down, I don’t care what we eat,” Richard replied, stretching his back with a grunt.

The mention of ale stirred something within Luke—a flicker of memory, warm and bittersweet. He saw himself alongside Kayson, Arthur, and Brian, standing at the gates of Xiu Fortress, making that foolish promise to drink together once the war was over.

It had felt simpler then, even if it hadn’t been.But now, Arthur was dead.Brian was still at the academy in Clayton City.And that promised reunion would likely never come to pass.

A quiet melancholy crept in, sudden and uninvited, settling over him like a damp cloak.

“What are your orders, General?”

The voice snapped him out of his thoughts. Luke turned, blinking away the memory.

“Send a group ahead. Find the city lord and inform him of our intentions. If we can pass through without bloodshed, that would be best.”

“I’ll lead the group,” Tristan offered, placing a firm hand on Luke’s shoulder as if sensing his mood.

“I will go as well,” Richard added, stepping forward.

“No. Stay here with the Second Prince,” Luke said firmly. “You’re too imposing. If the townsfolk see you marching toward them, they might panic and attack out of fear.”

The real reason, however, went unspoken.Lyon still seemed... fractured. Whether it was grief over his brother’s death or the lingering trauma of being held as a political hostage, Luke wasn’t sure. But he didn’t want the young prince left alone.

No one argued. A moment later, Tristan and a handful of handpicked men mounted their horses and rode off along the riverbank, heading toward the town. The rest of the army continued their slow trudge on foot.

Luke moved through the ranks, quietly observing. He could feel the weight of exhaustion in their bodies—their soaked clothes clinging to weary limbs, the crunch of mud beneath their boots. Yet despite the downpour, the sleepless night, and the paltry rations, their spirits seemed unexpectedly high.

Some of it, no doubt, stemmed from the promise of reaching a settlement.But there was something else.Something deeper.

As he passed another cluster of soldiers, he felt it again—that quiet reverence in their gazes.

Not just respect. Not merely loyalty.

Reverence.

It was... bizarre.

As someone who had never been particularly attractive or popular in his previous life—and now, as a commoner in this world—Luke’s social experience had been shaped by dismissive glances and near-total indifference.

But the way these soldiers looked at him now…

It was something altogether different.

They gazed at him as if he were a figure carved from legend. Not with pity, envy, or even mere respect—but something closer to awe.

Do they… revere me?

Is this because of my Charisma?

His mind flashed to his system window. His Charisma stat had surged to S+—well into the realm of the superhuman if the grading scale mirrored that of his subordinates.

So this is what it feels like…

Perhaps this newfound presence could be leveraged in the future—especially once they returned to the capital.

That thought, however, darkened his mood.

He had been tasked with retrieving their troops from Yunxi. Instead, he had diverted the expedition to Xiandong without explicit orders. Even now, he had no idea if Yunxi had fallen—or if his failure to appear had cost lives.

Will I be ordered back to Yunxi?

But he shook his head.

There was an entire army approaching Ralis from the south. The king would have his hands full just holding the capital.

He won’t have time to worry about me… right?

In the distance, a cluster of rooftops broke through the mist and trees. A small smile crept onto Luke’s face as he spotted the town nestled on the banks of the Wu River.

It was humble—rows of wooden and thatch-roofed homes built along both sides of the water—but a long, sturdy bridge connected the two banks, its structure more complex than expected for such a place.

Activating Eagle Eye, Luke zeroed in on the bridge. It wasn’t just a crossing. His Scholarly Pursuit revealed more: sluice gates and woven netting beneath the beams marked it as part of an integrated fish farming system.

A clever design.Efficient.Sustainable.

He let out a breath he hadn’t realized he was holding. At least the men will eat tonight.

The promise of food and shelter worked like magic. The tired soldiers began to move with new energy, their complaints replaced by anticipation. Even the wounded seemed to stand a little taller.

As they drew closer, a group of riders emerged from the town’s gate. Luke recognized them immediately—Tristan and the scouts he had sent ahead.

Everything was unfolding as planned. That was, until Luke’s eyes fell on the object clutched in Tristan’s left hand.

He squinted.Then froze.

“…What the fuck?” he muttered.

Tristan’s expression as he approached was grim and exhausted. Without a word, he held up the object—revealing the severed head of a man, perhaps in his forties. His long black hair being used as a makeshift handle, now clutched like a gruesome trophy.

Even in death, the man’s expression radiated defiance. Or perhaps… stubborn foolishness.

“Our discussion didn’t go very well,” Tristan said flatly.

“You don’t say…” Luke replied, his voice heavy with distaste.

He grimaced, turning away from the severed head. “If I’d known you were going to lop it off, I’d have sent Richard with you instead.”

He pinched the bridge of his nose, swallowing a sigh that threatened to escape. “Did the townspeople get the message?”

“Yes, I’ve already ordered them to start the cookfires,” Tristan replied, his gaze drifting to the weary ranks behind Luke—soldiers running on fumes and held together by sheer discipline.

“How long are we staying?”

“Two days at most,” Luke said. “It’ll take another four days to reach Ralis. We need to press on.”

He didn’t mention the real reason they couldn’t linger. There was no point burdening morale with the knowledge that a larger army was right behind them. Let them rest while they could.

Tristan nodded. “Two days should be enough.”

The command was quickly relayed through the ranks. Soon, Luke and the officers led the battered column through the town’s eastern gate.

The eastern district was clearly residential. Wooden homes with thatched roofs dotted the rising hills, and narrow, winding streets curved naturally with the terrain. The town, despite its modest defenses, was large—housing nearly three thousand residents.

Most of them had vanished into their homes the moment Luke’s forces arrived. At his strict order, the soldiers kept their distance and refrained from disturbing the townsfolk. He had no intention of pillaging this place.

It would only wear down the men further—and for what? Bread and bruises?

“Where’s the city lord’s residence?” Luke asked.

Tristan gestured toward the river. “He was at the church on the other side of town.”

Luke’s eyes followed the direction, and his expression lifted slightly as they approached the wide wooden bridge that spanned the Wu River.

It was modest in appearance, but well-crafted—sturdy support beams, expertly joined planks, minimal sway despite the current. A work of precise, practical engineering.

If not for Scholarly Pursuit, I might’ve overlooked its genius…

His gaze dropped to the only paved road in town, a long stone path that led directly to the bridge—clearly designed for carts, trade, and foot traffic.

So the eastern district houses the residents, and the west… that must be the fishery and business sector.

The closer they came to the bridge, the more detail he could discern. Below, white foam frothed as water rushed along the rocks—a controlled rapid, not unlike the whitewater back on Earth. Just north of the bridge, the river expanded into a basin where the surface swirled more gently.

The fish farm.

Luke couldn’t help but marvel at the cleverness of it.

They use the rapids to keep the water oxygenated, preventing stagnation. The sluice gates hold the larger fish in place while the young are allowed to grow downstream. Then, during spawning season… the gates open, letting the adults swim back upstream to begin the cycle anew.

He studied it with the intensity of someone in the marine industry.

Back on Earth, he’d never cared for fish farming. But here, in a world where famine and sickness were common, such ingenuity could mean survival.

If I can gather enough data, I could recreate systems like this… Not just wartime tech, but essential infrastructure. Irrigation. Medicine. Water filtration. Sanitation.

So many died not from blades or arrows—but from hunger, filth, and fever.

If I want to change that…

His hand curled into a fist at his side.

I’ll need power.

The thought weighed heavy, grounding him in the moment as the bridge loomed ahead.

He glanced once more at the roaring water beneath his feet, then turned his gaze forward—toward the western bank, toward the church.

If the Marxx Kingdom fell to Lhair, Luke might never have the chance to implement any of these ideas.

A sudden spray from the river below kissed his skin, snapping him out of his reverie. Only then did he realize that ten thousand men stood behind him—silent, expectant, waiting for his command.

He turned.

Tristan stood at the front of the formation, a concerned expression creasing his brow. The silent worry in his eyes made Luke… smile.

“Let’s go. I want to see this church you mentioned.”

With that, he led the group across the bridge. Still unsure of the bridge’s weight limit, he instructed the newly appointed lieutenants to stagger the crossing, allowing only small units at a time.

Once across, Tristan guided Luke down into the western district of the town.

This side of the river bore little resemblance to the winding, hilly residential quarter. The buildings here were broader, sturdier, built for function rather than comfort. Wide roads and reinforced wooden structures dominated the layout—designed to accommodate wagons and cargo.

Many of the paths were paved, and the strong, briny scent of fish filled the air. Luke inhaled deeply, surprised by the wave of nostalgia it brought. It reminded him of seaside towns on Earth—of bustling docks and noisy fish markets.

A smile crept across his face before he even realized it.

“The church is just ahead,” Tristan said, pointing toward a building in the distance.

Unlike the paved roads around it, the path leading to the church was dirt—well-trodden, the footprints of hundreds pressed deep into the earth. Clearly, this was a place the townsfolk visited often.

The church itself had a tall, pointed roof, made to appear even loftier by the squat, wide frame of the building. It reminded Luke of rural churches he'd seen in old countryside towns—humble, unadorned, and full of quiet reverence. Instead of stained glass, the windows were covered in thin wooden latticework, allowing light and air to pass through.

They approached the heavy wooden doors, which were already slightly ajar. Tristan pushed one open without a word.

Inside, the air changed.

The church’s interior was plain, simple wooden pews and unadorned walls. But before Luke could fully take it in, his body suddenly seized.

His breath caught.

A fleeting image surged through his mind—so vivid it felt like a memory, and yet… not his own.

His hand flew to his forehead as the room swayed slightly.

What the hell was that?

“Young Luke?”

Tristan spun toward him, catching him just as his knees weakened. Strong hands steadied him, and the worry on Tristan’s face deepened.

“I’m fine,” Luke murmured, shaking his head. “Lack of sleep, that’s all.”

It wasn’t entirely untrue. He had barely scraped together six hours of rest across the last two days. The excuse seemed to ease Tristan’s concern—at least enough that he didn’t press the matter.

But Luke barely noticed.

His mind was spinning, locked onto the remnants of the vision.

The sharp scent of salt water.The creak of rubber boots shifting through mud.The cold rush of water up to his waist.And ahead—just beyond reach—a wide-backed man, moving through the current.

Those… weren’t my memories.

A cold shiver ran down Luke’s spine.

His eyes lifted toward the altar at the front of the church. A towering statue loomed over it—a massive serpent coiled around a sword plunged into the ground, its stone head frozen in a poised strike.

A sudden, unnatural hissing filled his ears—sharp, guttural, and close.

His entire body trembled.

The sound swelled, pressing against his skull, and then—

Darkness.

He felt himself falling.
