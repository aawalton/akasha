162, The Weight of Gold.

Just off the title alone, Amy could already tell this chapter was going to be a pain in the ass. Maybe that's why she couldn't bring herself to actually start reading it.

As she sat there, in a corner of the obsidian room, a long sigh escaped her, loud enough to have Lyra's eyes on her momentarily.

They involuntarily locked eyes for a single second before Lyra rapidly snapped back to Crow, continuing his healing. Contrary to Zayd who was still minding his own business and Ash who was still napping, the girl had been staring intermittently in this direction for a while, the reason still a mystery.

Amy was almost tempted to ask her outright what her problem was, but there was a little thing that took precedence over everything else right now, the manga chapter… And yet, despite all this, she still did not start reading it.

She knew losing time was the worst possible thing she could do right now; the inevitable return of Lain and Iris along with Libris' imminent shutdown were things she had to worry about. However, the thought of re-experiencing even a modified version of her past unsettled her deeply.

Yet, again, she had no time to lose. Libris was going to go into "sleep" at any moment. Her hand was basically being forced…like always…

It felt surreal sometimes, to think about her situation. There was always something going on, always something she had to take care of, and always something that forced her to keep pushing more and more.

She was tired, had been for a while, not only physically or mentally. It was something more primal. Playing this character of hers, dealing with this growing responsibility… She felt not just exhausted, but also numb.

To feel that way… It honestly wasn't so surprising after everything that she went through. Yet it still felt unnatural; it wasn't the tiredness from back during the week of training before the infiltration in the headmistress' office, this one was different somehow.

There was a growing numbness in her chest, almost as if her feelings were slowly being drained and burnt. Crow's severed arm, the headmistress' corpse, and even Elias' death, she regarded it all with blurry eyes, if that made sense. She did not know if that was a good or a bad thing, but she did know that the feeling would probably only grow bigger from now on.

Okay, okay— enough of this.

With a shake of her head, she forced her attention back to the chapter. Enough was enough. Regardless of her feelings about this, time kept ticking. She needed to get to it ASAP.

She closed her eyes, taking deep breaths for a few seconds, before finally opening them once again. Then, without wasting any more time, Amy finally got to the chapter.

She ignored the cover that she had already seen and went directly to the first page. And of course, she immediately regretted it.

The chapter started with Amy, perhaps eight years old, sitting at a desk while doing some kind of weird activity. She seemed to be channeling some weird magic with a book reminiscent of Libris.

The room was made of wood, simple, and had nothing special about it, just like Amy's old room—a little different here and there, but the layout was the same. A bed to the side, a desk next to a brown wall, and a yellow round carpet. A simple room with the only thing standing out being the two people in it.

Little Amy wasn't alone. To her side was a woman the present Amy recognized—instead of brown, her hair was now blonde. She also looked younger and somehow less jaded than she remembered, yet still cold…and angrier…

Ania Stake, Amy's mother was staring as Amy seemed to be practicing something.

The manga panel showed little Amy struggling with the book-like artifact, her small hands trembling as she seemingly tried to channel magic through it. Golden light flickered weakly around her fingers before fading entirely.

"Again," her mother said, her speech bubble purposefully big.

It took the real Amy more than a couple of seconds to internalize this was really happening, but also to make the connection with her memories…because indeed this was a memory, and not a particularly pleasant one. In hindsight she should have seen it coming, that out of all her memories this day would be picked.

Every single one of her interactions with her mother had always been sorta memorable, in a bad way. So it wasn't surprising she remembered this one so easily. Amy could count with her fingers the number of times she had seen her mother with a genuine angry expression, so she made the connection instantly.

It wasn't hard to figure out the meaning behind the art and what was really going on. She guessed that whatever she was doing with the book was changed to fit the manga's story. In her memory, it had been school’s advanced homework, but here, she was probably practicing something else.

Amy let out a sigh.

Lots of sighs today…

She once again shook her head, the feeling of numbness growing once again in her chest. After staring at nothing for a few seconds she once again returned her attention to the manga.

Just words on a page. You already experienced this in more than one nightmare…literally…

An extra sigh later, Amy finally went back to reading the chapter.

"I'm tired, Mom," little Amy whispered, sweat drawn on her forehead.

Her mother's golden eyes—so different from her old brown ones—showed no sympathy. "I said again."

"But—"

A slam on the desk made little Amy freeze in place, choking out her words. She didn't even dare to gaze back at her mother as she tried once again, focusing all her energy on the book. A brief spark of light appeared, then immediately died.

"I just can't understand—Amy! How can you really not do something as simple as this?" Her mother's expression was hidden from the panel, only her words were shown. "I can't believe you are barely able to even provide a glimmer. How would you explain that to your father?"

"I'm trying—"

"No, you are just lazy. Not trying hard enough, that's what you are doing."

Little Amy kept trying. There was less light each time, and her mother had something to say about every failed attempt. She only got angrier as the girl tired herself out.

Amy knew this part far too well. It didn't matter whether she was doing homework or trying to make a book glow. She could exhaust herself trying, and her mother would still find something wrong with it.

Even if the contents were adapted into this world, it did not differ from her memories. Maybe even worse since she could actually see them.

It felt as if she could be swallowed at any second in them, the memories. It took a lot of willpower to not let her mind drift freely.

And even yet, she could see herself losing concentration. Falling into the past.

She continued reading—nothing much happened in the next couple of panels, just more barrage from her mother. The more she read the deeper she fell.

Until at some point, it no longer felt like she was reading, but instead living it herself.

She was shivering despite how hot the room felt. Amy—twelve years old, small for her age—clutched the small reed-yellowish grimoire with both hands, bringing it to her chest as she breathed heavily.

"Again. And try to focus this time on the love of the Goddess," Her Mother instructed from behind her. Amy did not dare to look at the expression on her face.

Amy shut her eyes and tried to do it the way Father had shown her before leaving. Find the energy in her core first. Then let it move down her arms, through her hands, and into the book without forcing it.

She felt warmth in her stomach, then a tingling in her fingers. This time, surely. She opened her eyes to check.

Nothing.

"I think I felt something this time," she lied quickly, not wanting to see that look of disappointment again. "But, I just need—"

"I don't want to hear it. I don't want to hear any of it, Amy. Just do it already. Do I look like I have the entire day? Tell me, do I seem like I have nothing better to do?" Her mother moved closer, her heels clicking against the wooden floor. "Show me the light. That's the only thing I want. Can't you do even something as simple as that?"

"I'm trying..." Her voice was barely a whisper.

"Trying isn't enough!" Her mother was shouting now. "He's coming tonight, Amy. What am I going to show him? We've been doing this for months, and you still can't make a light. What do I tell him?"

She kept walking back and forth behind the chair. Amy couldn't see her, but she could hear how quickly she was moving.

"The other children in the village—"

"The other children don't have your blood. Stop comparing yourself to them." Her mother sounded almost frightened as well as angry. "You can't settle for what they can do. Too much depends on this. Do you understand, Amy?"

Tears began forming in Amy's eyes. "But I can't do it."

"Again."

"I can't—"

"Again"

Amy shut her mouth and tried again. And again she failed. Amy held on tighter as the grimoire slipped in her sweaty hands. She tried again, barely catching her breath first. How long had they been doing this? It felt as if she'd been sitting here all day.

"Please work," she whispered to the book. "Please."

Nothing happened, no matter how hard she held it.

The footsteps stopped behind her. Amy waited, listening to her mother take a breath.

"Enough. Just stop." She wasn't shouting anymore. Amy would almost have preferred it if she were.

She let the book drop a little. Her arms were shaking from holding it up. "I'm sorry, Mom. I really tried. Maybe... maybe I'm just not like Dad. Maybe I can't—"

"I said enough. You clearly can't do it."

"But he could always make it work," Amy continued, too tired to stop the words from spilling out. "He made it look so easy. And you could too, before. Maybe if instead of you alone, if he was here too, showing me—"

The slap came without warning. Not hard enough to knock her down, but sharp enough to turn her head, to leave her cheek stinging and her eyes wide with shock.

The silence that followed was deafening.

"Amy, I—" Her mother's hand hung in the air for a moment before dropping to her side. "I didn't mean to— Ugh… You shouldn't have mentioned your father— He is a busy man…"

Amy touched her burning cheek, tears finally spilling over.

"Stop…"

The tears did not stop despite her mother's words.

"Stop, Amy… Look, I'm sorry. Stop." Her mother knelt down, reaching out as if to touch Amy's face, then pulling back. "I'm just... stressed. You know how important this is. Our objective is the grandest of them all… We—No, you, can't afford to fail… You are the only one, Amy. No other person in this world can, not even me, only you… I have to be strict, even if I don't want to.

"They're watching. They're always watching, and one day they will come for us, just like they came for all the others. And when you can't even manage a simple light after months of practice… Your father is already disappointed in you being a woman, if he finds out that in addition to your gender, I can't teach you—" She caught herself, and took a shaky breath. "It's not fair to put this on you, but life isn't fair. You need to understand that."

Amy said nothing, just held the grimoire tighter.

"We'll stop for today," her mother said, standing and smoothing her dress with trembling hands. "You're too emotional now anyway. Your father will…" she paused for a second, before speaking. "Your father will hopefully understand…" she finally added.

A knock at the door interrupted her.

Both of them froze.

"Amy?" A young voice called from outside. "Amy, are you there? It's Mira!"

"Speaking of the devil…" Her mother was already pulling herself together. By the time she reached the door, she looked ready to receive a guest.

"One moment!" Nothing in her voice would have told Mira what had been happening. She looked back at Amy, still at the desk. "Wipe your face, add makeup if necessary. I won't have the neighbors talking."

[Amy?] Libris's voice seemed to come from far away.

The magic grimoire replacing school's shit—the one that looks similar to you but not quite—then all the talk about 'them' watching us, my mother's hair being blonde instead of brown… It really feels like I was reading someone else's life… mostly…"

To Amy's surprise, it took very little convincing—almost no convincing—for her mother to let her go outside with Mira.

Currently, Amy found herself walking under the bright sky alongside Mira, down the village's main dirt road. Her cheek hurt. She listened to Mira so she wouldn't have to keep thinking about why.

"You missed it yesterday," Mira said. "That woman selling books at the market? The really pretty one with the gold hair that floats about, and those eyes like stars? She tripped over Mrs. Chen's golden retriever. The cart tipped over too. I don't think a single book stayed in it."

"No way." Amy was smiling now. "I missed all that?"

"There were red ones and blue ones, and some had those little bells tied on with ribbons. All on the road. She had to pick them up around the dog. He wouldn't get out of the way." Mira adjusted her glasses. "I found out about the food too."

"Go on." Amy would've listened to just about anything that kept her mind off home.

"Everyone calls him a thief, right? Yesterday I followed him to the bakery. He sat outside watching everyone eat until they felt bad and started giving him scraps. He doesn't have to steal anything. They hand it over."

"Clever dog."

"Isn't he?" Mira sent a little stone rolling ahead with her shoe. "The baker's son did try to get rid of him. Wouldn't move. Wouldn't even look at him."

Amy kept watching her as she spoke. Mira used her hands so much when she told a story, and she looked so pleased to be telling this one that Amy didn't want it to end.

"He never seems to know what he's doing," she said.

"The baker's son? You should've seen him when Mrs. Hart came in. Kept dropping things the whole time she watched. Then he nearly caught his apron on the oven getting the bread out."

"Maybe he gets nervous when people watch him."

"Maybe. Doesn't explain the bucket, though." Mira grinned at her. "He couldn't get his foot out of it. So he had to hop to the well with it still stuck on. The clanging! Half the road must've heard him coming."

For once, Amy didn't have to make herself laugh. "That's awful."

"It was very funny."

Amy missed nearly all of this. She was usually at home studying or practicing, or hearing about how badly she'd done at one of those. It was nice to listen to someone whose problems involved festivals and boys getting stuck in buckets.

"I'm still surprised at how you manage to know this many things about the village."

"It comes with time. Your family—with the exception of your father—are new to the countryside, so it's normal you don't know the art of gossiping." Mira seemed to get distracted by the thought. "I'd like to see the capital. Imagine having that many shops to go to. And people everywhere, even if it is noisy."

"Would you want to live there?"

"Maybe for a while. I'm a bit tired of knowing exactly what I'll see when I go outside. It'd be nice to go somewhere I didn't know anybody." She looked over at Amy. "Don't you ever think about that? About going back there?"

Amy felt her chest tighten. "Sometimes."

"Do you miss it?"

"I... I don't know. I like the quiet here, but… It was nice before, when nobody knew me… Though I don't remember much since I was kind of little… but still…" The words came out before she could stop them.

"Huh…? Why? Don't you like the attention, Miss Popular Girl?"

The words hit Amy strangely. They felt... distant. Like Mira was talking about someone else entirely.

"Yeah, maybe," Amy said, forcing her voice to stay light. "Either way, wasn't there something you wanted to talk to me about before?"

"Oh, right! Nah, it was nothing important. I just wanted to comment that your mom's so cool," Mira said, swinging her arms as they walked. "She always smells like those fancy soaps from the capital, doesn't she? Mine just smells like flour and disappointment."

Amy grimaced for a single second before faking a laugh. "Disappointment has a smell?"

"Oh yeah, it's like... burnt bread mixed with the face she makes when she looks at me." Mira adjusted her glasses with a theatrical sigh. "Speaking of which, you will not believe what happened at breakfast today."

"Tell me."

Good. Anything but my mother.

"Porridge again this morning. You know when it's so lumpy it looks like somebody's already eaten it? I told her I didn't want any."

"What did she do?"

"What do you think? Started telling me off. I can't even remember everything she said." Mira waved her hands to show how her mother had done it. "Then Dad joined in. He always does that, like one of them having a go at me isn't enough."

Amy nodded. She liked looking at Mira when she talked, liked how excited she got over the smallest things. She could listen to her for ages. It was only what she was saying that made Amy uncomfortable.

"That must be annoying," she said, taking care with her voice.

"It is! I wasn't asking her to cook a feast. Just something I actually wanted to eat." Mira sent another pebble down the road with her foot. "Apparently that makes me picky. She said I needed to learn to eat what I was given."

Amy made herself relax her jaw before Mira noticed.

"They do worry about you, though. Your parents, I mean." She wanted to know more, even if it wasn't very pleasant to hear. "Like with the festival last month."

"Ugh, Dad thought a merchant was going to carry me off or something. Your mother let you go without all that fuss."

Her mother had let her go because she'd done well in practice that day. Amy almost said so, then left it. Mira had noticed her getting permission. She'd actually thought about Amy's family enough to compare it with her own.

If only she could have a life like Mira's…

"And he keeps saying I won't find a husband if I'm this fussy about food." Mira pulled a face. "I'm twelve. Why would I want to think about that? There are normal things I could be thinking about."

"Like what?"

"Like the baker's son staring at you every market day."

"At me? I hadn't noticed." Amy had. It would've been hard not to.

"Oh, come on. He's awful at hiding it. It's kind of cute." Mira grinned. "I told him not to bother, though. You're too clever for him, and you never have time for anything except studying." She nudged Amy's shoulder. "Your mother really does keep you busy."

"Yeah. More than usual lately." Amy didn't like talking about it, but she wanted Mira to ask.

"More than usual how?" There was concern in her voice this time. Amy wanted to tell her everything and was frightened by how badly she wanted it.

"She wants me to get everything right. With my studies, with… everything else too, really." Amy chose her words carefully, not wanting to say too much for fear of her parents.

"Well, that's what you get for being so popular," Mira said with a laugh, nudging Amy again. "There is always a downside to everything, isn't there?"

Amy dug her nails into her palms. Mira could spend half the walk complaining about breakfast, but when Amy tried to tell her something, that was all she got? She wanted to turn and ask what Mira thought she knew about her life.

Mira was still smiling at her, eyes creased behind her glasses. The longer Amy looked at her, the harder it was to stay angry.

She hadn't told Mira anything. How was the girl supposed to know? Maybe Amy ought to leave it that way, as she did with everyone else.

"Maybe," she said, making herself smile back and letting her hands loosen. "That merchant you were talking about. The one at the market?"

"Oh, her!" Mira was immediately interested in the new subject. "She is just so weird and eccentric, also— Hey, how about we go see her?"

"Mmm… sure," Amy said after a few seconds of pondering. Anywhere away from her house was indeed perfect.

"Race you there!" Mira took off running, her laughter trailing behind her like music.

"Huh- W-wait!"

Amy ran after her, struggling to catch up on her shorter legs. They followed the dirt road into the square, where wooden stalls stood around an old stone wall.

"Over there!" Mira pointed past the nearest stalls. "That's where she was."

There weren't many people to get through, and none of them had floating gold hair. At the spot Mira led her to, a man was selling turnips and onions.

"Excuse me," Mira said, still breathing hard. "Do you know where the woman from yesterday went? She had books with ribbons on them."

The man left the vegetables for a moment. "You mean the one with that hair? She went this morning. Said she was moving to the other side of the village. I reckon it's worth trying the old mill road."

Amy looked at Mira, who looked back at her.

"That's quite a walk." Amy checked the sun. It was already past midday. "I have to be home before dusk. Mom said."

"Let's split up, then. I'll try by the mill, on the east side. You could look in the western district." Mira seemed pleased to have somewhere to search.

"Alright. But we meet here when the sun starts setting. I can't be late."

"Deal!" Mira was off before Amy had thought of anything else to say. The market was emptying out around her.

She went west, through quieter streets with houses and the occasional little shop. Most people she asked didn't know anything about the merchant.

"Amy! Over here!"

Some children were waving from a courtyard between two houses. She'd played with them before, though she couldn't quite put names to all their faces.

"We're starting again! Come play," a red-haired boy called. He pointed to a game laid out with sticks and stones.

Amy smiled before she'd even decided what to say. She needed to be perfect at all times, never let anyone see through her disguise. Just like her mother and father wanted it. The less people knew about her, the less suspicion her family enemies would have about her. "Oh, that's so nice of you to ask, but I'm actually looking for someone right now. Could I join you another time?"

"Just a little while?" asked a girl with pigtails. "You used to play with us."

"I know. I'm sorry, I've had so much studying to do." Amy didn't need to think about the excuse anymore. "I'll try to come soon. I promise."

They looked so disappointed that she felt bad for saying no. She was still glad she had a reason to leave. Being pleasant for a few sentences was easier than keeping it up through a whole game.

"Okay," the boy said. "Next time."

She waved and went on. She kept feeling as if someone was looking at her, and when she turned into a lane, she saw two old women on a bench. They'd leaned toward each other to talk.

"Poor little thing," one said, supposedly quietly. Amy could hear her perfectly well.

"What do you expect with that mother?" the other replied.

Amy slowed before she could stop herself. Her heart was beating faster.

"Only wanted his money. She came here from the capital, all pretty and innocent, and trapped him. You could see it a mile off."

She should keep walking. Her mother had told her what to do about this. "They're old, Amy. They don't remember things properly, so they make them up. Don't pay any attention."

Amy clenched her hands and made herself go on, even though she could still hear them.

Her face was hot by the end of the lane. For the rest of the afternoon she kept asking people about the merchant, but she barely listened when they answered. She was still hearing the two women, and she hated that she couldn't stop.

When the sun began to go down, she gave up and headed back. Mira was sitting on the edge of the stone well in the square, swinging her legs.

"Did you find her?"

"No. Did you?" Amy had tried to sound cheerful. It hadn't worked very well.

"Yes! By the mill, where he said. She was odd, though." Mira stopped looking quite so pleased. "I asked about the books and she started saying things like 'not conviction' and 'not the one she was searching for.' I don't think she's quite right."

Amy nodded, still thinking about what the women had said.

"Are you alright? You're not really listening."

"I'm fine. Just tired."

"Well, we know where she is now. We could try again tomorrow and—"

"Maybe. I have to get home. My mother will be worried."

Mira smiled in a way Amy was finding increasingly difficult to look at. "You're lucky, you know. My mom would probably be glad if I stayed out past dark. She acts all protective and stuff, but the moment I get out her sight I can see the big grin on her face. I really can’t understand it."

Amy couldn't listen to any more of this. She'd spent the day failing, being told about it, overhearing what people thought of her family, and pretending none of it bothered her. And Mira still thought she was lucky.

"Lucky?" She turned on her. "Is that really what you think?"

Mira stared. "Well… they worry about you. They care where you are, and—"

"They care whether I can do what they want." Amy could hear herself getting louder. "Whether I'm clever enough, whether my ability works. Whether I'm worth having around."

"Amy, I wasn't trying to—"

"You never are. You don't think before you say it." Amy couldn't stop her hands from shaking. "Your breakfast was bad. Your parents let you go out. That's what you've been complaining about. I'd like to do one thing without being told it wasn't good enough. Just once."

Mira looked pale. "I'm sorry, Amy. I didn't realize—"

"You didn't want to. You'd already decided I had this wonderful family. You never bothered to find out." Amy stepped back. She hadn't meant to say all that. "I have to go."

She ran before Mira could answer, leaving her at the well with her mouth still open.

***

By the time she got through her own front door, Amy was out of breath and trying not to cry.

"Amy?" her mother called from the kitchen. "Come in, it's time for dinner."

She shut her eyes and took a breath. Then she made her face behave. "Sorry I'm late. I didn't realize the time."

"Go and wash. Your father will be here soon."

Amy washed her hands and splashed water on her face. There was a little mirror over the basin. She smiled at it a few times until she looked alright.

***

Her father came in through the creaking door. He was tall and thin, and his hair was gold too, like her mother's was now. His eyes were pale blue. Amy found them hard to look at for long.

"My beautiful girls. I've missed you." He kissed her mother's cheek and ruffled Amy's hair. He sounded pleased to be home, though Amy couldn't quite feel it.

"Welcome home, dear." Her mother was smiling without much behind it either. "Sit down. Dinner's ready."

They ate at the little table while he told them about the eastern provinces. Strange weather, caravans he'd passed, things like that. He could make a journey sound interesting even when very little had happened. For a few minutes it was a normal dinner, as far as Amy knew what that meant.

"And you, little star? How are your studies?"

Her mother was looking at her. Amy chose her answer carefully. "It's been difficult. But I'm learning."

"Well, it has to be difficult if you're going to improve. Let's see what you've learned."

Amy swallowed. "Right now?"

"I've been looking forward to it." He was smiling at her, but he watched her too closely. "Your mother hasn't told me much in her letters. Not about what you can actually do."

Amy looked over at her. She'd gripped her spoon so hard her knuckles had turned white.

"Let her eat first, dear. She's been practicing all day. She's tired."

"Tired from what?" He still sounded pleasant, almost. "We're talking about a few basic exercises."

"They're hardly basic. She's a child, and you're asking her to learn advanced techniques. Give her some time."

He put his spoon down. "How much? She's had months. We don't have endless time to wait for her."

"She's doing what she can—"

"Then she needs to do more."

Amy stared into her stew. She couldn't eat any more of it. She should have known he'd ask, and she should have been ready to show him something.

"She has the potential. We both know that," her mother said, louder now. "But someone needs to help her develop it. You can't just keep demanding things."

He laughed once. "With her blood, this ought to come naturally. And you want to tell me how much potential she has when she still can't do it?"

"She's trying—"

"She isn't succeeding. Neither are you." He stood, dragging his chair back. "You have my books. I showed you the techniques, gave you everything you said you needed. What else was I supposed to leave you before you could teach her?"

Her mother got up. "You could have stayed. You leave for weeks, and I have to manage everything here. Then it's my fault when you don't like the result."

"Do you think I go away for fun? I'm trying to make sure we survive what's coming, that there's a place for us afterward. And I come back to this. You playing house and telling me she's doing her best."

"Playing house? I'm raising your daughter by myself! Do you have any idea what it takes to get her ability to develop?"

"It might develop if you stopped indulging her—"

"It might help if you stayed here to teach her instead of coming back to tell us everything we've done wrong—"

"I'm here. I'm asking to see what she's learned. Apparently that's nothing, and you're—"

"I'm what?" She stepped toward him, hands clenched. "Finish it. Go on."

"Weak. You always have been. You've taught her to be the same. Every time she fails, you find a reason it isn't her fault instead of making her work."

"You want to make her into something she isn't!" her mother shouted. "She's a little girl. She can't be a weapon for you to use in whatever you're doing."

"She's no use to us if you keep treating her like something to pet and spoil. She has work to do."

"Listen to what you're saying. That's our daughter you're talking about!"

"She's our future! We both know it. Our only chance at—"

"At what? Saving ourselves? At power? At proving you're more than just a useless failed prince? Nothing more than a deserter?"

Her father stopped moving. He looked at his wife, and Amy wished her mother hadn't said it. She didn't like that look at all.

"Say that again." He'd become so quiet.

"You heard me."

"Stop," Amy whispered, but neither of them heard her.
