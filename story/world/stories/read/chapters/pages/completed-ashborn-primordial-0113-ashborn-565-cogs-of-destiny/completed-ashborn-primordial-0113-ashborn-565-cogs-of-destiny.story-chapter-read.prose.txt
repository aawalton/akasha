“Not a chance in the Ash,” said the Altani general-in-chief of the Hiranyan territories. “I’ll not send my mejai to die in a pointless battle. Not until the demons are at Daha’s walls, and perhaps not even then. We failed at Kanthaghati Pass even with the benefit of a fortress. What makes you think we could ever win in an open field that benefits the demons?”

He was not the first to voice such concerns that morning, nor, Tia was certain, would he be the last.

Princess Tiyana Matali was determined to hear each of them out regardless.

Many of the displaced Sawai lords and military leaders had come to Alt Janak. Not to discuss any solution to the mess they found themselves in, but to tell Tia in several different ways that the demons could not be stopped. That the hundreds of towns they’d pillaged and burned had proven it, and that their only option now was to surrender wholesale and pray for the Akh Nara to return and show them mercy.

It took everything Tia had not to gag at their spinelessness.

They were all wrong. Each and every last one of them.

Vir would show them mercy, she knew. Despite everything, she respected him more than any demon. Perhaps more than any living being.

Assuming he returned in time, he would ensure they were ruled over as equitably as possible.

Of course, there was every chance he didn’t return. Not until it was too late. Until every man, woman, and child had been cut down by the rampaging demon army.

An army she doubted even the Akh Nara could control.

And yet, even in the best-case scenario, even the fairest and most generous rule was still life under the thumb of another.

Never again would humanity stand on their own. Doomed to be little more than glorified slaves.

She looked around the long table at the fearful, uncertain eyes of all these leaders, each of whom believed defeat was inevitable.

Tia knew better. She’d killed their greatest Samsara. He’d risen again, true, but she’d killed him all the same. That was something thing none of these men understood.

“We have all learned much about demons these past weeks,” she said, once the general had finished. “More than in all the years before it, I feel. I’ll not throw all that away on the off chance an enemy whose brutality puts Andros to shame shows us mercy.”

King Riyan Savar spoke up from across the table.

“While I wish to turn back the demons as much as you, princess, I must agree with our generals,” he said. “The situation seems untenable. My Saians were at that pass too. They threw everything they had at those giants to no avail. What did we learn there, if not that we cannot win?”

“Only that defending from a wall is the wrong strategy against their strengths,” Tia said with a shrug. “Which is why I do not intend to fight behind one again.”

Riyan replied with a hard stare. “Where we failed behind a fortress and every strategic advantage humankind could bring to bear, you mean to succeed in an open field?”

Riyan sat back in his chair. “Forgive me if I do not share your confidence. I doubt even our Saint could accomplish that feat.”

The mention of Maiya soured Tia's mouth, bringing with it a toxic medley of feelings she’d rather not deal with at that moment.

“Our Saint failed because she chose to,” Tia said. “She had the most powerful weapon in the realm hanging over that valley and didn’t use it. She had an army of Asura who could have taken on that entire army all on their own, and yet she ordered them to spare every demon they faced. So don’t speak to me of what Maiya could not do. She very much chose her side, and she has made it very clear she does not side with us.”

“There is some truth in that,” Riyan admitted, and a few of the Sawai lords murmured their agreement.

Tia looked down the table to where Ira sat. The Kin'jal Imperatrix hadn’t said a word all morning, which would have been shocking just a year ago.

There had been a time when no one could speak ill of the Saint in Ira's presence without receiving a stern rebuke. Coming from the ruler of the most powerful human nation, that meant something.

That time was gone, it seemed. Ira just stared at the table, looking lost and conflicted. Whether with her own decisions or with Maiya, Tia could not say.

The silence that filled the room said more about how far the Saint had fallen than any words Tia could have spoken.

She chose to evacuate several towns and villages, aiding hundreds of souls when she could end the war entirely and save hundreds of thousands.

Tia forced herself to stop thinking about that failure of a human. She’d learned there was no hope of changing Maiya’s mind when she got like this, as frustrating as it was.

“You have all been blinded by our losses,” she said, “and it is little wonder why. All across the realm, we have suffered loss after loss, and not a single reprieve in sight.”

Heads nodded all around the room. “But we have not lost this war yet. The issue is that in losing Kanthaghati Pass, we’ve allowed the invaders to scatter across the whole realm, forcing us to dilute our own forces. A few thousand here, a garrison there. The very thing that prevents them from annihilating us wholesale is what makes it impossible for us to drive them out. Is it any wonder we’ve lost so many villages and towns? Scattered, and with equal numbers, we are weaker than demons. Of course we would lose.”

“And united?” Vason asked from beside her, ever her faithful ally.

“United, and in overwhelming numbers, we bring to bear everything we learned at Kanthaghati Pass and in every battle since.” Tia stood up, slamming her fists down onto the table. “I ask you this. How many demons were at Kanthaghati? Maybe twenty thousand? And how many of us are there, across every city still standing? Every soldier, militia, and able-bodied man and woman who can hold a spear? Ten times that? Fifty?”

“Numbers we cannot hope to bring to bear,” the Altani general said, shaking his head. “That is precisely the problem. They burn our towns one at a time precisely because we cannot gather. The realm is simply too vast.”

“Then we just have to give them a target too tempting to ignore,” Tia said, meeting his gaze. “I say we empty the remaining towns and villages. We arm every soul who can hold a weapon and give them what training we’re able. Two hundred thousand in one place, having sucked the prana dry before the battle to prevent anything like Khantaghati Pass from happening again. I assure you, those are numbers no demon is prepared for. In all their history, they’ve never once fought a battle that large.”

“What of the Samsara?” someone asked. “How do we deal with their immortals?”

“By keeping them occupied,” Tia replied. “Leave that to me… and the Saint.”

“Maiya?” Riyan asked. “Youv’e secured her cooperation?”

“I will,” Tia replied confidently. “She refuses to take lives, but she knows as well as anyone that the Samsara cannot die. Nor will they lay a hand on her, being the wife of their esteemed god. Her presence alone will cause them to falter, and if she does need to fight them, well, I think I can convince her to do so.”

For the first time that morning, Tia was surrounded by expressions of something other than despair, and she’d be lying if she said it didn’t feel good.

“If it could be done,” Riyan said slowly. “But even if we managed to field an army that large, you would still need the demons to meet us there. They are no fools. Why would they stop attacking us piecemeal, where they win with ease and little danger to themselves, to face us in a field where you believe they have a higher chance of losing?”

“Oh, that? That’s the easy part,” Tia said with a smile, though there was no humor in her eyes. “They won’t be able to help themselves, you see.”

“They won’t be able to help themselves walk into a trap?” the Altani general asked, unconvinced.

“You have to remember that for all our similarities, demons are a different people. They don’t think like us,” she said.

“Yes, they believe themselves our superiors,” the general muttered, earning himself some chuckles.

“They do,” Tia replied. “Especially considering their entire society is based around battle. Which is why they won’t see it as a trap. They’ll see it as the chance to gain eternal glory. To finish us off once and for all. All it takes is one clan. Just one showing up at the battlefield, and the others will be forced to join in, lest they miss out on the glory. We won’t have to drag them to the field. They’ll come to us.”

She fell silent after that, just watching the expressions change. The despair giving way to, if not hope, then at least guarded optimism.

At the very least, they no longer felt defeat was a foregone conclusion.

There assaulted her with a hundred questions after that. Where the army would muster, who would lead each battalion, how the cities could be emptied without putting them at risk of demon attack.

All things that would be ironed out in time.

She let them argue amongst themselves, keeping her thoughts to herself. The less she said, the more weight her words bore.

Besides, while the battle was necessary, it wasn’t sufficient to end the war, no matter how badly the demons lost.

She’d promised them a way to deal with the Samsara, and while Maiya’s presence may well stall them, it would not end their threat. Only one thing could do that.

So long as the Akh Nara lived, the immortal Warriors would rise, and as long as that state of affairs continued, no human victory would ever hold.

So she would give the realm its battle. And then, while the demons reeled from the rout, Tia would win the war in earnest. She would cross into the demon lands alone, find their god, and end him.

Once and for all.
