The dragon lay coiled along the ridge, its scales fractured like glass over cooling lava. Its eyes were locked on them. So was its mouth, steam leaking through bared teeth.

"Okay, damn it, my goggles are getting fried," Barry said, tapping the side of the frame. "The heat signature's off the charts. Are you getting this? That thing's reading like the damn sun!"

Thor never passed up a fight. Right now he hadn't so much as lifted a finger. He was staring at Barry instead. "Do you know what that is?" he asked, easing one foot back.

"Yeah, I do. It's just a lizard with the power of the sun. Don't tell me the god of thunder's scared of an overcooked lizard."

"It's not just a lizard. My grandfather made it. A failed experiment, and the first of its kind." Thor's voice dropped. "It's only a matter of time before it takes its first real breath. When it does, the whole world goes with it."

"Grandpa of the year," Barry muttered. The heat was getting to him now. He jabbed the emergency button on his wrist, and the cuffs of his lab jacket sealed tight, though it would only buy him so much time.

Thor raised Mjolnir and stepped between Barry and the ridge. "Don't get any closer. I'll cover you while you slip away with your gadgets."

He needn't have bothered. Barry was already backing off, tugging his goggles into place and hunting for somewhere safe to watch and study the beast that could destroy realms. One click, and the data flooded his vision: density readouts, the nervous system traced in glowing lines, every layer of the creature magnified.

"Oh, great," he called. "The spine and skull both read denser than your divine alloy. You sure that hammer's going to do anything?"

"I'm not the god of hammers." Thor ducked another swing of its tail. "I'm the god of thunder."

The dragon's head whipped around before he could say more. One horn smashed into the ridge, and the rock burst open, spewing magma.

Thor was already in the air, hammer raised.

"Oh, I need that horn," Barry said, eyes glued to the readout. The goggles were pulling live density data, and even he was surprised by the number: ten times denser than divine alloy. "It's the strongest thing on the whole animal. Worth the risk, right?"

"Can I ask whose risk we're talking about?" Thor roared back. Both his hands were locked on the dragon's skull as it thrashed to throw him off, and the heat was burning deeper into his arms by the second. "Because I don't see you up here!"

"You're immortal. Come on, walk it off."

The dragon wasn't having it. It twisted its neck hard enough to fling Thor loose, clipped him with a wing on the way down, and swept its tail low across the ridge. Thor rolled underneath it, came up on his feet, and swung.

Boom!

The sound of Mjolnir meeting horn rolled across the valley, shaking the air and the ground alike. Rocks broke loose from the slope above and came tumbling down.

And still the horn held. Not even a chip. The dragon rounded on Thor, eyes wild.

Roooaaarrr!

The roar brought wind. No, a whole storm, shoving everything in its path aside.

Barry drove the spike of his mini-hammer into the stone. It changed as it bit, dragging earth up around itself like hot glue and anchoring him in place. Through his overlays he kept tracking the fight, the angle of every strike, the way the beast moved, and bit by bit a weakness came into focus.

"Base of the horn!" he shouted. "Left side! There's a wound that never healed. Hit it there, Thor!"

Thor poured every last bit of lightning and strength he had into his arm and struck that exact spot. The storm gathered around Mjolnir, the air itself shuddering, and when the hammer came down it wasn't a swing anymore. It was an explosion.

Boom!

The horn sheared clean off. The dragon's scream cut out, and the silence that followed was total. Unnerving. Thor stood there with his chest heaving. "It's dead."

Barry glanced at the dragon's vitals. Flat. Then, all at once, a spike.

"Thor! It's still alive!"

The warning didn't reach Thor in time. In one last, furious lunge, the dragon snapped its jaws shut around him and swallowed him whole. Then it turned its glare on Barry and began stalking toward him.

Barry didn't back away. His little hammer was already sparking with lightning, a lot like Thor's. "You want some of this?" he said, and armor unfolded from his back, pure divine alloy.

He was just about to swing when the beast toppled, eyes closing, and slid into a heap of rock and ash. After that came nothing but quiet and the clatter of loose stone still tumbling down after it.

"Oh," Barry said. The armor folded back into his jacket. He checked the vitals again, and this time they stayed flat.

He scrambled down to the carcass and got to work. He scraped off skin samples, caught the cooling blood seeping from the wound, and sealed each in its own container. He hadn't even caught his breath when the body shifted. Barry stayed where he was. His goggles showed a life signature, and it wasn't the dragon's. It was the one inside.

Thor hauled himself out of its mouth. Most of his skin had burned off, arms and face included, and his armor was charred to ruin.

Barry barely looked at him. All his attention was on the broken horn. "That's everything we need," he said. "Let's go."

Thor didn't even glance at the horn. He kept staring at the spot where the dragon had fallen, and it was a moment before he spoke. "You have what you asked for. But what about the rest of the ingredients the Bifrost needs?"

"What about them?" Barry frowned, then snapped his fingers. "Oh, right. Forgot to tell you. The bridge is healing."

"Huh. What do you mean?"

"Let's just say it's getting fixed."

"What?" Thor asked again, and this time there was an edge in his voice. "You mean we never needed to come here? I got burned for nothing!"

He walked right up to Barry, so much bigger that Barry looked like a puppy beside him.

"Thor, are you going to keep asking questions I've already answered, or are we going home?" Barry said, his divine alloy armor zipping back up over his whole body. The little hammer in his hand crackled with thunder again.

[Warning: not enough charge left.]

Barry ignored it. He kept his chin up and held that murderous stare.

Thor said nothing. A full minute went by while Barry waited him out.

Then Thor's grip tightened on Mjolnir, and slowly he hung it back on his belt. The burns across his body began to heal. "We're going home," he said.

He looked up at the sky. "Heimdall!"

The Bifrost came down around them, showering them in rainbow light.

Neither of them noticed the green shadow watching from the ridge. It had been there since they arrived.

In her chamber, ringed by glowing scrolls and darkness, just the way she liked it, Loki saw everything the shadow saw. "Brute force, as always," she muttered. "Utterly inefficient. All that ancient magic, wasted."

She'd heard the rumor in the halls of Asgard, the one about a jacket that killed on contact, and she'd wanted to see it for herself. To watch the engineer do his science magic. What she got instead was, in its own way, more interesting than the dragon. A mortal who had absorbed a trace of the Odinforce and lived. And Thor, who had always wanted nothing but solitude, now running missions with him.

"What in the name of the All-Father are you?" she murmured.

She stayed where she was. Before Barry could step into the Bifrost, she raised a hand. Light hummed, and a clone appeared at her side, same face, same signature, identical at a glance. She sent it across space and down into the valley, right behind Barry. The Bifrost's light was just beginning to wrap around him when the clone's hand reached for his shoulder.

"Don't," Barry said, without turning around.

The fingers touched fabric.

The jacket didn't react like a weapon. It simply registered that the contact was unauthorized, and that was all it needed to bring death to whoever had touched it. Before Barry could turn, the Bifrost carried them home to Asgard.

Thor turned at the flash. "What happened?"

Barry could still feel where the hand had landed. The signature lingered, tangled up with a residue of Asgardian magic, but for now he didn't care.

"Nothing. Look." He pointed at the bridge, smooth and shining again.

Thor's blue eyes went wide. The last time he'd seen that stretch of the bridge, it had looked beyond repair. Now it was whole, and dwarves were sweeping ash from a spot that by rights should still have been a crater.

"You lied to me?" The warmth had drained out of his voice, and Barry heard it. "Didn't you say it would take nine damn days?"

Barry turned to face him and stepped in close, his gaze sharp but steady. "I have a lot of respect for you, Thor. More than that, honestly. For you, your father, this whole kingdom. But respect isn't obedience."

"What are you trying to say?"

"Listen up. I don't answer to anyone but myself." He looked back at the bridge and nodded. "Doing the repairs myself was beneath me, so I gave the dwarves specs and a proper procedure. And see? It's done and running. Faster than waiting on me."

Thor's head dipped, the start of a nod, and then he stopped himself. His glare eased, just slightly, and Barry noticed. "So now you know. You get what I'm saying?"

This time Thor nodded. "I do. You don't have to follow me, but the All-Father, you need—"

Barry hefted the horn and started walking. "Okay, okay. I'll listen to your dear old dad. But listen to me more, Thor. Me. Your life will be a lot less complicated, I promise." He was already heading for his workshop.

"Hmm," Thor grunted, eyes dropping to Mjolnir. For once he didn't reach for it. He used his head instead. "I shall inform the All-Father that the bridge is secure."

He paused. "And, Barry," he called. "Something touched you. You don't have to hide it, but it may be deadlier than the dragon. I'm warning you!"

"Doesn't matter!" Barry shouted back, not turning around. "Whatever it was, it's lying on the ground dead by now!"

But his jacket had already taken a sample from that touch, and it was quietly working out what it was.

***

His workshop was exactly as he'd left it. The floor readout hadn't budged: absolute zero.

He lowered the horn into a green fluid bath wired to a board of chips that ran on magic instead of electricity, then dropped into the chair at his desk and started paging through book number one hundred in his arcane-coded stack.

A blue panel flickered into the corner of his vision.

[Congratulations. Bridge repair complete. Reward incoming.]

He closed it without looking. He hadn't asked for a system, and he'd never agreed to be hooked up to one. He sure wasn't going to let some background process decide what counted as a reward for work he'd already been paid for in materials, either. If it wanted his attention, it could earn it. Until then, it was just noise.

He crossed to the board where he'd been building a chart that nobody in the nine realms could have deciphered. Aesir script cross-referenced against a fragment of Heimdall's spear. The bridge patch notes. The waveform of Thor's lightning, cleaned up and laid flat. The Bifrost's own resonant signature, pulled apart into its base frequencies.

He set them side by side and studied them. Four readings, four different methods, all pointing back to one source. In a sense, it was all magic, just filtered four ways. He wrote a single word in the center of the board and circled it twice.

Yggdrasil.

"The tree. It's the only theory that makes sense." He drew a line from the word to the drawing of Odin. "It pulls something in from every being across the nine realms. Belief. Attention. Whatever people want to call it. Then the tree turns that into output, and as far as I can tell, that output has exactly one receiver." He looked at the drawing. "Odin."

But by his own theory, that shouldn't be possible. No living being, not even a god, could carry that much weight or power. And yet here was the proof. Odin had built a kingdom on top of that conversion without ever asking what it cost. Everything has a price, and a conversion on this scale most of all.

Barry sat with that for a moment. Faith had stopped being a metaphor here. It was literal fuel, and Odin had been burning through it for centuries without once checking the load.

"There's some filtering built into the tree," he said. "Some rule embedded in its structure from when it grew. It's still not enough."

He wrote it down anyway: Yggdrasil throughput. Unaddressed. Flagged for later.

It wasn't his concern right now. Today's problem was already taking shape on the bench in front of him: the horn, cooling in its bath, slowly losing its curve and settling into the flat planes of a rectangular housing.

He lifted it out and seated the lava-forged crystals inside, still bathed in Bifrost light. At last, something that could hold a charge this size. He fitted a trigger, then a small, stable screen run on the magic chips.

"Prototype one, complete," he said, adjusting the settings. "I need to get around this place without Thor or Heimdall clocking every exit. If I can push energy through this thing, it should resonate close to the Bifrost frequency. And then, bye-bye permissions, bye-bye gates, bye-bye taking orders."
