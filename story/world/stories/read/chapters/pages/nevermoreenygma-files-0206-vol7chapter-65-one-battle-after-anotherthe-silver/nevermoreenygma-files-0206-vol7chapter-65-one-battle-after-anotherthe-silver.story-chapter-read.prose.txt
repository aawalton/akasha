Chapter 65

One Battle After Another – The Silver Witch

Part 2

After a few weeks spent quelling the disturbances, Mai was sent to the mountains of Changbai.

The mountain range, white and enclosed, now functioned as a trap: the valleys retained sound, the fog played with visibility, and evacuation routes were scarce.

Reports spoke of hundreds—not dozens—of a new type of fractus, collapsing checkpoints and devouring supply lines.

Mai arrived at dawn with a group of Nevermore agents, now turned soldiers. Their task was to contain and help the troops in place, delay, and collect cores for study. Everything else came afterward. They were quickly assigned to various smaller squadrons. They must have been more than used to changes and new situations, because there wasn't much of a ceremony. The fact that there were already feys there helped a little with that.

The deployed formation were Mai was two companies in green exoskeletons, a fey platoon specialized in nocturnal maneuvers dressed in black, three field mages in blue, two aeons in high-speed quadrupedal bodies for reconnaissance and retreat, and mobile railgun and Tesla coil teams. Among the heavy equipment, Mai encountered a familiar face from previous missions—Dmitri, always tinkering with the heavy gear.

Never before had they anticipated an engagement on such a scale in such enclosed mountainous terrain. The chief aeon’s plan was to cut the swarm into narrow corridors, force them through electric “grids,” and strike with concentrated arcs when they materialized on the new point.

The fractus arriving at Changbai were not copies of those seen before. They were small to medium-sized, with faceted—crystalline—surfaces that refracted individual electric arcs, and they had a mass-jumping ability that allowed them to cross ravines like bursts of energy. Never still, they moved like a swarm with tacit coordination: advancing in bands, probing points with larger “leaders” directing the groups.

They were like irregular polyhedral volumes, like broken crystals that never quite close. And they had a kind of communication with their counterparts, extending nodes from the parts that passed for vertices in their geometry. When they connected in those points, it reminded Mai so much of ants communicating with each other by touching their antennae, and at the same time, those node diagrams she had seen the Aeons use so many times when they ran probabilistic simulations on the battlefield.

Mai mentally nicknamed them “Shards” for how they glimmered when sunlight struck them among the snow. The air fractured into prisms. Each ray bounced off their bodies, turning the valley into a lethal kaleidoscope.

The first clash for her occurred at a mountain pass, where one of the aeons had ordered the companies to set up a new sensor grid. Mai positioned at the edge of the slope with her reconnaissance unit. Her echolocation, reinforced by magnetic sensors, provided a real advantage: detecting movements before three-dimensional appearances emerged. When the first block emerged from the fog, its profile rippled like a wave: it appeared, flashed facets, split, and reappeared through another crack. Fast. Miles of combat, condensed into seconds.

The tactic worked in fractions. The mages placed rhythmic condensers—field markers—while engineers and Dmitri deployed coils for the big weapons. The idea wasn’t to use a single discharge, but a pattern: push the swarm through the grid, saturate the volume with multiple arcs, and create enough interference to extract the cores from their three-dimensional projections. Railguns fired projectiles wrapped in arcs on the sides, arc pistols launched synchronized micro-discharges for the small ones, and the mages synchronized pulses with invocations that channeled and amplified air conductivity.

The first waves suffered, many “Shards” exploded in cascades of light, leaving trembling cores similar to quartz on the snow. But the swarm learned in real-time. A pattern emerged: the formations refracted the arcs, separating impacts so no single discharge carried enough coherence to destabilize their forms and directing saturation back against the devices.

A temporary aid in an operation of that kind arrived in a ship at midday, gliding down from the sky in the middle of a storm that was just starting. The ship's hatch opened, and Rein and a dozen witches on tactical attack brooms, which basically looked like brooms, but were actually modern hovercraft vehicles, took to the skies to join the battle.

Rein, from the rear along the so-called field witches, opened controlled micro-storms to fragment the enemy’s attention, while the maneuvering feys and human soldiers used the distraction to fire at point-blank range. Mai, moving among the debris, marked safe corridors for the railgun snipers. Each recovered core was immediately sealed in armored containers: for some reason, command was interested in these fractium cores for analysis and reverse-engineering.

During the brief respite—barely long enough to finish a couple of cans of beans for lunch—Mai had the chance to catch up with Rein about how things were going on the other fronts, but the situation was no different. The group of witches was unfamiliar to her, yet they welcomed her warmly. Chaos reigned across almost every battlefield. Rein looked exhausted, though she was doing her best not to let it show.

The halfling dragon had become a key asset in operations all over the world thanks to her mastery over storms. Even so, Rein admitted that she disliked cold-weather missions, since the atmospheric conditions made it far more difficult to generate storms within the necessary parameters. Warm climates and tropical latitudes were much easier to work with. To compensate for the cold, the witches played an important role. Besides using magic, they employed electrical weapons to seed the clouds and subtly alter local weather conditions, though creating the right environment for electrical storms in winter demanded exhausting coordination and constant effort.

Mai didn't have many stories to tell. Almost every mission had been the same: strike, infiltrate, halt enemy advances, or assist in evacuation zones. Aside from the countless times she had nearly lost her life, there was nothing particularly exciting to recount. What truly seemed to interest Rein, however, was Mai's state of mind. Her hair had turned completely white, as white as the surrounding snow, and that could easily be a sign of accumulated stress.

There wasn't much else to say. Shutting down her emotions was simply the best way to survive. Given everything she had been through, silver-white hair was the most natural thing. In fact, she would have been far more worried if nothing about her had changed. The stress of the war manifested itself in different ways for everyone. In Mai's case, she had noticed it when her menstrual cycle, normally every two months, stretched to three. Her antennae would also twitch on their own while she slept, and sometimes she thought she could hear distant explosions echoing in her ears, making her shudder just before drifting off. Eventually, she had grown used to it. Rein experienced something similar, except with her tail. At times it twitched uncontrollably, and more than once she had jolted awake in the middle of the night, leaping out of bed on instinct.

Fortunately, everyone else seemed to still be alive somewhere out there. Communications had improved compared to the early days of the war, but with battles raging across the globe, it was still easy to lose contact with someone for weeks at a time.

Rein, on the other hand, was worried because her mother had joined Nevermore over the past few months to help on battlefields around the world as well. It did little to ease her mind, even knowing that her mother was a seasoned veteran of the hidden world's conflicts.

Mai had never actually met Lizbeth, but after hearing so many stories about her over the years, she had no trouble believing it. Even so, she did her best to reassure her friend.

At last, once the cans were empty and the various squads received their recall orders, it was time to move out once again.

The second phase changed the equation. From among the masses emerged a “mother column”: a larger specimen, like a larger prism nearly eight meters tall, with geometric patterns on its shell resembling a honeycomb.

It didn’t seem alone: hundreds of smaller ones reconfigured into a mobile wall around it connecting nodes in perpetual movement, giving the appearance of a huge crystal covered with hundreds of smaller pieces from which crystal needles or thorns sprouted. One of the aeons recalculated and ordered a tactical retreat, its quadrupedal body entered the line like a cat, pushing blocks aside and carrying charges to support the condensers. Unleashing so much emotion on the battlefield without controlling it wasn't a good thing for the Aeons, but it happened so quickly that no one really had time to object. The maneuver enabled a concentrated counterattack: ringed electric charges synchronized with incapacitating detonations. The objective was to force the mother column to expose itself and extract its core.

The column responded. Its refraction pattern became more complex: it absorbed part of the electricity, redirected it in short pulses, and countered with micro-shards fractus that exploded in cascades. One of the companies lost many soldiers, and the snow on the mountain pass darkened. Smaller fractium fragments erupted into the air, the cold did nothing to soften the brutality of the combat on both parts.

What saved the operation was the combination of variables: the high rate of fire from railguns on elevated points, the aerial coverage of the witches with Rein, the squads preventing surprise charges, and the contribution of the field witches and mages directing conductive arcs toward the mother column. A small fractus nearly split Mai's head open, but she managed to blow it up before it could recover. She was left with an ugly scar on her nose, but she would have to continue with that operation while the tissue regenerated.

A coordinated strike in the exact sequence caused a fracture: the mother column yielded, its three-dimensional projection collapsed, and its core was exposed. Rein delivered a targeted amplified discharge with her tail, manipulating the lightning bolts from the clouds, the core emitted a luminous breath and then fell inert.

They had halted the advance. Hundreds of Shards and dozens of cores lay scattered across the valley along the corpses, bearing witness to the intensity of the battle. The cost had been steep: damaged vehicles, twenty-two confirmed losses, many more wounded. Mai, leaning on her rifle, noticed the aeon synchronizing data to extract patterns from the swarm’s behavior. That information would be forwarded to several research centers—likely ZAIEN—for modeling and weapon refinement. The recovered fractium was packed with top priority.

In the after-action report, the aeons synthesized the operational conclusion: the new type exploited refraction and energy redistribution to resist isolated discharges, only a multi-vector strike—coordinated sound, electricity, and magic—could destabilize it. Changbai had shown that, at this scale, war meant attrition and synchrony: not just hitting harder, but hitting on time, from different vectors, until the higher-order form could no longer maintain its shadow in three dimensions.

Mai stayed a little longer looking at the mountain range, watching Rein and the witches fly toward the ship, heading somewhere else, as the skies finally cleared of clouds.

There were no celebrations. Only checks: secured cores, triaged wounded, evacuation routes established. They had stopped the wave at Changbai, but the war continued.

Above her head, the enormous fragment—more broken each day—now stretched into a line that, even in daylight, could be seen with the naked eye.

It had been breaking apart, but had not fallen.

Not yet at least.

December 2096

Mai arrived late to the meeting she’d been summoned to. Not by much—just five minutes.Not because she wanted to, but because she had learned that if you arrived punctually to a command briefing, you inevitably left with more work than if you showed up just a bit late.

As an excuse, she put on her helmet to give the impression she had been doing something, even though she was wearing only work pants and boots, without the rest of the gear. Just a top with the shirt, that she button up to give a more serious look. Her now long again silver hair tied in a braid.

She was at the Joint Forces base in Dalian and had made her way to the Ground Command building. She’d been summoned to the third floor, where she had no idea what they did.

A drop of sweat slid down her cheek as she checked at the lobby where she had to go.

Administration and General Services Office.

Had she screwed something up? She didn’t recall doing anything wrong. A couple of days ago, her squad had helped themselves to an extra box of chocolates, but she seriously doubted they would call her in over that. It could also have been because they'd had to dismantle a railgun in the middle of a battle the previous month to recalibrate it, but the report clearly explained why it had been necessary. If anyone was going to get chewed out, it should've been the captain, not her. A few months earlier, they had also hijacked a satellite link to watch some newly released movies, catch up on the news, and see how the rest of the world was doing—but they hadn't left a single trace of the hack. Besides, she hadn't actually done anything.

The corridor of the joint base smelled like ozone and burnt coffee. Screens covered every wall, displaying tactical maps, unit distribution, performance data. And, to her misfortune, her name appeared more often than she liked in few lists.

Corporal Izumi, Mai — Field Operations, 2094–2096: 71 confirmed missions, 43 high-risk, 0 abandonments. She read and went up stairs to the office she was expected.

Lovely. I’m a statistic now. Mai could already see where this was going and she lowered her head.

The door opened before she could open it, or back away. As a result, it hit her forehead and nose.

“Uh… sorry.”

Mai rubbed her nose and looked at the soldier with a scowl. “All right...” she said grumpily.

“Please enter,” said a soldier, who ushered her in and he remained outside after closed the door.

Inside, an oval table. Three older human generals, a male fey commander… with translucent wings? And an esper, the kind who always looked like they saw more than they should. Fortunately, they were easy to identify. A few months earlier, the military had introduced special insignia for the different type and abilities, so espers and feys now wore distinctive markings on their uniforms.

All of them stared at her as if she were a lab specimen that had learned to walk.

“Corporal Izumi, rest” said the central general, with a voice made of granite. “Sit, please.”

She obeyed, trying not to show how much she hated being there.

She knew why they had called her: that absurd military habit of rewarding those who didn’t die.

The fey commander, stone-faced and blue-eyed, slid a tablet across the table.

“Your performance has been… very notable. In the last twenty-four months, you have participated in thirteen active fronts. Reports from everyone who has worked with you are consistent: natural leadership, tactical precision, zero unnecessary casualties.”

“I don’t perform miracles,” Mai replied, staring at the edge of the table. “I just try not to die.”

“That, Izumi, is a miracle,” the esper interjected, smiling in a way she deeply disliked. “That’s why the Joint Council proposes your promotion to Field Major.”

Mai felt a chill run down her spine. Not again.

Major. That word sounded like a marble coffin.

No. No. No. No. No. Nope.

“With all due respect,” she said “I think there’s been a mistake. I’m not qualified for… that rank.”

The three generals exchanged looks. One of them, the oldest-looking—gray beard, yellow-tinted glasses—cleared his throat.

“You have more combat hours than half a training division, and you’ve coordinated mixed units. Frankly, there is no mistake, Izumi. You are already, de facto, an operational commander. Frankly, you should have been promoted several ranks already, practically since the beginning of the war.”

Mai smiled with that polite expression that, in her language, meant I’m about to jump out the window. May I? Please? Let me go.

She knew what happened with high ranks: names recorded, files opened, histories reviewed. And her history… well, wasn’t exactly a gardening club.

She had done things. Things that the reports didn’t include, and Jinat had hidden, just as Leon and Rein had concealed certain details from her Nevermore file. Things the living preferred to forget.

At one time, she had fired from the shadows in Wishper, even sending two presidents to the other side: one a sadistic tyrant, the other a traitor disguised as a reformer. Not to mention corrupt ministers, judges, and board directors. And yes… several thousand assholes she had encountered in her life. And though that had been decades ago, humans had an innate talent for stirring up the shit until they found gold.

So she smiled, lowered her head, and said as convincingly as she could: “With all due respect, sirs… I’d prefer to remain a soldier.”

Silence.

The oldest general studied her over his glasses, then looked at the others and let out a sigh. “Is it humility or fear, Izumi? No one will say a word. You deserve it.”

She didn’t answer. It was neither. It was pure survival instinct.

The meeting ended with the promise that they would “review her case,” which, translated from bureaucratic-speak, meant she was already promoted—she just didn’t know it yet.

Hours later, in one of the many hangars serving as a workshop, Jinat was waiting, leaning against a pile of supplies and smoking. She was dressed as if ready for combat, exoskeleton and all. Didn’t she ever take it off, even to go to the bathroom?

Mai had arrived the day before, but she hadn’t expected her old mentor and former boss from Wishper to be around these parts. They had talked about battlefields, but Mai had a nagging suspicion that Jinat had nudged a few strings behind the scenes.

“You did it, didn’t you?” Mai said, grumbling, helmet tucked under her arm.

Jinat smiled, amused. She had that way of looking at her that always made Mai feel as if she were reading her soul without asking permission.

“I knew it…”

“You refused again?” she asked.

Mai let her helmet drop onto a crate. “I didn’t refuse. I just… didn’t accept. It’s different.”

Jinat tilted her head. “Being this humble is also a sin. You’ve always been like that. Even in Wishper, you didn’t want to move up, despite how good you were.”

“I don’t like leading people.”

“But you have, in some missions at least.”

“Not because I enjoy it.”

“Because you fear someone will die under your command…”

“You know if I have to take a weapon, I will. I just don’t like sending people to die on my orders. Is that wrong?”

“No. But in war, feelings don’t matter. Only those who survive and are capable. I told you the first time we met, remember?”

“I don’t want bosses or subordinates,” she said. “I just want to keep my head on straight until this is over.”

Jinat looked at her seriously. “If that’s what you want, you need rank. Without it, some idiot with fewer neurons than bullets can send you to the front without backup.”

Mai stared at her for a long moment, saying nothing. She was right, damn it.

Finally, she sighed, exhaling the exhaustion of two years of war.

“Fine. But no grand titles. A middle rank. Something that lets me survive without becoming a bronze statue.”

Jinat nodded, almost amused. “Done. Captain Mai Izumi.”

She muttered something that sounded like a curse and put on her helmet.

“Great. Now even the dead will salute me.”

She walked toward the platform, murmuring to herself: “I just hope nobody starts writing songs.”

Jinat raised her voice slightly to be heard. “When this is over, there won’t be enough musicians to sing all the shit we’ve done!”

Mai smiled bitterly, not believing it.

“Haven’t you heard?!” Jinat asked.

Mai half-turned without stopping. “Heard what?”

“Your nickname.”

“What nickname?”

“They call you the Silver Witch.”

Mai turned and kept walking. “I’m not a witch!”

“No! You’ll always be Mai, the moth-fairy. Moth-Mai!” Jinat laughed.

Mai continued, shaking her head, but she smiled. It was good to see that even in this war there were people whose spirit didn’t change. She just hoped that in the end she could say the same about herself.

The skies over the western Pacific were heavy with storm clouds and tension.

At the beginning of 2097, Mai stood aboard a Seventh Fleet destroyer, watching the sea churn unnaturally as Taketomi Island slowly emerged on the horizon.

The morning mist barely revealed the outline of the island, but the sensors confirmed what the reports had already warned: a new type of fractus had appeared in the region, lurking among the straits and coral reefs separating Japan from Taiwan. The island had been partially evacuated, yet the creatures' movements remained completely unpredictable.

Mai was accompanied by Nerea and Miria, now both Senior Sergeants.

The three had been assigned as liaisons between the naval forces and the mermaid patrols monitoring the surrounding waters.

Although...

Technically, Mai had absolutely no business being there.

To put it simply...

Someone had screwed up.

And by the time they realized it, it was already too late.

For some reason, someone had mistaken her echolocation ability for that of a marine-type fey. Assuming it was just another routine deployment, she had boarded the VTOL the moment orders arrived from Hainan Base. She hadn't asked any questions... and neither had anyone else when they saw the captain's insignia on her uniform among the soldiers boarding the aircraft carrier PM Samuel Johnson.

It wasn't until she disembarked that she realized the mistake.

Still, she could at least make herself useful.

Better yet, she had found Miria and Nerea aboard one of the destroyers. The three barely managed to hold back their tears as they embraced in front of the crew who looked at them as if they had just witnessed the beginning of a yuri romance in the shape of a field of flowers blooming in the middle of the ocean. Although, well, they didn't have that kind relationship. It had been an incredibly long time since they had last seen each other.

As always, Nerea showed almost no emotion, but the tip of her nose had turned red against her pale skin, which was enough to betray her feelings. Miria, meanwhile, was concealing her true form as usual, revealing only the spiral horn protruding from her forehead. Mai couldn't help imagining how striking she would look with her full centaur body and iridescent wings.

After the reunion, it was time to focus on the situation at hand.

The mermaids, equipped with tactical combat suits, had identified patterns in the movements of the fractus. They were long, eel-like creatures called Sunstone covered in coral-like armor plates, capable of swimming underwater at speeds comparable to modern torpedoes before suddenly breaching the surface to attack ships.

Some possessed tentacles that seemed to merge seamlessly with the surrounding sea, allowing them to generate dense curtains of fog that distorted radar signals once they surfaced.

Apparently, they possessed a specialized organ that worked almost like a steam boiler. It expelled superheated water under enormous pressure, and the instant it came into contact with the frigid winter air, the sudden temperature difference caused it to condense into thick banks of fog. The mist stretched only a few meters above the surface and lingered for several minutes, giving the unsettling impression that the fleet was sailing across a sea of clouds.

From the destroyer's bridge, Mai watched the first waves appear on the horizon, tracing glowing, sinuous arcs reminiscent of enormous mutant jellyfish before disappearing behind those drifting curtains of mist.

Every collision with the fleet triggered localized explosions.

Pontoons were torn away.

Gun turrets were disabled.

Sailors were wounded.

Their bodies looked flexible, but those coral plates were more than capable of ripping through a ship's hull.

Conventional defenses proved inadequate, and the possibility that those creatures might reach Okinawa—or even Taiwan—increased the pressure with every passing minute.

Blanketing the sea with electrical attacks wasn't a viable solution either. Controlled detonations could help, but localized EMP weapons couldn't be deployed safely while so many friendly vessels operated within the area.

The tactical plan was divided into several phases.

First, the mermaids would establish an early warning perimeter using sonic pulses and bioelectric signals to mark safe navigation routes.

Well...

In theory, Captain Mai was supposed to coordinate that phase.

Nope. That definitely wasn't going to happen.

She was there because of an administrative mistake, and her original plan was simply to observe the operation until someone found a ship willing to take her back to base.

Or at least, that had been the plan.

One of the senior officers eventually yelled at her for standing around doing nothing, so she ended up helping coordinate the operation alongside Miria and Nerea anyway.

Under the command of one of their own captains, the mermaids deployed the underwater surveillance perimeter while the combined fleets of Japan, China, and the United States formed a containment cordon. Assault vessels began firing railguns and concentrated electrical arcs in an attempt to neutralize the fractus' tentacles and coral armor.

Mai and her friends moved between destroyers and frigates aboard hovercraft, constantly repositioning the mages and elemental feys responsible for amplifying the electrical assaults.

Before long, the battle devolved into a chaotic dance across the sea.

The marine fractus vanished beneath the waves only to reappear seconds later somewhere else, opening breaches that left entire ships exposed.

Mai watched one group erupt almost directly beneath the bow of a cruiser.

Their translucent bodies reflected the winter sunlight while the sky was still covered with clouds, while blasts of energy erupted from their mouths, disrupting the vessel's electrical systems.

One of the mermaids signaled to Mai.

Using sonic weapons, the fleet began what the sailors jokingly referred to as underwater herding. By creating acoustic corridors, they guided the fractus toward predetermined kill zones where destroyers and cruisers waited with their railguns ready.

The tactic worked—at least partially.

Several creatures were obliterated, and the resulting explosions sent violent tremors through the destroyer's deck.

The survivors quickly adapted.

They vanished beneath newly created curtains of fog before diving once more into the depths.

Fortunately, the submerged mermaid squads were already waiting. Armed with sonic weapons of their own, they prevented the creatures from escaping by driving them back toward the surface, where the fleet could engage them again.

Then, amid the chaos, something unexpected changed the course of the battle.

From high above, the most experienced mages aboard one of the Chinese frigates summoned colossal elemental lightning dragons to strike any fractus that surfaced.

It was a desperate but carefully calculated measure.

Electric elemental magic performed poorly during the harsh winter season, and maintaining such powerful summons placed an enormous burden on their casters.

Even so, the gamble paid off.

The serpentine dragons unleashed concentrated thunderstorms that transformed the ocean's surface into a vast field of incandescent lightning.

The marine fractus were struck by direct electrical discharges. Some exploded into brilliant flashes of fractium mixed with seawater. Others absorbed part of the energy before convulsing violently and diving back beneath the waves.

Mai remained on the front line, coordinating the combined assault.

The mermaids guided the fleet from below.

The mages concentrated their lightning from above.

Meanwhile, Mai, Miria, and Nerea relayed firing solutions to the railgun batteries, directing their fire toward every large fractus that broke the surface.

The waves never stopped coming.

Despite their overwhelming power, even the dragons eventually became targets.

Tentacles erupted from the sea at impossible speeds, striking the summons one after another.

Some dragons fell outright.

Others merged with the very storms they had created, dragging nearby fractus into massive explosions and leaving enormous halos of light suspended above the raging sea.

The battle lasted for hours.

Every movement made by the fractus resembled that of an apex marine predator, exploiting every weakness in the fleet: broken formations, gaps in the electrical perimeter, or the moments when the mages were forced to recharge their condensers.

In the end, however, the coordination between the underwater forces and the surface fleet proved decisive.

The mages redirected electrical energy through floating cable networks while synchronized sonic pulses timed each detonation to coincide with the appearance of the largest fractus.

The final wave emerged from the central reef off Taketomi Island.

The fleet saturated the area with concentrated electrical fire, launching railgun rounds enveloped in crackling arcs of energy.

When the last fractus finally fell, the sea slowly began to calm.

The remains of the creatures dissolved into floating fragments of fractium, too light to sink, and were immediately collected for analysis.

Victory had come at a price.

The lightning dragons did not survive and several mages had to be evacuated to the medical wings. Multiple ships suffered heavy damage. And coordinating the operation had pushed everyone involved to their limits.

But the advance toward Okinawa and Taiwan had been stopped.

Amid the smoke and sea spray, Mai watched the mermaids, still wearing their tactical suits, gathering the fractium cores while smiling despite their exhaustion.

Fighting with sonic weapons in their own element couldn't have been easy, even with the specialized equipment designed to shield them from the acoustic panels they carried. After all, sound propagated far more efficiently underwater.

At least the waters were finally free of the threat.

Otherwise, the marine ecosystem throughout the region would have been wiped out.

As for the mermaids...

They had been fortunate.

Their insulated tactical suits had performed flawlessly, preventing them from being electrocuted.

Courtesy of ZAIEN.

Later after being yelled at a couple of times—and wondering why the hell Hainan was scolding her over a deployment she wasn't even supposed to be part of—Mai finally boarded a ship back to base. But she was happy to see Nerea and Miria again.

And so another year began.

Although some fronts had started to stabilize, the fractus remained a constant threat.

She also received news from her friends scattered across the world.

Their situations were much the same.

Jinat was now fighting in Russia.

Milena and Sari were safe and had joined the newly reorganized Seventh Pacific Fleet, where they had finally reunited with Miria and Nerea.

Aika remained in Tokyo, as always, ready in case anything happened.

The others...

Were scattered across every corner of the world.
