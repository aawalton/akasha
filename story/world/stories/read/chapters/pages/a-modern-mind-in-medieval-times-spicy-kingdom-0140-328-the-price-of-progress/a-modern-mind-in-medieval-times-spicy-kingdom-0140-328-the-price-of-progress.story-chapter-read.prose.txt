In the workshop, we introduced the Ducklings to the lathe.

They watched in open fascination as the cannon’s heavy bronze body revolved on it. The cutting bit slowly dug into the breech with a hiss, peeling away in thin curls of bronze.

We gave the breech a slight taper, then set the youngsters to polishing it smooth.

Giving the cartridge the exactly matching taper turned out to be more challenging.

The brass buckled in immediately.

“Work hardened,” Kovar complained, turning the ruined case in his hands.

“Hmm…”

“Why don’t you just push it in?” Frida asked innocently.

“What?”

“Heat the cartridge just enough and then push it into the breech. It will take the shape on its own.”

That could work.

I was too attached to the cannon to think of it as a sizing die.

We pushed a heated cartridge into the breech, using a mandrel from the muzzle to push the walls outward.

The cartridge resisted at first.

We kept pushing,

A sudden jolt inward.

It crumpled like parchment.

We tried again, this time heating a cartridge for a few seconds less.

Slow, constant force.

It slid inside.

It took some effort to pull it out, but nothing compared to the first one.

I shoved it in and out multiple times. As it cooled, it became more easy.

Satisfied, we filled the cartridge, took the cannon out into the valley a safe distance away, then fired it.

As the mountains echoed with the shot, I unscrewed the breech, hooked the extractor on the cartridge and pulled.

A bit of resistance, then it slid out easily.

I drew a sigh of relief.

“Good work,” I said, patting the thin girl on the shoulder, as pink crawled up her neck.

The cannon’s breech wasn’t a proper sizing die. We’d have to make a steel version for that, and one shot wasn’t enough to prove the design’s reliability.

But, we now had a clear path forward.

I’m definitely not telling Aprilia about this, or I’ll be hearing about it forever.

At the same time, the blacksmiths finished a set of ball mills to replace the one that had almost killed me.

One of the mills was to be reserved for grinding nothing but black powder, filled with brass balls this time. Not only that, I had ordered the men to build a separate building away from the workshops to house it, and surround the building with berms.

“Why brass?” Kovar asked. “Lead would’ve been just fine. You brought so much of it.”

“No,” I replied. “Lead is too soft. The balls will grind themselves down and contaminate the powder. I don’t want my best marksmen going mad just to save a few coins.”

Besides, I had brought tons of lead for other purposes besides ammunition.

I told some of the smiths to begin turning a few of its ingots into sheets, large enough to cover the walls of a small room.

The sky roared, announcing the arrival of rain.

A moment later, rain began drumming against the roof.

I frowned.

If the soldiers had to fight in this weather, the rifles could not be relied upon, despite all their firepower.

Those little umbrellas on the priming pans helped, but they did not make them all-weather weapons.

That would require a lot more chemistry.

Time to produce some sulfuric acid.

A message arrived from Master Wolmar that his men had finished the final boat, along with my modifications.

“Who wants to visit the Bay with me?” I asked the Ducklings.

Aldren’s hand shot up immediately.

I suppressed a smile and nodded.

Really eager to fly, this one. Best if he at least knew the principles.

As we left the industrial district, I slowed down.

The forest around it had almost vanished, save for the line of trees behind the palisade. Just thick enough to keep it hidden from anyone looking inside.

Dozens of pine and cedar trees had been cleared away, large and small, leaving stumps behind.

I sighed.

We had been cutting trees for construction and fuel nonstop ever since we had arrived. There were only a few thousand of us and the valley vast, so it wasn’t in any danger of ecological collapse.

Yet.

But I planned to turn Chadom into an industrial powerhouse, with bigger furnaces, forges and more power hungry machines.

If we kept consuming wood at our current pace, I feared we might strip the whole valley bare within a few decades. There was coal we could bring in from Nobhart, but it cost money to both dig and transport. And right now, we didn’t have the manpower to focus on both military and economic expansion.

After lunch at the castle, we headed towards the Bay.

As our carriage rolled out of the settlement, the wheels rattled over the newly paved section of road.

Dozens of men moved aside to let us pass, their shirts clinging to them with sweat, their backs bent from fatigue.

Hyde’s sixty supporters.

Pairs of soldiers wielding repeating crossbows stood at intervals, watching over them.

A reminder that they had been shown mercy, not forgiveness.

Mounds of stone and gravel lined the roadside. The men had already begun paving the worst parts of the road that had turned to mud in the rain. Without it, hauling stone, wood and water between the quarry, the Bay and Cradle would become very slow.

If we had the time and manpower, I would have had them make something like a roman road. A proper foundation with drainage. But Chadom had more immediate needs than roads so well made they could survive thousands of years.

Salt laden damp air announced that we had reached the Bay. The fragrant smell of pine tar accompanied it.

The immigrants were working on the four insulae with more gusto than before, with some of the Cha working along them. Hammers rang against timber, as men shouted instructions.

The eyes that fell upon me weren’t as suspicious anymore.

Their sharp-eyed leader arrived the moment I crossed the buildings.

“Master Wolmar, how are you doing?” I asked.

“Well, my lord,” he said, his lips curved in a smile. “The people are thankful to you for giving us such well-made houses.”

“They’ve earned them with their hard work.”

I looked at the harbor below us.

Well-built docks lined the shore, with piers jutting out into the water at regular intervals, sturdy enough to receive large ships. Three of mine were already there, rocking against the lines. A dozen fishing boats dotted the harbor farther out.

My nose itched.

The smell of pine was overpowering. I looked to the side. Men were stirring a thick black-brown sludge in an iron vat over a fire, not far away from the newly completed boat.

It had a mast, just as I had ordered.

“There’s just finishing up, my lord,” Wolmar said nervously. “A final coat of pitch. Thank you for providing it to us without charge. We… uh, didn’t expect you this soon.”

I waved him away.

“It’s fine.”

We had literal tons of pine tar left from producing ungodly amount of wood we turned into charcoal, that our furnaces and forges ate every day. The first thing I had done after figuring out cast iron had been to make condensers for the coal-makers. It would have been stupid to lose something this valuable.

I walked up to the men, my nostrils feeling like they were being forced open. Pine was one of my favorite scents, but I suppose ‘too much of a good thing’ was an adage for a reason. The pitch folded sluggishly around the wooden paddles with each turn.

The two men attending it straightened up when they noticed us arriving and bowed clumsily. They had thick beards and leathery skin, hardened by working under the sun for years.

Similar looking liquid that comes out of the ground. Thick and syrupy.
