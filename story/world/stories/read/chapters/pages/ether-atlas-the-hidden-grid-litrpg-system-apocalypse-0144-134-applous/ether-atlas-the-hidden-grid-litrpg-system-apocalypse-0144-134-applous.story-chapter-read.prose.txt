Chapter 134

I asked the captain what he feared on a crossing. He did not say the weather, nor the rocks, nor what swims beneath a hull. He named the bread. A crew that has eaten well, he said, stops watching the sea. — Codex of the Long Watch, Coastal Observation, the Saronic

Eleni pressed another parcel down into the mouth of the pack and watched with a smile as the bag declined to look any fuller.

She reached back to the table without looking, found a waxed bundle the size of a brick, and posted that into the bag as well. The canvas swallowed it without a bulge or complaint. Eleni peered into the mouth, unconvinced that anything could hold that much.

“It is a good bag. Sarah. This is a good bag,” she announced.

“Yes, Mamá, it’s magic. Now take out half of everything you have put in. We will be gone for a day, maybe two.”

“Well, it's not magic as the word implies,” Steve said, hovering over Eleni's shoulder.

“Would you like to explain Ether and folded space to her right now?” said Sarah.

“Oh, I don’t mind. It’s quite fascinating.”

“We don’t have time, Steve. Magic bag will have to do for now,” Donna said from the doorway.

The bag so far held bread, hard cheese wrapped in cloth, a jar of oil under a wooden bung, olives swimming in brine, a coil of loukániko, three lemons and a tin of something whose label Arthur couldn’t read. Eleni turned back to the table and put spanakopita in the bag.

“Mamá. A day. Aegina is right there. You can see it off the mole on a clear morning,” Sarah protested.

“And if the wind does not come for you? You sit on the flat water and you are hungry, and then you think of your mother.” She folded the cloth over the pastry with great care. “Po po po. Two days, she says, and she wants me to send them off with a heel of bread and my good wishes.”

“The honey is for Steve. He is too thin. The other jar is for Spyros, he would not have eaten anything decent in a month with all the fishing.”

Sarah gave it up, because she had been losing this argument in one form or another her whole life. She kissed her mother on the temple instead and let her get on with packing enough food for an Atlantic crossing.

Across the room, Arthur finished putting his gear back on, and was glad for it. He had spent the previous evening at Markopoulos's house without his gear, and the absence of it had bothered him more than the weight ever did. In a world where mortal danger was not a hypothetical any more, the familiar weight was reassuring. He did not understand the boat and trusted the sea even less. But the maul and shield he understood.

Petros peeked through the door.

“The tide is not going to wait. Come. Spyros is on the boat already and he does not like to be kept, he says it is bad luck.”

Eleni pulled the drawstring tight. “Good,” she said.

She pressed both hands to Sarah's face and kissed her on the forehead. “You eat the spinach pies first. They do not keep.”

The caïque bobbed at the outer edge of Petros’s yard, back in the water after her days up on the slip. Someone, Spyros most likely, had rubbed her down until the new caulking showed in every seam, clean bright lines worked into each place the old pitch had let go. The new rope was hemp-white against the tanned rigging.

Steve counted the small cores and handed them over to the captain. Four stones, as they had agreed. Spyros took them without ceremony, and dropped them into his own pouch.

“And the paper.”

Arthur handed over the transit paper. Spyros unfolded it, held it up to the morning light and read it through slowly. He nodded in satisfaction, folded the paper back and put it inside his jacket without comment.

Petros watched the loading, trying hard to look unbothered. He said the usual things, the jokes and the stories, the things you say to avoid saying what you really want to say.

Once the paper and the cores had exchanged hands, Petros slapped Arthur on his shoulder. “You get there and you get back.”

Sarah kissed her brother on both cheeks. “We will be back before you miss us.”

“What, miss you? I’m looking forward to a quiet house for two days.”

He then took Spyros by the forearm and told him something in a quiet, low voice.

“He is telling me not to make it sea. As though I have made it sea one time in forty years,” Spyros said, motioning the party to get on board.

“Once,” said Petros.

“That was the sea making it sea. I was a bystander.”

Petros did not laugh. He touched Sarah’s arm and said a single word in Greek. Sarah repeated it back to him.

“In. Sit where I tell you. Touch nothing,” said Spyros.

The caïque drifted out into the harbour. Spyros hauled on a halyard, and the sail went up in a single pull. The canvas filled. The boat heeled, and they were moving.

• •

For the first hour the sheer novelty of it carried them. Spyros worked the boat like they did before engines, using the wind and the long tiller, reading the water surface and making adjustments that none of the others had any frame of reference for. The wake unrolled behind them in a straight line, and the morning light glared off the swell.

Then the novelty settled into Arthur’s stomach. He lurched to the railing, and the boat lurched with him.

“Sit amidships if it troubles you. The middle moves least. And keep your eye on the land, not on the water going past the hull,” Spyros said, not unkindly, when Arthur had made his second visit to the lee rail. “And sit lower. You sit high, you fall in. I do not stop,” he added.

“Which one is amidships,” Steve asked, his notebook open, but got no useful answer.

It got worse when Spyros wanted the trim shifted.

“Off the starboard rail, both of you, you are burying her nose,” Spyros said over the sound of rushing water. Nobody moved, on account of nobody being entirely sure which rail had just been named.

“Starboard is,” Arthur began, and then looked down at his hands as though they might turn out to be labelled. “Which one was that, again?”

“The right. Facing the bow, the right,” said Sarah, from where she sat against the mast with her face tipped up to the sun. She was entirely at home, a Piraeus girl who had grown up with an avid fisherman for a father and a brother who fixed boats for a living.

“Steer-board,” said Steve, brightening up. Arthur knew Steve was having as rough a time on the boat as him, but now he had something to talk about.

“Starboard is steorbord, the steering board. The old boats hung the steering oar off the right side, since most men are right-handed, so the right became the steering side. Which is also why you bring the other side to the quay, to keep the oar from being crushed against the stone. The other side became the loading side. It was larboard first, because that was the side you did the lading from, and then became port, because port is the side you put to the port. So you see it could hardly be any simpler.”

“Does it ever occur to you that people might not actually be listening to your nerd nonsense?” said Donna.

“It’s hardly nonsense, and your subconscious absorbs fragments of it anyway. One of these days someone is going to ask you about the Temple Church, or the Hypogeum, or Aegina, and hopefully you would have learned enough not to embarrass me.”

“Come to think of it, all of you should be spending some time each day reading through my notes.”

“And you will just hand out your notebook, will you?” Donna asked, raising an eyebrow.

“I meant under supervision, of course. Handing out my journal, that would be preposterous.”

Arthur laughed, and regretted it at once, as he realised that laughing while being seasick was not a great idea.

The crossing settled into its rhythm: the sail drew, the wake trailed, and Spyros kept up a running commentary on everything they were doing wrong, which was, by his account, most things. Arthur sat too high. Steve sat too far forward. Donna's boots were on a coil of line. The field pack was in the wrong place. The shield was in the wrong place. They dutifully shuffled and adjusted until the boat found its trim.

“If I tell you to row, you take that oar and you pull. You do not splash. The oar is older than you, and I have not got a spare,” Spyros said, pointing at a heavy oar lashed beneath the port gunwale.

Over the hours, Aegina stopped being a smudge on the horizon and set about becoming a place. It rose out of the sea the way islands usually do, the high peak showing first, then the broad shoulders under it, and then the pale scatter of the town strung along the near shore. Spyros watched it come up with an expression that was not entirely friendly.

“There, Aegina. Forty years I sail out here, and every Aeginetan I have met looks at a Piraeus boat like I owe him something,” he said, looking at the island as if it was a particularly unpleasant neighbour.

“You don’t care for it,” Arthur said.

“I sail to it. Caring is another matter.” He spat over the lee rail. “Aeginetans. They still think they own this water, you understand. Four hundred years, five hundred, the whole gulf theirs by the grace of God and the length of their own memory, and each one of them will tell you his grandfather’s grandfather ran cargo when Piraeus was a mudflat.”

“They have some cause for the memory, in fairness,” Steve said, sitting up. “Aegina was a sea power when Athens was nowhere on the map.

“Athens could see her from the Piraeus, you understand, on a clear day. And a rival with a better harbour and a longer pedigree is not good for morale or business. Pericles is supposed to have called Aegina the eyesore of the Piraeus, the speck in the eye of the city, and wanted it scraped off, so the city could see clearly again.”

“Or Athens wanted the control of the shipping lane but couldn’t say so out loud. Nobody clears an island over a bad view, you clear it because it is sitting on something you want, and the view is the part you tell people about,” Donna said from the gunwale.

Steve nodded.

“Oh, undoubtedly. And the phrasing seems to have worked, because it was done as well. Athens conquered the island in four-fifty-seven, sent over its own Athenian settlers and then emptied the entire population in four-thirty-one, right at the start of the Peloponnesian War.”

Steve paused. “Plutarch. Life of Pericles, chapter eight.” He nodded in satisfaction, having checked his sources in his head.

“So the moral of it is either don’t have a nicer harbour than the big city next door, or don’t let the city next door turn into a big city,” Donna said, looking back over the stern.

• •

The talk ran down, as they tend to do amidst the wind and water noise. Arthur had either found his sea legs, or the sea had lost interest in him, one of the two. He worked his way to amidships where Sarah sat. He told himself it was because the middle moved least and had nothing whatever to do with who was sitting there. She shifted along the thwart to make room without being asked. Neither of them remarked on it.

Sarah had her face turned towards Aegina, the wind pulling her hair loose across her collar. Her weight was against Arthur's arm because the thwart was narrow, and neither of them had tried too much to give the other space. Arthur was studiously looking out at the water.

“You are staring at the sea, like a man who has lost something overboard,” Spyros said from the tiller.

“Just watching.”

“Watch the island. The sea is not interesting until it is, and then it is too interesting.”

Arthur took the advice. The caïque rode the water towards Aegina with the sail full and the sun high.

Then Spyros stopped complaining.

He had gone still at the tiller, listening for something the rest of them could not hear. The easy set of his posture was gone.

“What,” said Donna, her hands moving to the axe hilt.

“Sit down. All of you. Get low and hands off the rail,” Spyros said quietly.

The sea off the starboard side looked wrong, and not because it was rough. A patch of it had smoothed out, and the calm patch of water was moving towards them.

Then something struck the caïque from below, and the timber groaned. The whole boat lifted and then dropped from under them. A wall of grey water and grey hide went past the rail together, too fast to tell one from the other. Spyros's hands were white on the tiller.

Whatever had just hit them was longer than the boat and deep enough that Arthur could only see the shadow of it rolling under the surface. Then it turned, and a fin, the height of a man, broke the water.
