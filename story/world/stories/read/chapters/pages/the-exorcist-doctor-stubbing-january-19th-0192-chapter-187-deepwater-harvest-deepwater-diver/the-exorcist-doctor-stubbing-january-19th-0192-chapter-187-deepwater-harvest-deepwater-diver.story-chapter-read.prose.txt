Gael planted both boots against the railing, and—from what Vivi could tell—hauled his fishing rod back with everything he had. The rod bent into a painful curve between his hands, the line thrumming through the rain while something enormous thrashed beneath the black waters. Every pull from below made the old barge shudder, and suddenly, Vivi had the alarming thought that whatever he’d hooked might drag their entire vessel under before they ever got the chance to see it.

“Keep th’rod high!” Rebus shouted, scrambling towards the bow. “Don’t let it pull you sideways!”

“Loosen yer left hand!” Bran yelled over him. “No, yer other left!”

“Let it tire itself out first!” Nixy added. “Don’t yank too fast or ye’ll tear th’hook loose!”

But the children, at least, were fearless. So was Gael. His boots slid half an inch across the wet planks as he laughed, tightening his grip on his rod.

“Would the three of you shut up and let me do it?” he shouted, dragging the rod back another little distance. “Reeling things up is my specialty!”

“No it isn’t,” Maeve muttered immediately.

The line jerked again, nearly pulling the rod out of his grip. Gael staggered forward until Maeve grabbed the back of his coat and braced herself behind him, and for the next several seconds, the two of them strained against whatever fought below, Maeve anchoring Gael while he wound the reel in short, vicious turns.

It wasn’t much longer before the black waters before the bow started to churn. Something pale and immense rolled beneath the surface, flashing with too many moving parts, so Gael gave one final shout, threw his weight backwards, and wrenched the rod up.

A giant fish flew over the rails.

It struck the deck with a wet crash and immediately began flopping hard enough to make the whole barge shake. Vivi stumbled back into Jin, who caught her by the elbow, while Maeve dragged Gael out of the creature’s path before its tail smashed through the planks where his knees had been. Everyone else stared.

The fish was almost as long as Gael was tall, with a fat silver body covered in patches of oily black scales. Four jointed insect legs protruded from either side of its belly, each ending in hooked pincers that scraped furiously against the deck, while its mouth opened and closed in panicked gulps, revealing a second pair of jaws tucked behind the first. Clusters of dull red eyes even blinked along its gills.

It’s… hideous.

It was also, Vivi had to admit, rather interesting to look at—but it wasn’t a Myrmur, quite evidently. If it were, her vision would be pulsing bright red now.

Rebus, Bran, and Nixy didn’t look disappointed in the slightest, however. All three of them rushed around the creature with open mouths, shouting over one another as they inspected its limbs, scales, and teeth.

“Saints above, that’s a hookleg glutton!” Rebus cried.

“A full-grown one!” Bran crouched beside its insect limbs, narrowly avoiding a pincer. “Look at th’leg joints! Ain’t even cracked!”

“An’ all eight gill-eyes are clean!” Nixy added. “This one’ll sell for a heap! Meat’s good, legs’re better, an’ apothecaries buy th’gill sacs for fever salves!”

Gael looked down at the fish, readjusting his top hat against the wind. “So it’s a valuable fish?”

“Very!” Rebus said.

“But it’s not a Myrmur, so I remain wounded.”

Just then, three more sharp twangs sounded from the roof of the bridge. Both Evelyn and Liorin’s fishing rods bent so suddenly they nearly pitched over the sides, while Fergal’s line snapped taut at the stern of the ship, and he snapped two more spider claws around the rod just to stabilize himself.

All three of them began fighting their catches at once.

Very, very quickly, the old barge filled with over-the-top shouting, creaking wood, grinding reels, and the children’s increasingly frantic advice. Evelyn swore at her fish, Liorin grimaced through clenched teeth, while Fergal leaned so far back that Cara had to get out of his way—and Evelyn hauled hers up first. Her catch burst from the water and bounced off the railings before flipping onto the deck, resembling an enormous black carp with a row of yellow beetle shells fused along its spine. Each shell opened and closed independently, revealing thin translucent wings that buzzed uselessly in the rain.

“Shellback Flitter!” Nixy shouted, delighted. “Don’t break th’wings! Perfume makers pay good Marks for those!”

Liorin’s catch came next. He yanked it over the bridge roof with such force that it sailed clear across the deck and struck the opposite railing, before bouncing back onto the deck. The creature had the round body of a bloated river fish, but its lower jaw had split into two long mandibles, and a fan of short, stunted chitin legs rippled along both sides.

Bran gasped. “A crawlmaw! The ‘forest bug’ fish of the seas!”

Fergal dragged up the last fish with steady, brutal pulls. His catch was narrower than the others but nearly twice as long, with a dark eel-like body and a thick horned carapace around its head. When it struck the deck, the horn punched straight through a rotten plank, making Cara flinch and jump back a step further.

Rebus whistled. “Gravehorn eel! That headplate sells for armor work!”

Still, all eight of them from the Heartcord Clinic couldn’t help but look more than a little disappointed. They’d come here for Myrmurs, not normal mutated fish.

We just have to keep at it… I guess.

Once fresh bait was pressed onto the hooks of the four who’d reeled up the fish, they cast their lines again, and all of them resumed pair dancing. Meanwhile, the three children dragged their first catches towards the center of the deck and began gutting them with short fishing knives. Vivi tried not to watch too closely because it was gruesome work, but it was interesting. They cut along the bellies, pried out loose organs, and tossed different parts into separate compartments under the deck. Good meat went down into the hold, Gill sacs, shells, legs, wings, glands, and horn plates were wrapped or stacked seemingly according to value, and anything useless immediately went overboard.

Gael, however, had abandoned both Maeve and his place at the bow entirely. Instead he skipped towards the rear of the bridge, jumped onto the roof of the bridge between Liorin and Evelyn, and withdrew a thick metal tube from beneath his coat.

Vivi turned just in time to see him strike the end against the roof, and a green, blinding flare burst upwards with a harsh hiss. The light flooded the rain, painting the entire barge in sickly emerald while Gael waved the burning flare over his head.

“And what are you doing?” Maeve shouted back, scowling.

“Calling our ship!”

“The fortress that can’t fish?”

“We’re not gonna use it to fish! I thought we’ve established that already!” Gael shouted back. “But I’ve been working on a small side project whenever I had spare time these past few days, and it’s currently aboard that ship, so nevermind me! The seven of you just keep doing your thing! I’ll have the Saint’s Hands deliver my project to us!”

That explanation was apparently all they were going to receive, so they continued their dancing and fishing.

To Vivi’s growing surprise, however, the work was no longer entirely disappointing. Their next half a dozen catches weren’t Myrmurs, still, but the mutated fish they were reeling up were much larger, stranger, and possessed far stronger bug traits than the fish they’d been reeling up on their floating fortress. Maeve seemed to notice it after a while, and so did Vivi and Jin: there was bioarcanic essence inside these rarer catches they were pulling on board.

They could feel the essence in the fish.

As Maeve looked over the newest mutated fish she’d just dragged aboard—another large-bellied crawlmaw with mandibles for jaws—Jin looked over at the three children.

“How many of these super-mutated fish live in the lake?” he shouted.

Rebus, Bran, and Nixy paused in the middle of gutting a shellback flitter..

“Loads!” Rebus said.

“Thousands!” Bran said.

“Millions!” Nixy said. “A hundred barges could fish every night for years without thinnin’ th’schools!”

Whatever the truth was, the practical answer appeared to be the same: there were more than Wraithpier could ever catch alone.

So Vivi looked at Jin, Jin looked at Maeve, Maeve looked at Cara, and Cara—still moving through the slow paired dance while holding Fergal’s hand—looked at him. Vivi could almost see the calculations forming behind the man’s stern expression.

“This might be the best consistent source of points we’ve found for the Saint’s Hands, then,” he muttered. “We’ve relied far too long on our resident Exorcists for hunting Myrmurs, even though some of my boys with Jumping Spider Classes have been supporting them recently. If we can establish fish farms in these deepwater lakes, we’d be able to harvest points even without Maeve and Jin providing most of the firepower—and we wouldn’t have to deal with the hassle of waiting for Gael to surgically deal with the Myrmur heart, either.”

Then Fergal turned his gaze towards the three children, and all of them stiffened.

“After this,” he said, “I want the location of every deepwater lake in Wraithpier with large populations of these rarer fish. You kids know where they are?”

Rebus swallowed, glancing at Bran and Nixy.

“Aye,” he said. “We know most of ’em.”

“Good. The Saint’s Hands will pay you for the information. And if you’re willing, I’d like to hire all of you children as our local consultants and guides,” Fergal continued. “We’ve always brought locals into the gang when we expand our business into another cardinal ward—Wraithpier shouldn’t be any different.”

Nixy’s mouth opened. Bran dropped the fish knife in his hand, and Rebus looked between them before turning back to Fergal.

“Ye mean jobs?”

“Yes.”

“With pay?”

“Yes.”

“An’ we tell grown-ups what t’do?”

Fergal paused. “Within reason.”

All three children immediately lit up.

“We’ll do it!” Nixy shouted.

“We’ll be th’best lake guides in all Wraithpier!” Bran added.

Rebus nodded so hard his wet hair slapped against his forehead. “Best consultants too! We know every dock, every lake, every buyer, every crooked netman, an’ every place ye shouldn’t sail after midnight! We’ll be th’best consultants yer gang’s ever had, er…” He hesitated a little. “... What’s yer gang called again?”

“The Saint’s Hands,” Fergal said plainly. “We can talk details later.”

Fergal had barely finished speaking when Vivi’s fishing rod finally jerked hard enough to wrench her shoulder forward. A sharp tremor ran up the line, through the wooden grip, and into both her arms. It left her fingers tingling all the way to the elbows, and for one startled moment, she could only stare at the rod as it bent towards the black waters.

Then the line pulled again, and her heart leapt.

“I got one!”

It was her first catch. Her first twang. She reluctantly released Jin’s hand and seized the fishing rod with both of hers, and the loss of his palm left her oddly cold despite the alcohol still warming her cheeks, but there was no time to mourn the end of their dance. Whatever had taken her bait was dragging the tip of her rod lower and lower, forcing her to stagger towards the railing before she caught herself.

She planted both boots against the railings exactly as Gael had done, and she struggled harder than anyone else did.

Jin stepped nearer, watching her hands and the angle of the rod.

“Need help?” he asked.

“Nope,” she grunted.

“But you seem like you could—”

“I’ve got this,” she insisted, though the words came out between strained breaths. “Just watch… me… and…”

She planted one boot harder against the railing, threw her weight backwards, and yanked. The water erupted—and a gigantic shrimp burst halfway through the surface, larger than Vivi’s entire body from head to tail. Its shell was a filthy grey-green, plated in overlapping ridges that looked thick enough to turn aside a sword, while a forest of thin feelers whipped around its head. One claw was small and hooked close to its chest, but the other was enormous, and it was pointed directly up at her from beneath the surface.

Vivi blinked.

Her vision flooded red.

Before she even had the chance to check its status, the shrimp snapped its giant claw shut, and water exploded from between its pincers. If not for Jin yanking her away from the railing in a sudden harbor-step whirl, the pressurized stream of water would’ve torn off her head, though it still passed so close to her ear that she felt its cold bite across her cheek.

Unfortunately, the projectile made her drop her fishing rod, and the giant shrimp disappeared beneath the waves.

… No!

Desperate, she tore herself away from Jin and rushed back towards the railing, leaning over as far as she dared. Rain struck her face while she searched the dark waters below, but all she saw were disturbed ripples fading between the drops.

“It has to be tired!” she shouted, squinting harder. “It fought me for all that time, and it fired that water attack, so it couldn’t have gone far! It might still be underneath us!”

A rough hand patted her on the back.

“It’s alright,” Jin murmured, also peering down with her. “You did good.”

“Good’s not enough,” she insisted. “Before it runs away, we’ve gotta—”

A heavy thump crashed onto the barge behind them, and both of them whirled together. A massive wooden crate now sat in the middle of the ship, its lower edge embedded slightly in the old planks from the force of its landing. Thick chains and oil-darkened ropes were wrapped around every side, while metal plates reinforced each corner.

It was obvious enough where the crate came from. Beyond the barge, roughly a hundred meters across the lake, Gael’s armored fishing fortress loomed through the rain with spotlights blaring and several dozen Saint’s hands standing atop the lower deck. They must’ve launched the crate across the gap.

“Thanks, boys!” Gael shouted from atop the bridge, waving both arms towards the distant ship. “Excellent throw! Beautiful work!”

One of the gangsters aboard the floating fortress raised a hand in acknowledgement. In the meantime, Gael hopped down from the bridge, landed beside the crate, and immediately began pulling at the cords.

“Now, before I unveil our magic track,” he called, “do either of you still see our shrimp?”

Both of them turned back towards the water and narrowed their eyes. For Vivi’s part, the red glow had completely vanished from her sight.

“I can’t see it,” she said.

“I can,” Jin muttered, his gaze shifting slowly beneath the surface and following something she couldn’t see. “The red’s still there. You might’ve pissed it off by reeling it up, so it’s probably sticking around to kill you.”

Gael laughed. “Fantastic!” Then he lost his patience with the chains and knots, stepping back to kick the entire crate apart instead. The entire front side shattered inwards, and as the remaining boards fell away one after another, revealing the… the thing inside.

It took Vivi a moment to realize it was a diving suit, though only in the loosest and most distressing sense of the term. The body consisted of thick overlapping metal plates bolted onto a heavy canvas underlayer, with lengths of chain reinforcing the joints and broad lead weights strapped around the boots. Several mismatched hoses ran from the back into a cluster of tanks, valves, and hand-pumped cylinders, while the helmet was a round iron chamber with three thick glass windows set across the front.

The glass looked scratched. The metal looked dented. Something dark stained the inside of one gauntlet, and so it resembled the sort of armour a murderer might wear while walking along the bottom of a graveyard lake to collect the souls of drowned sailors, but Gael rested one hand proudly on the shoulder of the suit.

“... Behold! My deepwater Myrmur retrieval suit!” he declared, turning towards Maeve and gesturing grandly at the open back of the suit. “Get in, dearest wife.”

Maeve immediately scowled at him.

“No.”
