“So, you got depressed sorting through the bodies, so you decided to start teleporting at random?” said Yon after I finished retelling my day to him.

I don’t know how he did it, but the man had me unload everything.

He was sitting on his boulder after dragging it to the riverbank, while I sat on a tree stump.

“Does that sum it up?” He looked at me with a gentle smile.

“When you put it that way...” I grumbled.

“I thought I already warned you about reckless teleportation.”

“You did?”

“Yes, right after you saved us in Pikar,” he said, narrowing his eyes at me.

“But my teleportation has never steered me wrong,” I insisted. “I always add safety conditions to most of my wishes.”

“Just because it teleports you somewhere safe does not mean you will still be safe five minutes later.”

“Right, that makes sense,” I said, lowering my head.

“It is good that you want to help people, and it is also good that you are mourning those mercenaries.”

He paused.

“I have seen enough young people die doing stupid things to appreciate that much.”

“Are you thinking about Vals?” I asked, remembering that one of his protégés' deaths had been the trigger for this training isolation.

“No,” He shook his head, “While Vals’ death was unfortunate, it was only the latest in a long list.”

He sighed.

“I have been helping new freelancers because no one was there to help them, but that came at the cost of my own individual growth. I have been stagnating for two years. My last and only evolution was four years ago. I am only twenty-three, yet I am starting to act like a retired old man.”

“But if you focus on your own growth, who would help those new freelancers?” I asked.

He grimaced as if he had bitten into a lemon.

“People like Vals, like those mercenaries, deserve our pity, not our scorn. But I also know there is no real way to save them from themselves.”

He sighed again.

“I left Vals alone for a day, and he ended up stalking you. I left him alone for four days, and he decided he could fight a dragon. Some causes are simply not worth it.”

“But not all the Misfits are like Vals. What about Lera?” I asked, remembering the dejected girl I had met in the baths after Yon disbanded the Misfits.

“What about Lera?” he asked.

“She does not deserve to be left on her own because of Vals’ mistakes.”

“What is she doing without the Misfits?” he asked.

“She is clearing moles from the outer fields,” I said.

“See? Sensible girl,” Yon said. “The Misfits were not made for the likes of Lera. I created the Misfits to save people from themselves. Taking in Kan, Shingo, and Lera was just a bonus.”

“I guess that makes sense.”

“So tell me, if you were jumping around saving people, why did you end up here?” he asked.

“I honestly have no idea.”

“I do not exactly look like I need saving,” Yon raised an eyebrow.

“But that was not my wish,” I said. “My wish was to take me somewhere I could be useful.”

His expression shifted slightly.

“So you are here to help me?” he asked.

“Sorry for the poor showing,” I scratched the back of my neck awkwardly. “I somehow ended up telling you about my problems instead of actually being helpful.”

“Do not worry about it,” Yon said with an easy shrug.

“Still, I might actually be able to help,” I said, straightening slightly.

“Oh?”

“Explain what you were doing, and maybe I can help you optimize it.”

That got his attention.

“You see, as a kindred person, I need to be at the peak of my physical condition to attempt an evolution,” he explained. He gestured vaguely toward his body. “My arm and leg muscles are getting there, but not the rest of me. This is why I was dragging a boulder across a riverbed. I needed something that would exhaust my whole body.”

“Oh,” My eyes lit up.

“So you need back, shoulder, and core exercises.”

“I suppose?” Yon frowned slightly.

I immediately dug into my bag and pulled out my laptop.

Yon stared at the weird device.

I flipped it open and quickly loaded a few downloaded fitness videos.

“You are going to love this.”

He stepped closer, curiosity overtaking confusion as I turned the screen toward him.

On the display, a gym instructor demonstrated deadlifts, rows, weighted carries, squats, pull-ups, and various core exercises. Racks, barbells, and dumbbells also surrounded him.

Yon leaned in, eyes widening as he watched the footage.

“What in the world am I looking at?” He pointed directly at a rack of dumbbells. “What are those?”

“Weights. Different sizes for progressive overload.”

“And those?” he asked, pointing at a barbell loaded with absurdly large plates.

“More weights.”

He blinked.

“That seems redundant.”

“Yes, well, it is for convenience's sake. Not everyone has the grip strength to push boulders.”

I clicked on another clip showing a powerlifter performing deadlifts.

Yon’s brows climbed higher.

“That movement...” he murmured.

“It targets the posterior chain muscles,” I explained. “Back, glutes, hamstrings, core, and grip strength. Basically, everything you seem to care about.”

He stared at the screen like I had just shown him a miracle.

“This is amazing,” he muttered. “How much does each tool weigh?”

“Each plate is twenty-five kilograms. There are four plates on each side, so two hundred kilograms in total.”

By the end of my explanation, his enthusiasm dimmed slightly.

“That is not enough.”

“What?”

“That would not challenge me nearly enough.”

Right... the man was just pushing a boulder the size of a car.

“Fair point,” I admitted. “But the concept is what matters.”

That seemed to restore his interest.

“I can show this to Nina tomorrow,” I said, tapping thoughtfully against my chin. “She might be able to come up with a magical equivalent.”

“Such as?” Yon looked skeptical.

“I do not know. Maybe an adjustable weighted gear? Or something enchanted with Heavy Magic to simulate heavier loads.”

That made him pause, and his eyes narrowed in thought.

“That... could actually work.”

“Exactly.”

We kept talking for a while longer as I showed him additional exercise videos.

Some were bodyweight routines. Others involved resistance bands, calisthenics progressions, or yoga.

Yon nodded occasionally.

“Some of these things I already do,” he admitted. “But at some point after my first evolution, my own weight became too easy to carry. Even adding plate armor did not help much.”

He glanced over his shoulder toward the absolutely ridiculous car-sized boulder half-submerged in the river.

“So, boulders were the easiest way to do strength training. And even then, at a certain point, finding larger rocks becomes impractical.”

I snorted.

“Yeah, that seems like a design flaw in your current system.”

He chuckled softly.

Honestly, just seeing Yon laugh again made me feel weirdly validated. He had been in such a foul mood when he heard of Vals’ death. Maybe my teleportation was right to bring me here.

“I should probably do some of this training too,” I admitted. “I want to check if my soul has enough kindred alignment for physical growth to actually be worth pursuing.”

Yon glanced at me.

“You probably do.”

“Maybe. It is hard to tell. I cannot fell trees with kicks after all.”

I shut my laptop and stretched.

“Well, I should probably go to sleep now. I will be back in Hano in a few days, but before that, I will make sure Nina starts working on workout gear.”

Yon nodded. Then his expression shifted into thought.

“There is still something I do not understand, though.”

“What?”

“If your power was taking you places where you could save lives, why did it bring you here, too?”

He crossed his arms.

“You were certainly informative, but is this truly where you could have been the most useful?”

“Well, of course,” I smiled.

Yon blinked.

“While I am not a hero,” I said, standing up and dusting off the back of my pants, “helping a real hero get back on track is probably one of the most useful things I can do.”

Yon stared at me, “But I am not a hero either,” then frowned.

“Of course you are not,” I said casually.

He looked momentarily offended.

“Okay then, see you later.”

I lifted my spear.

“Wait,” Yon called.

I paused mid-motion.

“What? Did I forget something?”

“From what you told me, Vena must have overused her healing again.”

“Ah. Right.”

“She will probably be fasting tomorrow,” he continued. “Make sure you bring her nutritious drinks. Milk and honey, preferably.”

I stared at him for half a second.

Yeah. Not a hero, my ass.

A laugh escaped me.

“Got it.”

I raised my spear, activated the stored star mana, and vanished into the night.

The next day, I teleported back beside Vena.

She was standing just outside the slime village walls, hands folded in front of her as she quietly watched a group of surrendered mercenaries assembling a large wooden pyre from stripped logs and scavenged timber.

The morning was gray and cool, the kind of overcast weather that made everything feel washed out. Smoke from cooking fires drifted lazily over the village walls, mixing with the damp, earthy smell of mud, wet wood, and the faint lingering scent of blood from the battlefield.

Nearby, the dead had already been prepared.

Bodies lay in orderly rows, each one wrapped in cloth or covered with blankets, waiting for the pyre to be finished.

Honestly, cremation was probably the smartest choice. You really did not want dozens of fresh corpses buried this close to the undead dead zone.

Vena noticed me immediately as I appeared.

She turned and gave me a warm smile despite the exhaustion written all over her face. Then she pointed at her lips and shook her head.

Right... not only was she fasting, but apparently she was also observing a vow of silence.

People had died under her care yesterday, even if only technically and under extremely stretched definitions.

Clerics really do love making themselves miserable.

Without a word, I reached into my bag and handed her a small clay jar insulated in stitched leather.

Her brows lifted slightly in curiosity. She removed the lid, leaned in to sniff it, then cautiously took a sip. A second later, her eyes widened, and she smiled at me with visible approval.

Success.

Before coming here, I had teleported over to Louis and asked her to help me prepare something appropriate for a fasting Vena. The girl was her childhood friend, so it did not take much convincing for her to come up with something splendid.

The result was probably this world’s first latte.

Well... more accurately, it was warm honey milk mixed with strong coffee and a little cinnamon, but it counted in my book.

I got out my own jar and started sipping. Drinking from clay added an earthy flavor to the whole thing.

“You seem to be in a better mood,” Edmund observed as he approached.

He stepped beside us, arms folded behind his back, looking much better rested than yesterday. His armor was less dented, almost looking new.

I rubbed the back of my neck awkwardly.

“Sorry about yesterday,” I admitted. “I may have emotionally imploded a little.”

“A little?” Edmund chuckled.

“I understand very well what you were going through,” he said. “A lot of people feel the same when confronted with war.”

We stood in silence for a while, watching the mercenaries work.

There was no chatter among them either, just tired men carrying wood, hammering supports into place, and occasionally glancing toward the covered bodies.

Even Captain Wolfang was helping. Which was surprising to me at first. The way the man acted during the parley, I had written him off as an arrogant fool. For all his many flaws, I supposed he did choose to offer his men some dignity in death.

Eventually, the pyre was finished. It was large enough to hold everyone. The mercenaries began lifting their dead one by one, carefully placing each body onto the stacked timber. Once all the bodies had been arranged, Captain Wolfang stepped forward with a torch. There were no speeches for the dead, nor any added ceremony to the gesture.

The full Twenty Men Army stood nearby in silence, Loumpa and his wife with them as well, along with Raik, Katar, and the rest of our group.

For a brief moment, both armies simply faced one another.

Yesterday, they had been enemies. Now they were just tired people staring at a pile of dead they had brought into the world.

Wolfang lowered the torch, and the dry timber caught quickly.

Orange flames spread through the structure, climbing upward in hungry waves. Smoke began to curl into the pale sky, and the smell hit seconds later.

I tried not to think about it too much.

To the side, Raik had a look of concentration, and the flame shifted color from orange to blue. He was unmistakably using his mana to heat the flames, making them hot enough to burn bones.

Beside me, Vena quietly joined her hands in prayer.

Edmund, Widemore, the maid, and the witch all did the same, each praying to their own god.

With an Agame on hand, it did not take long for the flames to consume everything. Eventually, all that was left of the pyre was ash.

That seemed to be everyone’s unspoken signal that the ceremony, if this could even be called one, was over.

The mercenary camp started packing almost immediately, and without much fanfare, they prepared to march east toward Verraden.

“We will remain here another two or three days,” Lieutenant Widemore said as he approached our group.

He was carrying his helmet beneath one arm.

“No point sharing the road back with them,” he continued, glancing toward the departing mercenaries. “Just in case.”

It was probably for the best.

“If you are moving toward the dead zone, you may want to use the sanctified camp Vena created,” Raik suggested.

“Sanctified camp?” Edmund repeated, blinking. He turned toward Vena with sudden excitement.

“That is brilliant. Why have I never considered that?”

Vena looked mildly smug, despite it technically being Raik’s idea first.

Lieutenant Widemore frowned thoughtfully.

“How exactly does that work?”

“She used Sanctified Ground,” Edmund explained immediately, now fully invested. “It is the same miracle we use for temples and burial sites.”

Vena nodded and casually sipped her latte like an extremely pleased nun. Ja’a was standing over her shoulder, curious about the drink.

“Wait,” Lanka said, her eyes lighting up. “Does that mean we could establish temporary safe zones deeper into the dead zone?”

I recognized that expression on the woman’s face. She was worse than Katar.

Edmund nodded twice in excitement.

“Loumpa,” he said immediately, turning. “Do you still have your messaging card?”

“Yes?”

“I need to contact Jaime. He needs to come here immediately.”

Loumpa sighed and started walking back toward his village, his peg leg tapping against the stone ground.

“Follow me,” he grumbled.

Edmund and Lanka hurried after him.

Lieutenant Widemore watched them leave.

“Well,” he muttered. “I did promise them a battle.”

“Better it be undead than people,” I offered.

He looked at me and smiled, “That is right. We are freelancers after all.”

He turned toward Raik.

“What about your team?” he asked. “What do you plan on doing next?”

Raik crossed his arms thoughtfully.

“We will likely return to Hano.”

“I recommend avoiding the direct road through Verraden,” Widemore said. “Take either the northern road or cut south through the Kurco Range.”

“If we go south,” I asked, “does that take us near the Sunless Reach?”

Raik glanced at me.

“Yes.”

“Perfect.”

He narrowed his eyes.

“What do you have in mind?”

“There is an elemental I would like dead.”

I smiled.

“Oh, now that sounds fun,” Widemore grinned.

Raik pinched the bridge of his nose.

“Can I ask why?”

“Personal growth,” I said confidently.

“No, really, why?”

I sighed dramatically.

“Fine. There is an annoying sky elemental there that nearly killed me, and it is living on top of a tower I would like to claim.”

Raik studied me for a moment, then shrugged.

“Sure, why not? I have never fought a sky elemental before.”

“Yes!”

And just like that, I led my team into a side quest.

We spent the next hour helping with the final cleanup before departure.

By helping, I mostly meant standing guard over Vena as she made rounds through the village, assisting with minor injuries. Though she was sparse with her miracles, instead using bandages and helping the local herbalist.

At one point, she deliberately held up her empty latte jar and shook it.

Ah, a refill request... noted.

I teleported away, grabbed more from Louis, and returned a minute later, making sure to get an extra pitcher for the rest of the team to try.

Vena looked deeply pleased. Honestly, I might have accidentally created an addiction with this one.

Shingo drank it like water, barely tasting it. Katar was indifferent. Raik and Kan seemed to like it.

Ja’a was gushing over it.

But Calr had the most surprising reaction.

The boy started shaking, his eyes wide open.

“What is this drink? What have you given me?” he started speaking rapidly.

Apparently, he was super sensitive to caffeine. He looked like he was almost tweaking.

No more coffee for him, we unanimously decided.

By midday, our group had regrouped near the village gates.

The Burdrasses saddled onto the cart. The weather had improved slightly, with weak sunlight finally managing to pierce through the clouds, making everything look less miserable.

To everyone’s surprise, Captain Wolfang approached unexpectedly.

He stopped a few meters away, his posture stiff.

For a moment, no one said anything.

Then he inclined his head toward Vena.

“On behalf of my men,” he said, his voice measured, “thank you.”

He bowed deeply.

“You are welcome?” I offered, speaking on behalf of the girl who was still under her vow of silence.

He straightened and gave us one final nod, then turned and walked away.

We waited for the army to disappear beyond the horizon before deciding it was time for our own departure.

Lumpa showed up again before we left.

The village chief thanked us once more for the metal and for helping with the aftermath of the battle.

As we began riding south, I glanced back one last time.

Smoke still rose faintly from where the pyre once stood.

Beyond it, the village walls stood battered but intact, and the people were free to graze their livestock and work their forges once more.

No matter how dramatic yesterday had been, the world stubbornly kept going.

Beside me, Vena sipped her drink.

She caught me looking, then gave me a small smile.

I smiled back, and with it, most of the heaviness in my chest finally loosened.
