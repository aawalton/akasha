Opening the exit turned out to be significantly easier with the spawner destroyed. Without it, the door was just heavy rather than magically sealed. Shingo and Katar were eventually able to force it open from the inside.

The moment the doors gave way, fresh air rushed into the chamber, not good air, admittedly. It still smelled like dust, rust, and dead things, but at least we had more oxygen to work with.

The remaining undead were still active, but they were no longer behaving like an organized force. Without the spawner directing them, the mining skeletons became scattered and oddly aimless. Some continued wandering toward random tunnels carrying empty baskets, as if trying to resume their previous tasks, while others attacked us on sight, but individually, they were barely threatening.

Dispatching them was easy, with Vena handling the lion’s share through bursts of Holy Miracle, reducing them to scattered bones with satisfying regularity. It was less a battle and more like a cleanup duty.

We left the mine tunnel after that. The moment Raik stepped outside, he visibly relaxed. Then, standing a safe distance away from the entrance, he started releasing more of the stored heat. Waves of warmth radiated outward from his body, distorting the air around him, and steam rose from the stone beneath his boots. He looked significantly less miserable afterward.

“Well,” I said, watching him vent heat like some kind of magical radiator. “I guess you could not convert it to mana after all.”

Raik gave me a tired look.

“I could. It was just physically straining. I need to practice some more, next time with something smaller.”

“Your soul has grown to 25 SB,” Ja’a said. “Congratulations, that is a 25% increase.”

“It did?” He blinked.

“It makes sense,” Calr nodded. “For the first time, you are walking closer to your true path rather than your family’s path to power.”

We decided to rest and have lunch before continuing. Honestly, after nearly being cooked alive, everyone had earned it. The atmosphere was noticeably lighter. I was getting a little too comfortable with the idea of surviving death situations.

Looking back on it, I was not in any real danger, was I? I had a delayed escape-wish banked. I probably would have teleported out if I had lost consciousness.

As we ate our rations, I noticed Kan fiddling with something. I leaned closer and recognized the metal skull core. It was now shattered into four metal pieces, several smaller fragments, and the gemlike monster core beneath.

She picked up the smallest piece and, to my surprise, she actually bit into it.

I heard an audible crunch.

I stared at her.

“Is that safe?”

Kan looked at the rest of the skull thoughtfully while chewing.

“It is probably fine.” She shrugged. “While it has some death-aspected mana, it is mostly metal.”

“This is more potent than the golem core you ate last time,” Ja’a warned.

“I will only eat a smaller piece per day,” Kan nodded.

I was starting to wonder if I had a similar kindred biology. What kind of food would work best for my own metabolism? Electric eel? Starfish? Or maybe something more magical, like Nightmares.

After lunch, we returned to the forge. Now that we were no longer under immediate threat of cremation, it was time to properly loot the place.

“Should we gather all this ore?” asked Calr. “It should sell well to the metal slime farm.”

“First rule of looting,” said Ja’a, “is to organize the loot. We do not have unlimited carrying capacity, even with three bags of holding.”

“There was a second metal door inside the forge chamber,” I said. “We should check it out first.”

Naturally, we were a bit cautious about another trap. We had poor Shingo open the side door, too. Even if it was not as heavy, he was still the most durable out of all of us.

To everyone’s relief, the inside appeared to be a living quarter.

The room was small but surprisingly organized. A bedroll occupied one corner alongside a crude wooden crate serving as a makeshift desk, as well as some personal belongings scattered around the space in a way that made it feel strangely human after everything we had just experienced here.

Clothes, old tools, a few books, a cracked mirror, and stacked neatly against one wall were... metal ingots.

A lot of metal ingots.

I actually stopped walking for a moment after noticing them. Stack upon stack of processed metal bars filled nearly half the room.

“Jackpot!” shouted Ja’a.

Most of them were bone-steel, far more than I expected. There had to be over a hundred ingots of the magical metal. Next to those were roughly fifty pig iron bars, several dozen copper and tin ingots, a handful of cobalt, five silver ingots, and two gold ones.

I whistled softly.

“Well,” Calr said, “nearly burning to death was not too bad.”

Raik looked at the metal stockpile and visibly approved.

“Considering I was never in any danger, this almost feels like free money.”

Kan shoulder-checked him affectionately at that terrible joke.

While material goods were all well and good, I was wealthy enough that the windfall was not life-changing. So while the rest of the group started organizing the loot, I checked through the personal belongings.

I am an anthropologist, after all.

The clothes were old, but I could tell they had once been expensive. I recognized silk and costly dyes despite the age.

Eventually, I found what I was hoping for: a personal journal.

I sat down on the edge of the bedroll and started flipping through it while everyone else handled inventory.

The journal belonged to the mine’s former owner, though the word “owner” was a bit of a misnomer. I would call him more of a prospector.

Apparently, he had been a Bloodline noble, and more importantly, he possessed a rare affinity allowing him to both sense and manipulate metal.

According to his entries, he had illegally opened a mine here despite its dangerous proximity to the dead zone. Both Hano and High Rock had signed a treaty stating that only two population centers should exist near the dead zone, one east and one north, to prevent the spread of the calamity.

But after sensing the large amount of metal, the prospector either got extremely greedy or incredibly stupid… probably both.

At first, the journal mostly contained mundane mining records. Ore quality, extraction rates, and complaints about labor costs, especially since he had needed to hire through shady brokers so his illegal mine would not be discovered by Ectamel authorities. Typical scummy entrepreneur nonsense.

Then the tone changed as the undead arrived.

At first, he seemed confident. He forged the massive double doors himself, likely using his affinity and local materials, hoping to seal himself inside and wait out the danger.

Of course, being an evil little shit, he did not let the common workers in, so he could keep all the food for himself.

That part had actually worked for a while… a long while.

Isolation got to him eventually. He had been so good at covering his tracks that no one even knew his mine existed, and no one was coming to rescue him.

Food supplies eventually ran low, and judging by the dates, they had lasted him around six months of solitude.

The entries became shorter and shorter until eventually they were mostly messy scribbles.

The final pages were honestly unsettling. The handwriting deteriorated noticeably, and the words were full of paranoia. There were multiple references to hearing the metal whispering.

The final line on the last page read:

The furnace is hungry. Should I feed it... should I?

I closed the journal.

“Well,” I muttered. “That was fascinating.”

With less than a year in this world, I was hardly an expert on how magic worked yet, but if I had to guess, his death or prolonged magical instability had likely contributed to the formation of the forge spawner.

The thing had shared his metal affinity and vaguely industrial mindset.

A metal manipulator dying alone inside a forge surrounded by enchanted materials and undead energy.

Honestly, the conclusion was not hard to reach.

I was called over before I could think about it longer. Apparently, it was time to store everything important.

I slipped the journal into my bag before joining the others.

Ja’a, being our resident financial advisor, was in charge of inventory management.

And the task had become increasingly difficult.

Ja’a’s bag was full because of that enormous mechanical harvester we looted from the ghost farming village.

Raik’s bag of holding was nearly full as well. As team leader, he had been carrying most of the loot we found.

Mine still had space, but not nearly as much as I would have liked.

Especially if we factored in the supplies we left in the sanctified camp. I would rather keep my bathtub than carry a few extra ingots of pig iron, let alone unprocessed ore.

So I helped pack away the highest-value items first.

The gold and silver went in immediately, then the cobalt, and then as much bone-steel as we could reasonably carry.

“Take the tin and copper too,” Ja’a instructed. “They would sell well in Ectamel. The bone-steel, however, would fetch a better price in Hano, where it is more needed.”

“I could probably teleport here again later and pick up the rest of the metal,” I said. “Assuming no one else beats us to it.”

Then a thought hit me. Was I being shortsighted again?

“I will be back,” I announced.

I summoned my spear back to my hand, then I was gone.

Thirty minutes later, I returned carrying five more empty bags of holding.

They were the cheaper kind, the ones without soul-bonding protections.

I handed one each to Kan, Calr, Shingo, Katar, and Vena.

“I cannot take this. Those cost at least one gold,” said Calr.

“Only fifty silver,” I corrected. “These are the cheapest ones available since they do not require a soul dealer to bond them for you.”

Ja’a sighed.

“That was impulsive. There is not enough left here to justify a four-gold expense.”

“I am covering the cost, not the team,” I shrugged. “Besides, these are incredibly convenient. Everyone should have one.”

Our argument was interrupted by Calr suddenly shouting.

“Shingo, help me dislodge the doors. We are taking these.”

We all turned and watched, dumbfounded, as the duo began looting the massive metal doors. Then they started dismantling the forge itself.

“Okay,” Ja’a admitted, “maybe there is more than four gold worth of loot here after all.”

I laughed and joined them in dismantling the furnace.

By the time we finished dismantling everything worth taking, everyone was exhausted again.

Between hauling ingots, dislodging doors, stripping magical materials from the forge, and arguing over what was worth the effort, the day had somehow become even more physically demanding than fighting undead.

With the important loot finally secured, we returned to our sanctified campsite.

The first thing I did upon arriving was rush straight toward the bath. I was filthy from almost being roasted. So after setting down my bag, I had Vena purify the water and asked Raik to warm it slightly with magic.

Not too hot. I have reached my heat quota for today.

Once the privacy screen was set up, I slipped into the bath with a moan so dramatic I was mildly embarrassed by it.

“This is,” I muttered, sinking deeper into the water, “honestly my best investment yet.”

Five minutes later, Vena joined me. Shortly after that, Ja’a did as well.

At this point, personal boundaries in adventuring groups were practically non-existent, especially among us girls. I used to cover myself with a towel when I first arrived here, but since bathing was gender-separated, I stopped caring after a while.

The three of us sat in relative silence, soaking away layers of dirt, sweat, and mild soot from charring in the forge.

I leaned back against the edge of the tub and reflected on the day. Honestly, I enjoyed myself. That realization surprised me a little.

Not the nearly dying part, obviously. That remained highly overrated.

But the rest of it, the exploration, the weird magical mystery dungeon logic, the teamwork, and even the looting, exhausting as it had been, was weirdly satisfying.

There was something deeply rewarding about uncovering a hidden location, surviving its dangers, and then leaving with both answers and profit.

I apparently liked adventuring. Well, at least as long as I was not fighting actual people. Monsters were much easier for me to manage emotionally.

Today was particularly rewarding, not just monetarily. Raik’s breakthrough was probably the real highlight for me.

Not to take all the credit from him, obviously. He was the one who nearly turned himself into a thermic bomb.

Still, I had significantly helped him reach that magical breakthrough.

The thought made me smile. That feeling was familiar. It was the same quiet satisfaction I had felt when helping Luna and Hanna ascend.

Helping people grow, or helping them discover something new about themselves, might actually be what I enjoy most.

More than adventuring, more than treasure, and even more than uncovering magical mysteries. I wanted to help people discover their own potential, to inspire breakthroughs, to make weird leaps of magical logic suddenly click, and maybe have a few breakthroughs of my own along the way. That sort of thing.

It was an oddly comforting realization because it also confirmed something else: I could not do that, locked in a library forever, or hiding in the Sunless Reach as an isolated magical hermit.

As appealing as unlimited research time sounded, it was not enough by itself. Apparently, I needed people.

For someone who had professed throughout my school life to be an introvert, the hypocrisy was damning.

“You look smug,” Ja’a observed.

I blinked. “Do I?”

“A little,” Vena agreed.

“I was reflecting.”

“Oh no. Holy preserve us,” Vena laughed.

After the bath, we regrouped near the campfire.

Dinner was lighter than usual since lunch had been late, and most of us were still tired. Still, Raik called for a team meeting.

Which was fair. Our current expedition was clearly reaching a natural stopping point.

Raik sat near the fire, posture relaxed but expression focused.

“Okay,” he began, glancing around the group. “With this final mission, we have exhausted all known locations accessible from this sanctified camp.”

He gestured vaguely toward the surrounding wilderness.

“We can move the camp twelve hours north or south and continue exploring. Or we can call it here and return to Ectamel.”

Kan tilted her head slightly.

“The reason we came here was because of Elder Haro Okar’s challenge to you, thirty missions in thirty-six days. Where are we on that?”

Raik grinned.

“Done.”

I frowned.

“That cannot be right.”

He looked very pleased with himself.

“It is.”

“I do not think we have done more than fifteen missions,” I argued. “Even counting each point-of-interest investigation as a mission, we have only explored six major locations.”

“That leaves us with twenty-one missions,” I counted. “That still does not get us to thirty.”

Raik reached into his bag and pulled out several fist-sized monster cores.

Ah. Right.

“The extermination bounties,” I said.

He nodded.

“These count as mission completions when properly submitted.”

Ja’a looked mildly offended.

“But selling those to interested buyers would earn us significantly more gold than depositing them at an external guild branch.”

“That is true,” Raik admitted. “But rank progression has value of its own.”

Ja’a clicked her tongue but did not argue further.

“So,” Raik continued, “the question is simple. Do we stay another week and continue exploring the edges of the dead zone, or do we pack up and leave early?”

Ja’a answered immediately.

“Stay.”

Calr nodded.

“Agreed.”

That did not surprise me in the slightest. Ja’a leaned forward slightly.

“The loot has been excellent. The pearls, the metal, and especially the harvester,” she said. “And now that everyone has additional bags of holding, our carrying capacity is no longer a major issue.”

Calr nodded again.

“We are in a very profitable situation.”

Katar crossed his arms.

“I am willing to stay,” he said, “but only if we go west.”

“Why west?” I asked.

He gave me a bloodthirsty smile.

“Because the west is deeper into the dead zone,” he answered. “The further west we go, the stronger the threats. The stronger the threats, the better the fighting.”

That was extremely on brand for him.

Vena shook her head.

“I vote we return.”

She sat up a little straighter as everyone’s attention focused on her.

“I want to discuss something with the temple.”

“The Rift Gate?” I asked.

She nodded.

“The possibility of a stable path to the Mythic Realm is too important not to report quickly.”

That was fair, and actually a strong argument for leaving.

Raik turned toward Shingo.

“And you?”

Shingo thought for a second, then started shadowboxing and shook his head.

Then he dropped into squats and nodded.

Then back to shadowboxing.

Calr sighed.

“He wants to return to Hano and train more before doing anything significantly more dangerous.”

Shingo nodded vigorously.

Calr continued translating.

“His soul has been growing steadily, and he could significantly grow stronger if he focuses solely on training for a while.”

“As long as you watch your diet,” Katar gave him a stern look.

Shingo nodded at that.

Honestly, I was starting to get used to Shingo signing, even if it was mostly pantomime rather than an actual standardized language.

Raik finally looked at me.

“What about you?”

I considered it. Part of me did want to continue. The loot was great, the discoveries were fun, and I undeniably enjoyed the expedition.

But another part of me was increasingly aware that I had personal magical ideas I needed to explore.

“I vote we return,” I said.

Ja’a looked disappointed.

“Why?”

“I want uninterrupted time to work on my own magic,” I admitted. “I have been neglecting personal experimentation a bit too much.”

That was putting it mildly. My wish magic still needed work. I barely used my perception abilities at all, and I missed dreaming with Nina.

We looked at Kan, and she shrugged.

“I do not mind either way.”

Of course, she did not.

She was functionally adaptable to almost any environment and stoic enough not to need much. Plus, this expedition had given her a lot of quality time with her boyfriend.

That left all eyes on said boyfriend.

As team leader, the final decision was his. Raik sat quietly for a moment, considering the group.

Then he nodded.

“We return to Ectamel.”

Ja’a sighed dramatically. Calr looked mildly disappointed. Vena visibly relaxed. Katar simply shrugged as if all outcomes were equally acceptable. Shingo just nodded.

“That settles it,” Raik said. “Starting tomorrow, we pack up camp and head back.”

I leaned back, staring into the foggy sky.

Just like that, our dead zone expedition was over.

We had spent more than a week out here. Two weeks in Earth time.

We survived ridiculous situations, from undead to a cult ambush. We found absurd amounts of loot and nearly died repeatedly.

The standard adventuring experience, and now it was ending… Tomorrow, we will start packing.

Soon after, we would be back to civilization. To baths that did not require hauling water from rivers, to real food, and comfortable beds.

Overall, not a bad trade.

Still, I was going to miss this place a little.

Which probably said something concerning about the kind of person I was becoming.
