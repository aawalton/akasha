The cavern. Grenzheim district.

Hagen had been bent over the same sheet for most of an hour.

The survey lay across a folding table at the lower tier of the camp. Its corners were pinned with a lamp, an inkwell, and two stones. Academy draftsmen had drawn it after the first expedition failed. The ritual circle was black ink, each crystal seat marked in red, the channels between them traced in fine blue lines that met at the center like spokes on a wheel.

It was crude work.

Seven mages. Seven points of input. Every channel processed and carried mana from its seat to the next and handed it forward. The center took the sum and pushed it toward the Door.

Given a year and the right inscriptions, Hagen could have rebuilt it to run on four mages instead of seven. Possibly three.

He could think of ten different ways the ritual might have been built better. A cleaner channel geometry. Fewer redundant seats. A center that did not waste a third of its input as heat and light.

But he was not going to exhaust himself over it.

The Academy had designed the ritual. They would perform it. If they wanted to spend seven mages where three would do, that was their inefficiency to carry.

"—inspected twice," a voice was saying beside him. "And guarded between inspections. I will not have a repeat of last time."

Erzmagier Ottokar Sieghart had been saying some version of the same thing since the column left Grenzheim. He was past sixty, tall and narrow, with a beard trimmed so close to the jaw that it looked painted on. Academy robes the color of old wine. Six Circles.

This was his second expedition to the Door.

He had not stopped mentioning it.

"Last time, some fool let first-years polish the crystals." Sieghart's hand cut through the air. "First-years. Every seat cracked through the lattice. Hairline fractures. We did not find them until the ritual was already failing. A month of preparation, gone because a child pressed too hard with a cloth."

Hagen let him talk by himself.

It was easier than arguing, and Sieghart was right. The crystals had cost House Hexenzeit more than the column's entire fodder budget. A second failure would land on Hagen.

He straightened from the table.

The Door filled the far end of the cavern.

What did you lock away?

Nobody knew. That was the problem.

Still, Hagen had his hypotheses.

He suspected that even Roderich knew what waited behind the metal. Otherwise, the Archduke would not have been so insistent on sending part of the house army with the column. Two companies and the Magitek ranks were not an escort for scholars digging up old hinges.

For a few seconds, in the war room, Roderich had even offered to come himself.

Then the moment had passed, and the Archduke had returned to his maps as though he had never said it.

That was Roderich. A fire that could not settle on a temperature. Too hot one moment, cold the next. Explosive when Hagen expected calculation, calculating when Hagen braced for an explosion. After all these years at his side, Hagen still could not say what went on inside that man's head.

Hagen turned from the Door and looked at his soldiers.

Two companies of house troops held the middle tier. Behind them, the Magitek armor stood in a double rank near the approach ramp, tower shields grounded, staves crowned with blue crystal. The Academy had contributed its own mages: a dozen Fourth and Fifth Circle officers, research staff, and a gaggle of upper-year students who had fought for the privilege of being here and would spend most of it carrying equipment.

Near the gate, a squad of earth mages checked their gear by lamplight.

He studied them for a while.

"Master Hagen."

Captain Reinecke had come down the ramp at a pace just short of running.

"Captain."

"The Knights, sir. At the camp perimeter."

Hagen had already guessed.

He had guessed the moment the notice arrived at the column's command tent bearing the seal of Grenzheim's town hall.

Easy money mattered to everyone. It mattered most to a man trying to build an industry out of nothing. Kaspar had factories to feed now, a town to raise, researchers to pay. A dungeon sitting on his own land was not something he would leave to someone else's ledger.

"How many?"

"Six, sir. Five Knights and—" Reinecke's jaw tightened. "Him."

Nobody in this camp needed the name.

"He's asking for the commanding officer," Reinecke went on. "Says he holds a lawful claim on anything we recover and he intends to witness the record." He leaned closer. "Sir. Say the word. We have two companies. He has five men."

Hagen looked at him.

Reinecke had been at Kaltbach. Most of the officers here had. He understood the hatred they carried.

"Five men," he said.

"Yes, sir."

"Iron Cross Knights."

Reinecke's mouth closed.

"What will you do when the Chronicle asks why House Hexenzeit murdered the Hero of the Ridge on his own land, three weeks after he lost an arm taking ground from the elves?" Hagen kept his voice mild. "Do you want to be the man who explains that to the Archduke? Or should I?"

"He's not Hexenzeit anymore, sir. The Archduke said—"

"The Archduke was enraged." Hagen tapped the survey. "The Archduke cares more about results than what he said."

Reinecke's color deepened.

Hagen turned back toward the Door. He could make use of Kaspar.

If something beyond it killed him, the Chronicle would print a tragedy. A great loss. A hero fallen in the ruins he had insisted on entering. It would be sad, and it would be nobody's fault, and in a month the capital would be arguing about something else.

And if Kaspar survived?

Then he had been useful. His Knights were five more swords between whatever lived behind that metal.

And at the end, when the recovery record was read?

Hagen almost smiled.

Kaspar could not read the Ancient Language. Nobody could, not as good as them. Kaspar had no way to know which lump of old-world metal was a treasure and which was a hinge.

Hagen would give Kaspar something shiny.

A crate of salvage. Machine parts. Some intact artifact the Academy could not immediately explain and would sell to a collector for a pleasant sum. Kaspar would take it, sell it, and feel he had won something. He would sign the record acknowledging his share, and the claim would be closed.

Everyone would be satisfied. Some of them would even be correct.

"Bring him in," Hagen said.

Reinecke stared.

"Sir?"

"Bring him to the lower tier. Give him a position near the ritual site. Close enough to see the circle." Hagen folded the survey. "His Knights stay with him. If they want to stand between our people and the Door, let them."

"Near the ritual." Reinecke said it slowly, as if tasting the words for poison.

"He wants to witness the recovery. I can think of no better place to witness it from."

Reinecke went.

The crystals came out of their cases one at a time.

Each rode on a folded velvet pad carried in both hands by a Fourth Circle officer. Two more mages walked alongside every bearer. Sieghart stood at the center of the circle and received each report aloud before allowing the stone to be seated.

"North seat. Lattice clean. Pulse steady."

"Seat it."

"Northeast seat."

The crystals were palm-sized and blue, with slow mist-currents moving inside them. When the officers settled them into the carved hollows, the inked channels on the floor warmed faintly.

Hagen watched from the edge of the circle.

Six mages knelt at their places. Fourth and Fifth Circle, the best the Academy had provided. Sieghart would take the center.

Boots approached from behind him.

Reinecke came first. Two house officers flanked the column behind him, hands carefully away from their wands. Behind them walked the Knights.

A giant carrying a slab of iron the length of a man, scarred scalp shining in the lamplight. A lean blond in leather armor with pale eyes. A grinning man in mismatched plate. A second, quieter one in polished armor. At the front of them, a commander with a scarred face and a metal hand of his own.

And in the middle, Kaspar.

Kaspar was bigger than Hagen remembered. His hair was bone-white and long enough to tie back.

He walked carefully. A slight shortening of the stride on the left.

The right sleeve of his coat had been folded and pinned back at the elbow.

Beneath it, black metal.

A prosthetic. Dark overlapping plates. Fine glyphs in shallow channels. The hand hung at his side, fingers slightly curled, and it didn't move with his step the way a living hand would.

So the Chronicle had that part right.

Kaspar stopped two paces away.

"Brother," he said.

Hagen's thumb creased the corner of the survey.

Nobody called him that.

Alrik called him Hagen. The servants said Master Hagen. Roderich said Hagen. Saskia, since the wedding, had not yet called him anything at all.

Brother.

The word belonged to a different family.

Hagen kept his smile.

"Kaspar," he said.

Just the name. No title. No house. Warm enough to pass for courtesy.

For a moment, something moved behind Kaspar's eyes.

It was very small. Hagen might have imagined it.

Did you say it to see what I'd do?

Hagen could not tell.

"My notice reached you," Kaspar said.

"It did. You'll have a position here." Hagen gestured toward a stretch of floor beside a stone pillar, ten paces from the circle's edge. "You can see the ritual and the Door. When we recover anything, the record will be kept in the command tent. You or your representative may witness each entry."

"Thank you."

Kaspar's gaze went to the Door.

"How long," Kaspar asked, "before you begin?"

"Soon."

"It's night outside."

"It is." Hagen had expected the question. "The column reached the site yesterday. We spent today preparing and waiting on the crystals. They're rested. There's nothing to be gained by losing another night."

He watched Kaspar's face for a reaction.

He had been half-expecting an objection. A demand to delay. Hagen had prepared answers for all of them.

Kaspar only nodded.

"Then I'll take my position."

He turned and walked toward the pillar.

His Knights went with him. The giant planted his iron slab against the floor. The blond moved to the pillar's far side, out of the lamplight. The grinning one said something too quiet for Hagen to hear, and the commander answered with a single word.

Kaspar stood at the pillar with his back to the stone.

Hagen looked at him a moment longer.

Then he turned away. Whatever Kaspar meant to do beside that pillar, it could wait.

Hagen had a ritual to begin.

"Positions."

The word went through the lower tier.

Sieghart walked into the center of the circle.

He raised his arms.

Six rings flared into light along his forearms.

A few of the soldiers watching from the middle tier murmured.

There it is.

Hagen had been waiting for it all day.

The six kneeling mages placed their palms on the floor.

"Begin," Hagen said.

Sieghart lowered his hands.

The channels woke.

Light ran out from the center in six blue lines, reached the crystals, and came back doubled.

The light gathered at the center.

Then it moved.

It crawled across the stone toward the Door, a slow blue tide, and when it touched the base of the metal the runes nearest the floor ignited.

Row by row. Rune by rune. It raced upward faster than the eye could follow until the entire surface was burning with pale light, a wall of script so bright it threw shadows backward across the camp.

A sound came through the stone.

Deep. Grinding. Hagen felt it through the soles of his boots before he heard it.

A seam appeared down the center of the Door.

Air came through the gap.

Beyond it lay a corridor of carved stone pillars, broad enough to march a company through abreast.

"First company," Hagen said. "Advance."

The sergeants took it up.

Boots moved. The column stepped forward in good order, wands raised, the first ranks passing between the opening halves of the Door into the pillared dark. The Magitek ranks followed a dozen paces behind. Iris went with her squad along the left flank, one hand already resting on the stone of the first pillar as though listening to it.

Hagen let out a breath.

Under House Hexenzeit's command, on the first night, the Door had opened.

He could already see the report.

Behind him, someone choked.

Hagen turned.

All seven mages. One after another, lifted from their places as though pulled by their necks.

Hagen's thoughts went white.

It was not a spell he recognized. It was not any structure he could read. There was no caster.

What is happening?

He turned without deciding to.

His eyes went to the pillar.

Kaspar had not moved from it.

He was looking up, into the dark above the cavern, and his left hand had already drawn his sword.
