Rick reached the trunk, threw himself at it, and climbed. The bark tore at his palms. His boots slipped once, then found purchase again. Behind him, the boar crashed through brush with blind fury, its weight shaking the ground hard enough that he felt it through the tree. The squealing grew louder and harsher, until it seemed the wounded beast would smash straight into the trunk and bring the whole thing down with him still halfway up it.

So Rick did not look back. He climbed until his arms burned, until his chest felt tight with effort and fear, until the sounds below changed.

A heavy crash.

Then silence.

Not true silence, since the forest still retained its breathing rhythm. Leaves stirring,  insects singing, somewhere farther off, something small moved through undergrowth. But the enraged pursuit had ended, and the sudden absence of it felt strange and unsettling.

Only then did Rick dare glance down. The boar had collapsed beside the roots, its body twitching in intense, uneven spasms. One eye was a ruined mess around the broken spear shaft. Foam flecked its mouth. Its legs jerked and scraped weakly at the earth, but there was no strength left in them.

For a moment, Rick only stared. Then relief hit him so abruptly that he nearly lost his balance.

It worked. His backup plan had actually worked.

He pressed one arm around the trunk and let out a shaky breath. The idea had come from the beastiarium too. Not only the trap or the weak point in the underbelly, but this part as well. The book had explained that earth essence beasts were difficult to harm from outside because their bodies were reinforced against blunt force, cuts, and even many ordinary piercing attacks. But inside was different. Internal contact with opposing essence could provoke a much stronger reaction, especially if that essence had been introduced through a wound.

Fire, in this case.

The beastiarium had also explained why such beasts were so wary of food touched by fire essence. It was not caution learned from intelligence, but from the body itself. Their instincts rejected what might harm them from within. Which meant baiting the boar into swallowing anything useful had never been a real option.

So Rick had chosen a different approach. Before setting the trap, he had gathered fire-aspected herbs and made a simple extract from them. Not much. He lacked proper tools, better ingredients, and the skill to refine it well. Even while coating the spear tip, he had known it might not be enough. The wound might be too shallow, the concentration too weak, or the effect too slow.

The book had given him the logic, but not certainty.

He watched the beast a little longer, making sure it was not somehow about to rise again. That thought lasted a second. A creature smart enough to pretend death in this situation would have never fallen for into the trap in the first place.

Rick waited until his legs steadied, then climbed down carefully.

He checked the area first, of course. A wounded beast’s screams could draw trouble. Nothing had answered immediately, but that meant little in the forbidden forest. Time was at the essence.

When he approached the boar, he did so with his knife already in hand. Up close, the underbelly weakness was easier to see than it had been in the beastiarium sketches. The hide there was softer, thinner, less like plated earth and more like living flesh. The stakes had torn through that area generously, though not deeply enough to finish the beast outright.

He also noticed something else. The beast did not appear to be feeding young.

It would have sat badly with him to leave offspring starving because of this hunt. He killed beasts for work, survival, and advancement, not from hatred. Most of them were simply living the way the world had created them to live. Better, Rick thought, if they had time to breed and feed before returning to the forest in another form.

The boar gave one more weak, ragged sound.

Rick lowered himself slightly and ended it with a clean cut to the throat after offering a silent prayer. The Gods gave, and the living took. That was the order of things.

Then practical concerns returned all at once. He had brought down a real essence beast. A large one. Which was excellent, but also a problem.

Rick spent the next stretch of time working quickly and with as much care as he could manage. Blood, meat, hide, bones, organs—almost all of it had value. In an essence beast, even parts that would be discarded from common animals could be sold for food,crafting, or rituals. Waste was not something he could afford.

By the time he finished processing what he could on site, the scale of the task had become obvious. The haul weighed more than three hundred kilograms.

Rick stared at it in disbelief for a few moments. If someone had told him a month earlier that one day he would worry more about transporting loot than killing it, he would have laughed in their face. But here he was.

The obvious solutions were beyond him. There were artifacts for carrying weight, tools to lighten loads, even transport devices wealthy adventurers used without a second thought. Rick owned none of them, and he would not own any soon. The other option was to take only the most valuable parts and abandon the rest.

He rejected that immediately. Every piece of this hunt meant coin, and he needed them all. Anne had been right about that too. Progress was not built from one great victory alone, but from making full use of it.

So he thought instead. Essence materials spoiled slower than common meat, and slower still if they remained in an area rich in similar energy. That gave him room. Not much, but enough. He dragged the processed parts toward one of the shelters he had marked during his exploration and stored them there as neatly as he could, sealing the opening afterward. It would not stop a determined beast, but it would help against accidental discovery and ordinary scavengers.

As he worked, he calculated. He could carry about eighty kilograms at a time without exhausting himself. More than that and he would move too slowly, tire too fast, and make mistakes. That meant at least four heavy trips. Going all the way to town would be stupid. But Anne’s bookstore stood between the forest and Mountainend. If she allowed it, he could use it as a midpoint.

That would save him enormous time. From the shelter nearest the Wall to the bookstore, he estimated roughly thirty kilometers. At a fast pace, burdened, perhaps five hours. More likely six. Then rest, return light, sleep in the shelter, and repeat. If he pushed hard enough, he could finish in two days.

Rick did not wait until morning. The forest would become any safer, and he did not want to leave valuable essence materials unattended longer than necessary. By now he knew the paths well enough. After two weeks of mapping, recording landmarks, and noting beast activity, he no longer feared becoming lost every time the light dimmed. He still respected the danger, but that was different.

So he lifted the first load and started moving.

The trip took a little over six hours.

Longer than he had hoped, but not disastrously so. The weight dragged at his shoulders and hips. Sweat soaked his clothes. More than once he had to stop, breathe, and force himself not to think about how many hours of the same misery still waited ahead of him. But when the bookstore finally came into sight, the relief was almost enough to erase the ache.

He arrived while it was still open.

Anne looked up the moment he entered and, to Rick’s quiet satisfaction, her eyes narrowed when she saw what he carried.

“Mr. Rick,” she said, stepping around the counter. “That looks promising.”

Promising was a mild word for it. Rick was too tired to explain much, so he only gave her the short version. The boar, the trap, and the transport problem. Could he leave the materials here for a day or two while he retrieved the rest?

Anne agreed without hesitation.

Naturally, she did not do so lightly. She asked brisk questions about quantity, condition, and likely timing, then directed him toward a suitable storage space with practical calm. Rick had expected no less from the bookstore owner.

The brief rest she allowed him felt too short, but it had to be enough. By the time he started back, the night already seemed thinner somehow, less oppressive than before. He knew where he was going.  He even jogged part of the return.

Rick slept in the shelter, woke a few hours before dawn, and began again.

The second day was worse. His body had understood the work after the first trip, and now it protested every step. Still, he finished by noon. The last of the boar’s useful remains had reached the bookstore, and Rick stood there feeling as though his bones had been filled with sand.

He wanted nothing more than to collapse into bed and sleep until the next season.

Instead, he stayed. There was still business to settle.

Before renting a cart, he negotiated with Anne. Half the blood and ten kilograms of the meat stayed with her. In exchange, he did not ask for coin, but reading credit.

Anne approved of that immediately. After a little calculation, the trade became six more lends of the Beastiarium Generalis.

That alone made the entire ordeal feel worthwhile.

Then Rick returned to town, rented a cart, and came back for the stored materials. Once again he had to crush the urge to go home first. Sleep could wait a little longer. Profit could not.

Mountainend had no guild to absorb a haul like this, and the old inn was too empty to serve the purpose. That left the market. So Rick drove the cart there and announced what he had.

The response was immediate. Merchants and shop managers flocked toward him almost at once. Curiosity first, then hunger. Essence beast materials always drew attention in a town starved for opportunity. Less than half an hour later, the deal was done. Rick left the market with one gold coin and forty kilograms of meat.

Forty kilograms he had deliberately kept back. No matter how often he fought with his family, he was not about to bring home nothing after a success like this. Some would be for them. Some, he knew with almost complete certainty, his mother would divide and send out to relatives before sunset. That was simply how such things worked.

For the first time in a very long while, Rick felt eager to go home. He wanted to see his father’s face when he dropped that much meat onto the kitchen table. Would the old man still call hunting a fool’s path? Would he still sneer and ask what use Rick’s wandering had ever been? Would he finally, at last, look at his son and feel something like pride?

As Rick walked, the answers lost some of their old weight.

He had wanted praise for so long that he had mistaken it for necessity. But somewhere between the bookstore, the beastiarium, the mapped forest, and the dead boar dragged back through sheer effort, something had shifted. His father’s judgment no longer felt like the heaviest one.

His own did. And for once, it was enough.

Still, Rick admitted to himself as he adjusted the sack of meat on his shoulder, hearing a few of those words would be nice.
