Ilara’s father shouted, his voice echoing from his audience chambers. Ilara sat in her private room, trying to decide if she should go see what the problem was.

She already knew. Tavian was making his move. It would be time for fighting, soon.

A spike of stone the size of her forearm shot from over her shoulder, burying itself in the wall. A dozen other spikes were buried there, from her earlier experimentation with the new spell. Unlike the much simpler [Projectile] spell, which only fired magical essence, [Lancet] formed that essence into matter.

She’d get another chance at Tavian tonight.

Ilara ran her fingertips over the rough scar on her cheek. The cooling salve stung as she rubbed it in. Her face would be red and swollen for at least a few more days, according to the healers. She traced the scar up her cheek, over her eyelid, and through her eyebrow.

The poison on her blade had nearly killed her; had she not been strengthened by her Class and had the will power to escape from Tavian’s chambers, she’d be another of Farl’s—her father’s—ghouls now.

They said she was lucky to be alive. She said Tavian was the lucky one. His sword still rested on a table nearby. It was a good sword. Ilara liked it. It reminded her of the job she still had to finish.

Her eyes flicked to the mirror in the corner of the room. She’d covered it with a cloth. She couldn’t bear to look at herself, not like this. She knew that her beauty would be permanently marred.

Ilara didn’t blame Tavian for it. He was handsome, and friendly, if a bit naive. She’d been the one who tried to kill him first, and he’d only been defending himself. He even had the gall to look ashamed when he’d wounded her.

And then he’d gone and killed Anax. Ilara felt a cold numbness in her body when she thought about her dead brother. She couldn’t let herself unleash the white hot anger, buried somewhere inside. Not yet.

Something wet dripped from her swollen eyelid. She wiped it away, gathering strength.

Her father’s shouts continued echoing outside.

She finished applying her salve, and intentionally did not reapply the wound covering. Farl Anarvia needed to face the consequences of his actions. He’d done this to her, through his own inadequacy. Ilara was beginning to wonder if the oaf of a man could really have sired her.

Ilara replaced the lid of the salve pot and rose.

Before she left, she buckled Tavian’s sword around her waist. She’d had to punch a new hole in the belt to make it fit her. If she got the chance, it might be nice to finish Tavian with his own blade.

She hit the doorframe as she exited her room, and cursed, rubbing her shoulder.

She walked down the hall, trying her best to move straight. It was difficult to judge distances with only one functioning eye.

Her black gown flared behind her as she walked down the hall to Farl’s audience chambers. She’d been laid up in bed for too long, recovering from her wound. Farl had made some questionable decisions, without her there to consult.

The men-at-arms guarding the door stepped aside as she approached, keeping their eyes respectfully fixed to the ground.

Ilara waltzed into the chamber. A cold, silvery spike of metal jutted up from the floor in the center of the room. A trickle of her father’s [Mages] entered through a far door, each carrying a valuable in their hands. One by one they walked to the core, knelt, extracted essence from their offering, and pressed it into the core.

Despite the constant flow of essence, her father’s influence was only increasing slowly. He had overextended his domain, and increased the burn rate of his core.

Farl sat atop a partially completed dais in a partially completed throne. He held a crystal globe in his two hands, the size of his head, staring into it. The artifact pulsated with miasmic energy.

Halin, the coresmith, stood nearby, muttering. Sweet lies, probably.

Farl shouted something, his face red with anger.

Ilara approached, standing before her father’s seat. It wasn’t a throne, not yet. Not until the man ruled over Tar Corinath could he call it that.

It was time for her to reassert herself. Halin had too much influence while she’d been recovering.

“Halin, if you would,” Ilara said, gesturing meaningfully. “I wish to speak to my father.”

Farl raised his eyes to look at his daughter. His gaze bounced away from her face, scowling as he stared at her bare shoulders. She pushed her hair back, letting it tumble behind her. She had to remind the man that she wasn’t a girl anymore. He looked past her at a distant part of the room, unwilling to look her in the eye.

“You look cold, girl,” Farl said. “Have you forgotten your cloak?”

"Look at me,” she demanded. “See what your inadequacies and Master Halin’s whisperings have wrought.”

Farl lifted his gaze, meeting her good eye. The other peered out beneath an angrily swollen lid, an unseeing, milk-white marble.

“What do you want?” he asked her, waving Halin away. The coresmith lifted the crystal Witherstone from Farl’s hands, and placed it on a velvet tray, which he carried off the dais. He stood near the wall, around ten paces away, waiting.

Ilara mounted the dais and approached her father. “I hear from the messengers that our reinforcements are no more than a day and a half away, perhaps closer. They’ll be surrounding the city soon. Why are you so distressed?”

“Because, Ilara,” Farl snapped. “Your friends aren’t going to be interested in a ruler who doesn’t rule Tar Corinath. As long as Tavian lives, he confuses the matter.”

“They are mercenaries,” Ilara said. “They only have use for your gold. You haven’t sifted their payments into essence have you?”

Farl shook his head. “I’m not a fool.”

“Perhaps not,” Ilara said. She gestured at her face, shot a glance toward Halin. “But you might be listening to one.”

“I know you think you’re so much wiser than the master coresmith,” Farl said. “But what have you brought me? Suffering? Misery? I negotiated a wedding for you with Tavian Veros. If you’d just had a little patience, you’d soon be queen in waiting. At least he brought me the Witherstone.”

They were both looking at the coresmith. He pretended not to notice.

“Never compare me to him,” Ilara snapped. “I am your own flesh and blood! If you hadn’t listened to Halin then I wouldn’t have this.” She thrust her face toward him, motioning to her scar.

Farl flinched away from her. He knew she was right. If he hadn’t carved off men to go searching the fortress for Halin, they’d have reached and destroyed the core on time.

“Tavian would be dead," Ilara snarled. “He wouldn’t have been able to—her voice caught strangely in her throat—to kill Anax. You’d have no House Veros to worry about, if you had stuck to my plan.”

“Mandros is still imprisoned in Tavirost Fortress,” Farl snapped, unwilling to address her accusation. He pretended not to even care. “We couldn’t even protect the man they sent ahead. The Karath Amon are going to arrive; see a city for the taking, and loot everything. We need to be ready for them.”

“And terrorizing the population with ghouls is your solution to that?” Ilara asked. “Mandros’s capture is his own mistake.”

Farl ignored her. “Your fiance is causing problems again,” he said. “He’s sending carts full of soldiers out into the city.”

“It’s a trick,” Ilara said.

“If he manages to burn those supplies, I’ll be forced to start drawing back our territory,” Farl said. “We won’t be able to sustain the burn rate. What will it look like when your friends show up on our doorstep, only to find our house burning to the ground?”

“Strength is irrelevant,” Ilara said. “It’s the appearance of strength that matters. And when the Karath Amon arrive, nobody will be able to challenge you. Tavian will be crushed.” Which was a shame, really. She respected his passion, fighting recklessly against the overwhelming odds of a mightier foe. He simply had the bad luck of standing between her and her goal.

Farl pressed his head into his hands. “Will they demand their remaining payment up front? Will they want to see proof of ability to pay?”

“You’re missing the forest for the trees,” Ilara said.

A messenger boy slipped into the room, looking around worriedly. He spotted Halin and approached, whispering something softly to him.

Halin was doing something with the Witherstone. He waved his hand around, worry creasing his brow.

“What are you doing with that?” Ilara asked.

Halin jumped, then hurried back up the dais.

“My lord,” he said, “You need to do something about this. There’s a fire burning down there, and it looks like Tavian himself is leading the charge.”

“Then we need to seize him!” Farl said, springing to his feet in a passion. He grabbed the Witherstone from Halin.

Ilara reached out, placing a hand on his forearm.

“Don’t try and stop me,” Farl said, pulling away from her. “It would have been better to keep the Karath Amon out of this. And even now, thanks to the Witherstone, we can control the city, and stop Veros.”

Halin smiled as he felt the political favor tilting his direction.

Ilara glared at him. “Don’t,” she said to her father.

“You’re just rehashing the past. None of that matters now,” he said. “What’s done is done. We need to control this city before the Karath Amon arrive, and Veros has just given us a perfect opportunity for that.”

“I’m telling you, it’s a trap,” Ilara said.

“My lord,” Halin pushed. “If you can surround Tavian with your forces, he’ll be totally trapped. But you have to act fast.”

Farl glared at his daughter. Ilara crossed her arms.

“You do nothing to help, and simply work to undermine me,” he said. “How is this a trap?”

“You haven’t asked yourself what Donath is doing,” she said. They’d received word that Donath had fled to the fortress.

“Does it matter, my lord?” Halin asked. “When you have a chance to capture or kill Tavian? You must demonstrate your control over the city.”

Ilara shrugged.

Farl pursed his lips. “You’re right,” he said. “We have to get him before the Karath Amon arrive. Nothing else matters.”

He leaned in, using the Witherstone to direct his ghouls. “I’m doing what I need to do to protect you,” he said. “To protect our family. One day you’ll understand.”

As if he cared about his family. What he had left of a family. Anax was dead.

Ilara fought her urge to strike the man. Instead, she watched the man, staring into his magical stone, hoping for its power to save him. His faith in that stone had led directly to her disfigurement and her brother’s death.

Farl glanced at his daughter.

He looked pathetic, looking to her for reassurance like that. He didn’t deserve to win. He didn’t deserve her.

Farl was a lost cause. He couldn’t deliver her the power she desired. He’d already marred her beauty, and he was currently burning their wealth, extracting essence from it to hide his tactical mistakes. He was about to make the same mistake again. Her skin prickled at the thought.

She reached out and brushed a hair from his shoulder. Should she spell out the trap for him? Or did he deserve to fall into it, unwilling as he was to recognize her talents? She could save him.

“You’re right, my lord,” she said, demurely. Ilara would not throw her griffin feathers to the ghouls, not any longer. “For all your failings, you’ve done your best for our family. You don’t have to worry about the Karath Amon.”

Farl shot another nervous gaze toward her, but he visibly relaxed. He let himself be soothed by her words.

He was pulling ghouls away from all parts of the city, sending them swarming in to capture the boy he was afraid of.

Ilara was going to have to seize power herself.

She moved away from her father, as he feverishly directed his forces. Watching the doors to the hall, Ilara waited for the messenger she knew was coming.

It didn’t take long.

“My lord,” a page exclaimed, bursting into the hall and startling a mage at the kingdom core. “Enemies at the gates! It’s Donath!”
