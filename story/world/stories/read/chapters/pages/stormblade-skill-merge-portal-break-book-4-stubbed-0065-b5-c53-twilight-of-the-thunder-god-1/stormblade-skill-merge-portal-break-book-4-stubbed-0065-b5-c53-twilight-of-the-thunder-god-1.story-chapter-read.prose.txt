The last thing either of us wanted was…this. Whatever this was.

I raged against the mental space we’d found ourselves in. A single fire burned between us, its coals all but dying. Its flames didn’t even flicker. Nothing moved. The embers didn’t pulse with the light of something in motion.

“This is a trap,” I thought out loud. Words didn’t carry in the void, and even if they had, neither of us could move to speak them. But at the same time, we didn’t need them.

“Yes. Yes, it’s a trap. But I don’t think it’s one set by the God of Thunder. Let’s have a look and see what we can find out.”

There didn’t seem to be much to see. At first, only the dying fire lit the void, and the void seemed featureless and infinite. No sounds. No written words. No pillars of portal metal, no lightning pouring from the sky like rain. And the void—for all that it was one—was also shadowless. A gray, featureless space. But as I peered into the expanse, Ellen’s Path lit up for a fraction of a second in the sky behind her.

“I see your Path. The markers for each of your Laws,” she spoke into my mind. “They lead here.”

I swallowed. Or tried to. Motion didn’t exist here. Not in the void. The only thing that existed here was…

Pressure.

I had a very bad feeling that I already knew exactly what this place was, and exactly the way out of it.

“I refuse,” I said into the void.

“Refuse what?”

“To play this game.”

There had to be a way out. In our portal world, Ellen held the Stormshade Hammer high, ready to crash it down on the God of Thunder—and on my Stormshade Anvil. We needed to get back. We needed only to reach out, and victory was ours. But whatever this was—and I had a horrific guess as to exactly what the trial in front of us entailed—it’d ruin our opportunity.

I wished I could sit down. Cross-legged and meditating was the best way to confront the conundrum before us. To confront the ever-growing pressure building as the void exerted its influence.

“We’re about to hit SS-Rank, and I don’t think we can stop it at this point. I have no idea how it happened. It shouldn’t have happened—none of our skills are ready to push that far, and it’s too fast even if they were. We should have had a way out. There needs to be a way out. I swore I wouldn’t—“

“I know. I was there. You said you’d never push to SS-Rank. But if it’s happening, it’s not your fault.” Somehow, Ellen’s words carried the same weight as her hand on my back would have. “We’re both in this, though. And we both know what’s at stake.”

I tried and failed to nod. “We do. Only one of us is…”

“Only one of us is coming out of here, if the universe has any say in it.” Though she couldn’t move, Ellen’s skin went pale as blood rushed from her face. “We’re so far past what I’ve read of Dual Skill Advancement that…yes, this could be the end state of it. Dual Skill Advancement could be a shortcut to reach SS-Rank. But if that’s the case, then we’ve been locked into this end since that alien town.”

“Roswell.”

“Yes. Roswell. I refuse to believe that this is the only inevitable outcome.”

I also didn’t want to believe it. But here we were. The pressure. A burned-out fire. A forge that didn’t yet exist. And a hammer and anvil that could be used to start it again.

We fell silent. More silent than it already was. My anger burned inside of me—not against the God of Thunder, but against the universe. In order to pass this test and learn whatever Law there was to learn here—in order to reach SS-Rank—the fire had to be lit. In order to light the fire, a core had to be placed inside. We had two cores between us.

The math was brutal, honest, and clear.

Could I kill Ellen? Could she kill me? That was the obvious answer—the answer the math required.

“No. I won’t do it. We’ll find another way,” Ellen said, breaking the silence.

Another way. There was always another way. Always a way out of any trap we’d run across, to power through any setback we’d encountered. We simply needed to find this one.

I looked inward, inspecting the Stormblade Core. Its bands of portal metal rotated around a sphere of darkness and storm, lightning in green-black, white and blue, and the sinister orange-red color crackling inside. The wind howled around it, and rain splattered the bands from every angle as the weather shifted by the second. But what interested me the most wasn’t the core as a whole. Deep inside, at the center of the roiling stormshadow, lay a void.

A void much like this one.

I pondered the space. It had never existed before now, but I hadn’t felt it forming, either. Was it a weakness in my core? A failure of the Laws I’d learned? Or…

My consciousness zoomed in on it. And I laughed.

“It’s the space for other beings’ cores and Mana,” I said. Then I slowly explained through the laughter exactly what had to happen.

We had a way forward—a way forward that was only possible because, in so many ways, the Stormblade Path and Void Garden had grown together. The portal world. Our shared, Dual Skill Progression. The physical, emotional, and spiritual intimacy Ellen and I had built together.

“You’re sure? If we do this, it’ll be the end. Our cores won’t accept any more power. And there’s no guarantee that the pressure won’t push us to try ascending, anyway.” Ellen asked.

I nodded. This time, my head moved.

She reached across the fire. Her hands met mine. And, over the dying sparks of the Stormshade Forge, I opened room inside the void in my core for everything Ellen had put into hers. Her spells. Her Laws. The very essence of the Void Garden, and of her Shadow Core. All of it surged into me in a wave of energy and emotion. Fear. Bitterness. Frustration. Every emotion that had guided her down this path when her system awakened.

It all merged with mine. My Stormblade Core. The Path that bore the same name. My anger and rage and that sense of justice that put me at odds with bullies all the way from the playground to Bob Traynor to the Placid King. Nestled inside of that, Ellen’s core reformed, safe inside of mine.

Leaving us with three.

“Whatever cost we have to pay, it’s worth it,” Ellen said. “The God of Thunder has to be beaten. He has to be beaten here.”

“And we both have to live,” I finished.

Then we shattered the spare core, and it exploded in a burst of flame that lit the forge around us.

There was no way forward. No more steps to take on the Path. This was the end.

At least, that’s what I told myself, and I knew Ellen was trying hard to convince herself, too.

The God of Thunder waited.

His body—despite being at the pinnacle of what this universe could support, even with his Mana batteries shattered and his portal world obliterated—couldn’t move faster than reality would allow.  His mind had no such restraints.

The plan had been shockingly simple.

A being like the God of Thunder could store a, for lack of a better phrase on his part, ungodly amount of Mana in his core. More Mana than someone like Kade Noelstra or the Shadow Witch had ever experienced, more cores worth of power than they could comprehend. As his world died to the Shadow Witch’s sneak attack, he’d gathered all that power inside of him and crashed through the portal into their shared world. Then, he’d simply bled off the excess power into their Lightning Tower.

It was a disgusting amount of power.

But it was worth the temporary sacrifice. The God of Thunder hadn’t reached SS-Plus Rank, hadn’t gone through twenty thousand years worth of pupils, without hunting every conceivable type of Paragon. And he’d drawn on his experience with a pair of twins for this trap.

Isolde and Ingmar. The Hammer and Anvil. Paragons of the Thunderdrum Path—deviants much like Kade Noelstra himself, but unlike him, they’d been a pair in the God of Thunder’s service. He’d hunted them slowly, over months. Landed a lethal blow on Ingmar. Somehow, he’d survived, and in surviving, he’d gathered enough power that both he and his sister had reached the cusp of SS-Rank.

The universe never liked that.

It had forced them to choose, and after an instant eternity, Isolde had come out of the world-freezing trance. She’d come out an SS-Ranker, crushed by the same pressure that the God of Thunder had first felt when he reached SS-Rank himself and threw Stormlord Nalthelas’s corpse into the forest below his ruined fortress—and crushed by what she’d had to do to her Dual Skill Progression partner and brother. From there, she’d been shockingly easy to defeat.

Kade would survive the trap. The kid was a survivor; it was one of the things the God of Thunder liked so much about his newest, most interesting prey.

But he’d come out broken. The pressure would have forced him to kill his partner. It would already be working on him, showing him the path to power that lay through turning his own homeworld into a source of Mana and cores.

And from there…

From there…

The God of Thunder blinked.

He moved.

Something was wrong. It shouldn’t have taken this long. It should have taken longer. The Shadow Witch had to be dead, but had Kade killed her too quickly or to slowly? Either way, the God of Thunder’s body caught up with his mind—and with what his eyes were seeing.

Kade Noelstra. The Stormblade. His aura screamed SS-Rank. He’d reached the pinnacle, the highest point he’d ever reached. Part of the God of Thunder salivated at the thought of his core, even as a greater—though still small—part of him knew it’d be the beginning of a long, slow march back to the cusp of ascension.

But those small parts were drowned out by an emotion the God of Thunder hadn’t truly felt since he sat trapped in Stormlord Nalthelas’s contraption.

Fear.

Because of the Shadow Witch’s aura. But more than that, because against all odds, despite everything he’d done, Kade wasn’t alone. And because that pressure he couldn’t help but bow under?

It didn’t seem to push on either of them.
