The sisters Savomi and Isomi stood together, and Tesyb had somehow found his way next to Savomi again. Kivamus noticed the way Tesyb kept flicking his eyes toward her when he thought no one would notice. Good for him.

The senior guard Kerel was also present with his usual strict expression, standing straight and looking as if the gathered crowd was a thing he intended to keep in order by glare alone. Nesser stood off to one side near the newer recruits. Even from a distance Kivamus could see the way some of them kept glancing at him and then quickly looking away, already dreading whatever the next drill would be. Meanwhile, Yufim, the self-proclaimed best archer of Tiranat, seemed to have no such worries. He was talking to a maid with the same easy confidence he always had, his good looks giving him attention from the other gender wherever he went. Calubo's cousin Nurobo—the former archer who had lost a good amount of weight in the past months and had become the head medic of the manor—stood nearby with the other medic apprentices, speaking in his usual practical way.

And at the edge of the crowd, waiting for him, stood Hudan. His numerous scars and huge, muscular build made it easy to recognize him even from a distance. His habit of always remaining clean shaved hadn't changed from his younger years when he was training to become a knight in Cinran. It hadn't worked out for him there, for various reasons, but it meant that Tiranat now had a strong and reliable guard captain.

The captain noticed him first.

“The baron is here!” Hudan called out loudly.

The packed crowd shifted at once. A path opened through the middle, not neatly, but enough. Hudan came to Kivamus’s left as they advanced. Duvas moved to his right, the satchel held securely by both his hands.

By the time they reached the center of the crowd, Kivamus saw that a makeshift stage had been put together out of a few sturdy benches. It was plain, a little uneven, but good enough. He stepped up first, then Hudan and Duvas followed him onto it.

The crowd grew quiet in a way that made the whole place feel different. Most eyes had already gone to the satchel in Duvas’s hand. They knew exactly what was inside.

Kivamus let the silence settle for another moment before speaking. He knew the crowd wanted a speech for him, so a speech was exactly what they would get. It had taken a long time to get used to this kind of attention, but at this point, he couldn't say that he disliked it.

“My dear Tiranati people, we have come a long way since last autumn,” he began.

His voice carried well enough over the crowd that even those on the roofs leaned in instead of calling for him to speak louder.

“At that time, Torhan raided this village, and we could do nothing to stop him. He burned half the village, and escaped unscathed. Many of you did not even have a roof over your heads when winter came.”

The crowd stayed quiet. A few faces tightened. Most of the new guards had lived through that fire themselves.

“But we did not give up,” Kivamus continued. “We kept trying. We kept working. All of you kept working. The villagers kept working. We rebuilt, we dug, we cut, we carried, and we kept moving forward.”

He paused just long enough.

“And now, after all that work, we have finally reached the day where, from today onward, you all will be paid directly in coins.”

The reaction hit the training ground all at once.

A huge roar went up from the crowd. Guards shouted, maids clapped, some of the servants grinned widely, and those standing on the roof of the servants’ hall stamped their feet hard enough that Kivamus could hear it above the cheering. The guards sitting on the barn roof raised their fists and shouted as loudly as they could toward the stage.

Kivamus waited, smiling, until the noise dropped enough for him to continue. It took a while though.

“Many of you already know what happened in the counter raid against Torhan,” he said. “A lot of you were there yourselves. But I’m saying it anyway for those who weren’t present.”

The crowd quieted again, though the mood remained bright.

“Our guards attacked Torhan’s clay mining compound near Kirnos. They destroyed it. They burned the buildings. They freed the slaves there and brought them back here as free people and new workers for this village.”

There were more cheers at that, along with a spread of low murmurs through the maids and servants.

“And lastly,” Kivamus said, with a small hard smile, “they brought back a good amount of gold.”

That drew a louder reaction than before. Some of them clapped, others cheered. A few people roared in delight immediately. Others began whispering to those beside them.

“The exact amount,” Kivamus went on, “and the details of it are for me and Duvas to manage. But this much I will say—last autumn, it was Torhan who burned half this village and stole the village's savings. This time, it is again Torhan because of whom we are finally able to begin these payments.”

That broke the crowd up completely.

People laughed at the bandit chief. Guards jeered loudly at the man. Someone yelled a curse toward the west as if Torhan might somehow hear it from there. Another voice shouted, “Next time we won’t leave him alive!”

That drew another burst of approval and shouting.

Kivamus let it run for a few moments, then raised a hand.

When the noise lowered again, he continued, “Duvas has already told you about the wages you will receive. The deduction will remain a standard amount of 4 coppers a day to account for the cost of food and the other provisions for everyone living in the manor, whether you serve as a guard, a maid or as a servant.”

He looked across the crowd as he spoke, making sure everyone was able to hear him clearly.

“For now, we are only providing lodging to around half of the guards here in the manor. The rest of you are still living in the longhouse block. But within around a month, 2 new barracks will be built east of the manor.”

That caught their attention at once.

“They will be smaller than the longhouse blocks, and they will be built only for guards. One for the men. One for the women.”

The cheer that followed was immediate and loud. Guards on the roofs shouted down at the stage. Several of the women on the ground grinned openly. Even Kerel’s face seemed to ease by a fraction, though Kivamus could not be certain of that from where he stood.

He waited until the cheering dropped again before continuing.

“So after that,” Kivamus said, “the servants’ hall will be used by the servants, maids, and grooms, while all the guards will live in one of those 2 barracks.”

“Of course,” he went on, “I understand that many of you are married and have families, and you may not want to live there. So you have a choice. If you prefer, you can stay in the longhouse blocks with your families when you are not on duty.”

A few heads lifted at that.

“But since the manor is also providing food for the longhouses,” he said, “your pay will still be cut by 4 coppers a day in return for the food we provide there, just as it would be if you stayed in the barracks. However, there is a third option. If you can manage your own food and lodging, then you will get your full wages. That means anyone not staying in the longhouses or the barracks will get full wages without any deduction.”

Someone in the crowd called out at once, “But where will we stay then? There is no other place with shelter in the village.”

Kivamus had expected that. “Soon,” he explained, “there will be a new kind of housing built in the village, where you can rent a private room for yourself or your family. It won't be like the longhouse blocks or the barracks, where dozens and dozens of people live within the same walls. This new type of housing—tenement blocks—will have separate rooms where you can live by paying a rent. If you have a big family, you can choose a two-room house. If you live by yourself or with a partner, you can choose a one-room house. But they will only be available on rent.”

That quieted them a little.

“By rent,” he added, “I mean it will not be free. You will have to pay a certain amount to live there. But in return, that whole room—or rooms—will be yours, along with all the privacy and safety that comes with it.”

That got people talking.

He could hear the muttering spread through the crowd. A few of the married guards leaned toward each other immediately, probably discussing renting a room nearby so their families could help each other in need. Some of the maids were whispering to their friends already. Even a couple of the servants on the roof of the barn had turned to speak to one another.

“That will be a good option for those with families,” Kivamus continued, raising his voice enough to carry over them. “You can stay together with your wives or husbands, your children, or your parents. So once that tenement block is ready, anyone who wants to live there can pay the rent and stay in it freely. And because we will not provide food there, those staying in such housing will receive their full wages.”

The muttering grew louder again.

Kivamus let it run for a few moments, then lifted a hand.

“I will explain the details later once the tenement block gets ready in the coming months,” he said. “So do not worry about that yet. It is something for the future. For now, all of you will receive your wages with the standard deduction of 4 copper coins a day.”

He paused, and his tone shifted to something more serious. “The next announcement is about the medals. Many of you have already received medals—whether in the line of duty or for discovering something new—but until now I only gave the medals themselves. I postponed the reward amounts for later.”

The crowd was attentive again.

“Today,” Kivamus said, “they receive those rewards as well.”

That brought a fresh roar of approval.
