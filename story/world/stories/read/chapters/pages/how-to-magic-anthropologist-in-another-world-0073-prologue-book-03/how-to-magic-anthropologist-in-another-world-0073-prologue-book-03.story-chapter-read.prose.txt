Nakera

Infiltrating High Rock had not been particularly difficult for Nakera. Being Kindred came with certain advantages here, and the city was not especially selective about who it allowed through its gates, particularly when it came to people of obvious Kindred heritage. Kuru had faced slightly more trouble than her, mostly because Bloodline Realm visitors were treated with a bit more suspicion. She had to pay a small bribe and answer a few more questions than either of them would have liked, but in the grand scheme of things, it had not been much of a concern.

Their cover identities were simple enough. Kuru was supposed to act as a rich merchant’s daughter playing the role of a curious tourist visiting High Rock for the first time, while Nakera was a tribal warrior seeking the next challenge for her evolution. That meant Kuru’s days were spent hitting obvious locations, visiting markets, spending coins in taverns, and generally behaving like a party girl. The dangerous work, however, was always going to fall on Nakera: infiltration.

That was exactly the sort of thing she excelled at.

She still remembered when she had first joined the freelancer guild, and one of the sergeants had openly mocked her ability after learning what it was.

“A camouflage power?” Da’i had scoffed. “You can buy a better version of that for five silver.”

He had then gone on a small lecture about why invisibility was superior, arguing that one made you disappear completely while the other only made you blend into the background, meaning the slightest environmental change could expose you. Nakera had not bothered correcting him. She was not stupid enough to explain the strengths and limitations of her power to a room full of strangers, especially when half of them were competitors.

The important distinction was simple. Her ability was not Bloodline magic and not a Soulbook effect. It was a Kindred adaptation inherited from her mother, a biological trait rather than a magical technique. People called her the Chameleon because it was close enough to understand, though the comparison was not entirely accurate. The color changes were controlled directly through her brain rather than pheromones, which was a technical difference most people would neither understand nor care about. More importantly, it meant her ability did not consume mana.

That was what truly made it dangerous.

Bloodline invisibility might have been more visually impressive, but it still relied on external magical output. Any competent aura user could eventually notice the disturbance if they were paying attention, and a SoulSeer like Ja’a would see a glowing beacon. Nakera’s camouflage, on the other hand, was entirely biological. It had no mana fluctuations, no magical light bending, and no aura projection. Combined with strong aura suppression, it allowed her to move through places that would be nearly impossible for most infiltrators.

Of course, the ability came with limitations.

Only her skin and hair changed color. Anything external remained unchanged, including clothing, weapons, and armor. This was why she needed her equipment to be custom-made from Camerian leather, a rare material that naturally adapted alongside the user, copying the wearer’s attributes. It was expensive and irritatingly difficult to maintain, but it solved a problem that would otherwise render her ability nearly useless.

It was also why she hated being caught without it.

She still remembered the Pikar Steppe incident with lingering irritation. Having to wear Alice’s clothes while acting as her double had been amusing enough, but being forced to fight Izair in little more than her underwear because of that weakness had easily ranked among the most frustrating experiences of her life, especially since she had been outmatched by him.

Still, she would not trade her power for anything.

At that exact moment, it was proving its worth.

Nakera was currently lying motionless on the rooftop of the royal palace, her body pressed flat against cold stone while her skin and armor perfectly mirrored the colors of the surrounding rock. Anyone glancing in her direction would see nothing more than another section of rooftop.

High Rock itself was a ridiculous city.

Rather than being built around a mountain, it seemed to have been carved directly into one. Buildings protruded from stone at impossible angles, layered vertically in ways that made navigating the city a nightmare for anyone unfamiliar with it. The royal palace was among the most impressive structures, carved directly into the mountain face and overlooking most of the city below.

Even then, it was only the second most absurd thing in sight.

The first being the Rift Tower.

The structure extended high above the city, terminating in what looked less like a proper portal and more like an open wound in the sky itself. Unlike Hano’s far more reasonable gate, which was accessible without requiring a pilgrimage, High Rock’s interdimensional travel required a climbing exercise. Anyone wishing to travel to the neighboring Kindred Realm first had to ascend the mountain city, then climb the tower itself.

Nakera still thought the design was idiotic, although they had to work with what they got. It was the Silent One who had created the rift after all, and it was not as if the people here could simply relocate it.

However, it was particularly inconvenient for them, considering High Rock’s strained relationship with Hano, and by extension, their allies in Treegate. Their political hostility meant reduced trade cooperation, which resulted in fewer Bags of Holding circulating through the city since the Soul Emporium was the primary vendor of such items. That meant most goods were carried physically across the mountain, making it an overall less efficient commercial system.

Still, despite these obvious disadvantages, High Rock remained prosperous. Access to the Kindred Realm came with its own benefits. Chief among them were rare beasts, unique plants, exotic materials, and direct control over one of the few stable rift connections in existence, which gave the city significant economic leverage.

Nakera had chosen this position because it provided an excellent vantage point over both the city and the palace rooftop. More importantly, she had learned through several days of observation that King Palsha liked coming here at night.

She could see why. The view was admittedly impressive.

The elderly king stood near the ledge with his hands clasped behind his back, quietly observing the lights of his city below. From this distance, he looked far less imposing than his reputation suggested. He was still broad-shouldered and physically sturdy, but age had clearly begun to wear him down.

Nakera found herself briefly wondering if she could beat him in a fight.

Probably not.

She dismissed the thought almost immediately. Her mission was information gathering, not assassination.

As if rewarding her patience, footsteps approached. Prince Ustet stepped onto the rooftop.

“Father, why did you call for me?” the prince asked.

King Palsha turned, his expression troubled.

“I received concerning news from Elder Haro Okar, Grand General of Hano.”

Nakera’s attention sharpened immediately.

“Lieutenant Izair has been accused of being in league with the Old Realm cult.”

The prince frowned deeply.

“Are you seriously considering trusting Hano over one of our own?”

“I think your pride blinds you to how matters in the Contested Realm actually function.” The king sighed.

“Hano and the freelancer guild are our enemies.”

“Our rivals,” the king corrected. “Sometimes our enemies, yes. But the cult is something else entirely. Okar would never fabricate accusations involving them. Not like this.”

Ustet’s expression darkened.

“A great many Kindred people died after the failed revolt fifteen years ago. Karda, the Legendary Adamantine warrior, among them.”

Nakera frowned at the mention of Kan’s father. What did that have to do with anything?

Ustet’s expression darkened further as he stepped closer to his father, his voice taking on the restrained intensity of someone who had clearly rehearsed this argument many times before.

“He was only one example,” the prince said. “Whenever a Kindred rises too high in Hano, they somehow end up falling. Sometimes it is politics, sometimes it is sabotage, and sometimes it is conveniently timed tragedy, but the result is always the same. The Bloodline nobility keep their positions while everyone else is reminded where they belong.”

King Palsha gave him a long, tired look. “Karda was born in Hano, raised in Hano, and died fighting for Hano. He was respected there more than he ever was here. What claim do you even have on avenging him when all of his children still live and continue to serve the same city?”

“It is not about Karda alone,” Ustet snapped. “It is about the system that keeps repeating itself.”

Nakera frowned as she listened. The conversation was veering into territory she had not expected. She had come here hoping for useful intelligence, perhaps a minor scandal or military dispute, not whatever this was turning into.

The king shook his head slowly. “You are young enough to still believe the world is simple. Hano is not innocent, but neither is it the root of every grievance in this realm.”

Ustet laughed bitterly. “That sounds exactly like something a king who spent too long negotiating with them would say.”

“And you,” Palsha replied, his tone sharpening, “sound increasingly like a man who has spent too much time listening to radical people.”

That made the prince freeze. Only for a fraction of a second, but Nakera noticed. So did the king.

“You know exactly what I mean,” Palsha continued. “This obsession with purging the Contested Realm, with tearing down bloodline nobles, with cleansing corruption through violence, these are not new ideas. They are cult rhetoric.”

For a brief moment, neither man spoke.

Then Ustet smiled, and it was not a pleasant expression.

“Maybe they simply understand the problem better than you do.”

Nakera’s muscles tensed instantly. Well. That was about as explicit an admission as one could get.

She was just beginning to wonder whether this counted as enough information to justify retreating when a third set of footsteps echoed across the stone.

Izair stepped onto the rooftop, and Nakera nearly cursed aloud.

The lieutenant looked as irritatingly composed as ever, dressed in dark combat gear with both swords at his waist. Seeing him again brought back deeply unpleasant memories.

King Palsha turned toward him with visible disappointment.

“So it is true.”

Izair gave a shallow bow that somehow managed to feel mocking despite technically looking respectful. “I am afraid so.”

The king exhaled heavily, and for a moment, he looked less like a monarch confronting betrayal and more like an old man exhausted by yet another confirmation that people were disappointing.

“You could have left,” Palsha said. “If you were unhappy here, you could have gone anywhere.”

Izair tilted his head slightly. “And miss the opportunity to participate in meaningful change?”

“That is one way to describe treason.”

The lieutenant smiled faintly.

Nakera began mentally planning her escape routes. She was not particularly interested in being implicated in what was about to become an assassination. Unfortunately, she was also in an ideal observation point, and the spy in her found it difficult to leave.

King Palsha moved first. For an older man, he was absurdly fast.

One moment, he was standing still, and the next, he had crossed the distance between himself and Izair with enough force to crack the stone beneath his feet. His fist connected with the lieutenant’s torso in an impact that sent a shockwave across the rooftop.

Izair flew backward.

Nakera blinked. That was stronger than expected. The old man still had it.

Before the lieutenant could fully recover, the king pressed forward relentlessly. His skin began hardening, stone-like textures spreading across his arms, shoulders, and chest.

So that was his adaptation. Nakera had heard rumors, but now she was seeing it firsthand. Elemental adaptation through Kindred power was rare, especially since she did not notice any obvious bestial characteristics.

Prince Ustet activated the same transformation moments later, rocky plating spreading over his limbs as he joined the fight. Father and son now looked disturbingly similar.

The fight itself was brutal from the start. All three combatants relied heavily on melee attacks. Stone shattered beneath their feet with every heavy step, cracks spreading across the rooftop as blows collided with enough force to pulverize ordinary flesh.

Izair, however, was clearly the least durable participant.

At first, Nakera thought he had miscalculated. The king was overpowering him badly. A heavy elbow shattered part of his guard and sent him skidding across stone. A follow-up strike broke what was probably at least two ribs. Another exchange left a deep cut across his shoulder.

And yet Izair did not seem remotely concerned.

That was when Nakera realized what he was doing. He was intentionally accepting damage. Not all of it, obviously, but enough to be suspicious.

She narrowed her eyes toward his cursed blade. That power was disgusting.

Instead of avoiding damage efficiently, he could weaponize suffering itself, storing injuries and redistributing them later. Nakera still remembered how well it had worked against Lloyd back at Pikar.

King Palsha seemed to understand as well. His offense became even more aggressive, likely attempting to overwhelm the lieutenant before he could capitalize on the stored damage.

For a while, it almost worked.

The king fought like someone who had once been terrifying in his prime and was still dangerous despite his age. He forced both opponents onto the defensive and nearly sent Ustet tumbling from the roof a few times.

Had the fight remained a straightforward battle, Nakera suspected he might actually have won.

But of course, it was never going to stay fair.

During one particularly violent exchange, Ustet deliberately allowed himself to be thrown backward, using the momentum to reposition behind his father.

It was subtle, but clearly planned.

Before Palsha could react, his son wrapped one arm around his throat and locked him in place.

Only for an instant. A fraction of a second. But that was enough.

Izair grinned and stabbed the king with Malifice, the Unholy Blade. Throughout the fight, the blade had failed to pierce the king’s hardened skin.

But this time, the strike landed somewhere soft: the eyelid.

It was only a nick, barely more than a scratch, but it was enough. All of the accumulated damage was transferred to the king at once.

Nakera physically recoiled at the sight.

The king’s body convulsed violently as injuries bloomed across him all at once. Bruises surfaced instantly. Bones snapped audibly. Blood sprayed from his mouth as internal damage ravaged him faster than any normal healing factor a Kindred adaptation could compensate for.

His stone skin receded in patches as his concentration failed.

He stared at his son in disbelief.

“Son...” he gasped.

The word sounded less angry than heartbroken.

“What have you done?”

For a moment, Ustet hesitated. It was only for an instant, but Nakera caught it.

Then he twisted sharply.

The crack of King Palsha’s neck echoed across the rooftop.

The stillness that settled over the rooftop felt unnatural as King Palsha collapsed, dead.

Nakera stared at the corpse, her heart racing.

Izair exhaled, wiping blood from the corner of his mouth.

“That could have gone worse.”

“You say that after nearly getting yourself beaten to death?” Ustet asked dryly.

Izair shrugged. “It was under control.”

Even from here, Nakera could tell the lieutenant looked rough. He may have completely healed, but the memory of the pain clearly lingered.

Ustet stepped over his father’s corpse and looked down at it for several seconds. When he finally spoke, his voice had returned to calm professionalism.

“Take the secret tunnel,” he said. “Find Donas and tell him I am committed, regardless of what he hears in the coming months.”

“Understood.” Izair nodded.

He departed quickly, clearly eager to leave before the next phase began.

Nakera did not like the sound of that. She remained perfectly still, watching as the newly fatherless prince stood alone beside the corpse.

Several minutes passed.

Long enough that Nakera briefly wondered whether he was actually grieving.

Then he suddenly threw himself backward off the rooftop.

Nakera nearly broke camouflage from sheer surprise.

What the hell?

She crawled forward just enough to peek over the ledge, her skin shifting to a midnight blue to better blend against the sky.

Ustet had caught himself on a lower window ledge.

A moment later, his voice rang out through the palace.

“To arms! To arms!” he shouted. “The traitor Izair has murdered the king!”

The palace immediately erupted into chaos.

Lights flared across windows, doors slammed open, and alarm bells began ringing somewhere below.

Nakera stared in shock.

Did he just betray his allies, or had this always been the plan?

She shook her head. More importantly, if she stayed any longer, she was probably going to die in the resulting disaster. So she began making her exit.

Nakera did not waste time lingering around the palace after that.

By the time the first wave of guards began flooding rooftops and balconies, she was already halfway across the upper district, moving from rooftop to rooftop with the practiced efficiency of someone who had made a career out of going places she was not supposed to be. Her body remained pressed close to stone and shadow whenever necessary, her skin and Camerian leather shifting seamlessly with every new surface she crossed.

The city around her was rapidly descending into chaos.

High Rock was waking up.

Alarm bells rang from multiple directions now, their deep metallic resonance bouncing off stone walls and echoing through the mountain city, windows opened, voices rose in confusion, and guards shouted contradictory orders while civilians began spilling into the streets to figure out what disaster had interrupted their night.

Nakera almost pitied them.

By the time she reached street level, Nakera’s mind was racing, trying to piece together what had just happened.

She slipped through an alleyway and only finally relaxed after reaching one of their prearranged safehouses, a modest rented property tucked between a herbalist shop and a tailor. The place was intentionally forgettable, which made it ideal.

Inside, she finally allowed herself to breathe properly.

Then she sat on the floor and stared at the wall for a full minute.

“That could have gone better,” she muttered.

Not because her mission had failed. Quite the opposite, in fact. She had obtained far more valuable intelligence than expected. The problem was that the intelligence was terrible.

Hearing that a local prince had cult sympathies was concerning. Watching him murder his father, fake an assassination, and frame someone else was something else entirely.

Nakera rubbed her face.

A few hours later, she was woken by a knock at the door.

She did not even remember falling asleep.

Three slow taps. A pause. Two quick ones.

It was Kuru.

Nakera sighed in relief before opening the door.

The other woman stepped inside, looking grim enough to confirm Nakera’s suspicions before she even spoke.

“Well?” Nakera asked.

Kuru shut the door behind her and leaned against it.

“Bad news.”

Nakera gave her a flat look.

“I watched a prince murder his father, fake an assassination attempt, and blame a cultist accomplice for regicide. You are going to need to be more specific.”

Kuru blinked.

“That sounds substantially worse than what I was about to say.”

“What did you hear?”

Kuru sighed and crossed her arms.

“The king is dead, and Prince Ustet officially claimed the throne less than an hour ago.”

Nakera stared.

“That was fast.”

“Well that's High Rock for you.”

Kuru moved toward the table and dropped a folded pamphlet onto it, likely stolen from somewhere official or semi-official.

“He already gave a speech.”

Nakera unfolded it, and sure enough, it was a hastily transcribed address.

“That was also fast.”

She skimmed through it, her expression darkening with every line.

“This bastard is good,” she muttered.

The speech was exactly what she would have written if her goal had been to unite an unstable population through shared outrage.

There was grief, righteous anger, condemnation of betrayal, promises of justice, vows to hunt cult influence from the city, and just enough patriotic language to make the entire thing emotionally intoxicating.

Nakera was almost impressed.

He had even managed to subtly frame his father as a noble victim of misplaced trust, which conveniently preserved the previous king’s image.

“He called for vengeance,” Kuru added.

“Against Izair?” Nakera asked.

“No. The whole cult.”

Nakera looked up sharply.

At first, it did not make sense. Why not blame Hano? But then she understood. The wound would tell a different story, and people here were familiar with the man’s fighting style.

But blaming the cult... were they not his allies?

“People rally around threats,” nakera finally said.

Kuru nodded.

“Is everyone buying it?” Nakera asked.

Kuru gave her a humorless smile.

“Not just buying it. They are eating it up.”

Well.

That was bad. Very bad.

Nakera leaned back in her chair and tried to think through the implications.

If Ustet genuinely aligned himself with Donas while publicly declaring anti-cult rhetoric, that gave the Old Realm faction something incredibly valuable.

A hidden asset with full state backing.

“They sacrificed a lieutenant to gain a monarch,” Nakera said quietly.

“That is my assessment as well,” Kuru agreed.

A legitimate ruler with military authority, resources, territory, and public support.

The sheer scale of that problem was difficult to overstate.

Nakera exhaled slowly.

Izair was dangerous, but ultimately replaceable. A loyal king was infinitely more valuable.

This had not been a setback for the cult.

It had been an investment in the future.

And unless someone exposed Ustet immediately, it was going to pay dividends for years.

“We need to leave,” Nakera said.

“Already packed.”

“Good.” Nakera sighed, “We cannot stay long enough for border restrictions to begin,” Nakera continued. “Once the city fully stabilizes, they might start controlling departures.”

Kuru nodded. “I thought the same.”

There was a brief pause. Then Kuru studied her with visible curiosity.

“So what exactly happened on that rooftop?”

Nakera stared at her.

“Once we are far away from here, I will tell you everything. For now, we need to go.”

The city had not yet fully understood what had happened that night, but by morning, the official version of events would already be cementing itself into public consciousness.

King Palsha would be mourned.

Izair would be condemned.

And King Ustet would rise to power, carried by grief, outrage, and carefully manufactured legitimacy.

Nakera hated every part of it.
