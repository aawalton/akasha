Chapter 133

An exposure is rarely reported by the observer. It is found later, in the record: a name entered twice, a road no cart could travel in the time allowed, a witness who does not recall the face. The forgery is careful, and it is the care that betrays the hand.— Codex of the Long Watch, Operational Directive

Steve had the maps under his arm, rolled tight and held against his ribs. They had walked to the residence in ordinary clothes, and Arthur felt extremely vulnerable without the twenty kilos of steel weighing him down.

“Cover is the same as last time,” Donna said as they climbed towards the professor’s house. “Bored survivors. Steve the archaeologist. Sarah, the MSF daughter come home. Nobody mentions anything that involves a glow, a notification, a floating intelligence or a talking rod.”

“My focus doesn’t talk.”

“You know what I mean. And this is especially for you. The last time you were in a room with an audience, you forgot to stop talking for an hour and forty minutes.”

“It was a very good conversation.”

The professor opened the door himself with a book in one hand and his thumb playing the bookmark.

“You came,” Markopoulos said, and Arthur could not help but recall how Papadakis had welcomed them with the exact same words. “Ela, ela. I have been looking forward to this all day. Steve, I have found the Pausanias passage you were wrong about. Come in. I have charts. Don’t mind the books, I have surrendered the hallway to them.”

The hall was as it had been the last time they were here, volumes stacked course on course down both walls like drystone. The professor steered them through it to a long room where the shutters stood open on the last of the fading light. Papadakis rose out of a chair with both her hands coming forward to greet them. There was a thick folder kept on the table next to her. It looked new.

“There. Now the room is worth the coffee. Kostas has talked of nothing else since he heard you were back. I hope the family is safe?” Papadakis said.

It took Arthur a moment to realise that by family she was referring to the story they had concocted about checking on extended family who had a farm on the road to Sounion.

Sarah, thankfully, was better prepared. “Yes, they are well. Thank you.”

“Good, I am glad to hear it, and glad you are back safely. Now Kostas has better-read company than only me.”

“She is too modest. She reads more than I do. She simply does not argue about it afterwards, which I find unnatural,” Markopoulos said from the stove, where he had gone straight to the briki without asking anyone whether they wanted coffee.

“Oh, I argue. I do it quietly, in my head,” Papadakis said with a smile.

Donna took the chair nearest the window with her back to the wall and sat facing the door.

Markopoulos poured the coffee and launched into the stone-balls argument before anyone had a cup. He had brought diagrams for the argument. He had, in fact, made a hand-drawn overlay of the triangulation grid Steve had proposed in his letter, and another competing overlay from his own reference material. He intended to prove that Steve’s alignment was off by four degrees at the Hermion baseline.

“Four degrees. I measured it from the museum records. Your Hermion angle does not close cleanly,” he said, laying both sheets on the table side by side.

Steve leaned forward with both elbows on the table and the look on his face that meant everyone else in the room had stopped existing.

“The museum records predate the 1920 triangulation. Bousquet resurveyed the whole baseline in sixty-three. Your four degrees is the old datum,” Steve said.

“Bousquet did not survey Hermion, he surveyed Epidaurus. He extrapolated Hermion,” Markopoulos replied.

“He surveyed the visual line from the Temple of Aphaia on Aegina to the Hermion headland. I can cite the page number,” Steve argued back.

“Please cite the page number.”

Arthur drank his coffee and let them go at it. Sarah was talking quietly to Papadakis about her mother’s café and whether the fishing boats were still bringing in red mullet. The evening had the comfortable feeling of friends meeting for a casual dinner party.

Markopoulos got up to put on a second briki, delighted by how the evening was going and humming to himself. In that small silence, Papadakis set her cup down on the folder and looked across the table at Steve with the same warm face she had worn all evening.

“Loipón. May I be a poor guest for a moment, and tell you what I know already? It will save us both a great deal of polite lying, and the patience for it I no longer have,” she said.

The change in topic was so abrupt that nobody asked what she meant, and Papadakis continued.

“Vlachos wrote to Stefanos, you see, and the reply came back a few days ago: he does not remember any English tourists.”

Arthur realised she was talking about the Mayor of Nafplio, who had been a student of one of the academic wing’s old guard.

“I found it curious, so I wrote to Nafplio myself. I know a man in the demarch’s office there, a careful man, who keeps good lists. I asked him about four foreign travellers who had been caught at the coast when the System arrived. He asked around. People, they remember things. They remember the Italians who were trapped on a yacht, they remember the German family in the hotel, they remember a group of Scandinavians who walked to Argos.”

She picked up her glass and took a sip of water.

“Nobody in Nafplio remembers four English speakers.”

Her resting heart rate has increased by nine beats per minute since she started this sentence. She has been preparing for this exchange since before you arrived.

Arthur adjusted his posture in the chair. Steve’s hand had gone still on the edge of a map, and Donna had not moved at all, which was her way of preparing for violence.

“Our route did not take us through the town itself. We skirted it,” Steve said carefully.

“Of course, of course,” Papadakis said, in a tone that made it clear she did not believe him. “I am not accusing you of anything. You have helped this city, and I do not believe you are people who mean harm.”

“Then there is Sounion. The temple is in a state that requires explaining. There is also a camp that had been taking people, now dismantled quite thoroughly. Many thanks for that, by the way.”

“What are you accusing us of, exactly?” Sarah asked.

“I am not accusing you of anything, Sarah mou. I brought you to a house full of books, not to the Parliament. If I wanted a hold on you I would have used other methods. I am only laying out the facts as I see them.”

Markopoulos came back with the second briki and realised that something in the room had changed. He looked at Papadakis, trying to understand why the jovial conversation had turned cold.

“Kostas, I told you I needed to speak with them,” Papadakis said gently.

“You told me you wanted to talk about archaeology,” Markopoulos said.

“I do. And I need to speak with them.”

She turned to Steve and continued.

“Lastly, there is Luxor. Ilias is still upset that young archaeologists do not remember their own reis. I think you do not, because that part of the story never happened.”

Papadakis placed a hand on the folder lying in front of her. “It is plain that you are more than you claim to be. You walked to Sounion through monsters and gangs. You walked into the Kastella compound and cleared an anomaly that a company of soldiers could not enter. I have been reading the Council’s reports and watching four people do things that cannot be explained by a holiday that went wrong.”

“I’m still not sure what you’re getting at. You have some theories. So what?” Donna said, her tone sharp.

“This is the uncomfortable part. I am sure you know more than you are letting on. No one goes to a temple in the middle of an apocalypse. I want to know what you know. What is happening, why is it happening, and how to stop it.”

Papadakis held her hands up in a calming gesture.

“I am a scholar, first and foremost. I need to understand the world around me, but I also represent one of the four wings of the Council. Which is to say, you can either satisfy my curiosity here in private, or I can start having conversations with my colleagues about four foreigners who are clearly not who they claim to be.”

“You had Kostas call us over to blackmail us?” Sarah said.

“Sarah, I know how it looks, and I am not going to offer you hollow excuses. The Council bears the responsibility for what remains of this city, and in our position I will seize any advantage available. I need to understand how the world has altered if we are to make a genuine difference here, and I suspect you hold the exact information we lack.”

She paused, letting them absorb the admission before she continued.

“So. My cards are on the table. Help me understand what has happened to our world, and you will have the support of the academic wing whenever you need it. If you choose not to, it will not change the scholar in me. I will still ask questions, push forward and pull on every thread I can find, but I will be doing it without the context you could have provided. And is not a little knowledge a dangerous thing?”

She got up and looked at Kostas. “I am sorry to have involved you. I did what I thought was necessary. Come. I will answer any question you have. We should give your guests some time to think.”

Markopoulos got up and followed her inside with a bewildered, hurt look on his face.

The moment they were gone, Donna leaned in. “I’m getting tired of people trying to use us, one way or another.”

“The cover is well and truly blown, at least with her,” Steve said, not looking up from the map.

Sarah sat looking out of the window. Arthur thought he knew what was running through her mind. Her family was here, and when the four of them eventually left to chase the next node, they would have to stay behind.

“She already knows enough to be dangerous,” Arthur said. “If she goes digging without context, she will start filling in the blanks with dodgy assumptions, and a wild theory circulating in the Council is worse than the truth. We give her just enough to stop her guessing.”

Donna held his eye for a long time and then sat back. She did not fully agree, but she accepted the decision.

The low table had disappeared under Steve’s papers. The pastries were somewhere under Crete. Both brikia sat cold on the stove, and nobody had noticed. Markopoulos was on his feet, jabbing two fingers at a coastline and arguing a point with the enthusiasm of a much younger man. Steve was arguing back.

“And the boy simply put his hand on the stone and it activates. Fascinating,” Markopoulos said, not for the first time.

Arthur could not remember the last time he had been called a ‘boy’.

“It is a node, a relay. The relay connects to a larger network. The network spans the entire Mediterranean, at minimum.”

Markopoulos was already planning fieldwork in his head. Arthur could see it. Markopoulos kept glancing at the charts and then at Steve and then back at the charts.

Through all of it, Papadakis had stayed quiet and written. She sat at the table with three pages already covered in a small, tight hand and had begun a fourth. As Arthur watched, she picked up the second sheet and underlined a passage.

When she looked up and caught him watching, she offered him a smile. It was a tired smile, but genuine nonetheless. Arthur had a hard time reconciling the warmth of the gesture with the woman who had, only hours earlier, quite literally blackmailed them into handing over their secrets.

They left when the lamp oil was running low. Papadakis saw them to the door with neither animosity nor embarrassment. It was as if she had completely forgotten the things she had said.

• •

Papadakis walked home through the empty streets with a lamp in one hand and the evening’s notes folded inside her jacket. The house was cold and dark when she unlocked the front door. She lit the desk lamp, sat down at her heavy writing desk and pulled a fresh stack of paper from the top drawer.

A faint smile touched her lips as her pen met the page. For the first time since the sky had turned violet, the relentless questions that had plagued her mind finally had real, concrete answers. She had not lied to them; she was genuinely grateful for what the four foreigners had shared, and she fully intended to ensure that this knowledge was used to protect and rebuild the city.

She wrote well into the early hours of the morning, meticulously documenting every diagram, term and detail she could pull from memory. When she was finally done, she gathered the finished sheets, slid them into the desk drawer, snuffed the lamp and went to bed.
