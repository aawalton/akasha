Chapter 16

Pottage

I take the time, now that we have it, to explain everything I’ve attempted to do with the dungeon to my teammates as we make camp. The sheer quantity of information and explanations that involves means that it’s getting quite late, and I’m dipping up campfire stew as I finally finish. We could have just eaten meals from storage, but all of the ones I have prepared are Essence meals, and I’d rather not make a spectacle of those in front of the coachman.

It’s quite fragrant, courtesy of the many wild herbs I’ve been stockpiling with auto-loot in our travels, with a peppery undertone that tickles my tongue nicely. The thickness is provided by a hearty pack of tack biscuits, which, I admit, I basically upended into the pot, but let’s be honest, it’s not like there’s anything else you can do with the things. Daikon and taro get cubed up and tossed in for the vegetables. They’re hardy travel staples in the Empire, and once they cook down, they’ll pair well with the alliums and cress.

For Kyuuga’s benefit, I don’t use rabbit meat even though we’re so close to Dabun that it basically restocks itself. Instead, I use duck. Grand duck, specifically. Like the local horned rabbits, they’re a monster species that replaced the original creature, larger, stronger, and more obnoxious than the original. And they breathe fire. Weird trait for a waterfowl, I know, and we aren’t talking full-on dragon fire, but it’s enough to burn a village to the ground when they fly right over your walls and decide it would be amusing to quack on your predominantly wood-based architecture. I picked up a huge supply up around … oh, it doesn’t really matter. The village was so glad to have the whole angry skein gone before they reached their gates that nobody complained about us disposing of the bodies, too.

One culinary reveal I do insist on dropping, however, is bouillon. Tired of having extra broth go to waste all the time, I took a card from my merchant friend in Giri and dehydrated it into a powder, then added just enough fluid back to shape them into pre-measured balls for easy mixing. Since it’s a big pot, I throw in eight of them, and I’m quite pleased with what they do to what is normally a very diluted dish of reheated water and jerky.

“You’re such a good cook, Hero Remmi,” Korrigan enthusiastically praises me after her first bite. “Even your pottage is so delicious!”

I grin at the energetic wizard-kid. “My people are obsessed with food and all the ways to prepare and eat it, even about what can be considered food. We’ve dedicated a lot of time to figuring out just how to get as much flavor out of it as possible.”

The coachman chuckles around his own spoon. “Is that so? It’s hard to believe an entire culture of people can cook like this! Don’t let word get to my wife, but it’s better than anything she’s ever made!”

My grin’s a little thinner at being compared to his wife, but I roll with it. “Eh, you’re too old for me, anyway,” I joke back. And it’s true, at least for this body. If he’s broken three decades, it’s only barely, and I’d personally peg him at just under, which would put him at a comfortable gap of approximately five years with my old self. Now, though, he’s basically twice my age, and that can’t help but bother me.

It gets a bark of laughter out of him, though. “I’d discover the secret to eternal youth if it meant getting to eat like this every day!”

“That would make your wife really mad,” Korrigan chirps innocently, which makes him laugh again.

“Yeah, it probably would,” he agrees.

It’s all in good humor, so I don’t allow myself to dwell on it. He’s joking and trying to be complimentary about it. It’s the, You’re going to make a wonderful wife for somebody one of these days, sentiment. Nobody ever says it without the best of intentions. It’s not said with malice or lechery. It’s just an old-world way of saying that one’s domestic skills are top-notch. Getting offended by it would be as silly as it would be disrespectful.

So I put on a hostess smile. “Maybe instead of you upsetting your wife by running off to chase after a mystical elixir, I can just bring her a gift basket of some of my favorite spices.”

Sure enough, he’s not even put off by the suggestion, a definite sign that he wasn’t serious and didn’t think anyone would take him seriously. “Oh, would you? She’d love that!”

Ayre remains silent throughout the dishing out of the food, and I recognize it as him processing everything I’ve told them. It’s not until the banter settles down that the archer speaks, and it’s after taking a delicate bite of the pottage.

“So, Remmi, if I understand everything clearly, you’re pushing your training dungeon idea off on an ancient arcane wellspring in the hopes that it understands you and agrees to the remodeling. If successful, the dungeon will expand in a different direction than its domain, and it will turn itself into a fully-stocked amusement park. Dabun will grow and flourish from the tourism, and everyone in Toleste will be free to come up with suicidal strategies in the knowledge that they won’t die. Which we can’t actually test without someone dying.”

I frown back. “Just say you didn’t listen, then! I didn’t give my training dungeon idea up to anybody! I still wholly intend to come up with one I can install anywhere, at any guild hall! Just because I used the same principles here, that doesn’t mean I’m pushing it off! It just so happened to be right for the situation!”

As I continue my objections, I cross my arms and turn away sharply. “And don’t call it an amusement park! It’s still a dungeon, with point multipliers and loot. And pain!”

“You included an inn and bar, Remmi,” he points out as he jabs his spoon at me.

“To keep as many people on site as possible,” I counter. “If the wait times are long and there are no accommodations, they’ll just wander off! Or worse, get bored! A bored adventurer is a dangerous one!”

That gets me a scoff, which I consider progress. “The only adventurer that’s a danger when bored is you. Nobody else gets into the habit of designing parks and recreation that explode.” Ayre turns toward me fully again. “And how are you helping Dabun if everyone’s staying in the dungeon?”

“Of course, the overflow will fall back and stay in the village,” I point out, “and adventuring supplies and the like will have to come from there too! No meat in this dungeon, either, so Kyuuga’s going to have to get to humping!”

Both Ayre and Kyuuga look offended at that, but Ayre just gives an, “Ew,” before circling back to his point. “They’re just going to make camp in the park area, Remmi. Adventurers make camp in dungeons all the time.”

“Nah, the guild people are going to crack down on that. They’re going to have a presence there all the time, too. And the inn and bar are still staffed by actual people charging actual tins, so that’s still money going back into Dabun, even if the exchange is happening here! And besides, would you really rather sleep on the temperaturemental ground, or on a soft inn bed?”

Ayre seems pained by the reminder of his old portmanteau, rubbing his temples with his fingertips. “Remmi, dungeon temperatures are very stable, day or night, unless they’re specifically an exception. We clear dungeons too fast for it to come up for us, but for most adventurers, barricading the room against monsters is a bigger concern than temperature.”

He points a slender, and quite well-groomed, finger at me, however. “But that’s not the point! You’re forcing things off topic again! Okay, so you just used your training dungeon idea because it fit the needs of the situation. Alright, I can understand that. And the accommodations are there for a reason, and camping in the common area will be addressed, with all funds raised either at this inn or in the village staying within the village. Okay.”

I’m nodding along as I spoon my stew into my mouth, and I talk around it, which slurs my words. “Sho whash je hrovlem?”

“Wife material,” he repeats with a sigh. “Maybe for a troll …” But he regathers his thoughts before I can swallow and object. “The problem is that you’re setting people up to be reckless!”

“You’re the one that said a training dungeon was a good idea!”

“Yeah, when it couldn’t kill them!”

“This won’t kill them!”

“And how do you plan to test that?”

I give a shrug as I fold my hands behind my head. “Dunno, was thinking of letting somebody get thumped.” The long, silent stare and perfectly level ears drive me to elaborate. “Look, the dungeon isn’t supposed to trigger on zero health points. It’s supposed to trigger on a threshold. If I let someone get down that low, to the point of failure, ready to intervene, if the dungeon’s retrieval doesn’t go off, there will be plenty of time to step in. How else do you propose we safely test a death flag?”

“Alright,” he relents reluctantly, “alright. But is this really going to be enough? Aren’t people just going to power through like always? I know I’m the one who pressured you to come up with an idea like this, but how is this even going to work? Is this the way it even can work?”

Now I roll my eyes, and it’s my turn to point with the spoon. “Ayre, this is the Forest Cavern we’re talking about! If it were any more noob-friendly, it’d be an Iron-rank sandbox dungeon!”

That makes Ayre’s pretty face twist in confusion. “I don’t understand what a sandbox has to do with this …”

“Sand box, put a frame out, fill it with sand, and toys, for children.”

“I know what a sandbox is, Remmi. I don’t understand what it has to do with a dungeon!”

“I’m saying it'd be for children, with padded corners and no small choking hazards!”

That groan again. “Okay, no, even an Iron dungeon isn’t fit for children. Wood, maybe, and I don’t know if that’s a rank of dungeon that even exists.”

“That’s not the point.” It feels weird to be the one correcting the course of a conversation. “I’m just saying that we would be hard-pressed to find anywhere a new adventurer is more likely to come. And the whole point of new adventurers is that there is no ‘like always.’ They’re learning and discovering how best to use their skills in real combat. And, hey, if the veterans want to come in and give pointers, all the better! Also, shouldn't you be encouraging a place where we can lower the adventurer mortality rate?”

“But will this do that?” The archer kind of folds in on himself now. “Remmi, I know it was my idea, I’m the one who pressed for it, but now it’s all so sudden, and in an actual dungeon, and everything is uncertain, we don’t even know if anything will work, and the consequences if something goes wrong …”

I nod in understanding. “You’re getting cold feet. I feel it, too, but the dice are rolled. There’s nothing left but to see where they land and decide where to go from there.”

Korrigan is slurping the last of the broth out of her bowl, but when she lowers it, she adds in her own thoughts. “Can a dungeon that won’t kill you really make you care, though? What if nobody takes it seriously?”

That makes Ayre sit up sharply, and he points to the oni mage. “That! That’s what I’m trying to ask! That right there!”

But that just drives me to grin, the firelight reflecting off of my pearly grill. “Are you kidding?! Nevermind the humiliation of the constant beatings, the penalties alone will make them more aware of the danger! Every time they fail, all of their precious goodies are gonna get mugged from them before they get ejected onto their leathered asses!” I lean into it, throwing my head back as I cackle like an evil genius, hands out like claws to either side of me.

My evil laughs are interrupted by the fated pairing of bow to skull again as I groan in pain.

“I’m starting to think you’re starting to like that,” Ayre mutters, “considering you keep asking for it …”
