8th Century - The Monolith of Ellora, India

The mountain was being born in reverse.

Ratan watched as ten thousand chisels rang against basalt in a rhythm that had not faltered in forty years. The workers carved downward, always downward, liberating the Kailasa temple from a single block of living rock: two hundred thousand tonnes of stone removed by hand, top to bottom, with no margin for error. One wrong stroke and the whole shape would have to be abandoned inside the mountain, unfinishable, forever.

His grandfather had told him the impossible story of the temple.

There were no foundations sunk into earth. No walls rising one measured course at a time. The first marks were cut into the crown of the basalt cliff, directly beneath the open sky, and from there the masons worked downwards.

The builders had opened three immense trenches into the mountainside, leaving a single mass of stone standing between them. Inside that mass was a temple nobody could yet see. The roof had to be finished while the chamber beneath it remained solid stone. The summit of the tower had to be carved while its base was still trapped inside the mountain. Towers, bridges, pillared halls, stairways, shrines, lions and elephants waited within the rock, and the work of the builders was to remove everything else.

Ratan had cut stone since he was nine years old. He knew basalt the way a sailor knows weather, by the sound a chisel makes. So when his chisel rang too hollow, he stopped his crew with a raised hand before the second blow could widen a fault.

The gap behind the rock was not a fault line. It looked like a wall.

He worked the opening larger with his fingers and brought his torch closer. What he saw was a smooth rock face without a single tool mark anywhere. "What is this?" he said, mostly to himself. "How could there be a wall inside a mountain?"

Before he could call for the Shila-hasta to inspect the wall, a hush spread around it. He looked up to find Kokasa standing behind him. The man they called the Sutradhara, the holder of strings, the chief architect. He was an old man, wearing blue robes and an ivory bracelet.

"Your men drifted four degrees to the left," he said, looking at the grade of the cut mountain.

"I... yes, master," Ratan stammered. "Master, there is a wall in the mountain."

"What is your name, child?"

"Ratan, master."

"Ratan, what we do here is as much in the glory of Mahadev as it is in the glory of the human race. And some revelations need to come when the time is right." He crouched down next to Ratan. "What you saw today needs to be covered over. Otherwise, what your great-great-great grandchildren are meant to inherit will be excavated instead of guarded."

Ratan did not understand any of what was just said by the great man. What he did understand was that there was something around the temple that was more important than the temple itself.

"Then we give everyone something else to find instead," he said, looking at the gap. "A niche. A relief. Maybe even Nataraja could have been waiting here all along. It would explain the hollow sound."

Kokasa closed his eyes and the finished temple changed in his mind to accommodate a new niche. The stairs shifted first. Then the northern plinth, the drainage channel had to move as well. One elephant turned across the space that had been left for it. Its body thickened and its lowered head moved towards the fracture.

"Ah, I will have to redraw the plans again," he sighed. "Make sure your crew does not make another mistake, young Ratan. The stars don't wait on humans."

Ratan lowered his head to the ground. "I'm sorry, master. I will make sure."

The Curator studied him, then added, "Your family will tend this seam, till the temple is finished. You, your children, then theirs. And you will not breathe a word of it to anyone outside."

Ratan went back to his crew and gave them a different set of instructions. Within the week, the split in the rock had a modest shrine over it, the kind of thing a temple this size could absorb without anyone noticing.

Visitors would spend the next twelve centuries marvelling at how the impossible temple was carved from the top down without a single recorded error. They would praise the planning, the labour and the precision, then admit that knowing the method did little to make the achievement comprehensible.

They would never find the narrow fracture behind the elephant.
