Vir, Maiya, and Ashani all arrived at the base of the tall city, expecting to find death and devastation.

What they found instead defied Vir’s imagination. While there was clear evidence of the Vimana’s destruction in the form of a large, scorched crater, it wasn’t the senseless ruin Vir thought it to be. This was targeted. Deliberate.

Vraj Parah was unlike any other demon city in that it was carved vertically into the side of a mountain, its streets and terraces winding upward toward the great castle at its peak. As a defensive structure, it was arguably the strongest city in the demon realm.

Unless, of course, a Vimana hovered over it.

And directly under the wondrous and terrifying machine of the gods was a wooden stage. Walking upon it was a figure who looked disturbingly familiar to them all.

“Andros,” Maiya seethed. “The worst part of knowing about the infinity of universes might just be knowing that the chal still lives in so many of them.”

Vir had to concur.

Imperator Andros Kinjal’s voice boomed, magnified by an orb as he gesticulated toward the crater. “Next! Witness the consequences of infidelity!”

Kneeling in front of Andros were over a hundred demons with their hands bound behind their backs, and an equal number of humans in the same state. On both sides stood dozens of Balarian guards watching over them.

Are they all to be slaughtered? Vir thought in horror.

As if answering his question, Balarian Guards shoved a demon woman—a Naga—into the still-smoldering pit.

The woman shrieked and yelled… and that was when Vir recognized her.

“That’s Tara, isn’t it?” he said, starting forth, only to find Ashani’s firm grip on his shoulder. Looking back, he found her shaking her head. “‘Tis too risky. You will be discovered. What good is saving one to damn them all?”

Vir hesitated, glancing back at Tara.

“This is the price you pay!” Andros declared, “for defying the greatest Empire ever to conquer the Realms!” He pointed toward the sky. “Any of you who speak against my authority will be struck down without mercy in the name of the great Kinjal!”

Thunder cracked, a blinding light forced Vir to shut his eyes.

When he opened them again, Tara was gone. Just… gone. No trace of the demon remained, and it took everything Vir had not to wail at the heavens. To curse the gods and the Fateweavers and everyone else for allowing such a tragedy to pass.

But he kept his mouth shut. For if there was someone to blame, it was himself, for being powerless before the tyranny of those who wielded Fate.

It wasn’t their Tara, Vir knew. This was a complete stranger, with quite possibly a very different personality and history. And yet… how could he not care?

Vir reaffirmed his oath, then and there. Not just to the people of his own universe, but to them all. To rid them of the blight that was the Fateweavers.

“That’s enough, I think,” Andros snarled. “Begone, the rest of you, and let this serve as a reminder. Do not let this happen again.”

“The only one ‘letting’ anything happen is you, you Ash Damned chal,” Maiya muttered.

Vir had scarcely ever seen such hatred from her face. Somehow, it soothed his own seeing her like that, for she bore anger enough for the two of them.

The guards shoved the prisoners away like livestock, herding them back to whatever grueling labor undoubtedly awaited them.

Maiya whispered, “What is this?”

Ashani, who had shifted into her demon form, ushered Vir and Maiya aside into the shadows, trying not to draw attention. The wolves had, as always, disappeared the moment they’d arrived.

“It would seem,” Ashani said quietly, “that this universe is one in which Imperator Kinjal was not struck down by Vir. And instead somehow seized control of our Vimana.”

“How is that possible?” Vir asked. “Andros knows nothing of Imperium technology. If anyone in the human realm could’ve done it, it would’ve been the Altani, and even they never managed to unlock the Vimana in millennia.”

“He must have had help,” Maiya said. “But from whom?”

They didn’t have to wonder long.

A white-haired gray demon in a lab coat strode out with an entourage, though these seemed less guards and more honor guard. He approached Andros, and to Vir’s astonishment, the two men embraced like old friends.

Andros clapped an arm around the demon’s shoulders as they boarded a skyship that had just landed, laughing together as they discussed who knew what?

The skyship—an Altani skyship—took off and sped away, disappearing into the horizon.

“Was that…?” Maiya whispered.

“Saunak,” Vir said bitterly. “Somehow, in this universe, Andros and Saunak met and joined forces to form an empire that spans realms.”

“But how?” Maiya asked. “Saunak lives in the deep Ash. Andros would’ve died just trying to get there.”

“Either he was never banished in this universe,” Vir said, “or something else happened entirely.”

Maiya shook her head. “This is unbelievable. I genuinely can’t imagine a worse combination.”

“Neither can I. Saunak’s madness and genius, paired with Andros’s ambition,” Vir murmured, shuddering at the thought. “Saunak must’ve had a field day with Andros at the helm.”

Knowing Andros, Vir doubted the imperator had restricted the demon in the slightest. Saunak would’ve been given all the resources he could dream of. Staff, coin, freedom… and test subjects of all kinds.

“Is this the result of their union?” Vir wondered aloud, looking up at the Vimana hovering above the city. “Did Saunak unlock its secrets and repower it in this universe?”

Is this incredible feat the result of unfettered experimentation? The work of a Saunak unchained?

If so, it was nothing short of a marvel.

Vir forced the thought away.

A marvel, yes, but could anyone argue that this Kin’jal Empire’s accomplishments were worth the sacrifices?

Vir thought not.

No advancement justified treating people like disposable tools. No progress was worth a world built on suffering.

“Andros had the chance to create a utopia,” Vir muttered. “Instead he condemned this world to something worse than death.”

“The question is, what are we gonna do about it?” Maiya asked, glaring daggers at the Vimana that hovered in the sky like an all-seeing executioner.

“Nothing,” Ashani said, “As I mentioned, we should seek a way into the Vimana posthaste.”

Maiya was about to retort, but Ashani spoke first. “I understand your feelings, Maiya. Yet as bad as this situation is, imagine how much worse it would be if they became involved? If they waged war upon this universe, there would be nothing left to save. At least now there is life, and perhaps somewhere in remote corners of this world, maybe even happiness. But with them… there would be nothing.”

Vir scowled, and he felt blood in his mouth. Ashani had a point, and he knew it. Inviting the Fateweavers’ ire would doom them all, and that would hardly be better than the reality unfolding before them.

“Besides,” Ashani added, “this is but one of an infinity of infinities of universes, as Yuma put it. Even if we managed to save the lives of those here, even if we made a difference here, how would it matter? There are endless worlds in the same situation. No matter how great your power, you cannot fix everything.”

“Then why bother helping our own realm?” Maiya snapped. “By that logic, anything we do back home is useless too.”

Ashani bit her lip, unwilling to say more.

Vir could see truth in both of their arguments. Still, there was one flaw in Ashani’s logic.

“Whether correct or not,” Vir said, “What matters in the end is whether we can be proud of the lives we lived. Of the way we conducted ourselves and the actions we took. Even if these universes are infinite, and even if nothing we do makes a difference on the cosmic scale, I, at least, will never stop regretting it if I walk away when I have a chance to help. I’ll never forget that I let Tara die before my eyes.

“And that goes double for our own world. Everything we built there… all the people depending on us? Abandoning them simply because we learned the universe is bigger? No. That isn’t who I am. That is not who we are.”

He looked toward the horizon that suddenly seemed so very close to the color of blood.

“I will not abandon these people. But,” he said, raising a hand before Ashani could argue, “I understand the concern. Drawing their wrath would be far worse, and furthermore, our highest priority is our own universe. Regardless, I believe there is a middle path. One where we keep ourselves hidden, but still make a difference. Even if a small one. If we can better this world in any way, the risk is worth it.”

“If that is your decision,” Ashani said softly, “then I will stand by it.”

“The question is,” Maiya said, “how exactly do we do that? Saunak and Andros just left, but maybe we could catch up? Do you want to assassinate them?”

“As much as I’d love to do just that,” Vir shook his head. “No. I fear that might be too much of a risk.”

Ashani nodded. “Any direct action we take will draw attention from them. We have to find another way.”

“But then what options do we—”

Maiya’s words were cut off by shouting and screams in the distance. The chained prisoners being herded by Balarian guards were suddenly under siege by another force.

Not an army, but a ragtag group attacking with mejai and Talent wielders wielding whatever makeshift weapons they could muster. They wore a mix of armor styles and uniforms, clearly pulled from whoever and whatever they could find.

“Now!” one of them roared. “Now’s your chance! Run while you can!”

Chaos erupted. Some prisoners bolted immediately, others froze in terror. As the attackers overwhelmed the guards, even the most hesitant of the prisoners found enough courage to flee in all directions.

“A resistance movement?” Vir said, more than a little surprised.

“So there is a spark of rebellion, even here,” Maiya muttered.

The question was how strong they were. And who led them.

Even in this bleak world, Vir felt a faint thread of hope tug at him. Hope, after all, was a stubborn thing. It refused to die, and burned brightest in the most oppressed of places.

When his eyes landed on a blonde-haired woman in full armor, he stopped dead.

Maiya gasped, and even Ashani stared.

“It can’t be…”

“Is that—” Maiya whispered.

“Princess Tiyana Matali. It would seem so,” Vir said, staring as she cut down a Balarian Guard with precise, practiced skill. “And I believe we just found our way to help this world.”
