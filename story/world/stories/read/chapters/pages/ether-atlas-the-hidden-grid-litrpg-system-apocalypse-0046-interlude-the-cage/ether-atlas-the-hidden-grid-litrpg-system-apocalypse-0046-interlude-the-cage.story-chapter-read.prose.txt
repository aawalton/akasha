Interlude

“Maintain the infrastructure. Protect the bloodlines. Embed the knowledge. Do not expect gratitude. Do not require belief.” - Codex of the Long Watch, Founding Tenet

The compound was better than the hospital. Sarah hated that this was true, but her MSF brain measured outcomes rather than preferences, and the outcomes here were measurable. Nine patients treated on the first day, in a room with walls and a roof that the creatures hadn’t found yet. Rainwater collected in old fishing barrels. Oil lamps instead of torches. A wood-burning stove that one of the Baron’s crew had assembled from salvaged kitchen parts, hot enough to boil water for sterilisation. Enough to feel like a clinic instead of a ruin.

And the Baron kept his word. He did not ask for preferential treatment and his fighters joined the queue. Sarah triaged by severity, same as she’d triaged in Yemen, same as she’d triaged at Mater Dei while the world ended around her.

A dockworker with an infected laceration before a fighter with a sprained wrist. A child with a fever before a grown man with bruised ribs. Nobody argued. The Baron had made it clear to his people that the doctor’s word was final in the medical bay, and whatever else Noel Zammit was, his people listened when he spoke.

Civilians came from outside the compound. A woman carried in from the fishing village with a compound fracture. Two teenagers who’d been hiding in a church basement for a week, dehydrated and covered in scratches. An old man whose hands shook and whose eyes were empty and whom Sarah treated for shock because there was no medication for grief.

Each session was faster than the one before, her control improving through the repetition that had smoothed her surgical technique over hundreds of patients, pathways wearing cleaner with use. She could feel the Ether moving more efficiently now. Less waste. Less of the hollow exhaustion that had flattened her in the first days.

At night she slept in a room that had a door she could close and a bed with a mattress and clean sheets that someone had scavenged from a hotel on the coast road. She lay in the dark and listened to the Mediterranean and thought: this could work. A clinic in a defended compound, treating anyone who needed it, protected by people with weapons and walls. The MSF model. Security enabling medicine. It was almost believable.

The Baron’s fighters started jumping the queue on Day 9.

There was no announcement and no policy change. A fighter named Marco simply appeared at the medical bay door at eight in the morning with a gash across his forearm, saying the Baron needed him in the field by twelve. Sarah looked at the three civilians already waiting, a woman with an abscess, a man with a cough that might be pneumonia, a boy with a broken finger, and asked Marco to wait.

Marco sat down to wait and twenty minutes later Enzo appeared, polite and apologetic. “The Baron says Marco’s patrol leaves at twelve. If he’s not healed by then, the southern route is unprotected today.”

Sarah ran her eyes over Enzo. Twenty-three years old and thin, clever beyond his years. He’d explained the levelling system to the Baron in the first week. He’d hit Level 5 two days ago and discovered his technique, some kind of enhanced awareness that made his eyes sharpen during fights. He was the Baron’s translator for a system that didn’t come with a manual.

“The woman with the abscess has been waiting since yesterday,” Sarah said.

“I understand, Doctor. But if Marco’s patrol doesn’t go out, the creatures on the south route get bolder. They’ve been testing the fishing village perimeter. If they breach it, you’ll have more patients than the abscess.”

The logic sounded reasonable, which was the problem. It was the resource allocation calculus she’d fought in every hospital corridor in her career. The loudest voice always, somehow, got treated first. She’d fought it in the NHS. She’d fought it in South Sudan. She was tired of fighting it.

She healed Marco’s arm, and the woman with the abscess waited until ten.

By the end of the second week, the Baron’s fighters were always first. The justification was always operational. Always reasonable. And always delivered by Enzo with an apologetic shrug and a logic chain that Sarah couldn’t fault without accepting that creature attacks on unprotected civilians were an acceptable trade for triage purity.

She told herself the queue order didn’t matter. She was still treating everyone. The civilians just waited longer, first an hour, then two, then sometimes a full day.

She told herself a lot of things that second week.

The Baron brought her to the temples on Day 13.

“Enzo tells me the system gives one experience for being near the kills,” he said. He was standing at her door in the early morning, dressed for the field, a machete on his hip that had been a boat tool a month ago and was something else now. With darker metal and a denser weight. “The people doing the actual fighting get much more. But it adds up. And Enzo says at Level 5, you’ll unlock a technique. Your healing will get better.”

He said it like he said everything, a business proposition. An investment in shared infrastructure. I’m upgrading the equipment because better equipment serves everyone.

Sarah went because the logic was the same logic that had brought her to the compound. If her healing improved, more people survived. If more people survived, the mission continued. The mission was the thing she held onto when everything else had been taken away.

She stood behind the line while the Baron’s fighters killed things while doing some quick field triage in between fights.

The temples were beautiful and terrible. Megalithic limestone older than the pyramids, the stones fitted so precisely that to Sarah they looked like surgical sutures. Creatures swarmed around the sites, drawn by the same energy that made her hands warm when she healed. The fighters formed a half-circle and worked through them methodically. The Baron directed from the centre with the calm efficiency of a man running a harvest.

Sarah absorbed Ether in small amounts after each kill, a trickle compared to what the fighters received, the system recognising that standing behind a line wasn’t the same as standing in front of one. But it accumulated.

ETHER ABSORBED: 12

“How much?” the Baron asked on the walk back.

“Thirty-five. Total.”

He nodded with visible satisfaction. “The fighters averaged a hundred and forty each. But we have time, Doctor. It all adds up.”

She heard what he meant: I have you indefinitely. The slow drip is fine because the tap never turns off.

He took her to the temples every second day for the next week. She levelled. The notifications appeared between the screaming and the blood.

LEVEL UP: LEVEL 4

INTEGRATION: 0 / 336

She noticed the change on Day 17.

The medical bay was empty except for two of the Baron’s fighters recovering from the morning hunt. The civilian queue was gone. With all the creatures and all the fighting the queue should never be empty.

“Where are the outside patients?” she asked Enzo.

Enzo’s face tightened into something like discomfort, the first time Sarah had seen that expression on him. “The roads are bad, Doctor. Creature activity has been worse this week. The Baron’s patrols are focused on the temple routes. The coast road to the fishing villages isn’t covered.”

“So people can’t reach us.”

“It’s not safe for them to travel.”

Enzo finished the sentence and Sarah had already reached a conclusion. A medical facility that doesn’t admit external patients is a private clinic. The Baron’s fighters got healed. The Baron’s civilians got healed. Everyone outside the walls got nothing. The fishing villages, the families barricaded in churches, the hospital staff she’d left behind at Mater Dei.

“I want to visit the hospital,” she said.

“I’ll mention it to the Baron.”

The Baron came to the medical bay that evening. He sat on the edge of a cot, unhurried, with the body language of a man in his own living room.

“Doctor. Enzo tells me you want to make a house call.”

“I want to check on patients at Mater Dei. There might be people there since there is no one here.”

“Of course. And I respect that.” He leaned forward. “But the road to Mater Dei runs through three kilometres of unsecured territory. Two of my fighters were injured on that road yesterday. Creatures are nesting in the residential blocks between here and the hospital. I can’t spare a patrol to escort you and I can’t let you go alone.”

“I’ve worked in conflict zones. I can assess the risk.”

“Doctor.” His voice was gentle. “You are the most valuable person on this island. Every fighter I have, every civilian in this compound, every person those fighters protect on patrol, their survival runs through your hands. If something happens to you on that road, everything we’ve built here collapses. I am asking you to consider what your life is worth to the people who depend on it.”

It was an airtight argument. The kind that used your own values as the door and your own compassion as the lock.

Sarah had heard a version of that argument before. Versions of it had come from hospital administrators who wanted her to work doubles, from MSF coordinators who needed her to stay one more rotation, from every institution that had ever confused her willingness to help with permission to use her. The difference was that those institutions let her walk out the door when she said no.

“I’ll think about it,” she said.

The Baron patted the cot frame and left. The door stayed open behind him. Sarah counted the steps. Six guards between the medical bay and the compound gate. She’d been counting without realising it.

She tried to leave on Day 19, early morning, before the fighters assembled for the hunt. She packed a medical bag. It held bandages, the salve she’d been making from salvaged pharmacy stock, a splint, a flask of water. She walked to the gate.

Two of the Baron’s men stepped into her path. Their faces polite but firm.

“Sorry, Doctor. Baron’s orders. Nobody leaves the compound without an escort.”

“I’m a doctor making a house call. I don’t need an escort.”

“We’ll get the Baron for you.”

The Baron arrived in less than ten minutes. He looked at the medical bag, then at Sarah. His expression carried neither anger nor threat. He looked like a man dealing with a staffing issue.

“Doctor. We discussed this.”

“You discussed it. I’m going to check on my patients.”

“No.” The word was quiet and final. The first time he’d said it without a justification attached. Then the justification arrived, because the Baron never left a flat refusal stand alone. “The road isn’t safe. My patrols can’t cover it today. Tomorrow, perhaps. We’ll arrange an escort.”

“You said that three days ago.”

“The situation three days ago was also unsafe.”

Sarah looked at the gate and the two guards, at the Baron’s calm face. She gripped the medical bag and walked forward. One step, then another.

A hand closed around her upper arm. The guard on the left, his fingers applying the careful pressure of someone who’d been told to stop her without hurting her.

Her body stopped moving before her mind caught up. The hand on her arm was the hand of every checkpoint guard she’d ever talked her way past in Congo, every militia fighter in Yemen who’d decided whether the medical convoy got through. Except those hands had always let go when she showed the MSF credentials.

The Baron stepped closer, and his voice dropped. Private, intimate and terrifying.

“Doctor. Go back to the medical bay. There are patients waiting. The escort will be arranged when the route is secure.” He paused. “This is for your safety. Everything I do here is for everyone’s safety. Including yours.”

The guard released her arm. Sarah stood at the gate with her medical bag and knew for certain that the escort would never be arranged, the route would never be secure. The door was open and the cage was her own compassion. She wouldn’t turn her back on young men working for the Baron who routinely got hurt. The Baron understood this better than she did.

That night, the door to her room locked from the outside.

She heard the bolt slide. A small clanging sound. The kind a warehouse door made when someone secured it for the night. She tried the handle. It didn’t move.

She didn’t bang on it or shout. She sat on the bed and looked at her hands. The hands that healed. The hands the Baron had spent three weeks investing in. Bringing her to the temples, watching her absorb Ether, tracking her levels, waiting for the technique that would make her faster and more efficient and more valuable and more trapped.

The blue lattice flickered faintly under her skin, stronger than it had been a week ago. Her Level 4 body processing Ether it hadn’t asked for, building toward a threshold she couldn’t prevent, becoming the thing the Baron needed her to become.

In the morning the door would open. There would be patients. She would heal them because that’s what she did. The cage would hold because the cage was made of the thing she couldn’t stop doing. The Baron had understood this from the beginning. He hadn’t captured a doctor. He’d acquired a resource that imprisoned itself.

Somewhere past the walls, past the creatures, past three kilometres of road the Baron would never secure, the patients who had reached Mater Dei were waiting for a doctor who wasn’t coming. Sarah stared at the locked door and made a decision she didn’t have a plan for yet.

This was not how it ended.
