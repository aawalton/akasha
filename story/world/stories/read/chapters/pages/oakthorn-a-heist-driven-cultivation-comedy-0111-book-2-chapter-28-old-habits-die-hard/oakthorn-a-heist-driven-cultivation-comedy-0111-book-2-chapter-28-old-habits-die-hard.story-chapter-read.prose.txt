The crypt breathed.

Cade felt it in the subtle pulse that traveled through stone ancient enough to have witnessed the birth of gods. He could sense it in the whispered rhythms that lingered just beyond hearing—a heartbeat not alive but not quite dead either.

Something vast and patient was waiting.

He sat with his back against a pillar carved with symbols no living scholar could translate, keeping watch while his exhausted crew grabbed what rest they could. The battle with the Arbiter horde had left them all battered, bloodied, and dangerously close to collapse. Even Orro’s legendary stamina had finally faltered, the assassin’s eyes sliding shut against his will as Rayka tended his wounds with gentle hands.

Rage and relief warred in his mind. A part of him was incredibly grateful all of his crew had survived what this horrid place threw at them. Another part of him—the louder part of him—demanded he strip this place of all its valuables and then set it on fire.

Twice.

His eyes roamed over his eclectic family yet again, confirming their chests still rose and fell. Jer and Elena slept back-to-back, their fussing over the other’s wounds finally ended, even if it was heartwarming to see Elena act like a mother hen over her twin’s injuries.

Near them, Nora sat in a half-meditative state, one hand still resting on her massive sword, somehow vigilant even in stillness. Bunny had curled into a protective ball around Chubbs, the dragonling’s white scales glimmering faintly in the eerie phosphorescence that served as their only light in these depths.

His mind raced over what had slipped out of his mouth just hours ago.

Sister.

He hadn’t meant to say it—hadn’t wanted the responsibility that came with it. But gods above and below, he knew he’d meant it the moment it had slipped out of his mouth. She was infuriatingly curious, innocently uncouth, and yet a beacon of hope and life in his crew.

It was weird to be in the same position as Hugh was when he’d taken Cade and Rayka in. And this Son of Ruin refused to be like his old mentor. He wouldn’t leave this young and impressionable girl to fend for herself.

He would be different. He had to be.

A large form stirred to Cade’s left, drawing his attention and thoughts away from the tiny goddess. He let out a sigh of relief when it was Only Gav. The poor lycanthrope had remained partially awake, his injuries from the transformation still healing as he sat in silent communion with himself, reconciling the beast and the man after their near-permanent separation.

The silence felt like a blessing after the chaos they’d survived, but Cade knew better than to trust it. Every victory in this nightmare labyrinth had been followed by a more dangerous challenge. Every moment of peace had simply been the calm before a greater storm.

His mind whirled with fragments of revelation and half-formed theories—the undead Arbiters bearing Honor’s ancient insignia, the strange architecture that predated human civilization, the pulses of unnatural energy that flowed through the walls like blood through veins. What were they truly walking into? What secrets lay at the heart of this ancient prison disguised as a tomb?

And most importantly—had Bernard already claimed whatever waited for them at the center?

Cade flexed his hand, watching silver-gold energy dance between his fingers before forcing it back beneath his skin. His core felt different since their battles in the crypt’s depths—more responsive but also more volatile, like a storm building pressure before breaking. The red energy that had briefly manifested worried him more than he cared to admit. Something was changing within him, evolving beyond his understanding or control.

“Existential crises are terribly unbecoming in someone with your panache,” observed a voice like velvet wrapped around a knife blade.

Cade didn’t startle. Somehow, he’d been expecting this visit, had felt it in the subtle shift of energies moments before the words materialized.

Death sat cross-legged on a fallen column directly across from him, looking for all the world like he’d been there for hours. His immaculate suit remained untouched by the filth and decay surrounding them, his perfect features arranged in an expression of mild interest, as if observing an unusual but not particularly significant specimen in a collector’s cabinet.

“Let me guess,” Cade replied, keeping his voice low enough not to disturb his sleeping companions. The last thing he needed after their near brush with this god’s namesake was meeting the man himself. “You’re here to tell me I’ve made a series of catastrophically poor decisions and we’re all about to die horribly.”

“If I visited every mortal on the brink of calamitous error, I’d never have time for my hobbies,” Death replied with a dismissive wave of elegant fingers. “I’ve simply come to observe a particularly interesting convergence of events.”

“Lucky us.” Cade’s eyes narrowed, studying the cosmic entity with calculated wariness. “I’m sure you get this a lot, but most people don’t consider it a positive sign when Death shows up for a personal viewing.”

“Most people lack imagination,” Death countered, absently adjusting the perfect crease in his trousers. “Besides, had I any interest in claiming you or your companions at this precise moment, this conversation would be significantly less verbal and considerably more... final.”

The casual dismissal of their mortality sent a chill snaking down Cade’s spine, though he kept his expression carefully neutral. “Then to what do we owe the honor of this visit? Forgive me if I sound suspicious, but our previous encounters haven’t exactly filled me with confidence in your… benevolence.”

Death’s smile was perfect, beautiful, and utterly devoid of genuine warmth.

“The Arbiter corpses were quite the revelation, weren’t they? Honor’s ancient shame, buried here far from prying eyes.” The deity gestured toward the passageway they’d fought through. “Did you never wonder why they remained here, perfectly preserved in their undeath for centuries? Why they weren’t simply allowed to decompose like proper casualties of war?”

The question caught Cade off-guard, triggering connections he hadn’t fully formed. “Honor sent them here to die,” he realized aloud. “Not just victims—they were deliberately placed here. Honor must’ve known the crypt would use them to protect itself. But why help this wretched place defend the darkness within? Doesn’t that go against, oh I don’t know, honor itself?”

“Honor is not goodness, Cade Stormhollow,” Death corrected, his obsidian eyes reflecting nothing. “The same force that demands a commoner’s death for bumping into nobility is the same that gives a lone knight defending a town from raiders the strength to stand tall. That dominion claims both the arrogance as much as the altruism you mortals display.”

Cade’s lip curled into a grimace. He could see it now. Hells, he’d lived both of those scenarios, though he’d been the commoner who managed to get away from a nobleman’s sense of honor.

Maybe Death was right.

“Look deeper, Cade. You have all the pieces. Why would Honor claim he had driven back the horrors of the Blightmarsh when you can clearly see many of them linger?” Death asked as he waved at the crypt around them. “Why would the creature controlling honor itself let glory slip past his fingers?”

Cade’s mind raced, pieces clicking into place with sickening speed. “The crypt predates the quarantine. It’s from the Progenitor era.” He paused, the implication suddenly clear. And it turned his stomach. “He wanted them alive.”

“Explain,” Death demanded.

“Honor’s celebrated triumph was a lie,” Cade started. “He didn’t defeat ancient horrors—he just found them already imprisoned and took credit for the containment. Because he knows what’s down here. I can see three options. Honor wants whatever’s down here, probably the crown. Or, he’s using the crypt to hide other secrets and it’s a convenient place for darker experiments or treasures he doesn’t want the public to know about.”

“And the third?” Death prompted.

Cade swallowed, glancing back at his crew. He met Death’s steady gaze.

“The third is that Honor tried to cleanse this place and failed. To hide his failure, he’s quarantined any and all evidence that might tarnish his stellar record,” Cade finished.

“You do not disappoint, Cade,” Death murmured, something almost like approval coloring his voice. “Yes, you are nearly right. The young upstart stumbled upon something the Progenitors had sealed away—something that offered him knowledge, power, and a reputation that would secure his position among the pantheon. All he had to do was maintain the containment, keep the secret, and ensure no one discovered what truly dwelled in the heart of the marshes. Oh, he tried to cleanse this place once upon a time, but now he needs it. Honor’s Light might be his capital, but this crypt is his seat of power.”

“And he slaughters his own soldiers when they discovered the truth,” Cade added grimly, thinking of the undead Arbiters that had nearly claimed Orro, Rayka, and the others. “Turning them into guardians to protect his lie for eternity.”

“Divine pragmatism at its finest,” Death agreed with a slight nod. “Though I find your moral outrage somewhat disingenuous, given your own tendency toward expedient solutions when properly motivated. I saw what you did to that dwarf connected to the paladin. His death wasn’t the only option, just the easiest.”

Cade did his best to ignore the barb but it was hard. “The Crown. The Soulkeeper’s Crown—it’s part of this, isn’t it? Not just some random artifact the Clover Clan is after, but something connected to whatever Honor found.”

“Now you begin to ask the right questions,” Death said, his perfect features sharpening with interest.

Something moved in the shadows behind the deity—a sinuous, many-limbed shape that slithered more than walked, its anatomy defying natural laws as it unfolded from darkness. It approached with predatory stealth, tentacles unfurling toward Cade’s unprotected back.

Death, without turning or acknowledging the creature in any way, simply raised one finger in a casual gesture.

Reality shattered around the horror. Cracks spread across existence itself, revealing glimpses of an infinite void filled with impossible geometries. The creature froze mid-attack, its form distorting as if viewed through broken glass.

Then it simply... ceased to be.

Not destroyed, not killed—erased, as if it had never existed at all. The fractures in reality sealed themselves with a sound like a distant sigh, leaving no evidence of the narrowly averted attack.

Death continued the conversation without pause, as if he hadn’t just casually unmade a creature that would have killed Cade instantly.

“The Crown is from the Progenitor era,” he explained, his voice unchanged. “Created during their final days, when they realized their doom was approaching. They sought to preserve... something of themselves. Something that could survive the divine purge.”

Cade swallowed, his mouth suddenly dry. “Is that... allowed? For you to just delete things from existence? To interfere like that?”

Death gave him a look that might, from a mortal, have been classified as pity.

“I am Death, Cade Stormhollow,” he replied, his voice carrying echoes of forgotten civilizations. “I do not require permission to perform my function. The rules that bind lesser entities do not apply to fundamental constants of reality.”

He folded elegant hands in his lap. “Besides, I find your continued existence... entertaining. It would be inconvenient to have it cut short by something as mundane as a crypt guardian.”

Cade filed away this casual omnipotence as yet another reason to tread carefully around the being before him, and returned to their original topic.

“What is the Crown exactly? What does it do?”

Death’s expression became contemplative. “The crown’s true purpose was obscured even from most Progenitors. Only their highest circle knew what truly dwelled within its twisted heart.” His obsidian eyes fixed on Cade’s with unsettling intensity. “It was designed to connect the wearer to the spirit realm, giving them access to all dominions. But the crown never quite managed such a feat. Instead, the path it forged between the two realms became a bit of a two-way street. Others hungry for a taste of this reality are always looking for a way across. That crown offers one.”

The implications hit Cade like a physical blow. “And they thought they could control it? Use it somehow?”

“The Progenitors believed they could harness its power to shield themselves from divine wrath.” Death’s perfect lips curved into something adjacent to amusement. “They were mistaken. You cannot harness what you cannot comprehend. They awakened something that had been dormant since before time had meaning, then discovered they couldn’t control it.”

“But they managed to contain it,” Cade pressed. “To seal it away.”

“At a devastating cost, yes.” Death’s voice carried the weight of witnessed annihilation. “The crown became its prison, and the seven orbs form the resonance pattern that maintains the dimensional barrier around it.”

Cade’s blood ran cold. “The orbs. Like the ones Elena and Gav retrieved from those death traps.”

“Components of the ward system,” Death confirmed. “Keys, if you will, though not in the way your Bernard believes. He thinks they’ll grant him access to the Crown itself. What they actually do is maintain the containment spell that keeps the crypt’s central chamber sealed.”

“And collecting them is weakening that seal,” Cade realized with growing horror.

“With each orb removed, the containment field deteriorates exponentially.” Death’s voice remained calm, but Cade detected a subtle tension beneath his perfect composure. “Your Bernard already has four more, taken from chambers you haven’t yet discovered. That’s six of the seven total.”

“Seven,” Cade murmured, the number striking a familiar chord. “Why seven? There are sixteen gods. Sixteen dominions to draw from. Sixteen pillars of magic.”

“The Progenitors came from a… wilder period of this world’s history. Magic, as you know it, is far more rigid than when they ran their dark experiments,” Death explained. “They made seven keys for seven powers they saw as superior to the rest, each containing an essence that resonates with its divine counterpart.”

“And if all seven are removed?”

Death’s expression became grave. “Then nothing will prevent the crown from connecting with whoever touches it. From establishing a conduit between mortal consciousness and primordial awareness. From transforming whoever wears it into a vessel for something that would regard gods as unruly peers and humans as less than the wobblewumps stomped underfoot.”

Something stirred in the darkness nearby—not a physical entity this time, but a ripple of awareness, as if some vast intelligence had momentarily directed its attention toward their conversation before turning away once more. It left Cade feeling hollow and exposed. He suddenly wanted nothing more than to be away from this horrid place.

“So we need to get those orbs back,” Cade said, fighting to keep his voice steady. “All of them. And return them to whatever containment system they’re part of. After stealing the crown, of course. I’ll make sure none of us put on it, rest assured.”

“Locking its powers away once more would be the prudent course,” Death agreed, examining his perfect nails with studied nonchalance. “Though prudence has never been your defining characteristic.”

“I’m wounded,” Cade replied, placing a hand over his heart in mock offense. “I’ll have you know I’m famous for my cautious approach to extremely dangerous situations.”

“Which is why you’re currently in an ancient crypt containing a primordial horror with a crew of wounded miscreants and a toddler goddess,” Death observed dryly.

“Exactly,” Cade grinned despite the gravity of their conversation. “Playing it safe, as always.”

Death studied him with those fathomless eyes, something almost like fondness flickering across his perfect features before disappearing like morning mist before the sun.

“You remind me of someone,” he said finally, the words carrying unexpected weight. “Someone from long ago, when the world was younger and possibilities stretched wider.”

Something in his tone made Cade pause. “Someone important to you?”

A shadow passed across Death’s face—not physical darkness, but something deeper, more personal. For the briefest moment, Cade glimpsed something almost human beneath the cosmic mask—regret, perhaps, or nostalgia.

“Someone who understood that endings are merely transitions, not absolute cessations,” he replied, his voice softer than Cade had heard before. “Someone who saw beauty in the cycle rather than tragedy in its individual components.”

Before Cade could pursue this unexpected glimpse beneath Death’s perfect façade, the deity straightened, composure returning like armor sliding into place.

“Your companions will wake soon,” he observed, glancing toward where Rayka was beginning to stir. “And decisions must be made.”

“Will you tell me where Bernard is?” Cade asked, recognizing that their conversation was drawing to a close. “Or just give me an advantage against whatever he’s planning?”

Death’s smile held secrets older than civilizations. “Where would be the fun in that? Some things must be earned through your own cleverness, Son of Ruin.”

“Worth a try,” Cade shrugged, unsurprised by the refusal.

“Indeed.” Death adjusted his immaculate cuffs, the gesture absurdly mundane for a cosmic entity. “One final piece of advice, freely given: the cathedral’s heart lies beneath what appears to be its foundation. What seems solid is merely misdirection.”

With those cryptic words, he began to fade, his perfect form becoming translucent, then transparent, until only his voice remained—a whisper that seemed to come from everywhere and nowhere simultaneously.

“Remember, Cade Stormhollow: don’t let that curiosity of yours be your downfall. Some puzzles are designed to remain forever unsolved.”

Then he was gone, leaving Cade alone with his sleeping crew and the weight of revelations too vast to fully comprehend. Across the chamber, Chubbs sat up, rubbing sleep-crusted eyes with the back of one hand.

“Were you talking to someone?” she asked, voice thick with lingering exhaustion.

Cade hesitated, considering how much to share and how much to shield them from. They deserved the truth, but the whole truth might crush what little hope remained down here. When they were back on the surface, he’d tell them everything.

“Just thinking out loud, little one,” he lied, forcing a casual shrug. “Force of habit when keeping watch. Helps stay awake.”

Chubbs nodded and smiled sleepily at him. “I trust you, big bro.”

His chest tightened, and Cade was grateful the low lighting prevented the adorable toddler from seeing the strain his smile gained.

As the others began to stir, Cade marshaled his thoughts, arranging what he’d learned into something he could share without terrifying them unnecessarily. They needed to know enough to understand the danger, but not so much that they lost the will to continue.

A primordial consciousness older than gods wanted to possess anyone who wore the crown they were tasked to steal.

Fantastic.

They now needed to steal from a group of elite thieves, collecting the seven orbs that maintained a barrier which kept the spirit realm from reaching them.

Easy.

Bernard was collecting those orbs without understanding what he was unleashing.

Wonderful.

And in the center of it all, a cathedral built atop secrets that had claimed the lives of those who discovered them.

Just another day in the life of Cade Stormhollow, he thought with grim humor. Just another impossible situation with the fate of the world potentially hanging in the balance. We got this.

And for the second time in as many minutes, the young thief lied to himself.
