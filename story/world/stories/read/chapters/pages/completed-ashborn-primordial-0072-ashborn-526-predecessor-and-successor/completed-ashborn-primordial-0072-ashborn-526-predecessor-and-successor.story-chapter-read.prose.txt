“I knew you would come,” Janak muttered, his mind seemingly elsewhere.

He was right. He was always right, as Vir had come to learn. Despite lacking the enemy’s vast advantage, Janak had thwarted their efforts and woven fate against their design. His existence alone gave Vir hope that this insane plan might actually work.

Using Dance of the Shadow Demon, Vir had guiltily slipped out from Maiya’s embrace without her noticing. He hadn't wanted to leave her to ride the lift down to one of the many empty halls of the great spire, but there was no choice. His mind refused to sit still and his stomach roiled with uncertainly. It was only through Prana Vision that he located the Imperium deity. Janak stood with his back turned, gazing into the distance in a room whose walls, floor, and ceiling perfectly simulated the ruins of Mahādi outside.

The god blazed like a black sun, his ash prana signature nearly identical to Vir’s own. If nothing else, it made him easy to find.

“And to answer your question, their locations will not matter,” Janak continued. “You may move every living being across the realm if you so desire. So long as their forms remain the same and their souls are ferried over, they will not notice any difference, aside from surprise at their new surroundings. Nor, to answer your other question, will Ash Tears be a problem in the unified realm.”“I don't understand,” Vir said. “What's to stop them from doing the same in the future?”

“Have you not wondered why the Imperium realm you visited was not ravaged by Ash Tears? Or even the second realm you saw, in which Andros reigned supreme?”

Vir had, in fact, wondered at that. “If I were to venture a guess... because they can't?”

Janak nodded. “For one, your understanding of the blight plaguing our realms is mistaken. It is less that they are ripping open Tears, but rather that Tears are opening due to the weakened fabric that underpins reality. Think of the realms as a great cloth. When its threads are weathered by wind and sun, they weaken and eventually snap. A hole forms in its place.”

“And I take it weakening the fabric of reality is a laborious process?”

Janak chuckled. “Quite. No, Akh Nara, you need not worry about Ash Tears plaguing the new realm. Of that at least, I am quite certain.”

The words should have been reassuring, yet Vir found himself more on edge. The confidence in the old god’s voice only served to unsettled him further. How could he place this much trust in another, even if Vir was the reincarnation of his predecessor?

“You’ll have to forgive me for not sharing your optimism,” Vir said.

To his surprise, Janak laughed.

“I admit, I am entirely unsure I can pull this off,” Vir continued, looking up at the ancient man. It was still difficult to believe he was here, speaking with him like this. “I should clarify. It’s not a matter of will. I will do this. I’m simply uncertain my body will endure long enough to complete the process.”

“That is not in question,” Janak replied, as though correcting something obvious. “My predecessor designed your body to last as long as it needs to.”

“At least one of us is confident,” Vir muttered, relieved despite himself.

“I am not,” Janak said.

Vir froze.

“Not of the mundane,” Janak continued with a dismissive gesture. “Not of the physical concerns. The machine is sound. You are sound, and my solution should work. No, young Akh Nara, it is not the act itself that troubles me.”

“It’s them, isn’t it?” Vir said. “What they’ll do to sabotage us. But you have a plan, right?”

The words slipped out before he could stop them, and he felt foolish as soon as he spoke. If Janak didn’t have a plan, then who did? What hope could they possibly have?

Janak turned, and Vir found himself held in place by the god’s glowing blue eyes.

“In all my searching across timelines, I have never found evidence of a universe in which my counterpart succeeded. Not once have I found any that progressed as far as we have,” Janak said. “Which may simply mean we are the first.”

“Or it could mean they destroyed every universe that attempted this,” Vir said quietly, “and erased all the evidence.”

“Perhaps,” Janak said with a frown, “though I find it difficult to believe them capable of such feats.”

Vir nearly scoffed at that. “What's to stop them?” he asked. “When they can rip open the very fabric of reality on a whim? When they can cause the Ash to consume all life?”

Janak shook his head. “You misunderstand the bounds of their power. Yes, they move in unknowable ways, affecting unpredictable events that set Fate down a certain path. But they are not omnipotent. In fact, there are but two factors to their power,” he said, holding up two fingers.

“Just two?” Vir asked in disbelief.

“Attention... and time,” Janak continued, retracting each finger as he said them. “Have you not wondered why they do not simply open Ash Tears in every universe that goes against their will? It would certainly be simpler.”

Upon reflection, Vir realized that was true. Why go through the hassle?

“Their attention is split among the great infinity of universes. As fearsome as they are, not even they can be in all places at once.”

“And in our case, your predecessor attracted their attention,” Vir said, realizing the implication of Janak's words.

“Indeed. As for time, five thousand years is plenty for them to weaken the fabric of reality. To inject eyes and ears and strengthen their control over the flow of Fate.”

Vir felt as though he had just learned something profound, but he struggled to see how the knowledge would benefit him... or the realms. The damage had been done. That they'd survived as long as they had was a miracle in and of itself.

“I once hoped to learn from my counterparts’ failures,” Janak continued, pacing slowly around the room with his hands clasped behind him. “I doomed entire universes in that pursuit, believing I could use what I learned to resist them. To truly fight back, rather than simply evade their notice. But I found nothing. Not even proof that such attempts ever existed.”

He stopped and faced Vir.

“You see my concern. After too many decades to count, I have run out of time while the enemy has basked in it.”

Vir met his gaze. “Except now you’re not alone. I don’t know what help I can offer—I don’t claim to understand even a fraction of this—but as you said, I am your successor. That has to count for something.”

“Indeed,” Janak said. “My kind has never been omniscient, despite what the realms believe. The longer I have lived, the more aware I have become of how much I do not know. Yet I must succeed. If not for our universe, then for all those I have convinced to aid me, even at great risk to themselves.”

Vir tried to imagine what those people must have felt. To risk drawing the attention of such a foe for even the faintest chance of success required either great wisdom or complete recklessness.

“There’s one thing that bothers me,” Vir said.

Janak chuckled. “Only one?”

“If we’re truly the furthest along,” Vir said, “shouldn’t they be focusing everything on us? They could have destroyed me directly, but they didn’t. Instead, they attacked our cities.”

Janak shook his head. “You continue to think of them as you would any enemy. They are not. If they have an agenda, it is not a plan in any way we can understand. From what I have learned, they act on instinct—an instinct for what the totality of universes should be. They examine futures and react according to some unknowable design. They see time as a whole in a way we cannot comprehend.”

“And what do they see when they look at ours?” Vir asked.

“Nothingness, perhaps. It may unsettle them, if they are capable of such a thing. But they cannot act directly upon our universe. They may be omniscient, but they are not omnipotent. They influence events through the smallest changes because they cannot do more. Even the Ash Tears are the result of weakened spatial conditions. Whether intentional or accidental, I cannot say.”

Vir frowned. “I don’t understand. What are they trying to do?”

Janak raised his hand, and the image of Mahādi vanished, replaced by darkness dotted with countless points of light. Lines connected them, forming a vast network.

“Every choice creates another universe,” Janak said. One of the points split into two. “From there, those spawn more of their own.”

“Cascading into infinity,” Vir murmured as the lights multiplied until the darkness vanished.

“Precisely.”

The projection faded and Mahādi's ruins returned.

Vir exhaled.

“Infinity is a strange concept,” Janak continued. “We see no evidence of it in our world. Lifespans are finite. The total prana in all realms is finite. All energy is finite. So how can our foe be limitless? Where does that energy come from?”

Vir thought it over, though he knew he lacked the knowledge to answer.

“Which means,” he said slowly, “if they are finite, then they must have limited energy to divide across universes.”

Janak nodded. “And if the number grows too vast, I fail to see how they can maintain control.”

Vir’s thoughts raced. He thought of Ash Tears appearing unpredictably, of careful manipulations across time.

“It’s because their attention is divided,” he said. “That’s how we fight them. By ensuring more universes survive.”

“Death means something different to them,” Janak said. “I doubt they understand it as we do. They are not evil, nor are they good. They simply are. Perhaps it is we who doom everything by opposing them.”

The thought sent a chill through Vir.

“So no matter what we do, they’ve already seen it,” he said. “If they don’t like the outcome, they’ve already laid the groundwork to stop it.”

“Assuredly so.”

“And you still think we should go through with this?” Vir asked, reeling at Janak's words. It made no sense at all. There had to be something he was missing. Another piece to this puzzle he was yet to unlock.

“What choice do we have?”

“Choice?” Vir said, throwing up his hands. “I have a choice. I could live out my life with Maiya. I don’t have to sacrifice myself for nothing.”

Janak was silent for a long moment.

“We must do what we can to live without regret,” he said at last. “When the time comes, you will make the right decision.”

He turned and walked away.

Vir watched him go, waiting for the weight of it all to settle.

It wasn’t that Janak didn’t know if the plan would succeed.

He knew it wouldn’t.

Vir felt a surge of disgust. After thousands of years, after everything he had done, Janak was just as uncertain as he was.

He had come seeking reassurance. Instead, he had found doubt.

Vir let out a short laugh.

It was absurd. Trying to understand reality. Trying to fight back against something like this.

Impossible.

And yet, wasn’t that what he had always done?

Hadn’t he learned magic when he had been told it was impossible? Hadn’t he overcome every obstacle placed before him?

This was no different. Only larger.

He returned to his mindscape strangely relieved.

There was no grand plan.

There was only him.

And somehow, that made everything simple.
