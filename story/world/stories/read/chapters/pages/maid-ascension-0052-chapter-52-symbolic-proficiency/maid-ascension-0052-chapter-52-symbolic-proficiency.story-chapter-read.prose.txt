When Lira had first been preoccupied with finding a way to secure Steward Dalwin’s cooperation, she had only skimmed through the runic primer after discovering it. At the time, she had learned a single spell, tested it once, and then promptly decided that the entire discipline looked far too involved to casually dabble in when she was already far too busy with political maneuvering.

She had told herself she would return to it later, once her life became less chaotic.

That had proven to be a deeply optimistic assumption, as she doubted her life would ever be completely mundane. Still, now that the library was stable, her alcove was functional again, and the immediate threat of magical discovery had temporarily subsided, Lira finally had what she had originally lacked: uninterrupted time, even if it came at the cost of sleep.

So at night, seated cross-legged on the floor of her alcove with the runic primer open beside her and her father’s lexicon spread out nearby, she decided to properly learn more about runic magic.

The first thing she wanted to test was the spell she already knew: Runic Ignition.

The first time she had cast it, she had used the common script to write the word “fire.” At the time, she hadn’t thought much of it. A symbol was a symbol, and the spell had worked. But then she had watched Edward Emberborn carve a rune instead. Not the common written word, but the actual rune associated with the magical word Fila.

Was it simply an aesthetic preference? Was it a magical tradition? Did it serve as a security layer to obfuscate magic, making it seem even more mystical in the eyes of common people? Or did the rune itself carry some kind of inherent efficiency?

There was only one sensible way to find out. Testing.

Lira gathered several sheets of paper and placed them carefully on the stone floor of her alcove, deliberately away from anything remotely flammable. She had salvaged the papers from an old storage box upstairs. Most were already used, covered in faded handwriting from someone repeatedly practicing elegant penmanship.

Fortunately, that was easy enough to solve.

“Asatan. Tir. Kisar.”

The ink across the pages shifted obediently beneath her will, peeling itself from the paper like liquid shadow before reassembling according to her intent.

When she was done, four test pages lay neatly arranged before her.

The first page had the common word fire written plainly in standard script.

The second contained the rune for Fila, carefully copied from her father’s lexicon.

The third displayed the rune for Maha, water.

The fourth page she intentionally left covered in meaningless scribbles, random loops and lines with no coherent meaning whatsoever.

She studied them thoughtfully for a moment before deciding that wasn’t enough.

A fifth page was added.

On this one, she wrote a short popular poem about fire in the Common script:

Ardent flame, born of wind and earth.Water’s bane, cause of ruin and hearth.

Lira leaned back slightly and observed her experimental layout with satisfaction.

Now came the interesting part. She pointed at the first page.

“Kisar. Fila. Curad.”

Immediately, the word fire ignited. The ink itself transformed into a small line of flame, flickering to life across the page. It burned brightly for perhaps two seconds before consuming the surrounding paper entirely.

Lira nodded and noted the result on another sheet of paper.

Next.

“Kisar. Fila. Curad.”

The second page activated. This time, the rune for Fila ignited with noticeably greater stability. The flame emerged cleaner, steadier, and far less chaotic. Most importantly, it lasted significantly longer, nearly a full minute before the page finally burned through.

Lira’s eyebrows rose. The rune was somehow far superior to common script.

She noted the result immediately.

Third test.

“Kisar. Fila. Curad.”

Nothing happened. The water rune remained stubbornly inert. That was to be expected.

Fourth test.

“Kisar. Fila. Curad.”

Again, nothing. Random scribbles did absolutely nothing, which was reassuring. If arbitrary markings could accidentally trigger spells, society would likely have collapsed already.

Finally, the fifth page.

“Kisar. Fila. Curad.”

This time, the entire poem ignited at once. Not just the lines directly referencing flame, but every word on the page.

Lira blinked. The fire remained surprisingly stable, nearly as long-lasting as the dedicated Fila rune itself.

That was unexpected. She leaned forward, watching carefully as the paper eventually blackened and curled into ash.

After several moments of thought, she began organizing her observations. The conclusion seemed fairly straightforward. The writing absolutely mattered. While not all written forms were equal, the meaningfulness of the writing carried weight, or maybe the symbolic aspect of writing existed on varying levels of efficiency. The common word worked, the poem worked better, and the rune worked best.

That suggested runes were likely a more efficient symbolic shorthand, a distilled magical language designed specifically for spellwork.

Meanwhile, the medium itself, the paper, only burned because the spell eventually exhausted its initial fuel source. An inefficient spell consumed its own canvas faster. A more stable spell prolonged the process. Which meant what she truly needed to test next was a spell that did not inherently destroy its own medium. Or perhaps a canvas that was more resistant to burning.

No, carving on metal would be too time-consuming. She could manipulate ink with magic, and she should better utilize that advantage. Besides, she was a scribe at heart.

Hopefully the primer would contain more diverse spells.

Lira reached for the book and resumed reading.

Unfortunately, the runic primer proved far less generous than she hoped.

Rather than offering a collection of beginner spells, most of the text shifted into theoretical discussions. It rambled extensively about concepts such as action and reaction, triggers and delays, passive states followed by abrupt changes.

There were analogies everywhere: a loud noise triggering an avalanche, crushing a bee causing a swarm attack, or a taut rope snapping after gradual strain.

All of it seemed to revolve around the idea that runic systems could exist in dormant states until specific conditions were met.

Finally, it offered another spell.

Kisar. Paldina. Fila. Curad. Taksel.

Below it, someone had helpfully scribbled a note in faded ink.

Contact Trigger Ignition.

Click.

Lira immediately broke down the components, as she already knew most of the words.

Kisar. Symbol.

Paldina. Passive.

Fila. Fire.

Curad. Ignition.

Taksel.... was unfamiliar to her.

She flipped open her father’s lexicon and scanned through the entries until she found it.

Taksel. Contact.

Her eyes widened slightly at the realization. This spell could be used to create traps. It magically charged a symbol into a passive state until physical contact activated it.

That was significantly more interesting than simple ignition.

She immediately prepared another test sheet, drawing the rune for Fila again.

Then cast.

“Kisar. Paldina. Fila. Curad. Taksel.”

Nothing happened. The rune remained perfectly inert.

Lira nodded slightly, that was to be expected, then cautiously poked it with the end of a broomstick.

The reaction was immediate. The rune ignited violently. Lira yelped and nearly dropped the broom, quickly jerking the stick backward before the flame could spread to the wood.

The fire burned with familiar stability, lasting roughly the same duration as the previous iteration, before eventually consuming the page.

She stared at the ashes with genuine fascination. This was far more complex than she had originally realized.

Runic magic was almost a magical syntax. It wasn’t merely spellcasting through writing. It was a modular language with components assembled into magical instructions.

Lira felt something inside her spark with excitement. This was exactly the sort of system she loved. Like learning a new language and deciphering its grammatical rules, patterns, and internal logic.

Unfortunately, the primer ended there. That was it. Only two spells: a cantrip and a Tier One spell. A handful of theoretical notes and nothing else.

Lira stared at the final page in disbelief.

“That’s all?” she muttered. “I want more!”

Which meant from this point onward, if she wanted to continue learning, she would have to do it on her own. She would need to invent her own spells again.

Lira slowly smiled.

Challenge accepted.
