There was no Virelle.

Aiven stood on the grass with the silk-wrapped talisman still half-unfolded in his hand, the islet absolutely quiet around him. The single seabird had finished its one call and not returned. The pale haze on the sea had begun, very slowly, to thin.

Lyra cried out.

It was a short, sharp sound that did not have a word in it. She had pushed herself most of the way up on the grass, brushing the hair back from her face, when her arms had given out beneath her and she had folded forward onto the grass with her forehead pressed against her own wrists. Her whole body was shuddering. A small, choked noise was coming out of her throat. Her hands were trying to make fists and not quite managing.

“Lyra!”

Aiven was beside her in two strides.

The pain on her face was bad. Her teeth were clenched so hard he could see the tendons standing out along her jaw. Her eyes were screwed shut. A single tear had escaped from the corner of one of them and was running down through the dust on her cheek.

Lucien.

It had to be. The compulsion. The pain Aiven had seen him inflict in the clearing six days ago, the same lash that had buckled Lyra’s knees in the grass. Whatever he had done to her, the spell apparently did not care about dimensions. It could find her across the Fold and back. It could find her anywhere.

Aiven’s hand was already under her shoulder.

He did not have time.

He scooped her up.

Her weight came against his chest, lighter than it should have been. He pushed off the grass with a hard, ugly shove of wind beneath his feet, the kind of flight that was less elegant than yesterday’s had been but was at least going to get him there. The deck of the scout-ship was thirty paces away. He covered it in seconds.

“PELKA!”

His voice was hoarse.

Pelka was already running.

She had been frozen at the foot of the ramp with both hands clapped over her mouth, but she was running now, faster than Aiven had ever seen her move, her clipboard nowhere in sight, her bangs flying. She met them at the railing of the deck, one hand already darting into the satchel at her hip.

“C-counterspell artifact, sir, please put her on the deck, on her back, here, this is—”

She was already producing it.

The artifact was a brass-cased syringe.

That was the most accurate way to describe it. The case was elaborate, a small ribbed casing of antiqued brass with a fine etched filigree running along its length, the kind of decorative work Marnie would probably have called overdesigned and snorted at. But inside the brass, the chamber was glass, and the chamber was full of a clear, deep blue liquid that shimmered faintly under the haze-light, the way deep water shimmers when something quiet is moving under it.

At the end of the case was a needle.

Pelka knelt beside Lyra. She did not waste motion. She rolled up the sleeve of Lyra’s robe at the elbow, found the soft hollow at the bend of the arm, and pressed the needle in with the calm precision of a clerk filing a final document.

She depressed the plunger.

The blue liquid disappeared into Lyra’s skin.

Pelka withdrew the needle and capped it with a small click.

“Sir,” she said. “It should begin to act within thirty seconds.”

Aiven knelt beside Lyra.

He took her hand. He did not say anything. He simply held it, and watched her face, and counted backward in his head from thirty, the way the clerks in the customs office used to count when they were checking if a stamped seal would hold.

Twenty-three.

Twenty.

Fifteen.

Around twelve, the shudder in Lyra’s shoulders began to ease. By ten, her jaw had unclenched. By six, her breathing was no longer the small, choked panting of a person in too much pain to breathe properly. By two, she had opened her eyes.

She looked up at him.

She tried, weakly, to smile.

“I’m all right,” she said with slightly heavy breaths. “I’m all right, Aiven.”

Her voice was rough at the edges but the agony had gone out of it.

Aiven let out a breath he had been holding since the grass.

Pelka, beside them, was repacking the brass casing into her satchel with a small, methodical bow of the head.

“It appears to have worked, sir,” she said. “The blue liquid is a refined counter-mana solution. We sometimes call it agent-blue, in the lab. Most spell-links work by mixing a small thread of the caster’s own mana into the target, ah, like a kind of remote conduit, sir. The agent-blue is calibrated to identify any foreign mana signature embedded in a target’s system, then dissolve and flush it out, neutralizing the link entirely. It is, ah, well, it is fairly sophisticated alchemy.”

She paused.

“I am sorry, sir, you probably did not need the explanation right now.”

Aiven shook his head, slowly.

“I did not need it,” he said. “But it was useful to hear. Thank you, Pelka.”

Pelka flushed and made several small bows that did not seem to know where to stop.

Aiven turned back to Lyra.

“Are you really feeling okay now?”

His voice cracked a little in the middle of the sentence. He had not noticed how rough his own throat was until he had tried to speak.

Lyra squeezed his hand.

“Yes,” she said. “I think so. The pain has stopped. The line is gone. I cannot feel him on me anymore.”

She paused.

“It is. It is the first time in a long time that I cannot feel him on me.”

She did not elaborate.

Aiven did not ask her to.

He did not know how to say it.

He drew in a breath. He looked down at their joined hands instead of at her face.

“Lyra,” he said. “Virelle is not here.”

Lyra blinked.

She turned her head, slowly, on the deck. She looked along the rail of the ship. She looked across the islet. She looked, finally, at the silk-wrapped talisman that was still in Aiven’s other hand, and then up at Aiven’s face.

“I thought you used the artifact,” she said. “To get us out.”

Aiven shook his head.

He lifted the silk. He unwrapped the rest of it, slowly, and held the talisman flat on his palm so she could see it.

It was whole. It was intact. It was unused. The single charge sat inside the smooth carved disc with the soft, untouched stillness of a thing that had not been triggered.

Aiven’s hands were shaking.

He held the talisman out, almost mechanically, toward Pelka.

“Pelka,” he said. “Check it. Please.”

Pelka took it with both hands.

She turned it over, twice, with the careful focus of a clerk authenticating a seal. She angled it to catch the light. She murmured a small phrase Aiven did not catch, and a faint diagnostic shimmer ran across the surface of the disc, blue-white, fading.

She looked up.

“It is unused, sir,” she said softly. “The charge is intact. The activation glyph has not been touched.”

Aiven nodded, several times.

He had needed to hear someone else say it.

Lyra had pushed herself up to sitting now.

Her hand had not let go of his. Her eyes were on his face, and there was a careful, quiet hurt in them, the kind that comes from someone who has just realized the thing they have been resisting in their own head was actually true.

“Does that mean,” she said softly, “that Virelle is still in the Fold?”

Aiven swallowed.

“I’m afraid so.”

He could feel his voice closing. He pushed through it anyway, because if he stopped to feel it he would not be able to start again.

“It is unlikely she sent us out. Wherever the teleport came from, it was deliberate. And we had agreed. We had a plan. We were supposed to leave together. All three of us. She would not have stayed unless. Unless she chose to stay.”

He stopped.

He did not finish that thought either.

There was a long, soft silence on the deck of the scout-ship.

Aiven’s clerk-brain was already moving.

It moved without his permission. It moved the way it always did, the way it had moved the night before the fight, the way it had moved in the manifest line before any of this had begun. He could not feel anything yet. The body was busy filing.

“Does Lucien,” he said. “Does he know teleportation magic? Could it have been him?”

Lyra looked up.

“If it had been him,” she said slowly, “he had many opportunities. He could have ended the fight at any point by simply removing one of you from the room. He did not. Whatever else Lucien is, he is not someone who wastes leverage.”

She paused.

“And. Teleportation across dimensions is, in any case, not something I have ever known anyone to cast. Not a mage. Not even Lucien. It has always required an artifact. The portals to the Fold were made by an artifact, originally. The system was very specifically designed so that mortal mages could not breach folded space on their own.”

She held his eyes.

“But I do not know all of Lucien’s power, Aiven. He has held things back, even from me. There is a chance. I cannot rule it out. But it would surprise me.”

Aiven nodded.

“Then I need to know how to get back into the Fold.”

His voice was flat. He was reading from the inside of his head.

“I have to go back for her. Lyra, how does it work? The portal. The way in.”

Lyra shook her head.

“I do not know how to open one myself.”

Aiven looked up sharply.

Lyra met his eyes without flinching. “I used to have an artifact. The same kind Lucien uses. He gave one to each of his inner circle so that we could return to the Fold from our assignments. Mine was taken from me, the day he took me back from the clearing. I have not held it since.”

She drew a breath.

“He holds the rest of them.”

She paused.

“Or he gave them to his other people. Valerion. Sylphaine. Daran. Anyone else he trusted enough to send out.”

Aiven’s mind turned the list.

Valerion was unreachable.

Sylphaine was the same. Unreachable. Probably worse than Valerion, because Sylphaine was the kind of unreachable that liked the game of being unreachable.

Daran. Aiven did not know where he was, what he looked like, what he wanted.

Lucien’s other people. Anyone else.

The list narrowed.

It narrowed.

There was one name left at the end of it.

Cyria.

Aiven’s head snapped up.

“Pelka.”

Pelka, who had been kneeling at a respectful distance with the brass casing held neatly in her lap, straightened immediately.

“Y-yes, Sir Aiven.”

“Contact Cyria. Now. Right now. Open whatever comms channel Vulpine uses with the scout-ship. We need her urgently. Tell her the we—” he was groping for the right phrasing and could not find it “tell her something has gone wrong. Tell her we need her.”

Pelka did not move.

Her hand had risen, halfway, toward the satchel at her hip, and then stopped.

Her face had gone, quite suddenly, very pale beneath her bangs.

“Sir,” she said.

“Sir, about that.”

She swallowed.

“The Boss has been unreachable since this morning.”

Aiven stared at her.

“What?”

Pelka was twisting the strap of her satchel in her hands now. Her glasses had slipped a fraction down her nose and she had not noticed.

“O-our regular morning check-in. Six bells, ship-time. Every active operation phones in. The Boss always confirms. This morning, she…she did not.”

Pelka swallowed again.

“I assumed she was busy with the confrontation. I thought, perhaps, she did not wish to be disturbed. I did not raise it with you because. Because it did not seem to be the sort of news that should be brought into a fight.”

She bowed her head, slightly.

“I am sorry, sir. I should have—”

“It is fine, Pelka.”

It was not fine.

Aiven’s mind was moving very fast.

Cyria.

Cyria, who yesterday afternoon had sat in the meeting room of the Cinder-Fox with him and Virelle and openly admitted she had been Lucien’s subordinate for years. Cyria, who had handed him a teleportation talisman with a small bow, calling it a hedge.

Aiven did not know yet what it meant.

It might mean Lucien had finally moved on her, the way he had said he would move on anyone who opposed him, and she was in a chamber somewhere in the Fold with thin chains drawn across her ribs.

It might mean she had moved first, and was somewhere making the next play on her own board.

It might mean other things.

He did not know.

He did not have time to know.

He had to be somewhere a person could be told things.

Aiven stood up.

“Pelka,” he said. “Wake the helm. We are leaving for Fangreach. Now. As fast as this ship can move.”

Pelka was already on her feet.

“Y-yes, sir.”

Aiven turned back to Lyra.

She had pushed herself fully up now. The robe was a little dusty. The brown scarf had been straightened. She was watching him, with that same careful, wary blue gaze he had seen on her face in the hall of the Fold.

“Lyra,” he said quietly. “You should rest up. I…have a lot to do.”

He swallowed.

“There is a lot to do.”

Lyra nodded.

She took his hand.

The scout-ship’s engines were already cycling up, the low warming hum of the mana-core ramping toward flight, the rigging tightening in its braces.

Far behind them, in the middle of the islet, the thirteen earth pillars stood in their crooked rows. They cast long, flat shadows across the grass in the haze-light.

Aiven did not look back at it.

He could not afford to look back at it.
