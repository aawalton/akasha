The megaphone wasn't built for a crowd this size. It was supposed to be a prototype, nothing more, but it was all I had, so it would have to do.

"Mosha, table thirteen, that's it, keep going!" My voice came out of the wooden cone sounding like it had been dragged through a fireworks display, but at least it carried well. "Sir! Walk, don't run, please!"

Taka Memorial Square had stopped looking like an open space an hour ago. Rows of rented tables stretched out from the statue's shadow in every direction, and every single one of them was already full or filling. I had to make two more last-minute teleports just to bring in extra chairs, letting Shingo and the kids expand the seating outward as fast as they could set it up. Somewhere north of four thousand people were already seated. Even so, more stood at the edges, watching, some hoping a seat would open up, others content just to be here.

Glancing up, I noticed a handful of flying figures overhead, mostly Valkyren, but a few other kinds of flyers too. I recognized Nina's wing pattern among them and gave her a wave before spotting someone else who looked familiar. It took me a moment, but I placed him: the man from the Great Dream forum, the one who had delivered that meticulous exposé on my rumored sainthood. I shuddered and lowered my eyes back to the crowd.

"Grandmother, this way, please." One of the orphanage boys, Dean, was guiding an elderly Soulit woman toward the roped-off section near the fountain we had requisitioned. We had set aside two dozen tables specifically for the elderly and anyone with small children, mostly for safety. Dean led her by the elbow patiently while she complained loudly about her knees the entire way.

I made a mental note to pay him double. Honestly, everyone was doing a great job managing a crowd this size on such short notice.

Most of the square was well organized. Apart from the tiramisu lines, that is, where most people were collecting their lottery tickets for a shot at the dessert that had summoned a goddess.

"Section two, keep your line tight!" I called through the megaphone. "You'll get there faster if you're not spread out!"

Somewhere behind me, Ja'a's voice cut through the noise, sharp and businesslike. "You! Stop bringing out cheese twists. Vary the servings. Send more pizza slices instead."

I hadn't asked her for help, but Ja'a had shown up on her own and appointed herself logistics officer of the whole operation, and frankly, I was grateful. Left to my own devices, I would have had the kids wandering around with platters, offering food to whoever wanted it. Ja'a overrode that entirely, splitting the servers into sections so each table got one random dish per pass. The logic was simple once she explained it: people needed to see the variety, not taste all of it. A little scarcity kept the line moving and kept the wonder intact.

The official opening was still happening back at the main shop, where Silnar and Louis were handling paying customers, which meant this square wasn't getting anything beyond what we had prepped specifically for it: a thousand servings of tiramisu, two thousand slices of Sicilian-style pizza baked in square platters to optimize oven space, and another two thousand assorted puff pastries. Everything went out the moment it came off the tray. To keep a line from forming back at the shop itself, I had stationed two kids whose only job was to redirect anyone drawn there by the rumors, pointing them here instead, where a chance of a free taste of tiramisu was waiting.

By the time the bulk of the crowd had found somewhere to sit, stand, or lean against whatever wasn't nailed down, I climbed onto the low stone platform at the statue's base. Raik's father looked down at me, flames frozen mid-burst from both bronze hands.

I raised the megaphone.

"Hello, Hano!"

Both H's hissed like static, but the words came through clean enough.

"My name is Alice Hecate, and on behalf of Louis, Silnar, and everyone at The Delightful..." I gestured toward the red banner strung between two lampposts, its white circular heptagram crest catching the light, "...thank you. Genuinely. We did not expect this many of you to show up for a pizza shop's opening day, and your being here is the highest compliment you could give us."

"As you can see, we've placed a few donation boxes around the square. Every coin dropped in will be matched in full and go straight to the Temple's Soup Kitchen Initiative."

I found Sana near one of the boxes and Gray manning the other. Both gave me a small nod when our eyes met.

"So enjoy the food, and know that your being here means more people get fed tonight."

The cheer that followed was louder than the speech deserved, honestly, but I wasn't about to complain about enthusiasm.

"We do apologize that food is going out in waves. If it hasn't reached your table yet, you haven't been forgotten, I promise. In the meantime..." I gestured toward the small raised platform beside the fountain, "...we have entertainment."

I stepped down before the crowd could ask any follow-up questions and made my way toward the stage, where Riri was waiting.

She had, thankfully, found an outfit more modest than the feathered bikini top I had last seen her in, though "modest" was only relative to what she usually wore. Her magenta hair was pinned up. Zira sat a few feet away in a wooden chair, her face stuck in an expression of deep concentration.

"Ready?" I asked.

"Was I ever not?" Riri grinned, already cradling her mandolin against her hip. The instrument's strings shimmered faintly, the same ethereal glow I remembered from Nina's Hall of Memories.

"Just don't start a riot."

"No promises." She winked and stepped onto the platform.

I stood over the stage and called on the megaphone. "I present to you: Riri, the songstress!"

Riri didn't bother speaking or even waving; she just started playing immediately.

The first few notes were simple, a single clean melodic line plucked out slowly enough that half the square barely registered the music had begun at all. Then, without dropping a beat, Riri looped the phrase, and the mandolin kept playing it. On its own. While her fingers moved on to something else entirely.

I watched recognition ripple outward through the crowd as they placed the melody.

A second layer joined the first, a low, rolling bassline that had no business coming out of an instrument with so few strings, looping the same way the first had, held in the air like it had been recorded and set loose. A third layer followed, higher, brighter, threading between the other two. By the time a fourth voice joined, weaving counterpoint through all of them, it no longer sounded like one woman playing a mandolin. It sounded like an entire string quartet had crowded onto that little platform, and Riri was simply conducting all four of them at once, her hands a blur, her eyes half-closed in concentration.

Behind the stage, Zira clapped once, sharp, and the crowd noise dropped as Riri's performance swelled outward, reaching the back rows without losing an ounce of clarity up front.

Then Riri began to sing.

I recognized it immediately. The same song, in the Holy tongue, that had turned into a meme back in the Great Dream, the one the fox-girl bard had been butchering with nonsense syllables months ago, the one I still caught myself humming without meaning to. Despite the foreign words, Riri sang it properly, every syllable landing exactly where it belonged, her voice riding easily over the four-part harmony her own instrument kept spinning out beneath her.

"Flying sky high, a lasting lullaby, of peace and friendship, love and kinship, dream with me."

Somewhere to my left, I heard Ja'a start humming along under her breath. A few tables over, a cluster of children had abandoned their pizza entirely to sing the chorus at full volume, badly, joyfully, and off-key in at least three different directions at once.

I leaned back against the statue's base and let myself relax for a moment, just standing there, listening.

Thousands of people… A dessert that had summoned a literal goddess… A mandolin playing four parts by itself in the middle of a public square… Flying people watching from overhead.

None of this would have been believable to me six months ago, before that fateful night in Sidi Bou Said.

I was still smiling when Calr appeared at my elbow, breathless, smelling faintly of tomato sauce.

"Alice," he said, panting, "something weird is happening."

I guessed that thirty seconds of relaxation was all I was going to get.

We walked together toward the pavilion where the food was stored, the front of the tent guarded by Knight Ami and Shingo, who had joined her the moment he finished hauling the last of the tables into place.

"So what is it?" I asked.

"You know about my eidetic memory, right?" Calr said.

I nodded.

"Well, I've been counting the trays that leave storage."

As he spoke, a kid ducked out of the tent carrying another tray of cheese twists, balanced on one arm like he had been doing it his whole life.

"That's tray sixty-two of the cheese twists," Calr declared.

"Nonsense. We've only brought fifty trays total."

He gave me a look.

"Are you sure you didn't miscount?" I asked, already knowing the answer.

"I can't miscount," Calr said, dead serious. "My brain literally cannot make mistakes. Especially not with numbers."

"What are you saying?" I frowned. "Trays of food don't just appear out of nowhere."

"That's why I came to get you."

I ducked into the tent and found the baking racks, the ones that were supposed to be nearly stripped bare by now, still full. A few empty slots here and there, sure, but most of the food sat completely untouched, stacked exactly where it had been an hour ago.

"What the..." My voice trailed off. By now we should have been scraping the bottom of everything except the tiramisu, which we had deliberately held back for last. Instead, half the tent looked like we hadn't served a single soul.

"Are you seeing what I'm seeing?" I asked, turning back toward the entrance.

Calr didn't answer. He was still outside, one hand raised against the tent flap, frozen mid-motion like someone had pressed pause on him.

No, not just him. The whole world had gone still.

"I suppose I should have expected that a Thinker would see through my miracle," said a woman's voice, calm and unhurried, from somewhere behind me.

I spun around.

A blonde woman stood in the middle of the tent, where a second ago there had been nothing but empty air and stacked crates.

"La... Lady Laurel."

She smiled at me, and every ounce of exhaustion I had been carrying since dawn simply evaporated. I felt rested, like I had just woken from the best sleep of my life. Her smile was, apparently, a healing miracle all on its own, and she hadn't even bothered lifting a hand.

"I owe you an apology," she said. "For the commotion I caused, manifesting the way I did over your dessert. That was careless of me."

"I... Is it fine? Great, actually, but fine."

"I only meant to bless this tent quietly," she continued, glancing around at the untouched racks. "So that no one here would go hungry today, no matter how many showed up. The servers weren't meant to notice that the food never ran low. The guests weren't meant to notice they had eaten their fill or that there was a miraculous amount of food being served."

"Except Calr noticed."

"Except Calr noticed," she agreed, a note of genuine amusement creeping into her voice. "A Thinker counts everything, whether he means to or not. I imagine it must be exhausting, being unable to stop yourself from knowing things."

I glanced back at the frozen shape of him in the doorway, hand still raised, eyes fixed on nothing.

"Why not just appear to him, then? Explain yourself, ask him to keep quiet, whatever it is you do?"

Laurel laughed, a sound far lighter and younger than I expected from someone who had been alive for two thousand years.

"I stopped manifesting in front of impressionable young men a long time ago," she said. "It has a habit of going poorly. One of them gets a little too taken with me, and before you know it, he's founded a religion, or started a revolution, or declared a holy war in my name. I learned that lesson thoroughly. Once was enough."

I thought of Kurt. Of a boy who had loved her so much he had bent three thousand years of history just to stand beside her as an equal.

"Fair," I said, wincing.

"You, on the other hand," she said, tilting her head at me with something between fondness and evaluation, "are a much safer choice. I doubt seeing me is going to make you devote the rest of your life to my worship."

"I guess not," I said. "No offense."

"None taken. It's rather refreshing."

"By the way," she added, waving a hand almost carelessly, "I already swapped every lottery ticket for Laurel's Delight into a winning one."

I blinked. "Every ticket?"

"Every one. Don't worry, no one will question it. People rarely question good luck. This way, a few thousand people get to taste something rare today, and it takes pressure off the main shop, besides. Good for the dessert's reputation, too, once you're ready to lower the price and let ordinary people afford it regularly."

"Thank you. I guess." I hesitated, then decided I might not get an opportunity like this twice. "While I have you here, can I ask you something?"

"You may ask anything. I make no promises about answering."

"The Saint rumors. Could you... I don't know, make them go away? Or at least set the record straight?"

"No." She reached out and booped me on the nose with one finger, light as a feather.

"That one, dear girl, you'll have to figure out on your own."

I blinked, and she was gone.

"See?" said Calr, entirely unaware of the divine presence that had just been standing three feet from him.

I sighed.

"It's divine intervention. The Lady just blessed this tent so we don't run out of food." Behind me, I heard Knight Ami's armor clink as she turned sharply toward me. Even Shingo gave me a look.

"Please keep it a secret," I said. "We don't want a riot."

The three of them nodded.

I started running through what needed to be done next.

"Calr, stay here. Tell the kids they can keep serving the same tables again, but only after they've made sure every table has eaten at least once already. If anyone asks, tell them I'm teleporting in more food. They shouldn't question it, but just in case."

"And have a few of the older boys carry trays out to the people standing at the edges," I added. "Shingo, set up a table over there and wave the flying folks down so they can get a taste too."

"I'll go tell Ja'a about our change in fortune."

As I headed toward her, Riri had already moved on to a new song, one I recognized because I had heard Mosha sing it before. Though Mosha seemed to have sung a translated version in common, while this one was the original, in the ancient Holy tongue. The song was about Lady Laurel and her secret love for Kurt, long before the final reveal.

“Oh, people, hear me... hear my song,for I bear a tale... short yet long!

Oh sir, brave knight... heed thou my plea,this tale I sing... not of thee!

I sing of a man... lost to history,the third of two... an endless mystery!

Oh, lost man... whither hast thou gone?Since the holy wars... seen by none!

Oh, timeless Lady... Lady so fair,safer stand we all... beneath thy care!

Strong and mighty... art thou as well,yet in thy heart... only he shall dwell!

Through years and ages... thy regard unchanged,for love we grasp... we bards, unrestrained!

What is thy secret?... thou wilt not share,and asking the question... none would dare!

Lonely is the Lady... without a word,and the voice she craves... shall it be heard!”

I hoped the Lady wasn't offended by the song. Then again, it was apparently a fairly popular one among the Holy Faithful, so I figured she had probably heard worse by now. I explained the situation to Ja'a in a hushed voice and asked her to start the tiramisu serving immediately. She nodded once and headed off toward the lottery line without a single follow-up question, which I appreciated more than anything.

I returned to the pavilion.

Looking around at the sheer volume of food still stacked on the racks, I remembered something. We had already sent everything precooked out to the square, which meant Louis and Silnar were stuck cooking for each new order back at the shop as more and more guests kept arriving. They had to be drowning.

I hesitated, then lowered my head.

"Lady, would it offend you if I took some of this back to the restaurant? Louis and Silnar could really use the help."

I waited a beat. Nothing. I was about to open my eyes and give up when the answer arrived, warm and unbothered, directly in my mind.

"Feel free. Just know that any excess will vanish come the rise of the magenta moon."

That gave me roughly twenty-four more hours before it disappeared.

"Thank you."

I loaded several trays into my bag of holding, summoned my spear, and teleported straight into Louis and Silnar's kitchen.

The space was packed. Ten apprentices moved shoulder to shoulder with the couple themselves, and Lera burst through with a fistful of written orders, snatched up a few finished plates, and vanished back out the door before I had fully taken my bearings.

I found Louis and asked where I could help.

"I need more pizza slices for table seven, but the ones in the oven aren't ready yet," she said, glancing at the order slip in her hand, distracted.

"Let me see what's queued." I started pulling trays out of my bag of holding. "Six pizzas, a few quiches, three servings of tiramisu."

Louis didn't even blink or ask where any of it had come from. She just grabbed the pizzas, slid them back into the oven for a minute to remelt the cheese, and handed the tray back to me. I was already dressed for the job, apron and all, so I started serving.

To my surprise, the first table I was assigned turned out to be occupied by Commander Marina, Captain Yoka, Captain Kerissa, and a thoroughly starstruck Nada, who kept staring at Marina like she couldn't quite believe she was sharing a table with her.

Especially not once the composed, poised Commander of Hano demolished three servings of tiramisu single-handedly.

"Alice, you seem to be handling things well," Yoka said.

"Yes, Captain. Thank you for the extra freelancer patrol."

"Don't mention it." She smiled and took another spoonful of tiramisu.

"I must say, this is genuinely good," said Kerissa, crunching through a bite of Napoleon cake before continuing. "Though I'm afraid I would gain weight if I ate like this every day."

"You should come by training room three at the guild sometime. Yon's testing out some new equipment. As a Kindred, you might find it interesting."

"New training equipment, you say?" Her eyes lit up. "I'll be there."

I glanced around the shop, still full to bursting, though the crowd here skewed heavily toward elite freelancers and Soulit Guild managers rather than the everyday middle-class families we had actually built the menu for. Not entirely our target audience, but understandable, given the location. Hopefully the next branch, closer to Market Street, would draw a more ordinary crowd.

My eyes landed on Lord Mick Ira in the corner. He gave me a small nod as he sipped his coffee, a plate of tiramisu waiting half-finished beside him.

The rest of the evening blurred into motion. I made three more trips between the square and the kitchen, teleporting in fresh trays whenever Louis or the apprentices started looking frazzled, and by the time the yellow sun had dipped low enough to paint the whole square gold, most of the crowd had finally been fed at least once, some of them twice.

Every single person who had gone through the trouble of collecting a lottery ticket got their serving of tiramisu, every one of them a winner, though none of them noticed that. So did every child in the square, and most of the elderly, though I had Vena quietly check on the older guests first, just in case anyone's constitution couldn't handle that much sugar and cream in one sitting. She had waved most of them through with a smile and pulled aside exactly two, gently, kindly, redirecting them toward a plainer dish instead.

I climbed back onto the platform as the light started to fade, megaphone in hand, and let myself have one last look at the square before I started talking. Full tables. Empty plates. Children asleep in laps. Somewhere behind me, Riri had finally let her mandolin rest, and the square had gone quiet enough that my voice, hissing and crackling as it was, carried easily to every corner.

"Before everyone heads home," I said, "I wanted to say a proper thank you."

"To Riri, for the music, and to every kid who spent the whole day running trays back and forth without a single complaint." A ragged little cheer went up from somewhere near the fountain, where most of my hired help had finally sat down themselves. "To the Temple of the Holy, for everything they do every single day, charity event or not." I found Sana's eyes in the crowd and held them a moment. "To the Freelancer Guild, for keeping four thousand people from turning into a stampede." A polite ripple of applause went up, and I spotted Yoka floating somewhere overhead, giving me an approving nod.

"And to every single one of you. For showing up. For donating. For making today mean something beyond just a new restaurant opening its doors."

I should have stopped there. I knew I should have stopped there.

I hesitated instead.

"I'm sure everyone with the Faithful class has already noticed," I said, and felt my own voice waver slightly through the megaphone, "that I'm Alice Hecate. The one from the rumors. But I'm not a Saint. I have enormous respect for your faith, genuinely, but I'm not even Faithful myself. You can tell, can't you? I've heard the Faithful can sense that sort of thing in each other." I took a breath. "So I'm sorry for any confusion, but I'm really not a Saint. Okay?"

"That's exactly what a Saint would say!" someone shouted from the crowd.

"Yeah!" another voice called back. "The prophecy said you will be a Saint, not that you are one!"

"You're on the right path!" a third voice encouraged, entirely unhelpfully. "Keep at it, and you'll make it in time!"

"Saint Alice! Saint Alice!" A teenager somewhere near the front started the chant, and it caught immediately, spreading table to table until half the square had picked it up, laughing, delighted, utterly convinced that I was rejecting the moniker out of saintly modesty.

I dropped the megaphone.

Both hands came up to cover my face, and somewhere far above me, in a place I couldn't see and had no business hearing, I could have sworn I heard a goddess laughing at my misfortune.
