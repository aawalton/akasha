It was a cool Bianjing night.  The courtyard of the Guan estate was lit by glowing lanterns – and humming with the delicate plucking of a zither.  High-ranking ministers, wealthy landholders, and ambitious scholars drifted across the marble paving stones, their embroidered sleeves brushing against stone tables laden with roasted chestnuts and vintage wines.

Yan Shu adjusted his sapphire-blue scholar’s robes, stepping through the moon gate and into the high-society atmosphere with an aura of relaxed nonchalance.

Amongst the gathered aristocrats, the arrival of the young Imperial Collator was an event in and of itself.  At fourteen, Yan Shu had sent shockwaves through the Empire by passing the highest level of the Imperial Examinations, earning a personal commendation from the Emperor himself in the process.  Blessed with Imperial recognition, effortless charm, and a catalog of poetry that made the song girls swoon, he was considered one of the most eligible bachelors in Bianjing.

Invitations like this arrived on his desk occasionally. Wealthy lords with eligible daughters were constantly throwing elaborate banquets, eager to snare a young genius with Imperial prospects into their family lines. Normally, Yan Shu declined them without a second thought. The daughters of these noblemen were infallibly painted like delicate porcelain and trained to nod demurely, but they possessed all the intellectual depth of an empty teacup.

Tonight, however, the invitation had come from Censor Guan.

The Censorate was the highest-ranking watchdog within the Imperial bureaucracy. Censors were the auditors, investigators, and enforcers who made sure the rules were followed. They had the power to drag even a high-level Minister before an Imperial tribunal, if one was suspected of corruption.  Censor Guan, in particular, was notorious for being a man of iron discipline and terrifying ambition…with a predatory eye for any high-profile scandal that could elevate his status at court.

Tucked neatly within the left sleeve of Yan Shu’s robe lay a single folded document, sealed with the official mark of the Imperial Library.

The Black Ink Consortium had done everything properly when they’d finalized their bid for the Shandong highway toll rights. All the paperwork was in order, and they had submitted an archival copy to the Imperial Library, exactly as required. But no one is perfect, and every multi-page transcript always comes with some small copying errors.

And who was to say which discrepancy was a small copying error, and which was a deliberate omission? Surely not Yan Shu.

“Collator Yan,” a soft, melodic voice called out from beside a cluster of blooming orchids.

Yan Shu turned and offered a deep, formal bow. Standing there in the moonlight, looking right at home amongst the flowers, was the beautiful Lady Guan. She was dressed in an elaborate gown of woven purple silk, her hair was pinned with a large, golden binyeo, and her soft skin reflected the moonlight almost like a mirror. She looked fairly calm, but Yan Shu picked up on the subtle, expectant glances she was occasionally casting towards her father, across the courtyard.

“Lady Guan! I am honored to have received your invitation. I had heard rumors of your family’s gardens, but…their beauty pales in comparison to that of their host. I’m fairly certain those orchids weren’t originally red; you’re just making the flowers blush.”

The young lady looked down towards her feet, her cheeks flaming up to match the flowers. “You flatter me, Collator Yan. You know, I…I read your recent work, the one you titled Mu Lan Hua. ‘Entranced by the zither; untying pendants of jade. One couple, finding immortality in the divinity of love.’ It…moved me.”

“A poem is only as good as the mind that appreciates it,” Yan Shu replied, stepping closer. “You know, my lady. I find the noise of the main pavilion to be somewhat…overwhelming. Would you perhaps be willing to give me a tour of the private courtyards your father is so famous for?”

Her eyes brightened. “Of course! It would be my absolute pleasure.”

The young noblewoman led him away from the bustling crowd, guiding him down a quieter, stone-paved corridor that led out into the main gardens. As they walked, she spoke animatedly about poetry and court music, clearly doing her utmost to impress him. Yan Shu listened and fed her occasional nods, playing the role of a dreamily captivated scholar. As he did, he began to memorize the layout of the estate.

After about a ke of casual, meandering walking together, the young collator found what he was seeking. Through a set of elegant lattice doors, he spied what seemed to be a private writing desk, overlooking the gardens. He gestured towards it.

“Ah, to be treated to such a view every day! To a poet, what could be more inspiring than a writing desk that comes with its own built-in muse? I find myself deeply jealous of the man who owns it.”

“Oh! That. That’s just my father’s study. He does his work there. But yes, he loves these gardens.”

“Ah yes, the gardens,” Yan Shu murmured. He stepped inside the doorway, gesturing towards a series of framed calligraphy scrolls hanging on the wall. “And…what exquisite brushwork! Is this his hand?”

“Yes! He practices calligraphy every day at dawn. He says it brings him peace…”

Yan Shu stepped into the room. Lady Guan rushed to follow him, increasingly unsure of what was happening. She came up alongside him, placing a hand on his arm. “My lord, what…”

The young collator pulled her in front of himself, taking a step forwards and gently guiding her back until her hips brushed against the front edge of her father’s mahogany desk.

Lady Guan’s breath caught in her throat as Yan Shu leaned in slightly, his face mere inches from hers. He looked directly into her eyes, his voice taking on a soft, hypnotic rhythm as he recited the continuation to the line she had quoted earlier.

“Yet even they will one day part. Eternity is longer still, and silk robes tear when clutched too hard.”

The young woman stood there in silent shock, her gaze locked onto his face, her heart hammering in her chest. She closed her eyes and pursed her lips, clearly expecting to be ravished right there on her father’s desk.

Yan Shu’s right hand moved with the effortless precision of a master pickpocket. Slipping his fingers into his sleeve, he withdrew the audit dossier from his robe. Without making a sound, his hand glided across the mahogany desk, dropping the document into the center of a wooden box labeled: “Priority Petitions.”

Before the moment could cross into genuine impropriety, the young Collator stepped back, taking her hand and bowing with deep respect.

“Forgive me, Lady Guan,” he whispered, offering an apologetic, slightly sheepish smile. “A poet is easily intoxicated by a fine evening and inspiring company. I must not lose my composure in your father’s house.”

“I...um…it is quite alright, Collator Yan…”

“Shall we rejoin the banquet?” he asked, offering his arm.

An hour later, Yan Shu slipped out of the Guan estate and stepped into a waiting carriage. He leaned back against the silk cushions, letting out a soft, contented sigh as the vehicle rattled over the capital’s cobblestones.

He could already picture the chaos that would erupt tomorrow morning.

Chae-ha extended a single thumb, pressing it firmly into the seam between two granite blocks on the lower tier of the gatehouse wall.

The gray paste yielded beneath the pressure like soft clay, leaving a shallow, dark imprint in the masonry. She withdrew her hand, rubbing the gritty lime residue between her fingers. The surface had skinned over under the mountain sun, but beneath that thin crust, the core remained damp.

It had been three full days since the Meng-shan force had secured the crossroads. By her calculations, they should have cleared the fallen timber, bypassed her other traps, and finally seized the Deer Valley mine late last night.

A faint, wry smile made an appearance as she pictured the scene. The Meng-shan would have been expecting to face a garrison – only to find the entire site abandoned. They had spent three full days capturing a hollow shell.

The issue now was what they would choose to do next.

At least one plan of action was obvious. The default thing almost anyone in this situation would do was to continue holding the crossroads. The Meng-shan already had what they wanted, and they were in a position to keep it. Simply staying put would allow them to work on repairing the roads, getting the mine up and running, and establishing a secure supply line back to Mt. Meng. That process would take weeks. It was the sensible, methodical line – and if they took it, Chae-ha’s carpenters and masons would have all the time they needed to finish the construction.

There was also a second possibility. Less probable, but infinitely more dangerous.

If their strategist recognized the delays for what they were – a stalling tactic – his pride might demand an aggressive response. If he sensed a trap being laid, he might choose to push forwards pre-emptively to trigger it.

Should he choose that path, the opening move was obvious: his main column at the crossroads would sweep aside the two hundred men of the Tai-shan decoy force, pushing north along the main thoroughfare. A half-day’s hard march would bring his scouts within sight of this engineered clearing. Instead of weeks, they would be lucky to have two full days.

Chae-ha looked up at the skeletal timber framing above her. The mortar still needed at least three more days to set.

There was a silver lining here. The scouts would have no way of knowing the masonry was still wet. The most likely outcome was that the Meng-shan would see the wall, realize that the clearing was a death trap, and refuse to engage. By the time they managed to settle on a counter-strategy, the mortar would have dried completely.

Which left only the true disaster scenario.

What if the Meng-shan discovered the fortifications and – driven by arrogance, anger, or sheer stubbornness – ordered an immediate frontal assault, regardless?

Before Chae-ha could finish that wandering thought, a commotion near the southern border of the clearing attracted her attention.

A Tai-shan scout had ridden his horse directly into the staging area, completely disregarding protocol. The beast was lathered in white foam, its head hanging as the rider tumbled from the saddle, stumbling towards the command tent where Commander Shin was reviewing supply manifests.

Chae-ha wiped her hand on her robes and marched across the dirt road, arriving a little too late to catch the beginning of his report.

“...iron braces all over,” the scout gibbered. “Hitched to a team of six oxen. Three linked composite bows, each one bigger than I am tall…”

Commander Shin’s face drained of color. “A siege ballista? Here?”

Chae-ha stopped dead in her tracks. Her mind raced at dizzying speeds. A siege ballista?! The Meng-shan were dragging a device meant to smash through castle walls over gravel roads and through the mud? Why? Did they already know about the fortifications? Since when? Was there a spy?

No. Even if there was a spy, the timing was impossible. To have a multi-ton siege engine positioned at the crossroads – right now – meant they had procured and moved the weapon weeks ago. Either way, it rendered her entire stone-and-timber fortification meaningless. A single bolt from such a ballista would punch clean through the wood, explode the solid mortar, and collapse a section of the wall.

The noise of the camp faded away, replaced by a dull thrum that vibrated in Chae-ha’s ears. Her veins pulsed against her temples as the startling reality of the threat began pressing down on her. The wall was not ready. The stone would crumble. The position would fall. And if the Meng-shan already had a siege ballista…they were not just going to wait around.

She sat down abruptly on an overturned supply crate, her body moving on pure instinct as her mind spun out of control.

“Chae-ha!”

Mu-cheon’s voice cut sharply through the ringing noise in her head. The Young Master was suddenly kneeling beside her, his large hands resting on her shoulders, his face filled with concern. Behind him, Commander Shin was pacing back and forth, muttering incoherently about ordering an immediate retreat to the inner pass.

Chae-ha blinked, forcing her focus to narrow.

She looked at the panicking Vanguard Commander. She looked at the exhausted, dust-coated scout. She looked at Mu-cheon’s steady eyes. And finally, she turned her gaze back to the gatehouse wall.

She looked at the gray, un-cured paste seeping between the granite blocks.

Her eyes widened as a strategy formed in her mind, locking itself into place. The young girl sprang up from the crate so fast that Mu-cheon nearly lost his balance.

She spun on her heel and sprinted down the central thoroughfare of the camp, cutting through lines of startled lumberjacks and stone-cutters. Mu-cheon and Shin exchanged a bewildered glance before breaking into a run behind her.

She bounded past the perimeter guards and headed straight for the supply depot, where Patriarch Yeon was overseeing the unloading of his wagons.

“Patriarch Yeon!” Chae-ha called out, her scholar’s robes billowing around her as she skidded to a halt in front of his horse.

“Uh…Inspector? Is something wrong?”

“Cancel the iron runs from Laiwu,” Chae-ha gulped, catching her breath as Shin and Mu-cheon caught up to her. “Re-deploy every single wagon in your agency immediately. Send birds, runners, or riders. We need supplies from every military camp within riding distance.”

Yeon blinked, confused. “Supplies? What supplies?”

“Quicklime, river sand, volcanic ash, and water. As much as your horses can drag without snapping the wagons’ axles,” Chae-ha said, her eyes burning with purpose.

“I need raw mortar materials stacked beside that gatehouse before the dawn.”
