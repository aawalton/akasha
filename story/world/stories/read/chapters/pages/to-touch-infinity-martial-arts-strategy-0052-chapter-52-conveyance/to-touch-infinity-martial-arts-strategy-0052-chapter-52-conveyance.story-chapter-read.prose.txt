The echoes of iron axes biting into pine persisted for nearly three hours before the path was finally clear. When the final trunk was rolled into the dense underbrush, leaving a raw trench of mud and bark in its wake, the detachment of three hundred spearmen reformed their ranks and resumed marching east.

Wi Mo adjusted his seat upon his black stallion, his fingers brushing small chips of wood off his purple robes. The delay was vexing, but the road ahead appeared open as it wound deeper into the mountain’s folds. His men had done good work, and now it was time to go and claim his prize.

That satisfaction lasted less than two li.

As the path curved around a steep, rocky projection, the vanguard halted once more. Wi Mo rode forwards, his irritation rising as he saw a second obstruction. This cluster of fallen timber was far less substantial than the first – only four or five younger trees dropped haphazardly across the gravel. He directed his men to lop off the protruding branches from the trees on one side of the road, carving out a narrow pass that ran along the shoulder and through the weeds.

The solution, however, came with a cost. The spearmen were forced to pass through the gap in a single file, the long column bunching up around the opening before stretching out into a thin ribbon. The Lesser Elder watched the procession with distaste. The sun had already passed its peak; if these petty hindrances continued, his window of opportunity might vanish. For a brief moment, he considered ordering a full about-face to consolidate his forces at the crossroads.

The sound of quick, light footsteps approaching from the rear interrupted his calculations. A messenger, one of the runners Wi Mo had scheduled to maintain a continuous loop of intelligence, skidded to a halt beside his horse.

“Report,” the strategist commanded, snapping his fan closed against his palm. “How goes the engagement at the junction?”

“There…is no engagement, Elder. The Tai-shan vanguard hasn’t advanced. And our scouts have yet to spy the main column...”

Wi Mo snorted. “Our men are already deeply entrenched. If their main force hasn't arrived yet, a frontal assault would be suicide. They’re stuck just staring at us, at least for now.”

Whoever was in charge of the Tai-shan troops would probably be executed once his superiors arrived and found he'd failed to get to the crossroads first, but that wasn’t Wi Mo’s problem. The fact that the enemy's main column still had not been sighted meant his timeline was still safe. The Lesser Elder had hours left to claim his prize. He didn’t need to go watch men stare at each other across a clearing; the mine was still the only objective that mattered.

Despite what appeared to be perfectly sound logic, a faint prickle of unease stirred his thoughts. But when he considered his options, the strategist found himself boxed in. What alternative did he have? Give up and return to camp, leaving the mine unclaimed and a Tai-shan garrison on their flank? The only path that made any sense was to continue pressing forwards. They needed to secure the mine before the larger conflict broke out at the crossroads.

“Keep moving,” Wi Mo ordered his captains. “Double the pace once the column reforms.”

The line of spearmen moved out again, the iron points of their weapons glinting beneath the filtered forest light. For the next two hours, the march made slow progress, periodically interrupted in areas where single trees had been dropped across the trail.

But in these numbers, they were just an inconvenience. Everything was going well enough. At least, until a sharp cry of agony suddenly changed everyone's perspective on the path they were walking.

The front line slowed and nearly stopped as one of their members howled, causing the second line of troops to collide with their backs. The entire column stuttered as the continued momentum of the rear ranks pushed the front soldiers forwards a few clumsy steps. Before Wi Mo could demand an explanation, a second scream echoed from the front line, followed swiftly by a loud curse.

Wi Mo urged his stallion through the cluster of confused soldiers, his fan closed and resting in his palm. The Lesser Elder dismounted near the front of the line, where a spearman sat in the dirt, cradling his right foot in both hands. Fresh blood was already soaking through the thick leather sole of his boot.

The soldier pointed a trembling finger at a spot within the gravel that made up the road.

Wi Mo leaned down, his eyes narrowing as he located the object. It was small, nearly invisible against the gray gravel of the mountain path, but its design was unmistakable. A slender steel spike stood upright in the dirt, its triangular facets dully reflecting the dimming light.

He reached down, carefully picking it up by one of its points. The metal was cool, hard, and perfectly balanced. It was unnaturally thin, and would have been almost impossible to spot if it wasn’t also covered in blood.

A look of genuine confusion crossed his face. This was not the crude work of panicked miners or rough camp followers. It was forged from refined tool steel, oil-darkened to dull reflection, and sharpened with precise edges resembling a leatherworking needle. It was a tool engineered specifically to pierce military footwear.

How was this possible? A garrison stuck in a remote mine could not manufacture hundreds of precision iron components. Was this entire sequence of roadblocks a coordinated trap? If so, to what end? They weren’t being ambushed from the trees; they were simply being delayed, one foot at a time.

While his medics began treating the wounded and his officers debated how to best clear the path, a second runner from the west arrived, his uniform coated in a fine layer of trail dust.

Wi Mo turned to address the man immediately, his voice sharp. “Report. Has the primary Tai-shan column been sighted yet?”

“Not yet, Elder. The crossroads remain quiet.”

Wi Mo gripped his fan tightly, his teeth grinding together as he looked up at the sky. The sun was beginning to dip behind the western ridges, casting long, indigo shadows across the path. The day was entirely spent, and they had accomplished nothing. He looked back down at the trail ahead, where the dust hid an unknown quantity of precision steel. He was furious, but he was not a fool; he would not order his infantry to march over hidden spikes in the dark.

“Turn the column around. We’re marching back to the junction, so we can establish camp before nightfall.”

He turned back to his horse, his mind already structuring the orders for dawn. “Tomorrow at first light, we’ll send a specialized detachment of fifty men down this path. They will carry brooms and axes to sweep the ground and clear whatever timber remains. The main spear column will follow three hours behind them. We will seize the mine by midday.”

As he re-mounted his stallion, Wi Mo looked back towards the crossroads. Since the primary Tai-shan force would almost certainly arrive tomorrow, he would need to remain at the main camp to personally direct the defense. It was a pity he wouldn’t be present to witness the claiming of the mine, but it was an acceptable compromise. His strategy was still sound. The entrenchment at the junction would hold, the mine would ultimately fall, and the glory of the campaign would all belong to him.

With operations running both day and night, a perpetual cloud of dust seemed to hang in the air over the construction camp southeast of Laiwu.

Chae-ha positioned her feet carefully, her silk scholar’s robes rustling softly against her leggings as she slowly mimicked the positions her Phantom Steps would put her in. On her flanks, Sergeant Noh Gu-shim and his three squadmates moved as well, although their steps were halting and unnatural, and their eyes were pinned to the gravel beneath them.

A close inspection of the road would have revealed why. Mixed in with the gray stone were occasional dark pebbles, each standing in for an oil-blackened iron spike. They blended in with the natural shadows cast by the rough surface.

“There’s no point staring at the ground, Sergeant. You need to trust they are where they’re supposed to be. Move with confidence.”

Gu-shim grunted, lifting a burly leg to clear a dense cluster of the small “weapons.”

“Working on it. But every time we rotate, it starts getting hard to remember where they all are. And then you remind us how crippled we just got.”

Before Chae-ha could respond, the sound of heavy footfalls announced an arrival. Mu-cheon walked into the clearing, his expression uncharacteristically subdued. He glanced back towards the camp entrance before catching Chae-ha’s eye.

“Little sister, you have a visitor,” the Young Master said, rubbing the back of his neck. “He arrived at the rear checkpoint an hour ago. I wasn’t sure if it was wise to bring him all the way to the camp, given the security here... but he was pretty desperate. I couldn’t just leave him at the gates.”

Chae-ha wiped a bead of sweat from her temple and turned towards the path. “Who could possibly want to visit me, senior brother?”

Mu-cheon stepped aside, allowing the figure behind him to take center stage.

Patriarch Yeon stood before them, but the proud, iron-willed master of the 72 Springs escort agency was no more. The meticulously polished iron bracers were gone, his broad shoulders were hunched, and his posture was hollowed out by defeat. His face bore the deep lines of sleepless nights; his somber gaze remained fixed on the dirt, unable to meet her eyes.

“Inspector Han,” the man almost whispered.

“Patriarch…Yeon, was it? What brings you here? Did something happen?”

The veteran warrior let out a long, weary breath, his shoulders sinking further. “The Black Ink Consortium...happened. A prominent antiquities dealer in Jinan hired us to transport a sealed box. The cargo was verified by a registered notary before we even picked it up, and they were a reputable merchant, so…it seemed safe.”

Yeon wrung his hands in despair. “We completed the mission successfully. No one ever touched the crate. But when the client opened it at the destination...the box was completely empty. There was never any jade statue inside.”

He let out a bitter, hollow chuckle. “The Consortium paid out five hundred taels for the ‘lost’ artifact immediately, and now...they have come to collect the debt from me. I have two months left before I’ll default on the debt payments. And then…they are going to repossess my school, my agency, my family’s land, and my estate. Everything I have built over the last twenty years will be stripped away.”

He finally raised his head, his deep-set eyes emanating a profound, quiet agony. “I have nothing left now but my honor, Inspector. Before they take that from me as well...I wanted to come and find you. To apologize. I’m sorry I dismissed your warning. You were just trying to help, and you deserved better. And in the end…you were right.”

Seeing the formidable Senior Disciple reduced to this level of bitter melancholy sent a sharp pang of emotion through Chae-ha’s chest. This kind of economic execution was exactly the sort of thing that had driven her own father to his grave.

Externally, her mask stayed fixed rigidly in place.

“The contract you signed is fully enforceable under Imperial law. I’m sorry, Patriarch, but there is no judge in Shandong who would overturn it. The Black Ink Consortium has the legal right to seize everything you own.”

Patriarch Yeon nodded grimly, turning to leave. “I know. I didn’t come here expecting a miracle. I just wanted to settle my account with you, before…everything ends.”

“What if I could offer you a miracle?” Chae-ha replied softly, stopping him in his tracks.

“What? But you just said…”

“They have the legal right to seize everything that you own. We cannot change that contract. But we can redefine what you own, and ensure the Consortium is the one walking away with an empty box this time.”

Yeon turned back to face her, a look of utter bewilderment on his face. “What are you talking about?”

“Follow me,” Chae-ha said as she led him back into one of the larger tents. She sat down at a desk, picked up a brush, and began to write.

“As a vassal, your family owes a monthly tribute to the Tai-shan sect, correct?”

“Of course! In two decades, we’ve never missed a payment.”

“After this discussion concludes, please send a bird. You can use one of ours, is necessary. Your estate needs to stop paying us. Immediately! Save the silver you would normally give to the sect. Send it to the Black Ink Consortium instead.”

Patriarch Yeon stared at her as if she’d lost her mind. “Are you insane, girl? I’ve spent twenty years as a loyal vassal! Why would I withhold my tribute now, just to line the pockets of the vultures who ruined me? I am going to lose the estate either way – I would rather my wealth stay with the Tai-shan!”

“There’s an important legal avenue that this strategy unlocks. By intentionally withholding your tribute, you place your family into default with the Mt. Tai sect. The Tai-shan will then possess the legal right to repossess your assets in order to satisfy your debt.”

The realization hit Mu-cheon first. His eyes widened, a grin slowly spreading across his face.

Chae-ha continued, her gaze locked onto the stunned older man. “When that happens, you will not yet have missed a payment to the Consortium. The sect will take possession of your school, your wagons, your horses, and your family estate. That will allow us to keep them whole and operational under our banner. It will stop the liquidation.”

Mu-cheon gave the Patriarch a hearty slap on the back. “The consortium can’t take things someone else already repossessed!”

Yeon stood there for a moment, completely speechless. After several failed attempts, he finally managed to force some words out. “But...what becomes of me? Of my family?”

“The sect will require an experienced master to manage the escort agency it’s just acquired. So, we will hire you as Chief Administrator. You will receive a regular salary, lodging on the estate, and your family will remain in their home as employees of the Tai-shan. You will spend the rest of your life in debt to the Consortium, but all they’ll be able to take is a garnishment of your salary.”

With a flourish of her brush, the young girl finished the document she was drafting.

“And one more thing. We are officially requisitioning your agency. There’s a mountain of construction supplies that still need to get from Laiwu to here, fast. I need the 72 Springs’ wagons conducting transport, starting tonight. And I need your couriers around to improve communications with the main camp. Welcome to the Young Master’s entourage, Patriarch. We’re looking forwards to working with you.”

Chae-ha motioned towards the bottom of the document, where there was a space for both Patriarch Yeon and Mu-cheon to put their stamps.

The silence in the tent was palpable. Yeon stared at the slight girl in her oversized scholar’s robes, a complex storm of relief, shame, awe, and bitterness washing over his weathered features. He was still personally ruined, but his legacy was being saved.

Hesitantly, he stepped towards the desk, pulling out his personal seal. He looked down at it with an expression that could only be described as regret, before dipping it in a tin of red cinnabar paste that sat on the desk.

“I’m not foolish enough to reject your counsel twice, miss,” he said, firmly pressing the seal onto the bottom of the paper.

“Everything I own may belong to the Consortium now, but my riders and my wagons are yours.”
