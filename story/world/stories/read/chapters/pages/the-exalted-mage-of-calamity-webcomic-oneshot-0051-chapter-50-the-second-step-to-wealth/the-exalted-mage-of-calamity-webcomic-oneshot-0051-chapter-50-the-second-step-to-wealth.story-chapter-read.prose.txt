“What percentage of return are you considering?” a deep, weathered voice asked from the crystal.

Veronica maintained a straight posture, her hands folded neatly in her lap.

“I do not have exact figures to present,” she replied calmly. “I am not a merchant myself. I would prefer the final percentages be structured by the man I intend to sponsor. However… I can say with confidence that the return would fall somewhere between three and twenty percent.”

A faint crackle sounded from the communication crystal.

“That is… a considerable margin.”

The voice did not belong to Hugh. He sat across from her in his office, silent as he allowed the exchange to proceed. Between them rested a pedestal of polished stone. Above it hovered a clear crystal, softly illuminated, its surface faintly warped by distance.

The voice belonged to Rarran, Head of the Ronswick Mage Tower.

Veronica had expected this.

Hugh might oversee studies within the branch, but an arrangement of this scale exceeded his authority. It was only natural he would defer upward. What she had not anticipated was that he would summon the tower head himself to join the discussion.

Rarran, it seemed, was away from Ronswick, engaged in research alongside a small circle of mages. Even so, he had responded quickly.

The connection, however, was imperfect. The crystal’s glow flickered at intervals, and his voice carried a faint echo. Long-distance communication was costly. Even Ronswick lacked flawless relay artifacts—or the wealth required to acquire them.

That suited Veronica just fine.

She could not approach Galland empty-handed. All she needed was agreement in principle. Something tangible; even a provisional draft would suffice.

“Do you have a proposed contract duration?” Rarran asked. “How long would this arrangement remain in effect?”

“Two years,” Veronica answered without hesitation. “That should be sufficient time to demonstrate profit and progress. With the notes I am providing, your branch mages shouldn’t need more than a month to study and practice the theory. Production efficiency should improve in your branch shortly thereafter.”

Silence followed.

The crystal dimmed slightly as Rarran’s voice lowered, speaking privately with Hugh. Veronica waited. This pattern had repeated itself for nearly half an hour: she would propose a framework, and the two would confer before returning with further questions.

Each exchange, however, strengthened her position.

The more they asked, the more invested they became.

Sage’s private guidance threaded through her thoughts, offering specific phrasing and restraint where necessary. Veronica herself knew little of merchant contracts. Percentages, distribution rights, advisory clauses—none of it had been part of her life before. She was simply repeating all the terms Sage was echoing in her head.

After another stretch of muted discussion, the crystal brightened once more.

“In principle,” Rarran said slowly, “the Ronswick branch is willing to draft provisional terms. Final percentages and distribution structures will require revision once this merchant of yours is present. However, we see no reason to deny exploration of the arrangement.”

Hugh visibly relaxed.

Veronica inclined her head. “That is acceptable.”

A brief murmur passed between the two men before the crystal dimmed entirely. The light faded, leaving only the faint hum of residual mana in the pedestal.

The connection had ended.

Hugh exhaled and rose from his chair. “I will prepare a preliminary draft,” he said. “It will outline the general expectations on both sides—distribution priority, early-access windows, return structures… all of that messy business. Subject to revision, of course.”

“Of course,” Veronica replied.

Several minutes later, he returned with a small stack of pages. The ink was still drying.

He placed them in her hands.

“This is a provisional merchant agreement between the Ronswick Mage Tower branch and your future sponsor,” Hugh said. “It holds no binding authority until the final signatories are present—but it should show our intent.”

Veronica skimmed the first page.

The Mage Tower seal. Hugh’s signature. Rarran’s line left blank.

It was perfect—it was all the leverage she needed to convince Galland.

“Thank you,” she said quietly.

Hugh studied her for a moment before extending his hand. Veronica rose, and they shook.

“Honestly,” he admitted, “I did not expect such a proposal when you walked through that door this morning. And I certainly did not expect you to place such confidence in a man you have never even met.”

Veronica met his gaze evenly.

“I have a good eye for people,” she said. “I have high hopes for him. There is no doubt in my mind that he'll deliver.”

Hugh released a quiet, thoughtful sigh. “Well… perhaps that is what makes geniuses so difficult to understand,” he said. “You see what the rest of us cannot. Always enigmas, you lot.”

Veronica only smiled. His words held truth. She certainly did see what he could not. Perhaps she was the only one who could see that far, barring the Goddess of stories.

“Then I shall take my leave, Mr. Hugh.”

“Aye. May fortune favor the endeavor,” he replied.

She tucked the agreement carefully beneath her arm and made her way toward the stairs.

High Crates awaited.

“What? You have a trade agreement for Galland?”

The voice came from behind the front counter of High Crates.

The man stationed there was broad-shouldered and imposing, his arms marked with scars that spoke more of battlefields than bookkeeping. He looked better suited to slaying wyverns or splitting skulls than managing ledgers.

“Yes,” Veronica replied evenly. “I have a proposal I would like him to review and advise on.”

The man stared at her, brows furrowed and incredibly confused.

The High Crates was a moderately sized building, one of the larger merchant houses operating in Ronswick. Its interior smelled faintly of ink, parchment, and wood polish. Shelves lined the walls behind the desk, stacked with bound ledgers and sealed parcels awaiting dispatch. Several merchants moved about the hall, speaking in low voices or sorting through manifests.

“Galland?” he repeated slowly. “Our junior? How did you learn of his name?”

“Yes, that Galland,” she answered. “And I’ve heard of how well he does. Some simple rumors that he is quite competent. That is why I wish to meet with him.”

The man leaned back slightly, lifting his arms from the table and folding them over his chest. His gaze dropped to the small stack of papers tucked beneath Veronica’s arm.

He could see the faint seal near the top.

It was difficult to see whose seal it was, but that didn’t matter. The seal told him it was something important and official.

“And what sort of agreement would this be?” he asked.

Veronica kept her expression neutral. “Distribution. It involves the Ronswick Mage Tower.”

The man’s brows drew together.

A short laugh escaped him.

“The Mage Tower?” he said. “And you want Galland for something that important? That’s absurd.”

Veronica tilted her head slightly.

“Are you saying he lacks the competence to review a contract?”

“That’s not what I said.”

“But it is what you implied.”

He clicked his tongue, irritation flickering across his face. “Listen. He’s been with us a few years. Hard worker. Knows how to stretch some vix when he must. But he does not draft agreements with institutions.”

He waved his hand across the lobby, pointing to the clerks and runners busy with documents and packages.

“Miss, you see these people? They’re junior staff. They handle routine documents and work with everyday clients: traveling merchants, townsfolk, small consignments. Nothing that demands a sharp negotiating mind. Galland is the same. He manages modest exchanges. If you’re seeking a contract with the Mage Tower, you’ll want one of our senior heads.”

“I would prefer to speak with Galland.”

The man frowned and studied her more closely.

“You do realize he isn’t authorized to sign major contracts on behalf of our house,” he said. “If you’re carrying something official, he cannot approve it or represent High Crates. He’d likely be lost simply trying to decipher it.”

“I still wish to speak with him,” Veronica replied calmly. “I am not asking High Crates to notarize or endorse the agreement. My only wish is to consult him and have him review the terms.”

The clerk scratched his jaw.

“Fine. I’ll fetch him. But understand this: if your deal collapses, that will not be the fault of High Crates. I’ve given you fair warning.”

He disappeared through a side door leading deeper into the merchant offices.

Veronica remained where she stood.

Around her, the hall continued its quiet rhythm: crates shifting across wooden floors, documents passing from hand to hand, merchants conferring in low voices. She did not understand the logistics of it all, but it was clear the work was relentless and precise.

In the future, High Crates would not be a name remembered. It would remain an ordinary trading house, never rising to prominence.

Their biggest mistake would be not cultivating Galland—to use him as a resource.

Still… he had worked here for years. How could someone like him remain in such conditions for so long? The clerk had resisted even granting a meeting. She understood why Galland would eventually leave.

But that would not be for a few more years.

What had kept him here?

Footsteps sounded in the corridor.

The burly clerk returned first.

Behind him walked a man in his late twenties.

He was… underwhelming.

Tall, but narrow in build. He walked slowly, shoulders sloped slightly forward as if accustomed to leaning over desks. His clothes were tidy but plain: a brown vest, a simple shirt, and a modest belt pouch. His hair was dark and tied loosely at the back, though a few strands had escaped near his temples, and dark circles lay beneath his eyes.

There was nothing imposing about him. Nothing charismatic. He looked like the exact opposite of a confident titan of trade.

“You asked for me?” he said, his voice low and almost hesitant.

“This… this is an agreement with the Mage Tower?” Galland asked.

Both he and Veronica were now situated in a small meeting room. Leather chairs, a few shelves, a small round table before them.

She handed over the documents, letting him see their contents for himself first.

“This is remarkable,” he said at last. “They agreed to draft a trade agreement with you, Miss Veronica? To name you their first distributor… this is astonishing.”

His eyes shone with genuine surprise. She could hardly blame him. It was rare for any merchant house to secure direct dealings with a mage tower. Even rarer still under terms of exclusivity.

He continued reading.

“The structure is sound,” he murmured. “However… the return percentage here may be better expressed as a declining scale rather than a fixed range. If whatever advantage you provide significantly increases their production, their profits will grow disproportionately. A static return would leave you under-compensated in later years.”

He turned the page and skimmed further.

“From the language here, it appears your theoretical notes are their chief incentive and your primary leverage. If they value them this highly, I would suggest establishing a baseline return of eight percent. If agreed revenue targets are met within the first year, the return could decrease by two percent. Another two percent reduction could follow at the end of the contract term, provided renewal rights remain in place. You could then renew the contract at four percent, or set it at six once more if they decide to renew later.”

He tapped the page lightly.

“This allows the tower to retain strong early profit while ensuring you benefit more as production scales. It balances risk at the outset while protecting long-term value. You may even start off as high as ten percent to make the offer more attractive if you’re in need of their services.”

Only then did he glance up and seemed to realize how far he had gone.

“Ah… forgive me,” he said quickly. “That is only my perspective. The decision is entirely yours. I do not possess the authority to advise you otherwise.”

Veronica smiled. “No need. Please feel free to give me your full and honest opinion on the matter, Mr. Galland.”

She was back to talking formally, ingrained from her studies at Valeborn.

First impressions mattered. The more she could make herself look like a competent sponsor—the better.

He nodded meekly, and his gaze returned to the documents.

“The agreement itself is solid and sound. The only thing I’d like to add are the concrete numbers and some safer breach of contract terms. However, I’m more interested in the purpose of the contract overall.”

“Purpose?” Veronica repeated.

His gaze turned back to her. “Who exactly is this sponsor you’re talking about? It seems far too risky to put all this contingent on a single man without a proven track record. This is someone you noticed, and are investing in? It might be safer to draft up a trade deal with a merchant house, like our High Crates, instead of an individual.”

Veronica chuckled.

“Actually. The person I wanted to sponsor is here in High Crates. That’s why I wanted to come here today and meet with you.”

He raised a brow. “At High Crates? Then why speak with me and not—”

The thought caught up with him.

His eyes widened slightly.

“Miss Veronica,” he said slowly, “surely you are not… referring to me?”

Veronica held his gaze.

“I am,” she said. “I wish to formally sponsor you, Mr. Galland. Leave High Crates and establish your own merchant house. Use this contract with the Mage Tower to secure your footing.”

Silence settled between them.

Galland blinked, as though the words had failed to reach him.

“Why… why me?” he asked at last. “There are senior merchants here far better suited to such an arrangement.”

“I believe,” she continued calmly, “that you possess the judgment, restraint, and foresight to build something greater than what you have been permitted to manage here. I do not believe anyone else would fulfill this role as you could.”

He stared at her, speechless. Then he gathered himself.

“That is generous praise,” he said. “And I appreciate it. But how did you come to know of me? I am only a junior merchant at High Crates. The largest agreements I have handled involved grain contracts between farming collectives. Nothing grand enough to warrant facilitating an agreement of this scale.”

Veronica pressed her lips together.

That was the greatest obstacle in persuading him. She could hardly say she had seen the future—that she knew the man he would one day become, one of the pillars upon which humanity would stand.

She could say it.

There was nothing stopping her.

But belief was another matter entirely.

She knew the man he would become.

She did not know the man sitting before her.

Veronica’s intonation changed as she began to explain and make up a story on the spot. Years of being indoctrinated into nobility and high society still left a mark on how she spoke with others.

“Let us say,” she said at last, “that I have access to an intelligence network that gathers information on capable individuals. I am attempting to build something significant in the years ahead. For that, I require dependable hands. Hands, such as yourself, Mr. Galland.”

She folded one leg over the other, meeting his eyes.

“I was given your name. Your work ethic. Your integrity. Your restraint and bright mind. Those are the qualities I seek. That is why I chose you, and no one else. You are suited for this role.”

She allowed a faint smile to form.

“This agreement will not disadvantage you, Mr. Galland. As outlined, you would retain operational control. You would have resources sufficient to expand quickly. In time, you might oversee an enterprise surpassing even High Crates.”

She fell silent and waited.

The pause stretched longer than she expected.

When he finally spoke, his voice was quiet; his answer wasn’t one she expected.

“I’m sorry, Miss Veronica,” he said. “But I cannot do that.”
