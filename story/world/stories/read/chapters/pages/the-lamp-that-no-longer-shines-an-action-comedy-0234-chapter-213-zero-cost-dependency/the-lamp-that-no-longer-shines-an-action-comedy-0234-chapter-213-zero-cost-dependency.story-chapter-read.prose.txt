[Time]: Autumn Semester, Day 11 — 9:00 AM[Location]: West Campus · Yggdrasil Academy · Third Alchemy Tower · Tiered Lecture Hall

The doors swung inward, and forty-five sophomores stopped talking at the same time.

Professor Sharon Curie had come to work in a hazmat suit.

Standard containment gear, cut to her size: pearl-white alchemical polymer following her small frame without a wrinkle in it, containment runes welded into every seam, a spherical helmet locking to the collar on a heavy magnetic ring.

Hathaway sat in the second row with her quill hovering over a blank line and ran the day's syllabus against the equipment.

We are wiring elementary sensory arrays into liquid mercury. Her developer brain checked the parameters twice and got the same answer both times. It outgasses at room temperature, and every witch in this hall learned the filter hex before she learned to duel. Why is she dressed for a containment breach?

Then she found the brass valve.

It was machined into the lower curve of the faceplate, right about mouth level, beautifully finished, entirely too small for an oxygen line. Hathaway was still cataloguing possible functions when Sharon reached into the thigh pocket of the suit and produced a briarwood pipe.

Sharon packed it with the unhurried competence of a woman who had done this several thousand times, snapped her fingers over the bowl, and slotted the stem into the brass port. The seal took it with a small, satisfying click.

She inhaled.

The helmet filled.

Hathaway's quill came off the parchment.

Grey smoke rolled up the inside of the visor, hit the curve of the glass, found nowhere to go, and stayed. Inside four seconds the Grandmaster of Doll-Making had been reduced to a silhouette in a snow globe of her own making.

She is hotboxing herself. Hathaway's entire threat-assessment stack rebooted and came back with nothing useful. She engineered a delivery system for it.

Every safety manual in the discipline carried the same directive about open flame near a live crucible, printed in red on page one, filed under the general heading of things that void your life insurance. But Grandmasters paid taxes in a different currency of physics, and Sharon Curie had clearly sat down with the problem and worked it honestly. Smoking in a lecture hall was a fire hazard, which she could survive, and it was rude, which apparently she could not.

The seal held. Not one molecule of tobacco reached the tiered seats. Forty-five students sat in crisp, ozone-scrubbed air and watched their professor disappear into weather.

"Today," Sharon said, and her feather-light voice came through the collar speaker without a tremor in it, "we integrate auditory receptors."

She crossed to the demonstration crucible, leaned over a vat of liquid silver, and began executing sub-millimetre transmutations through a visor she could no longer see out of. The smoke banked and thinned. For a moment Hathaway got a clear look at the woman inside.

Sharon's golden eyes were squeezed to slits, the whites scalded pink, tears running steadily down both cheeks and pooling at her jaw with nowhere to be wiped.

Hathaway sat back, and her quill stopped.

There it is. Every lecture since the term opened, that damp golden shimmer standing in her eyes like water held level in a glass, the look that made an entire hall want to apologize to her for taking up her afternoon. She has been macing her own corneas twice a day and going straight back to work.

She bit down on the inside of her cheek until it hurt, copied the receptor runes, and gave up on being objective about this woman.

[Time]: Autumn Semester, Day 11 — 2:00 PM[Location]: West Campus · Yggdrasil Academy · Wondrous Item Hall

Emilia Lucas stood behind the podium in the same severe navy, squared her schematics against the edge, and did something no one in the hall had budgeted for.

"Metal will hold a paradox," she said. "It will not grow one. At this level we compel biology to host it."

The lumen-globes dimmed. A crystalline sapling bloomed into the air above the podium, leaves rendering like shaved diamond, every vein of it annotated.

[Wondrous Item Blueprint: Crystal Tea Tree]

The hall was still copying when Emilia flicked two fingers and dropped a second schematic straight through the first.

[Wondrous Item Blueprint: Light-Bug Tea Tree]

Hathaway wrote until the side of her hand went numb.

Oleander Radiance Tea Mountain. Last week's projection came back without being asked. If these are the trees planted on its slopes, then that mountain is not an artifact with contents. Every microscopic component inside it is a wondrous item in its own right.

Neither blueprint had ever been published, and she was dictating both of them, node by node, into forty-five sets of student notes.

In academia, generosity in the lecture is directly proportional to lethality in the exam. Something under her ribs went warm and stayed there. She is paying out the clear rewards before the raid has opened. Which means the boss fight is mathematically impossible.

Ten minutes before the bell, Emilia began stacking her notes without looking up.

"Your final for this semester is a wondrous item designed to a specific client profile," she said. "Check your terminals."

Forty-five terminals chimed on the same beat.

"The brief carries aesthetic preference, material taboo, categorized usage scenario, and as much of the client's collection as public record can account for. Nobody outside that vault knows the whole of it, so assume anything obvious is already in there."

Hathaway opened the file and went straight past the preferences to the inventory.

She scrolled. The list populated. She scrolled again, harder, and it kept rendering, category folding into category. On the third pull the bar on the right had not meaningfully moved, and she gave up and let her eyes fall wherever they landed.

[Item 1,206 — Oleander Radiance Tea Mountain. Artifact grade: Legendary.]

Hathaway stopped breathing.

She knew who the client was. Judging by the quality of the silence that had dropped over four tiers of seating, so did everybody else, and not one of them was going to say it out loud.

Across the aisle Yenna raised a hand, ears rotating forward, voice perfectly level.

"Professor Lucas. Are we submitting a theoretical blueprint, or a fabricated prototype?"

"A blueprint."

Emilia snapped her case shut.

The pause ran a fraction longer than a pause needs to.

"The winning design will be physically manufactured."

Hathaway's spine came off the backrest.

The whole shape of the course changed axis in real time. Some second-year in this room was going to draft a page of geometry, and the greatest artificer alive was going to forge it by hand and put it into the hands of the Fifth Seat of the High Council.

The grade stopped being the prize about four seconds ago.

[Time]: Autumn Semester, Day 11 — 4:15 PM[Location]: West Campus · Yggdrasil Academy · Unclaimed Classroom 4B

They took an empty room at the end of the corridor, dragged five desks into a rough circle, and sat around the projected brief the way you sit around something that has begun to tick.

Irene Habsburg was, by every metric the server tracked, a completionist whale. If a thing had supreme stats or historical weight she acquired it, catalogued it, and filed it. But her actual preferences, the ones a designer had to build against, read like draconic hoarding instinct and a load-bearing compulsive disorder holding hands.

[CLIENT BRIEF: REDACTED]

I. Structural alignment

II. Material constraint

III. Usage classification

IV. Existing collection

Hathaway rubbed the bridge of her nose and looked up.

"Spectra. This ghost train on the inventory. Is that yours?"

Spectra leaned back into the chalkboard, and the shadows around her boots declined to move with her.

"The [Midnight Express]," she recited. "Autonomous spectral locomotive. Compressed underworld geyser as primary drive. Integrated spatial-rending engine, twelve folded dimensions on a single charge."

Hathaway ran the numbers and briefly stopped being a person. Flagships did not do that. That was an unreleased prototype sitting three commercial generations ahead of anything a witch with money could buy, which meant the Ghost Castle had been holding that technology off the market for years and had then wrapped it in paper.

"And she accepted it."

"She did." Spectra's voice did not change. "She observed that she appreciates the scenery during trans-dimensional transit."

Surtrina snorted, shoved her own terminal away, and put both boots up on the desk.

"Page four. The crown with the seventeen-facet stone in it. My relatives sent that one."

Hathaway queried the index. A seventeen-facet Radiant Gem had closed at auction the previous month at three point two one million Solars, and that was the loose stone.

"Did she say anything?"

"What do you think she said." Surtrina's tail cracked once against the floorboards, hard enough to put a fresh notch in a board that already had several. "Diplomatic pleasantries. We thought we'd bought ourselves a decade of goodwill. A cousin got a look inside a secondary vault the next year." She bared her fangs at the ceiling. "She already had a crown built around a thousand-facet stone."

The room went quiet in a specific way.

Facets on a Radiant Gem were not cut into it, and the price ran exponential on the count. A thousand had never once appeared in a sale record anywhere in the Inner Sea.

The Ludwigs are on this list somewhere too. Hathaway scrolled past three screens of legendary weapons and folded dimensions without recognizing a single entry. I can't even identify our own, because our house names things like a hedge maze with a grudge.

And somewhere in the third screen her developer brain finished a calculation she had not authorized and did not want.

Every line on this index is somebody's best. Fourteen thousand nine hundred and two entries, each one the ceiling of what a family, a castle, a dynasty could reach, each one boxed and delivered and logged and never mentioned again. A graveyard with excellent bookkeeping. And the final exam is to add a headstone.

Across the circle, Yenna uncrossed her legs and folded her hands neatly in her lap.

"She likes sunflowers," the fox witch said.

There was no weight on the words at all, the same level voice she had used two nights ago to recommend a restaurant.

"She prefers valleys with a running stream. Overgrown with wildflowers."

Hathaway blinked, and ran the query against the index.

A folded meadow, six hectares of it packed into a brass case the size of a hatbox, thirty thousand blooms opening on the same second at dawn and closing on the same second at dusk. A cultivar bred for convergence and planted in a ring, every head tracking an anchored mana source instead of a sun. A single stem sealed in Legendary-grade crystal, the seed head lattice cut to two thousand cells, each cell holding one preserved second of a summer that had happened somewhere else.

Nine, out of fourteen thousand nine hundred and two. At that sample size the motif was weather.

She looked back at Yenna, and chose her next sentence with some care.

"The brief allows botany. It doesn't get anywhere near a species, and it has nothing to say about valleys. Where did you get it, and is it good?"

Yenna's three golden tails traced a slow, deliberate curve behind her chair and settled into something very close to a question mark.

"I heard it."

Understood. Hathaway logged it without pushing. A crack running through all that gold, and it had come from the quietest person in the room, for free.

Then Victoria set a fingertip against the edge of her terminal and tapped once, and the sound went through the empty classroom like a dropped instrument.

"She hates cats."

Four heads came round at once.

"That's not possible." Surtrina's boots came down off the desk. "Is there a witch alive who hates cats?"

Even Spectra's composure developed a visible seam. Her brow drew down, and something unsettled moved through that airless voice.

"She is the sole inventor of the Cat-Washing Machine. She authored the most scientifically rigorous manual on Lantern Cat husbandry in the recorded history of the discipline."

Hathaway caught herself nodding. The Cat-Washing Machine was a fixture of civilized life. The manual was scripture. Half the households on the continent owned both.

The cats loathe the machine, she thought, marshalling the case for the defence, but cats loathe a bath. You do not build the machine and then sit down and write the manual for a species you cannot stand. That is not admissible.

Something cold began assembling itself low in her stomach, because the intel was too specific and it cut too cleanly against the public record.

"Victoria." She kept her voice level. "Who told you that."

Victoria's pale blue eyes came up. Her mouth pressed thin for a fraction of a second before she answered.

"Evangeline."

The pressure in the room changed.

Yenna's ears folded flat. Surtrina's tail-flame guttered down to nothing.

Hathaway pulled the inventory back up and started scrolling, faster now. Witches loved cats. The vault was thick with them: Lantern Cat artifacts, feline motifs, summoning bells, three separate entries she could not parse that had the word cat buried in the middle.

She looked around the circle.

"Can anyone get a read on this? The houses that sent the cat gifts. What she actually said when she opened them. Anything."

Spectra considered it, and the shadows pooled deeper around her desk.

"I can try."

She met the expectant faces and shook her head, plainly unconvinced by her own offer.

"The Fifth Seat is not a woman whose feeling reaches her face."

She was quiet a moment longer, and then added it anyway.

"But I do not believe the Tarnished Crow lied."

Yenna nodded slowly. "I have heard the same thing said. The Tarnished Crow is a fraudster who never tells a lie."

Victoria's gaze had gone somewhere past the chalkboard.

"That she did not lie," she said, "does not mean the intelligence is clean."

Hathaway sat back and let the problem run.

If it's true, what does it buy me.

The standard read was obvious. Client hates cats, so keep cats out of the design, dodge the mine, move on. But dodging a mine only kept you from losing, and nobody had ever won a commission by successfully not doing something.

So: why would Irene hate cats. Or, standing further back and looking at the shape of the thing rather than its contents, what exact chain of events had left Evangeline confident enough to state it as a fact.

There was one efficient way to find out. Go to the source and ask for context.

Hathaway's threat arrays came up so hard she lost the thread of the room.

Her data is already inside the perimeter. She looked at the circle: five witches, five desks pushed together, an entire strategy session organized around one sentence a woman had handed them for nothing. We are running her payload. Right now. Voluntarily.

Her developer brain read the commit history of the intrusion and found it chillingly clean. Every clarification they asked for would make Evangeline more useful. Every answer would move her a little closer to the centre of the work. Useful became necessary, necessary became permanent, and by the time anyone thought to revoke her access she would be load-bearing.

The Tarnished Crow had shipped them the exploit for free.

Nobody had asked what she wanted for it.
