I ACCIDENTALLY BECAME A GUILD THERAPIST

Chapter 25 – Thorne and the Bird That Refuses to Help

ARC III: THE WORLD REMEMBERS

SYNOPSIS: Thorne Pinewhistle, Feral Archer Lv.94, former guild member, militant naturalist has been logging botanical utterances for three weeks and he requires an explanation. His crow has opinions. This is Thorne’s primary problem.

The note had been on the guild board for eleven days.

It was written on good paper in aggressive capitals, which meant whoever wrote it was angry but also prepared:

TO WHOEVER IS RESPONSIBLE FOR THE TALKING PLANTS: I have been logging botanical utterances in the Verdant Expanse for three weeks. Seventeen confirmed incidents. The flora are referencing events they should have no knowledge of. Two tulips described a wedding between a slime and an elf in enough detail to suggest either direct observation or genuine emotional memory. A fern recited something I said in private in 2021 back at me. I have been telling this guild that Aethelgard’s flora has latent sentience capacity for two years. I was called a “naturalist eccentric.” I require an explanation. I know this guild is involved. — T. Pinewhistle (Feral Archer Lv.94, former member, currently extremely inconvenienced)

Nobody had taken it down.

Specifically, three people had read it, looked around, and walked away faster.

On the twelfth day, Thorne Pinewhistle walked through the guild entrance.

He was carrying a bow, a field notebook with an alarming number of color-coded tabs, and a crow on his shoulder. The crow was jet-black, had the bearing of something that had been around long enough to form opinions, and was currently staring at Livia with the focused attention of an entity doing an assessment.

Thorne pointed at her. “You.”

The crow tilted its head.

Like it was confirming something.

“I’m Livia,” she said.

“I know,” Thorne said. “The plants told me.”

Thorne refused to have this conversation indoors, which he stated not as a preference but as a principle, so they went to the guild garden. He did not explain the principle. He sat on the bench and opened his field notebook. The crow relocated from his shoulder to the back of the bench, where it could observe both of them simultaneously.

The tabs were color-coded. Green, blue, red, yellow.

“Green: date, location, species, utterance,” Thorne said. “Blue: cross-reference by theme. Red: incidents involving impossible knowledge. Yellow—” he paused, —“is my personal emotional state during each incident.”

“You made a tab for your emotional state,” Livia said.

“Rigor demands a complete record.”

“Of course.”

The crow made a sound.

“Not now,” Thorne said, to the crow.

The crow disagrees with something.

I am going to find out what.

“Walk me through one,” Livia said.

Thorne flipped to Red Tab, Incident Nine. “March nineteenth. Eastern meadow. A cluster of forget-me-nots. I was reviewing my weekly data. Unprompted, they said:” he read directly, voice deliberate, “"You are angry at yourself for leaving. You were right to leave and you are angry at yourself anyway. These are not contradictory."”

Silence.

“What did you do?”

“I told them to mind their own business.”

“And?”

“They jiggled. I believe they found this amusing.”

The forget-me-nots were amused.

The forget-me-nots are correct and they know it.

The crow said something else. Longer this time, with the specific cadence of a crow who has a point and is making it.

“I said not now,” Thorne said.

The crow looked at Livia instead.

“Does your crow,” Livia said carefully, “agree with the forget-me-nots?”

Thorne closed his field notebook. Opened it. Closed it again.

“My crow,” he said, with the compressed dignity of someone losing an argument they have been losing for years, “agrees with everyone except me. This is a known issue. We do not discuss it.”

The crow looked directly at me.

The crow is telling me something.

The crow is saying: yes, I agree with the forget-me-nots, and also with whoever else has tried to say this to him, and I have been trying to tell him this myself for months, and you should know this context.

I trust the crow.

“The plants started responding after the wedding,” Livia said. “Phina and Glimmer’s. There was a significant empathic event. A lot of resonance released into the environment. It dissipated unevenly. Some of it seems to have—” she found the right word, —“settled. Into the flora. Particularly in the Verdant Expanse, within a certain radius of my position.”

Thorne stared at her.

“A slime wedding,” he said, “gave the plants emotional memory.”

“The resonance from a slime wedding that I officiated, yes.”

“Because you officiated it.”

“I might be the anchor point for the effect radius.”

“Two hundred meters.”

“Approximately.”

Thorne set down his field notebook. Picked it up. Set it down again. He was doing a thing with his jaw that suggested he was doing math he found personally offensive.

“I left this guild,” he said, “because I had principled objections to the raid prioritization framework. I spent eight months in the Verdant Expanse conducting legitimate scientific field research. And now the plants are emotionally sentient. Because of a slime wedding. That you officiated.”

“Yes.”

“I have been saying for two years,” Thorne said, “that the flora has latent sentience capacity. I was told this was naturalist eccentricity. I was dismissed by four different guild members, two of whom are in this garden right now.”

“Glimmer is not technically a guild member—” Livia started.

“The slime was at the meeting,” Thorne said.

Glimmer, from its eastern territory, pulsed once. A colour that read distinctly as technically correct. The crow made a sound that was unambiguously a laugh.

“Did your crow just laugh,” Livia said.

“My crow,” Thorne said, with great precision, “laughs at my expense frequently. It is a character flaw. I have addressed it. It has not improved.”

The crow laughed again.

[System Note: Entity: Rigor the Crow (Thorne’s crow, no registered stats, unofficial guild visitor). The system notes: the crow has been with Thorne for four years. The system notes: the crow has never been wrong. The system notes: Thorne knows the crow has never been wrong. The system notes: this is the correct framing for the situation. The system notes: the system is on the crow’s side.]

They sat for a while. Thorne processed through silence in the manner of someone who needed to arrive at things without being shepherded, and Livia had identified this in the first ten minutes and was accommodating it. The crow preened one wing with focused attention. Glimmer drifted along its territorial circuit and regarded Thorne with mild curiosity.

Eventually:

“The forget-me-nots weren’t wrong,” Thorne said. To the garden, not to her.

Livia said nothing.

“I was right to leave. The raid prioritization was misaligned with my values. That part is not up for reassessment.” He looked at the field notebook. “But I left without—” he stopped. Tried again. “I filed my objection internally. And then I went.”

“Without saying it to anyone,” Livia said.

“Without saying it to anyone,” he confirmed.

“And?”

He was quiet for exactly the length of time it took the crow to finish preening and look at him.

“And I’ve been angry at myself for that for eight months,” he said. “Not for the decision. For the manner of it. There is a distinction.”

There is a distinction.

Yes.

He drove himself eight months on that distinction.

“What would you have said,” Livia asked, “if you’d said it?”

Thorne looked at the crow. The crow looked back with the expression of a creature that had been waiting for this question for eight months and was not going to help him answer it.

“That I didn’t want to go,” Thorne said. Quieter than everything else he had said. “That I wanted them to change. And I couldn’t—” he stopped.

“Ask,” Livia said.

“Ask,” he confirmed. “I don’t ask for things. It’s a point of principle.”

The crow made a sound. Three short notes.

The specific tone of: we have discussed this before and I have not changed my position.

“I know,” Thorne said, to the crow.

“Your crow has a position on you not asking for things,” Livia said.

“My crow has positions on most of my life choices,” Thorne said. “It is very engaged.”

“Is it ever right?”

A pause so long the crow actually looked away, as if giving him privacy to answer.

“Always,” Thorne said. “That’s the problem.”

“What do you want now?” Livia asked. “Not eight months ago. Now.”

Thorne looked at the guild garden. The eastern flower beds. The guild hall visible past the hedgerow. Glimmer on its circuit. The familiar geography of a place he had left and come back to on a day with no good reason except that he needed to.

“The plants are going to keep talking,” he said.

“Yes.”

“I have three weeks of data on sentient botanical utterances that the scientific community will not take seriously without guild corroboration.”

“Probably true.”

“The Verdant Expanse is within the guild’s registered territory for research access.”

“Also true.”

Thorne closed his field notebook with the deliberate motion of someone who had decided something but was going to take the long way to saying it.

“I should probably—” he said, —“reapply for research access. To the guild. As a member.”

Livia looked at him.

“That’s asking,” she said.

A pause. Something at the corner of his mouth that was not a smile but was adjacent to one.

“The data requires it,” he said.

The data requires it.

Eight months.

He asked.

By a different name.

That counts.

The crow made a sound that was, without any ambiguity at all, satisfaction.

“Don’t,” Thorne said, to the crow.

The crow did not stop.

Miles came through the garden at the end of his afternoon rounds, which he did when the server was running stable and the guild did not need immediate management, which increasingly was most days. He stopped when he saw Thorne.

Thorne stood up. He had the posture of someone prepared for a conversation that might be difficult.

Miles looked at him for a long moment.

Then: “Pinewhistle.”

“Vexley.”

The crow looked at Miles. Miles looked at the crow. There was a brief mutual assessment.

“You’re back,” Miles said.

“Reapplying for research access,” Thorne said. “The plants are sentient within a two-hundred-meter radius of your therapist and I have three weeks of documented data that requires guild corroboration to be taken seriously.”

Miles looked at Livia.

Livia looked at the garden.

“The wedding resonance,” she said.

“Right,” Miles said. With the tone of a man who had decided to absorb this information without examining it immediately. “Research access. File it through Alaric.”

“Alaric still handles intake forms?”

“Alaric handles all forms now. It was his idea. We have not found a way to undo it.”

Thorne nodded once. He had the expression of someone who had asked for something and received it and was now processing what that felt like.

The crow looked at Miles one more time. Then looked at Livia. Then back at Thorne. The crow made a small, satisfied sound and rearranged its wings..

The crow is done.

Case closed, as far as the crow is concerned.

He asked. He got. The crow has been vindicated.

The crow was always going to be vindicated.

Thorne stayed for dinner, which nobody had invited him to and nobody asked him not to. He ate with the focused efficiency of someone who had been eating field rations for eight months and was recalibrating. The crow sat on the table. Bron tried to feed it and it accepted, which everyone agreed was a good sign except Thorne, who said the crow had low standards for food and that this was a separate issue from its standards for everything else.

After dinner Livia wrote in her notepad:

Thorne Pinewhistle: Presenting issue: talking plants. Actual issue: left without asking for what he wanted. Came back because the plants said it and he needed it said. He asked. He used different words for it. The crow has been right about this the whole time.

Note: the crow is an excellent reader of emotional subtext. The crow should be in the guild. I am not going to say this to Thorne yet.

She looked at the note about the crow for a moment. Then added:

The plants said: you were right to leave and you are angry at yourself anyway. This is not a contradiction.

He needed that said out loud. Somewhere other than his own head.

Sometimes people come back not because they changed their mind. Because something outside them finally said the thing they’ve been carrying alone.

She closed the notepad.

From the main hall, she could hear Thorne telling Bron at some length about the correct methodology for documenting sentient botanical utterances, and Bron asking questions that were earnest but not always correct, and the crow making occasional comments that Thorne was fielding with the specific resignation of someone who had stopped pretending the crow was not participating in the conversation.

He is staying.

He asked.

He used a different word for it.

The crow is satisfied.

I am going to let the crow be satisfied.
