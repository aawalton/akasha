Aya

In the months that followed the arrival of the colder seasons, Mai slowly found herself slipping into a world that—though entirely new to her—felt as if it had always been within reach.

In her spare time, with her usual calm and patience, Miho began introducing her to other feys living in Tokyo, hidden in the spots of the city like secret groups and some clans. More often than not, the feys blended into the crowd, as if humanity simply couldn’t perceive them—or perhaps didn’t dare to.

Some lived under the government’s protection, working as researchers or special agents within the Inoue Section’s offices, offering their knowledge and abilities in exchange for shelter and resources. Others chose to remain in the shadows, unseen yet woven seamlessly into urban life, as though their own existence were a secret even they could barely face, and adaptation was the only thing left to them. Still, most lived comfortably enough… unless someone asked about their memories.

Among them, the majority carried a deep sadness in their eyes whenever Mai tried to see whether any of them remembered anything.

Most feared remembering, but others just seemed indifferent to the question.

For the first group, it was as if something held them back, as if a fog of forgetting covered the pieces of a shattered puzzle—an old story too terrifying to revive.

Mai had heard this from Miho before, but now, seeing these feys marked by the same anguish, a shiver ran down her spine.

They didn’t remember their past, didn’t remember where they had come from. And though the old legends called them fairies, marebito or yōkai, she couldn’t help but doubt that tale. There was nothing stranger to her than the thought of being torn from somewhere—stripped of memory—and placed into another life.

For the second group, things were simpler: life had to be lived, and there was no damn point tormenting oneself over a past no one could recall.

Mai found herself wondering if she too shared that emptiness, that fear, that hollow absence that feys seemed to carry on their shoulders. Her mind, full of scattered fragments, whispered that she did, but also not entirely the same. That persistent feeling—that something didn’t fit—gnawed at her.

Was she just another fey, another marebito? Sometimes, walking beside Miho, she noticed how other feys avoided meeting Miho’s eyes, as if afraid that something inside them might awaken with a single glance.

"We are the forgotten," one fey girl had said during a recent gathering in a club, her voice low but cheerful from the beer. "They stole everything from us, even our memories. We live in a world that no longer belongs to us either, and all we can do is adapt and survive."

Mai couldn’t shake the impression that, deep down, that woman had already made peace with her fate—and that terrified Mai even more.

Was it truly possible to live without knowing who you really were?

In recent years, thanks to the secret advances of both science and magic, a new theory had begun gaining traction among occult scholars, though the mystery of the feys remained a shadow impossible to dispel.

The theory suggested that humanoid feys might once have been human in some distant and unreachable past. That they were, in some way, descendants of a humanity taken long ago—stripped from their time and space—like the figures in old legends who were spirited away to other realms and then returned to Earth at a different point in the future transformed into feys.

These ideas had begun forming within a hidden organization inside a university in the United States Kingdom, though the research was still in its diapers.

A control group needed to be assembled, deeper investigations conducted, and many years would have to pass before anyone could confirm whether there truly was a link between the feys and the long-lost humans of old.

To Mai, however, it all felt uncomfortably familiar.

She bit her tongue to keep from saying so.

She knew the theory could, or more likely, be true.

To her that theory it wasn’t just speculation—it was tangible, tied to the scattered fragments that drifted through her mind. Fragments of childhood, of a life before she became what she was now, before she became fully fey. Vague yet intense images of a time when she had been a little girl—images that dissolved each time she tried to hold on to them.

But… could it have been another world? Or was it the same one?

If the theory was true, then where were her parents? What happened in that accident in her dreams?

There were only a few certainties:

That dream before she awoke in the alley.

That pyramid.

Then the dream in which she was a child—and it had been Japan… but decades ago.

And yet, she was terrified of that unspoken truth. She couldn’t talk about it, couldn’t share it with anyone. Every time she tried to think about those memories, something inside her shut down, as if an invisible barrier were protecting her—or trapping her—from whatever those memories truly meant.

It felt as though speaking of them would unveil something worse, something even the other feys could not understand. Part of her sensed she wasn’t the only one. Many of the feys she had met, carried the same sensation—the same confusion. But the fear of speaking, the dread of unraveling a secret too large to face, stayed stubbornly lodged in her chest. Or… was it simply her own imagination?

Sometimes, in the stillness of the night, staring at the stars from Miho’s small balcony, she wondered if she herself was part of that legend.

What if she had been one of the people taken—severed from her humanity in some unknown way?But those questions found no answers.

To begin with… in her memories, the woman she knew to be her mother had pointed ears, her father did not. Her mother had been a fey.

Which meant she had been mixed from the very beginning—half fey, half human.

Then where in the world had the damned antennae come from?

There were stories—rumors—about feys who had tried to remember their past and who, upon doing so, simply vanished. No one knew exactly what happened, but some whispered that a fey’s memory, when forced into the light, could burn them like a lightning strike—disintegrating them as they attempted to reconnect with what they once were.

For those without a core, the disappearance was immediate, without warning, as if their very essence dissolved into thin air. It was as though something beyond their control kept them from remembering—or worse, kept them from speaking freely about it.

Mai couldn’t help wondering what would happen if she tried too hard to remember. She felt trapped in a sea of fragments that could never come together. Something inside her pushed her away from digging too deeply, as if doing so might cause her to vanish too—to be erased just like those other feys spoken of in hushed voices.

On the other hand, there was the question of disappearance itself. If all of this were true, then there should have been records, traces, hints of people who had vanished under strange circumstances. But Mai had never found anything. Not a single reference in archives or old reports.Supernatural disappearances existed in the modern world—there were traces of people taken by their own nature. But if the feys had once been human, then where were the records of the people they had been?

Or did people simply disappear as if they had never existed at all?

Miho, however, had once spoken to her about a theory circulating in academic circles at a university in the United States. A theory that, despite sounding utterly insane, seemed to have more substance than Mai would have expected.

According to Miho, it was said that when feys had once been human—or anything else—their existence was erased completely from the world. Those who vanished were not only forgotten by humans, but by the fabric of reality itself. No one would remember they had lived. Not even the people who had once known them. Their imprint on the world was wiped clean, as though they had never touched the earth.

The vanished were sent somewhere else—a parallel realm, a dimension that changed them.

The Other Side.

Tokoyo.

Sitra Achra.

The land of the dead.

Limbo.

Many names in different countries and cultures. The same idea.

They returned much later, transformed into feys. It wasn’t an idea Mai could dismiss so easily.

Absurd as it sounded, something in her made her feel that—perhaps, just perhaps—there was truth in it. The absence of proof was strange, and every story she heard seemed to point in the same direction.

Was she one of those feys?

Had she once been someone else?

That child with her parents?

What had happened to them?

That accident…?

As time passed, Mai began to feel a calling—a pull to dig deeper, to sink into the shadows of her own past, even if she had to do it behind Miho’s back.

Curiosity devoured her.

***

For hours, silently and in secret, she spent her time in front of the laptop she had bought after saving up, searching for any clue—any scrap of information—that might align with the fragments she carried. She didn’t know exactly what she expected to find, but she felt certain that somewhere in the vast digital world, something was waiting for her. Something that might give her an answer—or at least a lead.

And then, one morning, as she searched through chronicles of old accidents, she found it.

Her father.

Quincy Izumi.

A foreigner who, for some unknown reason, had become a naturalized Japanese citizen. He had taken his wife’s surname—a woman whose identity remained a complete mystery.

According to the records Mai had managed to find, Quincy had died in a car accident, a strange accident whose causes had never been clarified. He had been traveling in the vehicle with his wife, but she never appeared. Her body, as if it had simply dissolved, was lost somewhere in the shadows of the crash.

Additionally, two other people who had been traveling with them in another vehicle had also died—people who were supposedly acquaintances. Mai couldn’t help noticing that these acquaintances seemed connected to one of the very few crime families still operating in Tokyo. But that was all she had been able to uncover. The information dissolved the deeper she dug, as if it had been smothered by some unseen force… as if no one wanted to remember.

The accident had taken place in 1985, but the technology of that era had been far behind the modern one. Very few archives had been digitized, and most data had been lost to time. Still, Mai didn’t give up. She kept searching, combing every corner of the internet, until she finally found something else—one more breadcrumb. A name in an online list of the deceased resting in Nakano: Quincy Izumi was buried in the Nakano cemetery.

The very city where she felt so inexplicably at home. Had she… really lived there once?

That same afternoon, after a long day of work and training, Mai decided to visit the cemetery.

She didn’t know what she hoped to find there—maybe an answer, or at least confirmation that Quincy Izumi had truly existed.

When she arrived, the calm of the place wrapped around her.

She walked between the tombstones until she finally found Quincy Izumi’s grave.

A single grave. Not part of a family plot.

His name was carved into the stone, along with a date that confirmed everything she already knew. What caught her attention, though, was the immaculate state of the grave. No dust. No signs of neglect. The stone looked as if it had been cleaned with care, again and again. As if someone—after all these years—still tended to it.

Who would do that? And why?

The questions crowded her mind, but no answers followed. Only a growing unease that clung to her ribs, whispering that she needed to dig deeper. Something inside her insisted she couldn’t stop there.

Mai stood before Quincy Izumi’s grave, the cold stone facing her like an expressionless witness to her trembling heart. The wind stirred faintly, but none of it could soothe the storm breaking open inside her.

Silent tears slid down her cheeks—each one feeling like the last remnant of what she once had been. Those fragmented memories, scattered and jagged like pieces of a puzzle locked inside her skull, mixed with a pain so deep she couldn’t trace its origin.

Why couldn’t she remember all?

Why did those childhood memories feel so real—so intimate—yet so impossibly distant?

She couldn’t stop thinking about her mother, her father… about the life she had lived before the crash.

The thought that her entire existence had been erased from the world terrified her. Miho’s bizarre theory from months ago now seemed disturbingly plausible. If what Miho had said was true—if feys were beings whose past lives were wiped clean the moment they became what they were—then Mai was no longer who she thought she was.

She no longer belonged to this world.

Her parents, Quincy and his wife, had died in an accident in 1985, and Mai was now trapped in some kind of limbo—between two worlds, two realities that no longer recognized her. Over and over, the same question hammered inside her skull:

How was it possible that no one remembered her?

If she had survived—if she had lived long enough as a human to grow into what she was now—there should have been signs, traces, echoes of her life. But there were none.

The memories of her childhood—of being a little girl in that accident—should have left a mark. But no.

No one remembered her.

There was no record of Hisui, or Rei Izumi.

No gravestone.

Nothing.

Except for that nearly invisible scar on her chest.

The idea that the entire world—even those closest to her—had simply forgotten she ever existed sent a cold shiver through her bones.

And what if it was true?

If her existence had been erased completely, then what remained of her?

At what point had it happened?

In that accident?

Or—more likely—years later?

If what the doctor had said was true, she had already been an adult.

And when she added the fragments from her dream… that placed her disappearance in the late twentieth century.

1998, maybe?

1999?

The world kept turning, but Mai was no longer there—no longer part of it. Nothing of what she had once been, nothing she had lived, seemed to matter anymore. The traces of her existence had faded away.

She couldn’t shake the faces of the other two men she had seen in those flickering memories—the blurred silhouettes. Jack, Leteo. And the children.

Who were they?

Were they tied to her former life, or simply fragments of the strange puzzle she was desperately trying to piece together?

No answer came.

Only one clue—: a rocket scientist who had died decades ago.

Jack Piersons. Uncle Jack.

She recognized his face from her memories, but the information she found online said he had died long before, and was far older than the man she remembered. He couldn’t be the same person. Leteo, on the other hand, led only to some cryptic references about a mysterious scientist nobody knew much about.

But what did any of it mean?

How did it connect to her story?

The pieces still refused to fit. And fear—an uncontrollable fear, laced with raw frustration—tightened around her like a noose.

For months, Mai lived with that fear. It settled in her chest like a constant pressure, an invisible weight she could never quite draw breath against. She couldn’t sleep. Nights stretched out endlessly, and in each one, the anxiety of not knowing who she fully was—or what she had been—kept her awake. Miho, with her steady and quiet presence, had noticed the change, noticed how something inside Mai had started to shift. But she never pushed her to talk.

Mai suspected Miho already knew—or at least sensed—that she had uncovered something she wasn’t meant to. And that realization only made everything worse.

If the theory was true…

If she spoke…

If she searched too much…

If she dug too deep…

What would happen?

Would she vanish from the world like the other feys who had tried?

Would she die—struck down by some unseen force, the way the legends whispered?

The possibility of facing that kept her silent. And, in her heart, fear grew like a blooming darkness, because sometimes the worst part isn’t the question—it’s knowing that the answer one seek might be the last one you’d ever want to hear.

***

Mai had decided to visit the grave again.

The autumn afternoon fell gently over the Nakano, as she walked toward the western-style tombstone, she noticed a figure already standing beside it.

A woman.

Tall. Elegant. Her long dark hair cascading softly over her shoulders.

Something in Mai’s chest tightened sharply, and her eyes widened before she could stop herself. She had never seen this woman before, but a strange familiarity tugged at her—like recognizing a voice from a dream.

The woman was cleaning the gravestone. Her serene face, touched with a quiet kindness, lifted when she sensed Mai’s presence. Their eyes met. And in that brief instant, something nameless passed between them—like recognition without memory, as if the woman had once belonged to Mai’s forgotten life.

Mai swallowed, forcing her voice out.

“Hello.”

“Hello,” the woman answered in a calm tone.

“Mind if I help you?” Mai asked with a gentle smile.

The woman looked slightly surprised to her, but returned the smile. “No, that’s all right, I… I’m done already.”

She straightened, picking up the empty bucket to leave. And then she saw them—Mai’s pointed ears peeking out from her dark hair.

Her eyes returned to Mai, this time with a glint of curiosity. She studied her with a look that felt almost… familiar.

“Sorry, but… do I know you from somewhere?” she asked at last, breaking the silence. Her voice was kind, but touched with a faint confusion. “Ah—my apologies. I should introduce myself. I’m Aya. Himeji Aya.”

“Mai I… Ishigami.” Mai hesitated, biting her lip before she almost said Izumi. That was the surname on her father’s tombstone. She narrowly avoided the mistake.

Aya smiled softly, glancing at the grave.

“I knew them long ago. Quincy and his wife… Hisui. They were… good friends.”

“Yes. I… I was a friend of them too.”

The lie slipped from Mai’s lips with unsettling ease, but a tight knot twisted in her stomach. Something about the woman stirred too much familiarity inside her. Her eyes stung, unexpectedly.

Aya studied her for a moment, as though weighing her words. Then she nodded.

“Quincy and Hisui were very close to my sister and her husband. Masako and Ishida Yanagida. They were the ones traveling with them that night, in 1985. It was a terrible accident. We… lost them all.”

Aya’s voice, though steady, carried a grief that Mai felt like a cold hand closing around her heart.

Masako and Ishida Yanagida.

The names echoed inside Mai’s mind, hauntingly familiar. She had looked them up. The Yanagida family—famous for their ties to gambling games—had been involved in several dark incidents Miho herself had helped resolve, cases tangled with spiritual or magical disturbances.

And now, standing in front of her, was Aya Himeji—a woman whose own family, according to Mai’s research, was intertwined with that same shadowed network.

Mai kept the thought to herself, saying nothing. It couldn’t be a coincidence—two of the most powerful crime families tangled in the history of her family that she was desperately trying to unravel. And even less of a coincidence that Aya, the woman standing in front of her, was tending her father’s grave with such devotion. Destiny maybe had led Mai there… to her.

“It’s a quiet place, isn’t it?” Aya said, glancing at Mai with a faint smile as she continued wiping the stone. “My sister used to say this cemetery had something special. Something you can’t see, but you feel the moment you stand here.”

“Ah,” Mai murmured, unsure what else to say.

“She was an odd girl sometimes, At least that dark aura faded a little once she married Ishida.”

Mai nodded vaguely, unsure how to respond. A shiver crawled down her spine, and even though the sun was sinking behind the trees, the air felt heavier. Something in the conversation unsettled her deeply.

“Maybe you feel it too,” Aya continued, her voice almost a whisper. “Sometimes those who stay with us, even after they’re gone, linger in ways we don’t quite understand. I come here every week. To remember them.”

Mai fell silent, her thoughts twisted into a knot she couldn’t pull apart. The pieces of the puzzle kept piling up, but none of them fit. And now this woman, Aya Himeji, seemed like yet another piece—connected to her life, to her past, though Mai couldn’t understand how.

“You… knew him? Mr. Quincy and Hisui, I mean.”

“Yes. The four of them were inseparable,” Aya said, looking down at the grave with a nostalgic smile. “Masako, Ishida, Quincy, and Hisui… It’s hard not to miss them. But life goes on.”

Mai nodded, still silent, unsure how to reply. Aya’s words cut deeper than she expected. Something about that farewell, that grief, pressed against the hollow space inside her—the space where answers should have been, but never were.

Aya turned toward her, her expression gentler now. “My family is complicated. Their business pushed me away, and though my parents tried to bring me in, I refused. Masako and Ishida were always kind to me—more than anyone else. That’s why I keep coming. To look after them here. And to visit Quincy, too. It’s a shame Hisui isn’t here.”

Mai stared at her, understanding the weight behind her words. It wasn’t easy to walk away from the world you came from, the world you inherited. Aya had chosen her own path—maybe for the same reason Mai had spent months chasing the truth, even when that truth could tore her apart.

“They never found her… Hisui,” Mai said quietly.

“Never, main reason her name is not here. Maybe is time. Has passed years.” Aya nodded, looking at the grave, then back at her with a small smile. “You remind me a bit of her. The hair and eyes… hehe. And Hisui was small too.”

Mai held herself together as best she could. She could cry—she knew she could—if this conversation went a step further. She wanted to ask more. She wanted to know. But fear tightened her throat. She needed to let it go. Maybe if she pushed too hard… something might happen.

“The cause of the accident was just the poor condition of the road, right?”

“Yes, just that. Considering all the adventures they had, it was a strange ending. I guess in the end, even the most mundane things like an accident make us vulnerable, no matter how many dangers we've overcome before.” Aya sighed sadly.

“I’m sorry for everything you’ve lost,” Mai said softly, hesitant but sincere.

Aya gave her a gentle, sorrowful smile. “Thank you. We all need a little comfort from time to time. Even so many years after.”

Aya bowed politely, then turned, walking away with slow, steady steps.

Left alone before her father’s gravestone, a deep nostalgia washed over her. The encounter with Aya had stirred something she could not yet name—something that made her feel strangely tethered to a past erased, to a life no longer hers. The pieces kept falling, but they still wouldn’t fit. And although the answers remained out of reach, perhaps she had taken another small, necessary step toward them.

The sun had vanished completely, and the air had grown cool. Mai closed her eyes for a moment, letting the wind brush against her face, as if trying to lift away the heavy feeling that had lodged itself deep inside her.
