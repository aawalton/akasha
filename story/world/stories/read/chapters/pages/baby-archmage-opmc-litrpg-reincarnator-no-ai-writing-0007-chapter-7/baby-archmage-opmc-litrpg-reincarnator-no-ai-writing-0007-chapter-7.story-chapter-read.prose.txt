I felt such relief now that my parents knew of my secrets. And I was so happy that I had them in my corner. I had feared disappointing my father by not becoming a Fisher, and making my mother scared by being too different. But I was lucky to have such understanding and loving parents, who accepted me and wanted to support me.

I decided not to go outside before we figured something out. This had become a race. Marek was likely working Vorok twice as hard after a public humiliation. They would likely double that monster kid’s training, and nothing motivated bullies as much as their wounded pride. If I went outside I would have another conflict with him. That might get me injured and slow me down.

In nine months we would have our first fishing competition. Every household with a boy would prepare for that. It wasn’t real fishing. There was a lagoon in the jungle where all three nearby villages would meet. Then the kids would be split into age groups and given appropriate challenges to compete in. For the younger kids nothing was on the line but pride and reputation, but that would set the tone for the hierarchy a generation would take.

While I was coming to terms with the fact that seeking magic would eventually take me away from the village, I wasn’t about to let Vorok get too smug.

I needed to figure something out. My magic was not combat ready. I needed structured spellcasting or something like that. For that father talked to Elder Piraka. The Shaman came to our house soon after the incident with Vorok at my father’s request.

He looked at me as he stroked his beard.

“This fight with Vorok was unfortunate and predictable,” he said. “You were good to defend yourself, boy.”

“Thank you elder,” I said. “I didn’t like him stepping on my sandcastle. I don’t like him.”

The elder chuckled. “You should try to get along. You will be spending your whole life fishing with him. Just like your fathers.”

“That’s what we wanted to talk about,” mother said as she brought tea to the elder.

“We were thinking…” father said and I could tell he had to force the words out. He was such a good father. “Maybe he shouldn’t be a Fisher.”

The elder frowned. “What are you saying? He looks like a healthy boy who could stand up in a fight against a bigger opponent.”

“Which is why this is so painful,” father said. “But it’s right for Luka.”

“We want you to teach him the ways of the Shaman. Maybe take him as an apprentice?”

“Hm? Don’t be silly, Ramona,” Elder Piraka said. “The boy is barely two. He is too young.”

Father cleared his throat. “He has the System unlocked.”

The elder’s eyebrows shot up. He gaped at me and I gave him my best smile. He looked at my parents as if asking if they were fooling him. But my parents only smiled, doing their best to temper their pride with politeness. I didn’t need to act so modest, because I was a baby. So I just preened.

“Is it true what your father says?” the old man asked. “That you have unlocked the System?”

“Yes, elder,” I said, sobering up. “I have the Meditation skill. It gives me Perception and Focus.”

“Those are good Attributes,” the elder said. “I have heard of this Meditation skill. How did you learn it?”

I shrugged. “Maybe by accident. I like thinking and sitting.”

We had decided with my parents to hide my mana skills for now. It was best to boil this frog with patience.

“If an apprenticeship is too much, maybe you could help him in some other manner. He has more brains than muscles,” mother said.

“Hmph,” father said. “He lacks in neither.”

“I see…” the elder said. “This is very unusual, but you are an interesting boy Luka. What do you think I should do?”

He gave me a sharp look, waiting for my answer. I needed to temper myself. What would the old codger want me to answer?

“You should give me exercises to see if I am fit to be your apprentice.”

The elder stroked his beard. “Well now, a wise answer.”

He studied me and thought for a while.

“What kind of exercise do you suggest?” the elder asked.

“Maybe I could learn how to read?”

The elder stopped stroking his beard. He turned to look at my parents.

Mother shrugged. “We mentioned it. He seemed very interested.”

Naturally I was interested in reading. The reason was simple. SPELLBOOKS!

“Well, I do have my grandson Danek learning to read. He is a bright boy that one, but not exactly suited to be a Shaman… He could do well managing the village’s quotas and storages, like his father.”

“Danek is of the same generation, right?” Father asked.

“Yes, but older. He is already turning five. I don’t know what my fool son is feeding him, but he doesn’t seem to be fit to become a Fisher. I’ve decided to exempt him from the Moon Festival. Do you think we should do the same for Luka?”

“No,” I said immediately. “I’m not backing down.”

My father ruffled my hair. “That’s my boy.”

The elder considered all this and nodded to himself. “I don’t think it's a good idea for him to use a Skill Slot for Reading, Jonaka.”

“Yes, he needs to prepare for the Moon Festival,” father agreed.

Reading Skill sounded fantastic, even if I didn’t yet know of the implications. I did have a free Skill Slot, and maybe magic required a lot of study. However, so far I had been learning organically by playing and experimenting. With Skill Slots being limited, I would have to learn more. I could learn to read without a Skill, but I didn’t like my chances with magic without the appropriate System help.

“Then I’ll learn to read without the Skill,” I said. “I have good Attributes for it.”

The elder considered it. “Hm, very well. I will ask my son if he is willing to teach you along with Danek.”

“I will give him the quotas he asks,” father said immediately, and I felt very grateful.

The village didn’t have money. Instead trade and agreements were done with quotas. Fishers had to fill the quotas by catching food. Any excess would be marked and banked, so that it could be used to trade for goods. Even though father had been reluctant to accept my weirdness, he was now willing to pay for my education, even when I chose a different path. If that wasn’t paternal love, I didn’t know what was.

The elder rose. “I see that you are all of one mind. We will teach little Luka to read. And maybe when he is older and ready, we can discuss the apprenticeship again.”

Sooner than you think, old codger!

Soon after my mother escorted me to the elder’s hut. It stood slightly apart from the village towards the jungle near the creek mother got our water from. The house was elevated to stand on six wooden stilts , and had a staircase and a little terrace. The elder’s position was clearly honored in the village.

When we entered the hut, I was struck with the strong scents of herbs being crushed and cooked. The elder was working on a worktable with the herbs, muttering to himself in total focus. There was also a young girl, maybe eight or so, watching the elder work while cleaning a pestle and mortar. In a corner of the hut was a small table at which sat a fat boy with a wide face and sandy hair, absorbed in reading a book. Next to him stood a stocky man with a beard and a friendly face.

“Hello,” mother said.

The stocky man greeted us. “Ah, Ramona. I heard your son had taken up an interest in words as well.”

“I hope you don’t mind, Konako,” mother said. “Luka has been very eager to learn reading.”

“I’ll be happy to help,” Konako said and crouched to greet me. “My name is Konako. I am the village’s Record Keeper. What is yours?”

“Hello,” I said and bowed before pointing at the chubby boy. “I’m Luka. What is he reading?”

Konako smiled. “It’s the jungle manual. All the Junglers, like your mother, know its contents.”

“But mama can’t read,” I said.

“No, but I can,” Konako said. “I’ve read it for her and the other Junglers.”

“You know I maintain that you learned to read just to spend more time with girls, Konako.”

It was known that Junglers were mostly women. It was the profession adventurous women took instead of Fishing. Why did the village have such a clear gender culture even though there were Skills to even out the physical playing field? Eh, beats me.

Jungling was a very physically demanding profession anyway. It required endurance and resourcefulness to be able to spend days in the jungle. It… kind of made sense. Mother was like that. Tough and smart.

The wide man grinned and reached to ruffle the chubby boy’s hair. “And look how well it turned out.”

The boy swatted the hand. “Stop it! I’m trying to concentrate.”

“Look, Danek, aunt Ramona brought you a friend to study with.”

I walked up to the boy and extended a hand. “Hey. I’m Luka. What’s your name?”

“I—I’m Danek,” the boy said, losing much of his previous confidence. “What do you want?”

“I came to learn reading,” I said. “I heard you’re already really good.”

“Really?” the boy said, his face brightening. “You don’t think it’s weird?”

“Of course it’s weird,” I said and grinned. “None of the other boys are interested in reading.”

The boy looked confused and a little hurt. I scooted next to him into a chair Konako must have prepared. “But we can be weird together.”

The boy searched my eyes, as if looking for a hidden barb. When he found none, he broke into a smile. “Okay! Look. I’ll show you the Silverback. It’s the king of the jungle! There’s a picture…”

Mother left after chatting with Konako and the elder for a while, and told me she would return in the evening. Most of the time Konako taught us and demanded silence and concentration, but I snuck a few jokes in with Danek. He was a shy boy, but clearly very smart. He already had the Reading Skill, which allowed him to read with a speed and accuracy completely impossible for a normal five year old. I asked Konako about the Skill, and he told me it made it possible to read very fast and remember things you’d read very accurately and rarely forget. He told me one elder a few generations ago had developed the Skill so far, he could read two books simultaneously— one eye per book. The System was awesome.

When we took breaks, I watched the Elder work. He was working with potions and herbs and making all kinds of useful tonics and elixirs for the village. Antidotes were important for the Junglers. Healing salves for the crafters inevitably cutting their fingers and energy tonics to survive the long days of work for the fishers.

Apparently the latter ones were especially tricky. I activated my Mana Sight to watch Elder Piraka at work.

He was handling some kind of small bag that caught my interest. There was free-floating mana in their strange orbits everywhere. That was normal. But the bag was full of a liquid made of mana. It was thick and shined brightly in my vision. Piraka must have used some skill to scoop up some of the liquid mana from the bag and then he started transferring it to the potion he was making. Slowly the lightly green tonic was turning darker. The apprentice girl watched raptly while she was scrubbing a mortar and pestle that was well clean already.

“What are you doing, elder?”

“Hmph,” the apprentice girl said. “Don’t interrupt the master.”

“It’s alright, Nirama,” the elder said and turned to me. “I am infusing the energy tonic with mana to make it more potent.”

My eyes lit up with interest and I forgot all about reading. I walked to observe closer and Nirama scowled at me.

“Why is the tonic changing color?”

“I am taking the mana from this bag and letting it attach to the active ingredients.”

That looked about right. The liquid mana seemed to cluster around floating particles inside the liquid. When Piraka wanted to add more mana he swirled the vial to make the heavy particles move, so he could add more mana.

But this was really weird. Why didn’t Piraka just use the free mana in the atmosphere instead of the bag?

“What’s in the bag?” I asked.

“Master, why is he so noisy? Go back to read silently, you baby!”

“This?” the elder asked. “There’s mana in this bag. I get it from trading boats that come from Aligapa, the capital of the archipelago.”

“Why don’t you just use mana in the air?” I asked.

Everyone knew there was mana in the air, so this wasn’t suspicious. We breathed it in, which powered the System. It was more concentrated in the food, especially the fish father caught, which made advancing skills faster and gave the body and mind more energy.

Even so, both the elder and the apprentice stared at me blankly, and I wondered if I had revealed something foolish. Then the apprentice started to laugh.

“Stupid baby! That’s not how it works.”

The elder looked at me carefully. “She is right. This mana was harvested from an awakened beast’s monster core. It is a necessary reagent for the tonics. I have heard tales of mana harvesters that could use the mana of the air that spirits use, but I have no such legendary devices or skills.”

“Yeah!” Nirama said. “Nobody can use the mana of the spirits!”

I frowned. “That’s not true. I’ve seen the elder cast magic using his staff.”

The elder nodded appreciatively and Nirama’s face flushed red.

“That is true, Luka,” Piraka said. “The staff is a mana conduit. But I can’t use my Staffcasting Skill with the infusions.”

Staffcasting huh? So that was how Piraka used spells.

“Could you use the staff to harvest mana from the air and make this liquid mana?”

“Hmmm, that was a good question, young Luka.”

“Why are you asking all of these questions?!” Nirama demanded.

I ignored her and looked at the elder.

“The staff only works for the spells. The amber gemstone is a specialized magical focus that uses inherent structure. Magic requires structuring mana into complicated formations. It is very difficult to use without magical tools.”

This was extremely interesting, and I started to ask another question, but the elder suggested I get back to reading.

I nodded, deciding it was better to watch and learn than to argue.

Piraka continued the infusions and two things were happening which caught my attention. First of all Piraka was wasting a lot of that liquid mana. He took some out of the bag and infused the potion with the mass, but a lot of it just dissipated in the air and started orbiting like free mana. The other thing I noticed was that even though the particles weren’t saturated with mana, they would reject further injections. I looked closer for why and understood. Piraka was using some kind of skill to manipulate the mana. Maybe he had the same Skill as me. But he was working blind. He didn’t know when the particles were saturated, and he couldn’t accurately target them. This left the tonics half-baked!

Piraka had worked out some kind of cost-efficient happy medium and put the tonic down. I looked at the potion with my Mana Sight and felt a vague sense of disappointment at the shoddy work. I returned to reading, but I was starting to formulate a clear plan on how to win an apprenticeship from Piraka…
