At the very back of the jam-packed lecture hall, Vir stifled a yawn.

The lecture was torture. Absolute abject torture. Not on account of its topic—the basics of prana manipulation—which Vir was more than interested in, but because it took every shred of his not-insignificant willpower to stop his eyelids from closing.

He feared he was losing that war, however, as Maiya’s nudges from beside him became increasingly frequent.

Sleep was something he’d so long ago moved beyond that being suddenly confronted with it again irked him, to say the least. Without the ability to cycle the burgeoning prana reserves that resided within his body, his muscles languished, and thus all the benefits he’d come to take for granted were lost. In his current state, he required food and sleep like any other mortal.

He was so tired that he only noticed Mina Hiranya after Maiya pointed her out. The black-haired, bronze-skinned princess was sitting alone a few rows down, and bore all her limbs. Vir wondered if she was just as deranged in this universe as she’d been in his, or whether the end of the world had set her straight.

If that hadn’t, Vir suspected nothing would.

“It needn’t be stated that the fundamental nature of prana has changed,” Professor Vadin—a tall, middle-aged man with wavy black hair and a sharp beard—announced. “All of you are here to learn how to cultivate prana under this new paradigm. However, knowledge of the old rules is paramount if one has any hope of learning how they have changed.”

It made sense to Vir, and the fact that the professor had used the word “cultivation” was interesting. Had he meant that as a clue? Or was it just a slip of the tongue?

Regardless, Vir doubted anything of any real substance would be taught until the professors had confirmed that the students indeed knew what they were doing. They’d lowered the bar for entrance into the Academy, and that necessitated more handholding than normal.

Perhaps, at the end of the semester, the students might be given a taste of real magic. By then, Vir would have accomplished his objective and would be long gone.

Despite that, the content of the lecture was interesting enough to hold his attention. For Vir, learning how others perceived the world was almost as valuable as how the world really worked.

If only the boy sitting to his left wasn’t turning the experience into an agonizing nightmare.

“Honestly,” Vayren said, “I can’t believe the drivel they’re teaching at this level. Do they not have any respect for our time, to say nothing of our intellect?”

Daya rolled her eyes. “It’s a recap,” she muttered, “for students who might not have received the same level of education we have. Don’t you worry, we’ll be diving into prana manipulation soon enough.”

“This whole course is centered around prana manipulation!” Vayren spat. “How can you hope to manipulate prana if you don’t understand it? I scarcely think a refresher will aid those who need it. And for the rest of us, it is nothing but a slap in the face.”

Vir wondered about that. How much did anyone truly know how things worked? Vir, with all his knowledge, was essentially in the same place as everyone else. It was a humbling thought.

As strong as one became, there were always forces unimaginably greater in the world.

That truth existed at all levels. Most people in the world thought Talents were some completely different form of magic, entirely oblivious to the fact that they were nothing more than Earth Affinity mejai.

The Altani, at least, had known that fact, though they’d held it as a tightly guarded secret for reasons Vir only somewhat understood. No doubt it served as a sweet carrot, vaulting their reputation as masters of magic.

Sadly for them, all that knowledge was now useless. The playing field had evened, and yet the Altani had still managed to break new ground. For that, at least, they had Vir’s respect.

He thought back to the contents of the tome. It spoke of how the very principle that underpinned the energy of prana, which Vir took to mean its conservation—how prana simply shifted states from magic to energy to matter—was no longer applicable under the new rules.

That, however, didn’t sit well with Vir. Not at all. He felt there had to be something there. Something he was missing.

Finding it difficult to focus on such little sleep, Vir’s mind wandered, and he looked around at the hall packed with students. Students who needed to do nothing more than show up to class and diligently practice what they were told.

He couldn’t help but wonder. If he’d had proper magical instruction and tutelage back when he was first learning, how much quicker would he have grown? How much stronger would he have become? And how much better able would he have been to thwart the tragedies he’d faced?

His thoughts came back to Tia, and he could only grind his teeth. Where was she now? he wondered idly.

Unfortunately, the boy next to him prevented his escape into that daydream.

“What do you think, Darsh?” Vayren asked. “Surely this must all be banal to you as well? Even a commoner like you must know some of these things. Please tell me this isn’t new to you?”

“It’s not,” Vir replied, brushing off the thinly veiled insults. “But it is never bad to review the basics.” Vir glanced at Daya. “Daya is correct that to manipulate prana, one must understand it first. Especially if the nature of magic has changed as much as everyone claims.”

Even as he said the words, Vir knew it was at least partly a lie. That human mejai, whose understanding of prana was sorely lacking, could still wield it, then perhaps Vayren had a point. Perhaps there was hope for Vir to shortcut the process.

“A level-headed, intelligent response,” Daya said with a beaming smile. “Thank you, Darsh.”

Vir didn’t need to look to know that Maiya was rolling her eyes beside him.

Vayren threw up his hands dramatically, leaning back in his chair. “This is ridiculous!”

“What was that, Mr. Vastav?” Professor Vadin called, causing the hotheaded Sawai to go ramrod straight in his seat.

“Am I correct in understanding that you feel this class a waste of time?”

“That’s, ah, no, sir, not at all,” Vayren said. “Not at all!”

Vir watched Vayren’s cheeks go red and his eyes bulge as the boy went through a maelstrom of emotions. Vir only barely controlled the laugh that almost escaped his lips.

Maiya, next to him, wasn’t so discerning, bursting out giggling, which spread infectiously to the students around her, earning her a scathing glare from Vayren.

By the way she continued to giggle, feeding off the energy, Vir was quite certain she didn’t notice.

“If you feel so sure about your knowledge of the affinities, why don’t you come down and educate us? The scion of House Vastav must have plenty to teach even this old man.”

“Yes, sir. Of course,” Vayren said, before walking down the steps like a man walking to his executioner, all eyes in the class on his back.

Realizing this, he straightened up and his noble upbringing took over, allowing him to compose himself rather well, Vir thought. It might even have been impressive, had his panic attack not been on full display just moments prior.

“Thank you, sir,” Vayren said as he reached the front of the class, “for, er, the opportunity to present my knowledge.”

“Well then, what can you tell us of the various affinities?” the professor asked.

“Only that Lightning is by far the most useful of all the affinities, followed closely by fire. Or was, rather. Talent Wielders, of course, are another form of mejai as everyone knows. They, however, use a special form of prana not found in the natural world.”

Vir sighed. Truly? This was the level of his knowledge?

Vayren went on, blabbering about how the other affinities were far less useful and that the fastest, most direct spells ought to be prioritized above all else.

Despite his lack of sleep, Vir found he could take it no more and raised his hand.

“Yes, Mr. Darsh?” the professor asked, cutting off Vayren’s explanation. “Do you have anything to say?” he asked with a wry smile.

Before this, Professor Vadin had done nothing to correct Vayren during his entire monologue, which was obviously and patently wrong. Vir suspected that this was punishment in and of itself, based on the professor’s cold demeanor.

Vayren must have realized it too, because his once-sure, recently regained composure broke down once again. Wherever he had studied, he clearly hadn’t received an education on par with what the Altani could offer, that much was clear.

“Oh, just that everything he’s saying is wrong,” Vir said.

“Care to elaborate?” the professor asked, and Vir could’ve sworn he saw a glint in the man’s eyes. This was his first interaction with him, and he already knew they’d get along well.

Vir nodded, walking down as Vayren glared daggers into his skull. Vir ignored him.

“There are no superior or inferior affinities,” Vir said. “Prana is a form of energy and a tool to be used. Nothing more and nothing less. Vayren here is looking at affinities purely from a combat usefulness perspective. But I would argue that support magic is equally vital. Injuries are inevitable on the battlefield, and an army bereft of healing is one that will crumble fast.”

“Very good,” the professor said. “I would not have expected such insight from someone at your level. Please continue.”

Vir gave the professor an appreciative nod. “Moreover, even in combat, there are benefits and drawbacks to all the arts. Ice magic has the ability to slow enemies, especially useful against fast-moving opponents, while Lightning is good for direct attacks. Less so against entire armies, except at the highest levels. Oh, and Talent Wielders absolutely wield prana found in nature. They use Earth prana, which is why they need to be in contact with the ground for their abilities to function.”

How so few had uncovered that secret boggled Vir’s mind to this day. But Talent Wielders tended to be quite good at turning a blind eye to the obvious.

“Excellent,” Professor Vadin said, clearly impressed with Vir’s explanation. “Truly excellent. But you mentioned something earlier that intrigues me. You said that prana is a form of energy. Can you elaborate?”

“Of course,” Vir said. “Prana is simply one of the three states of existence. Prana can be converted into energy to power various magic. Energy can be converted into matter. And matter, by virtue of its existence, generates prana…”

Vir trailed off, his thoughts drifting elsewhere. To the tome, where a sudden thought occurred to him like a bolt of lightning. Had he misinterpreted its meaning? What if under the new regime, prana continued to be conserved? What if all the Fateweavers had done was alter its source?

“Truly marvelous,” Vadin said, even as Vayren continued to grow green with envy. “Everything Darsh has said is correct, and I advise the class to take it to heart.”

“As he just said, Earth Affinity prana is, in fact, the source of the magic Talent wielders use. I’m afraid it is not a well-known fact in this world. Many believe their power comes from a different source entirely. In fact, most Talent wielders pride themselves upon being different from mejai.

“We let them carry their delusions with them. Here at the Altani Magical Academy, however, we will train you to be proficient in magic of all kinds, including Earth affinity for those of you who possess it. It is why we admit as many Talent wielders to this school as we do.”

Vir had to wonder how much awareness the Altani of this universe had about the other, lesser-known affinities. Forget Ash prana, there weren’t even shadow prana orbs. He’d only learned of its usage in arts after coming into contact with the demons of the Pagan Order.

“But if that’s the case,” a mousy-looking girl asked, “then why haven’t the Altani created Earth affinity orbs?”

The professor nodded. “A good question. As you are aware, orbs are not such simple things. While the crystals are mined easily enough in the caves beneath Sai, and the Altani produce new ones all the time, the inscriptions themselves are ones that have been passed down from the time of our First Thaumaturges, who took the knowledge of the Age of Gods and formed them into physical magics.”

Well, that’s a load of Ash’va dung, Vir thought.

The Altani had absolutely nothing to do with the creation of orbs. They’d simply found the blueprints and managed to rediscover how to produce them, likely having used Imperium toy orbs as examples to follow.

They’d done no innovation whatsoever in all the millennia since then, as far as Vir knew. True, they had come up with new and novel ways of using the magic—fast attack skyships and magical ballistae came to mind—but fundamentally they had not created any new magic at all.

But was it truly the case that the Altani had never figured out how to create new magic? Or was it simply not allowed?

Even among demonkind, where experimentation was allowed and encouraged, the task was so difficult that only geniuses like Saunak had any hope of creating a truly novel inscription. What he had done for Vir’s airships was nothing short of revolutionary, despite all the naysayers belittling his accomplishments on account of his reputation.

“There was much we did not understand of magic, even before,” the professor admitted. “But so long as we know its core, we may channel it to perform magic more powerful than one can imagine. That is equally true under these strange new rules we find ourselves subjected to.

“This is the Academy’s goal for each and every one of you. Take your lessons to heart and ensure that you complete the homework by our next lesson. I don’t want to see a single person who’s failed to charge the test orb. It will not be easy, but this is precisely the point. Question its difficulty. Come up with theories. I expect good questions from you all next time. Dismissed!”

The students immediately broke out into conversation as they shuffled out of the hall, excitedly discussing the ease of this homework assignment.

Vir, meanwhile, stifled a sigh. He’d merely asked students to charge a low-level orb. Yet because he could not channel any of the known affinities, he’d be forced to fail this assignment. Vir feared his grades would take quite the beating until he figured out the secret behind Affinity conversion.

Which was hopefully soon, with the inspiration the lecture had given him.

Ignoring Vayren’s pointed stare, Vir nodded to Maiya to follow him.

It was about time he made some breakthroughs.
