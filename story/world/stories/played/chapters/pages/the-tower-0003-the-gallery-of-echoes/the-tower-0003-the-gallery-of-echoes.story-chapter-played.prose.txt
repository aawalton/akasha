# The Gallery of Echoes

You go up slow, and you do not charge the top. A body's length from the head of the stairs you drop low and ease the rest of the way on your own caution, until just your eyes clear the lip of the floor above — no more of you than you need to see with, ready to sink back down the instant anything turns your way.

It is a great long hall. After the drowned, close cistern it opens out huge and DRY, a vaulted gallery running away into the dark, the ceiling lost somewhere above you. From the far end comes a dim warm light, reddish, steady — not the cold black you climbed out of; something up there glows. And by that light you can see the floor of the hall, and the floor of the hall is a graveyard of OTHERS. Picked-clean leavings strewn the length of it — a rusted blade, a cracked pack, the pale shapes of bone gone grey with time. Climbers who came this far. Climbers who did not leave. There is precious little to hide behind between here and that glow; it is an open floor, a killing floor.

And at the far end of it, near where the warm light wells up and the way onward must be, there is a SHAPE. Large — larger than the ash-thing you fought at the bottom, large enough that you mark it at once even still and silent as it is. You cannot make out what it is from here, only that it is big, and that it has not yet noticed you; and underneath the seeing, that other sense of yours stirs and confirms it — the warm glow ahead is not just firelight. Something with a real weight of essence sits at the end of that hall. You hold there at the lip a long moment, taking the measure of it, letting none of yourself over the edge — knowing now the shape of what waits, and that it does not yet know yours.

You do not go up. Not yet. The first time you faced a floor of this Tower unready it nearly killed you — the cistern, the cold, the water closing over your head in the shallows — and you have learned the lesson the hard way: prepare first, then climb. So you go back down to the water's edge, to the very shallows that almost drowned you, and you set yourself to build something new before you ever set foot on that killing floor above.

You have two fires and one bar that holds a third. Ember Channel, that pours your heat into iron; Ember Burst, that throws it; and Burning Anger, warm through with the Ember you bound into it. You wonder what happens if you stop using them one at a time. You take the bar in both hands, point it out over the black water, and you channel into it the way you would to arm it — and then, instead of holding, you BURST, shoving the gathered fire down its length and out the end, all three things at once, and you push it all at the water that tried to take you.

Fire answers. Not a neat thing, not the tight burning ball you half-imagined — a WAVE. A great roaring wash of flame tears off the end of the bar and rolls out across the cistern, lighting the drowned room orange to its farthest corner, and where it meets the black water it throws up a hiss and a wall of steam that climbs into the dark. You feel the bar's bound heat go with it, the charge you set into the iron leaping out to lend the throw its weight, spending itself in the doing. It is raw. It is wide. It sprays where a master's would spear — you can feel how loose it is even as it leaves you. But it WENT. Fire left your hand and went where you aimed it, out over the very shallows that nearly drowned you, and burned the cold memory of that water to steam and light.

And behind your eyes the Tower takes note of the new thing you have made — not a borrowed trick but a fusion of your own, rough and Novice and entirely yours. You lower the bar, half-drained and still warm in your grip, your breath coming hard from the focus it cost, and you look at the steam settling back over the dark and know you have a weapon now you did not have an hour ago. It needs work. There is time to give it, before you climb.

The thing you glimpsed at the top of the stairs is large, and a wide soft wash of flame is not what you want to meet it with. You want a spear. So before anything else you take what you made and you go to work on it — and first you take its name back. Lash was wrong; it does not lash, it WASHES. Ember Wave, then. Call a thing what it is.

Then you drill. You point Burning Anger out over the black water and throw the Wave, and watch how loose it spills, and throw it again narrower, and again narrower than that — fighting the flow with everything your focus has, trying to choke the wide wash down into a single tight stream, as fierce and aimed as you can force it. And it COMES, partway. Throw by throw the wash tightens; the fire learns to run in a line instead of a fan; you can feel it growing more yours, more pointable, less a thing you spill and more a thing you aim.

But there is a wall, and you find it. Past a certain tightness it will not go — the last hard edge of it, the narrow searing lance you can picture so clearly, simply will not come, no matter how many times you throw. You can drill yourself to the counterfeit of it, the yard-trained near-miss, and no further. That final control is waiting on something an empty room cannot give you. You throw until you understand that, and then you throw a while longer out of stubbornness, until your focus is wrung dry and the fire guttering off the bar's end is the best you have left in you.

So you stop. You set Burning Anger across your knees, and you sit at the water's edge where the steam of your own fire still hangs, and you close your eyes and breathe. You do nothing and you reach for nothing; you only let the deep well of your focus fill back from the bottom, slow, the way still water finds its level. The cistern is quiet around you. The ache of the drilling eases. The new narrowness you bought settles into your hands and stays there, learned, even if its last edge eluded you.

When you open your eyes again you feel recovered — steady, clear, most of what you spent come back to you. Not the man you were at the bottom of this Tower; something harder and better-armed than that. You have a stream now where this morning you had only a wash, and a weapon in each hand with names, and a fire you can throw. The wall you hit is still there, waiting. But you are readier than you were.

Before you climb into anything with a guardian at the end of it, you want to know what your fire actually DOES — not against straw or memory or open water, but against metal, the stuff that armor and blades and big still shapes are made of. So you take up the Sentry pauldron, the steel one off floor two, and you set your Ember to it and you do not hold back. No channeling into iron to arm it, no throwing it wide — just heat, all of it, every degree you can wring out of yourself poured into one patch of steel to see how hot you can truly make it.

The metal answers. First a sullen warmth under your hand, then a deep red blooming up out of the grey, then brighter, the red climbing toward orange as you bear down and pour and refuse to let it cool. The dark around you fills with the light of it — a hard scorching orange glow throwing your shadow long on the cistern wall, heat rolling off the steel in a wave you have to lean back from. You hold it there, shaking with the effort, and push for more.

And more does not come. That is the thing you learn. You push until your focus screams and the steel sits there glowing a furious orange — hot enough to cook meat, to set cloth alight, to brand — and it will not climb past it. It does not yellow. It does not whiten. It does not soften or sag or run. The wall is real and you have found it: orange-hot is the top of what you are, today, against honest steel. A melt — metal gone to glowing liquid, the way a forge does it — is somewhere far above you, up a road you have not walked yet.

So you ease off, and the glow sinks back down the ladder it climbed — orange to red to a dull sullen ember to nothing, the steel ticking as it cools, kissed and discolored but whole. You set it down. It cost you to learn it, and it was worth the cost: you know your fire now in a way you did not an hour ago. It will scorch and sear and burn anything that lives. It will not yet melt the things that don't. Good to know, before you meet the big still shape upstairs.

You rest first. You learned that lesson at the bottom of the cistern and again on the stair: prepare, then climb. So you sit by the banked brazier on floor two until the ache is gone out of you and the deep well of your focus has filled back to the brim, until you are as ready as you are going to get. Then you stand, and you leave the camp behind — the brazier, the rope, the rivet, the soaking stone, the heat-kissed pauldron, all of it. You take two things and no more: Burning Anger in one hand, Cold Revenge in the other. Heat and cold, a bar apiece, and nothing to weigh you down.

You climb to the mouth of the third floor — and there you STOP. You do not step out onto that long open killing-floor where the bones of better-armed climbers lie picked clean. You stay in the throat of the stair, in the narrow of it, where a big thing can only come at you one width at a time. If it is going to be met, it will be met here, on ground you chose. You set your shoulders against the stone and you get ready to call it to you.

You lift Cold Revenge and you strike it against the doorway stone — once, hard, and the cold iron rings a flat hard note that goes rolling out down the length of that vaulted dark hall and comes back thinned from the far walls. You strike it again. And again, a slow deliberate toll, the sound of something that wants to be heard. Then you go still, both bars ready, and you wait to see what your noise has bought.

For a long breath, nothing. The reddish glow at the far end sits where it sat. And then — it MOVES. The big still shape you marked from the stairhead is not a shape any longer; it shifts, it rises into its own weight, and it turns toward you. It heard. It is coming.

It comes down the hall slow, and the slowness is the first thing you learn — no rush, no charge, a ponderous grinding advance, stone dragging over stone, each step landing with a weight you feel in the floor through your feet. And as it comes into the nearer dark you see it for what it is: a thing of fused rock and cooled slag heaped into the shape of a sentinel, big — bigger than the ash-thing you killed at the bottom, broad and hulking and heavy. Deep cracks run all through its hide, and down in those cracks a sullen reddish-orange light smolders — it is lit from the inside, banked full of heat, cinders sifting from its joints as it walks. The warm glow that fills this whole hall is not a fire on the floor. It is THIS. The glow is the thing itself.

And under your seeing, that other sense of yours opens wide and tells you the second thing: it is FULL. A deep dense well of ember-essence walks toward you on legs of stone — more heat banked in that cracked hide than in your rivet, your bar, anything you have touched. It is slow, and it is a furnace, and it stands between you and the way up. It comes on into the throat of the stair, into the ground you chose, near enough now that the heat of it reaches you — and it is your move.

You do not throw fire. You worked it out the moment you saw it: a thing lit full of its own heat will not much care about yours. So you set Burning Anger down on the stone at your feet, close enough to snatch up again in a heartbeat, and you take Cold Revenge up in both hands, and you wait — you let the furnace come grinding into the throat of the stair, into the narrow you chose, until its banked heat is washing over you and there is no more room for it to come — and then you swing with everything your shoulders have, the cold iron driving for one of those glowing seams in its hide.

It bites — cold meeting that buried fire with a vicious hiss and a spit of cold steam, a shower of slag spalling off where you land it. But the blow stops short of what you wanted. Under the glow the thing is STONE, fused and thick, and the stone turns your strength the way a wall turns a fist; the bar jars in your hands and rings up your arms, and the seam only cracks, shallow, where you meant to split it wide. Brute force is not going to break this open.

But you are not only swinging a bar. Even as your strength fights the stone, your WILL goes in — down through that shallow cracked seam, past the shell, into the hot heart of the thing where all that banked fire lives — and you PULL. You reach for its heat the way you learned to reach for the rivet's, and you drag it up and out and into yourself.

And it comes. A draught of living fire tears up the length of the bar and the seam and floods into you, and where it leaves the creature that one glowing crack guts and DIMS, going sullen and dark as you bleed the heat out of it. It is not much — the seam is shallow, you can only draw what the narrow opening lets through — but it is REAL, and it is the first time you have torn essence from a thing that did not want to give it. The heat floods in past where your body likes to hold it; you feel it scorch you at the edges, the overflow searing your own skin from the inside, and for one bad moment it is nearly more than you can keep. You keep it. You clamp down and you hold, brimming and burned and full of stolen fire — and you understand, all at once, the thing this fight turns on: your bar glanced off the stone, but your will reached the fire underneath. The shell is its armor. The heat is its weakness — and its fuel.

The sentinel feels it. Whatever dull patience carried it grinding down the hall is gone now; the dimmed seam in its hide and the bite of cold have woken it to you as a threat, not a noise. It does not move fast — it cannot, it is too vast and too heavy — but it MOVES: the great slag-heaped bulk hauls around, a limb like a fallen column dragging up off the floor, the inner glow flaring brighter through its cracks as it stokes itself to bring that weight down on the small burning thing in its doorway. You have a heartbeat, maybe two, bought by its slowness — brim-full of its own stolen heat, Cold Revenge gripped in both hands and Burning Anger lying on the stone at your feet, your back to the narrow stair. The blow is coming. What you do in the gap before it lands is yours to choose.

You do not stand and trade with a thing that size. The instant its weight commits — the great slag limb hauling up and over to come down on you — you give ground, fast and low, back off the lip of the floor and DOWN into the throat of the stair behind you. The blow lands where you were: the column of fused stone smashes into the floor with a crack you feel in your teeth, throwing chips and a gout of cinders, and it hits nothing but the place you left. You set Burning Anger down to free both hands, and there is no time to take it up again — it lies there on the stone at the lip, on the wrong side of the gap now, as you slide back into the narrow.

And the narrow is the whole point. You back down two steps, three, until the stair pinches close around you — and the Sentinel, grinding forward to follow, JAMS. It is too big. The stairmouth takes its head and one shoulder of heaped rock and no more; it wedges there, straining, a furnace crammed into a doorway, and it cannot reach you. You have put a wall of the Tower's own stone between its arms and your body. From here you can see the dimmed seam you tore in it, still sullen and dark against the glow. From here you can work.

So you reach again — not with the bar this time, just with your will, out across the gap to that dark seam — and you pull. The thread is thinner at this distance, no hide under your hand to anchor it, but it CATCHES, and the heat comes: a steady fine trickle of stolen fire drawn up out of the creature and into you, the seam guttering darker as you drink it, the thing bleeding heat it cannot stop while it strains uselessly at the stone.

And that is when the other problem finds you. You were already full. You filled yourself at the seam a minute ago and held it brimming and burned — and now you are pouring MORE in on top of it, into a vessel with no more room. The heat backs up. It has nowhere to go and so it goes into YOU, into muscle and gut and the back of your own skull, a deep internal scald that builds with every second you keep the thread open. You are draining the furnace, and you are becoming one. You cannot simply hold this. The fire you are stealing has to go somewhere, and soon, or it is going to cook you from the inside out.

You hold there a moment in the hot dark of the stair, taking the measure of where you stand. Above you the Sentinel grinds and strains against the stairmouth, wedged and furious, the glow flaring brighter through its cracks as it stokes itself — and the heat of it rolls down the stair in a thickening wave, the stone walls themselves growing warm around you, so that even out of its reach the air you breathe is climbing toward an oven. Burning Anger lies up at the lip in the cinder-light, a short bad dash away, on its side of the gap. Cold Revenge is in both your hands. The seam hangs dark and ready to give more.

You are safe from its arms and cooking from within and without, brim-full of a fire you have no place to put. The standoff is yours to break, one way or another — and what you do with the furnace you have stolen is yours to choose.

For one breath you nearly vent it — just open your hands and let the stolen fire roar off into the dark and be rid of the scald. It would be easy and it would be safe. But you stop, because underneath the pain a thought has caught light and will not go out: this Tower will not let you grow the ordinary way. It walled off the drilling, capped the reps, refused you the slow honest strength of practice. Fine. There is another way to make a blade — not by swinging it, but by putting it in the fire. So you do not vent. You take the furnace you have stolen and you turn it INWARD, down into your own arms, your own legs, the long bones and the deep muscle, and you will it to burn the weakness out of you and leave something harder behind.

It is the worst thing you have ever felt. The fire goes into your flesh and your whole body becomes the iron on the anvil — heat driving down into marrow, every nerve screaming at once, the taste of your own seared self in the back of your throat. You hold it. That is the thing that saves you: your WILL does not break, your control does not slip; you keep the fire doing what you told it to instead of simply cooking you, and you ride the agony down to the bottom and back up. It is crude — you can feel that, feel how much of it just BURNS where a cleaner hand would have tempered, your low and untried body taking the punishment a stronger frame would have shrugged toward strength. But it WORKS. The overfilling heat that had no place to go has a place now: you. It pours into the forging and is spent, and the scald that was cooking you from inside finally, mercifully, stops — used up, made into something.

And behind your eyes the Tower marks it: not a borrowed trick, not a drilled skill, but a new road entirely — the body forged in stolen fire. You found the door the reps could never open.

The dead hulk slumps before you, cold stone, drained, whatever it guarded now just so much grey rock and beyond it the way up off this floor at last. Smithing leaves you hollowed in a way that wants sleep, and you give in to it — not the bottomless collapse of before, just a nap, curled by the warm coals with the new mace under your hand. You go down into grey nothing and come back up a while later clearer, the forge-ache eased out of your arms, your focus pooled deep and ready again, the small burns gone tight and quiet. Good enough.

Because there is one more thing you mean to try while the idea is hot in you. The mace is a good weapon and a dead one — plain metal, no fire in it. Every time before, when you have put essence into a thing, you have moved it from somewhere else: cold out of the bar and into the stone, ember out of the coals and into the rivet. A ferry, always, carrying a charge from one vessel to another. This time you do not ferry anything. You take hold of the mace's heavy head, and you reach down into your own banked ember — your supply, the warmth that is simply yours now — and you push it out through your hands and INTO the steel, and you tell it to stay.

It fights you the way the cold stone once did, for a moment — your own essence resisting being left behind, wanting to flow back into you. You hold it there at the threshold, steady, patient, and then it gives. The ember sinks into the grain of the steel and takes, a low banked warmth spreading through the head and settling, staying when you take your hands away. The mace is warm now. It hums faintly against your palm, a held charge waiting to be spent, a channel your flame can run down when you want it to.

You turn it in the low light, the bound heat steady in it, and you understand that you have learned a real thing here — not just how to carry fire between vessels, but how to give it straight from yourself into a thing and make it keep. A dead lump of forged scrap is an ember weapon now, made and charged entirely by your own hand. You set it across your knees. The cistern is quiet, the coals low, and the way up off this floor is still waiting whenever you choose it.

You take up the finished weapon and feel the bound warmth of it steady in your hands, and you know what it is owed. The bar at the core of it carried fire for you from the bottom of this Tower; you spent that bar to make this, and the fire in it now is fire you put there yourself. So you give it the name it already had. Burning Anger. Heavier than it was, blunter, meaner — but the same anger, carried up.

Then you climb back up the stair you bled to hold, Burning Anger resting on your shoulder, to where the great grey corpse of the Sentinel slumps cold across the top of it, a mountain of dead slag wedged between you and the way up. Your tool is ready. It is time to work.

It is not a fight — the thing is dead, and dead it stays — but it is the hardest labor you have done in this place. You set your feet and you swing with everything your forged-deeper frame will give you, and the mace comes down on cold stone with a shock that jars up your arms and rings in your teeth, and a fist of slag breaks loose. Then another. The corpse is enormous and the dead slag is stubborn, grudging every piece, and there is nothing to do but hit it and hit it and hit it — head down, breath sawing, sweat stinging the half-healed burns, the heavy head of Burning Anger biting chips and chunks and slabs out of the carcass blow by blow. It takes a long time. It empties most of what the nap put back in you.

But it gives. Piece by broken piece you haul and kick the rubble clear of the stairmouth until the way up stands open at last — a dark throat of stair climbing on to whatever floor three becomes above here. The mace held: scuffed pale where it met the stone, dinged, but solid in your grip, the forge proven. And in the shattered heart of the wreck, laid bare by your work, sits the thing the Sentinel was built around — a dense dark knot of fused slag the size of two fists, the creature's drained core, cold and inert now, resting in the broken stone within your reach. The path is open, the core lies exposed, and you stand over it winded with the choice of what comes next.

You carry the dead weight of it down — the core, a fist of fused slag and dark crystal, cold all the way through, heavier than a thing that size has any right to be — back down the stair to where your brazier still burns low in its iron basket. You set it down in the coals, right in the heart of the flame, and you crouch there and watch it with more than your eyes.

For a long moment it does nothing. It sits in the fire and takes the heat the way any cold stone takes heat — dully, on the surface, and no deeper. So you reach in with a thread of your own banked ember and give it a push, a little of what you carry poured down into the dead thing to wake it.

And it WAKES. The instant your essence touches it the core stops being a stone in a fire and becomes a mouth. It drinks. Not just the thread you fed it — once it has the taste it reaches past you into the brazier itself and pulls, drawing the loose heat of the flame down into its body and keeping it, and deep in the fused dark the old cracks come up red and steady, filling, banking, a low ember light that was not there a breath before. You know it then the way you know your own pulse: a vessel made to hold fire. Feed it and it fills. It keeps what it takes. It does not give any of it back until something reaches in and draws it out.

You leave the core to its filling — it will keep, banking heat in the coals while you work — and climb back up the stair to the third floor, the mace heavy in your fist, to walk the whole of it slow and certain this time: every corner, every shadow, before you trust it at your back.

Nothing waits for you. The floor is dead and still and yours — no glow, no grind of stone, no breath in it but your own. And walking it slow you read it for what it is in a way you could not while the guardian held the stair: this whole floor was built around the furnace and the thing that kept it, shaped end to end for the making and the breaking of metal, every line of it bent toward the great cold hulk you emptied and the open stair beyond. Whatever the Tower made on this floor, it made with fire.

The way up stands open at the stairmouth, dark and waiting. Below you the core fills in the brazier's coals. The floor is clear, the mace is in your hand, and the next climb is still ahead.

You go back over the third floor a second time, and this time you are not looking for what might kill you — you are looking with a smith's eye, for what the place was made to do and what it might still give you. And it gives you its answer fast: this was a forge, and a good one once. You find the bones of it everywhere now that you know to look — the footings of a great anvil, a rack that held tools, the dark stains of old heat on the stone, shapes in the rubble that were hammers and tongs and gauges before the Tower's long rot got to them.

Got to them and won. The anvil is cracked clean through and half-sunk into the floor, no use to anyone. The hammers are rust-welded to formless lumps; the tongs come apart in your fingers when you lift them, red dust and nothing. Whatever skill built this place, time has eaten the most of it down to stains and stubs.

But not all. Digging through the wreck of the tool-rack you turn up one piece the rot only bit halfway: an iron hammer-head, its haft long gone, the face pitted and rough — but heavy, and when you knock it against the stone it rings instead of crumbling. The core of it is still good. It is no proper forge-hammer, and it is no whole tool yet, missing the haft to swing it by. But it is honest iron with weight behind it, and it is a great deal more than a river-stone in the fist. You knock the worst of the rust off it and you keep it.

You take yourself back down to the brazier by the water and you stop. That is enough — enough forging, enough drinking the dead, enough of the climb for one long day. You bank the fire down to a steady low burn, you set your back to the warm iron of the basket with the water at your side and the dead floor quiet above you, and for the first time since you cannot remember when you let go of all of it and sleep.

It is real sleep, deep and unbroken — nothing comes down the open stair, nothing stirs on the cleared floor, just the small sounds of the coals and the dark. And when you wake you wake whole. The ache is gone out of your bones; the scalds and the burns that the tempering left in you have closed and faded in the night; the deep-down emptiness where your strength and your fire and your wind had all run low is filled again, brimming, every part of you topped off and ready. You feel, for once, like a man at his full and not a man running on the dregs of himself.

The first thing you do, before anything else, is reach for the core. It has sat in the heart of the brazier all night, drinking, and you crouch to it and put your fire-sense against it to ask the one question — is it full? Has it taken all it can hold?

And the answer will not come clear. You reach for the shape of it the way you read iron, the way you read the Sentinel, and this time the sense slides off — the core's glow and the brazier's heat blur together into one warmth and you cannot find the edge between the vessel and the fire that feeds it. What little you do feel reads as appetite: a low, steady pull, the thing still reaching, still drinking, as though it is nowhere near done. You come away from it with nothing certain and only that one muddled impression to go on — that the heart is still hungry, still taking, not yet at whatever its limit is. Whether that read is true or whether your own sense simply failed on it, you cannot say. It sits there in the coals, glowing, and keeps its counsel.

This time you do it slowly. You set the Furnace-heart in your lap and the rivet by your knee and Burning Anger across your thighs, you settle your back against the brazier, and you begin the long careful work of taking yourself apart and building yourself back harder — not in one reckless flood like before, but a steady controlled draw, a thread at a time, exactly as much as your flesh can drink and set without tearing.

You start with the core, and the core surprises you. You had thought it half-done, still hungry — but the moment you open it the fire comes pouring out of it full and fierce and endless, far more than your tired sense had promised you in the dark. It was at its brim all along; your reading of it had simply failed. So much the better. You take it slow and you take it all, pouring its banked heat down into your bones and your sinew and your skin, and where the reckless way scalded you the careful way SETS — the ember going in not as fire but as forging, scouring out the weakness and hardening what is left, permanent, yours.

When the core runs dry you open the rivet and drink that too, and when the rivet is dead you reach into Burning Anger and draw the heat back out of the metal you put it into, unmaking your own binding, until the mace goes cold and plain in your hands and is only a club again. And when the last of the batteries is empty you keep going — into your own held fire, the whole guardian's hoard you have carried banked beneath your skin since the day you drank the Sentinel — and you spend that too, all of it, the bomb you have been pouring itself out as forge-heat for your own flesh. You burn it down to the very floor of yourself, until there is nothing left in any vessel or in you to give, and only then do you stop.
