I gave the girl her meal and her space, and turned to Tygryth. "It seems we have accomplished at least some of our goal in the south more quickly than we anticipated," I said. "What does your dragon's heart tell you? Have we finished our work here — or is there more ahead before we turn to the north?"

He turned with me, giving her room, though one ear, one fraction of his attention, never left her. Then he went quiet, and I watched an ancient creature do something he had not been free to do in three hundred years: he listened to his own blood. His gold eyes half-closed. His head tilted, the way a man tilts his to catch a sound at the edge of hearing. When he spoke, it was certain.

"No. We are not finished." His gaze drifted past me, past the jetty, out across the wide bright water to the haze-drowned far shore. "She is a gift I did not expect, and a wound I did not know I carried until it was salved. But she is not the pull I feel. That is still there — stronger, now that I stand at the water's edge. There is blood of mine across that crossing, and it does not move the way she moves. Hers raged in that cage. The one over there is still. Asleep with its eyes open. Kept so long it has stopped fighting the keeping. The closed eye your Wren read in the stone — that is no carving's whimsy. That is a true thing, and it is mine, and it is waiting."

He looked down at the dragon-girl tearing through her feast, then back to the cages, and something hardened in him as the pieces fit. "And consider what she tells us by being here. The slavers did not catch her wild in these woods — they had her collared in my iron, caged, and poled across this water on a barge. She was being shipped." His voice dropped. "From the far shore. Which means whoever keeps the still one over there does not merely keep — they harvest. They take my scattered blood and send it downriver as cargo. She is not the south's end, monk. She is the south's evidence."

Wren had already unrolled a corner of the rubbing-cloth across a crate, charcoal out, tracing the route with a scholar's grim speed. "Look — the crossing's a funnel. Every line on the map that touches water bends through here. If they're moving kin off the far shore, this jetty's the throat they all pass through." She tapped the closed-eye sigil hard. "Take their own barge across and we arrive where they don't expect their own boat to bring trouble. But—" she glanced at the cooling bodies, then upriver and down, wary "—a barge that's overdue is a barge someone misses. Whatever's on that far shore, the clock starts the moment their poles stop coming home."

There was one of them left who could shorten the guessing. I eased the weight off the pinned hauler's spine, and he dragged in a breath — and made the mistake of looking up. His eyes landed on the cage-girl twenty feet away, black-scaled and amber-eyed and elbow-deep in what had been his crewmates, and whatever was left of his nerve went out of him in a long, shaking moan.

I lifted my foot just enough to clear his ears so he could hear me, and I spoke to him plainly. "Now for you. I have no need for words from the likes of you. As far as I am concerned, your life ended the day you joined those who hold chains, and I am speaking to a corpse deluded enough to think it still draws breath. One way or another, we will fix that shortly. But out of respect for my friends here, I will make you an offer. If you tell Miss Wren everything she wishes to know, I will give you the mercy of ending you myself, before I hand you over to the one you held in a cage. What say you?"

"I'll talk — gods, I'll talk, just — not her, don't give me to her—"

Wren crouched in front of him, charcoal and cloth set aside, her scale-flecked face gone cold and scholarly. She was very good at it. She asked short questions and let the silences do the work, and it came out in pieces, and the pieces fit. The far shore was what the crews called the Stillwater — an old drowned sanctuary, half-sunk, that the chain-makers had taken for their own years back. The same chain-makers who had collared Tygryth. This crossing was no lucky find; it was a supply line, and the hauler and his dead friends had been only the river-leg of it. "We don't catch 'em," he babbled. "Rangers do that — inland, the wild ones, the ones with the scales. We just pole 'em downriver to market once they're collared and broke. That's all I do, I swear it, I just pole the boat—"

Wren's voice could have cut glass. "The collars. Where does the iron come from?"

He went grey, because here was the thing they did not say aloud. "The Well." He would not look at the far shore. "There's one they keep. Down in the Stillwater, under the old eye-mark. Been there longer than any of us — old, big, quiet. Don't fight no more, don't hardly move. The Warden bleeds it. That's where the iron gets its bite, see — they quench the collars in dragon's blood, that's why the chains hold what they hold. The Well's the wellspring. They'd never kill it. It's worth more bled slow than dead."

I felt Tygryth go to stone behind me. The still one. His blood. Kept alive three centuries to be drained drop by drop into the very chains they put on the rest of his line.

Wren held her voice flat by sheer will and drove for the part that mattered. "We're taking your barge across. You're going to tell me how to dock without a fight. How many. What signal."

He gave it all up. The barge ran the crossing at dusk — always dusk, never full dark, so the dock-watch could see the hull. On approach you hung a hooded blue lantern off the bow-hook; it was stowed under the steering-bench, he said, his voice cracking. The dock answered with two flashes; you answered with one, then shipped the poles. Cargo stayed covered until the Warden's man had tallied it — nobody uncovered cargo on the water, it was how the wild ones were kept calm. The dock itself was lightly held: three, maybe four, because who attacks a harvest from its own river? But inland, up in the drowned shrine, there was the Warden, a handful of rangers between hunts, and the cage-hands. "A dozen, fifteen, more when a hunt's in," he whispered. "But the dock — the dock's just us. Was just us."

Wren sat back on her heels, pale, the route suddenly real on the cloth before her — the funnel, the dusk crossing, the blue lantern, the closed eye, the Well beneath it. "It's enough," she said quietly, to Tygryth and me both. "We can wear their own skin right up to the dock. After that it's a knife-fight in a sunken temple against people who make chains for a living." She swallowed. "To free something that's forgotten it wants to be free."

He had talked, and he had kept his half. The offer had been simple: he talks, and I end him clean before the dragon-girl can. So I kept mine. I squatted and braced my hands on either side of his thick neck and twisted. He was stronger than most, but the correct application of force is enough, and his spine went. He died without pain, as I had promised — which was more than he deserved. "More than he deserved," I murmured, and rose, and wiped my hands on my ruined tunic, and that was the end of him.

For a moment no one spoke. The river filled the silence.

Tygryth watched the body settle, and watched me, and something moved in those molten eyes that was older and heavier than the fight that had come before. When he spoke it was low, and it was only for me. "You gave your word to a dead man." Not a question. "A creature with nothing left to offer you, nothing to threaten you with, no one to hold you to it — and you kept it to the letter anyway. Talk, and a clean death before the worse one. He talked. You gave it clean." He was quiet a beat. "Three hundred years I was bled on other men's promises. We will free you. We will not hurt the others. Tell us and it ends. Every one of them a lie, paid out in iron." His gaze did not leave me. "You did not have to keep that one, monk. No one living would have known. I would not have known. That is precisely why it matters that you did." A slow breath. "Mercy is a chain you wear by choice. Today I watched you choose it with your eyes open and your hands steady — and that is a different thing entirely from the killing choosing for you. I think I begin to trust the man at the other end of it."

Wren said nothing for a while. She rolled the rubbing-cloth, tied off the kin-fire vessel at her hip, and only as she straightened did she let it out, quiet. "My great-great-grandmother lit a fire with her own blood, so her line would have something warm to come home to. These people light iron with it." Her jaw set. "Let's go put their lantern on their boat, and sail it down their own throat."

Behind me the dragon-daughter had eaten her fill; the bank was quiet now but for the water. The barge waited at the jetty, broad and patient, the hooded blue lantern stowed beneath its bench. Dusk was hours off yet — time to bind my wounds, time for the freed one to find her feet, time to become, for one crossing, the very thing we had been killing. The closed eye waited across the bright water, sleeping and watching and kept. And we turned, together now and one larger than we had been at dawn, toward the boat.
