# The Cistern

You don't try it up here, not with the armored thing across the water. You go back down — careful on the stair, into the cleared chamber where the ash still lies in its heap and nothing moves. Safe as anywhere gets. You sit against the cold wall and take the cinder out.

It's the System's word that won't leave you alone: essence. Not rock, not ember. Essence — like there's something inside it meant to go somewhere. You close your hand around it, shut your eyes, and try to feel that something, to picture the cinder packed full of banked fire. Then you pull, the way you'd draw a breath, in through your palm and up your arm into your chest.

For a moment, nothing. Then it catches, and it's nothing like a breath. It's a wire dragged through you. Heat floods up your arm faster than you can think — too much — and your whole body locks around it. Your palm scalds. Something behind your eyes wrings tight and grey. You nearly drop it, and don't. You haul what you can into the center of yourself and hold it there until it stops fighting you.

When you open your eyes the cinder is dead. Grey, cold, light in your hand, the orange gone out of it for good. Your palm is burned and your head rings hollow, like you've lost two nights of sleep in one minute. But under the hurt there's something else, low and steady: the cold of the room has an edge you can feel now, and somewhere off through the stone you can sense banked warmth, faint, that you couldn't have found a minute ago. You don't know exactly what you did. You're certain you did something.

You get your feet under you and pick the iron bar back up. Just cold rust in your hands — but you're not the same as you were an hour ago, and you want to know what that means.

You close your eyes. The warmth is there when you reach for it, low in your chest, banked like a coal under ash — the thing you dragged in out of the cinder. You take hold of it the way you'd take a slow breath, and this time you push instead of pull: down your arms, into your hands, out along the cold iron toward the far end of the bar.

It fights you, then it goes. Heat crawls down the rod ahead of your attention. At the tip it gathers and catches — the rusted iron warms, darkens, then wakes to a dull ember-orange, a coal-glow you can feel on your face from a foot away. It isn't fire. It doesn't roar or throw light across the room. It's a low, concentrated heat held at one point of the metal, and holding it there costs you — a steady drain behind your eyes, like keeping your arm out straight with a weight in your hand. You can feel that if you stopped paying attention it would gutter and go cold.

But it's real, it's yours, and you did it on purpose. The bar in your hands is no longer just a heavy length of rust. Its tip burns.

You stop a step below the top of the stair, in the dark, and give the bar everything you've got. On the walkway you are, for this breath, out of its reach.

It has gone down off the center of its platform and lies half in the shallows, fire working through the seam in its neck, one arm beating at the stone in a slow ruined rhythm. It is not dead. You let the heat go out of the bar; the glow at the tip dims to nothing and the iron is just iron again, ticking faintly as it cools. You set it down beside you and lie back on the stone.

Then you do the thing you know how to do. You start at your feet and work up — clench, hold, release; calves, thighs, the long muscles of your back, your shoulders, your jaw — tensing each one hard and letting it fall slack, chasing the shake out of your body a piece at a time. The adrenaline has nowhere to go but out. You slow your breath and tie it to your pulse: four beats drawing in, eight beats letting go, the exhale always longer than the inhale, until your heart stops slamming and settles into something you can count without trying.

The cold stone helps. The shake leaves your hands. The wet-dread, the calf-grip, the burning thing in the dark — all of it loosens and drains off into the floor, and what's left is just you, breathing, steady, the way you are when you're all the way down and back in yourself.

One thing does not let go: a deep ache low in your chest and down your arm, banked and dull, where you pulled the fire into yourself a while ago. Rest doesn't touch it. It sits there like a coal under the skin, patient.

After a while you sit up. You feel clear — clearer than you've been since you woke at the bottom of this place. And you turn it over: the slow grinding thing that couldn't make its own turn, and how it came apart once you got behind it and gave it fire; the water that wasn't water, the cold mouths, the way they would not follow you up onto dry stone. You sit with it, and let it become something you know.

You climb back up the spiral stair, and this time you go slow. You keep to the dry stone of the broken walkway, you watch the black water the whole way, and you stop a full stride back from where it laps the edge. No closer. Not again.

Across the flood, on the submerged platform, the armored thing has not moved — because it cannot. It is a ruin. The fire took it: the husk has burned down to a collapsed shell of cracked, smoke-blacked plate, slumped half into the shallows, the dry rot inside gone to char and ash. A thread of smoke still lifts off the neck-seam where you put the bar in. It is finished.

Something in the air registers the fact a half-beat after you do.
