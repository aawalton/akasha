The ring comes off your belt, and the two of you go at the house methodically — every locked thing, every key, in order.

"Right — five keys, five locks, a morning to map them. This is my favourite kind of task, if you must know: a finite set, a clear answer at the end, and good company to do it in."

The gate you know. The second-newest key finds its answer fast: it turns the great front door's lock — an entirely ordinary lock. "Which means the door that drew its own bolt for you the night you came had a perfectly mundane lock behind the magic — the house chose to work it itself that night; it needn't have. One mystery honestly retired. I'll take a plain answer where I can get one; there are few enough in this place."

The fourth key is the opposite: it fits nothing. You try every locked thing left in the house, twice, and no lock will take it. "Which tells me something plain: its lock isn't here. Somewhere in this vale — or past it — is a door this key was cut for, and we've not found it. I'll note it, and we'll keep our eyes open."

But the middle key earns the morning. It turns a weathered door on the south side neither of you had tried — and opens on a walled garden.

She steps through and stops.

"...Oh, now. Look at this. A walled garden. Overgrown to the eyebrows — but it's alive, Alan. Those are old fruit trees gone feral, and look, the beds have held their shape under all that weed; someone laid these lines with real care and the ground's remembered them. And a glasshouse, most of its panes still in." She turns in the green light, and something in her opens along with the door. "And do you know what's remarkable? It's the first thing in this whole house the house can't do for us. It's kept it breathing all these years — but it can't prune a tree or turn a bed or set a pane back. This one wants hands. Ours." Real warmth now. "That goes straight to the top of my steward's list — and I'll confess it's not for the estate's sake I want it. A garden's the one kind of keeping where you get to watch the thing you tend actually grow back. I've kept a great many things in my life. I've not often got to keep something that blooms."

Which leaves the eldest key — seamless, toolmark-less, of no placeable technique — and the iron-banded door on the cellar stair.

It slides in perfectly. First lock it has fit all morning; it goes home like it was cut for this one and no other. She goes very still.

"...It fits. Look —" She turns it. Nothing. Not jammed — you can both feel it — not fighting her hand at all. "And it won't turn. Not stuck. It's waiting. Like a lock that knows its key perfectly well and has simply decided the hour isn't yet." Quiet, at the far edge of what she'll say aloud: "The house has opened everything for us this morning, gladly, unasked. And it's gone completely silent about this one door — and handed us the very key that fits it, and won't let it turn. I don't know what it means. I know it isn't 'no' — a 'no' doesn't fit the key so beautifully. It's 'not yet.' So we'll wait for the yet. Whatever's behind this door, the house means us to open it — in its own time, not ours."

And as you come up from the cellar stair, the hall window gives you noon: a neat carriage, Compact-crisp, turning up the hill from Amberford.

The steward snaps to — brisk, warm, complete.

"—And there's noon, punctual as a tax always is. That's your Compact, come up the hill in a very tidy carriage to have a very tidy look at you. Right. Keys down, steward on. The whole of it in one line: you're gracious and you say little; you agree the title should be sorted and tell them it already is being; you sign nothing. I do the talking about paper, and I do it in your name — that's what you gave me this morning, and this is what it's for." A small, certain smile. "You've a walled garden to prune, a house that adores you, and a steward who's sat across tables from harder people than these. Let them come. Ready?"
