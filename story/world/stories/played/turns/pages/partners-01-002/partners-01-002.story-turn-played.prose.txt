You don't run. You don't shout at the sky, and you don't waste breath demanding answers from a road that has already made its position clear. You find the flat stone where the road forks, sit down, lay everything you own out in the last of the light, and do the thing your hands know how to do.

The kit first, because method matters. Clothes: yours, honest wear, nothing altered. Boots: yours, broken in at exactly the places you broke them in. The knife is decent working steel, sharpened by someone who uses it rather than admires it — you recognize the someone. Journal, pen, tinderbox: dry, ordinary, intact. Whatever the crossing did, it did not touch a single thing you carried. You sit with that a moment. It is the first fact tonight that feels like solid ground, and you appraise it accordingly: worth more than the knife.

The coin is another matter.

It is heavy the way precious metal is heavy, but the color and the ring of it against the stone match nothing you can name — not gold, not silver, not any alloy your eye has a shelf for, and your eye has a lot of shelves. The edge is unworn. The face is unscuffed. This coin has never been spent, never lived in a purse, never passed through a market in its existence. And the warmth is constant — it doesn't fade in the evening air, doesn't bloom against your skin. It holds, the way a held hand holds.

The inscription is where your craft fails you, and it does not fail the way it should. Foreign script blurs. Worn stamps guess at themselves. This does neither. It refuses — steadily, evenly, the way a closed door refuses. In all the years you have been reading objects, your appraisal has never once come back empty. It comes back now with something you have no drawer for: declined. The coin does not consent to be read.

Yet, your professional judgment adds, unprompted.

The key-ring, by contrast, opens to you like a ledger.

Five keys. Five distinctly different ages — you'd stake your name on it. Five generations of lockmaking on one iron ring, which means the house these open acquired its locks across a very long time. The newest is unremarkable. The oldest is the finest object you have ever held, by an order of magnitude: no toolmarks, no seams, no evidence it was ever cut at all — as if someone drew it whole out of somewhere metal comes from. You cannot place the technique. There isn't one.

And the ring is tended. No rust. Faintly oiled. Bright at the shoulders of the keys where fingers live, unworn at the bites where locks do. Often handled, rarely turned. Someone has counted these keys, faithfully, for years, without opening much of anything.

The brass tag is far newer than any key it labels, stamped in a careful, clerkish hand: HEARTHHOLT.

The light is nearly gone now. Behind you, back up the rise, one window is still lit — the window that lit itself. Ahead, the road forks, and the east fork is the one that no longer goes back.

You are calm. You are holding five keys someone kept faithfully, a coin that declined you politely, and a name in brass.
