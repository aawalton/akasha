You go back to the throat of the shaft and this time you do not stop at the lip. At the first landing you find what your scouting promised: a bracket-lantern and a flask of old oil, sealed. You fill the lantern and you do not bother with flint — you set a fingertip of your own ember to the wick and it catches at once, and for the first time the lower shaft has light that is not the far grey seam. The dark pulls back off the broken flights and the rising slabs, and the whole murderous geometry of the place lays itself out for you to read.

So you climb. The flights are bolted to the wall, solid where they hold and simply gone where they don't, and you take the gaps the way your legs have always wanted to take everything — a long step here, a driving leap there, your lower body doing what it is built for while the maul rides glowing on your back. Where the stairs give out the slabs begin, rising and sinking on the slow patient beat you watched from the lip days ago, and now that you can SEE it and you already know its rhythm in your bones, the timing is almost easy: you wait, you read the lift, you go — slab to slab, up through the moving dark, the long fall breathing cold under every jump and never once getting you. You are, it turns out, very good at this. You climb until the wall-stair is far below and a narrow stone ledge snags out of the dark ahead, and you swing onto it to breathe.

You are not the first to rest here. A man is on the ledge with you — or was; he has been dead a long time, dried and still, his back to the cold stone and a burnt-out hooded lantern gone cold in his lap. You take the lantern. It is a better tool than your open flame for what is above — it throws its light one way and keeps the glare out of your own eyes — and you wake it with a touch of ember and hood it down to a directed beam. He has no more use for it, the way the stalker had no more use for its hide.

Scratched into the wall by his shoulder, in a hand that goes ragged and then frantic toward the end, is a message:

  "To whoever climbs after — the stones do not move on their own. The thing at the top moves them. It sits in the chain and it sleeps, and the slabs are only its breathing; come near the grey light and it wakes, and there is nowhere up there to stand that is not inside its reach. I could not get past it. Three days on this ledge now and I am done climbing. If you mean to try — the weight is the whole of it, the chain and the drum it turns on and the catch that holds the—"

and there it stops, unfinished, the last stroke trailing off the stone.

You look up. Far above, past more rungs of rising slab, the grey seam of the exit is brighter now — and below it, where the moving stones seem to dock against something, the dark gathers into a mass too square and too still to be more shaft. You cannot make out what it is from here. But you know now that it is there, and that it is waiting, and that it wakes to the climb. The lantern burns steady in your fist. The slabs go up and down their slow beat. The ledge holds you, for the moment, in the middle of the dark.
