You look at her carefully before you say anything at all — the long, open read you'd give a piece you intended to be honest about.

Your eye gives you this: wool chosen for quality and worn without a thought for show; boots that have done real miles and been kept well; a lantern trimmed properly; hands that work. The basket on her hip was packed with intent, not haste. And every surface of her agrees with every other surface — nothing performed, nothing held at an angle. You have appraised a great many things that were exactly what they appeared to be, and they were never once people.

"I'm Alan," you say — and the honest thing walks out ahead of your manners, the second time tonight it has done so: "and I'm very confused. Where am I? What is this house? I woke on the road a while ago, and I can't quite recall how I got there."

She takes your name the way you'd catch something tossed gently — no fumble, no ceremony.

"Alan. Good — a man who still has his own name on him isn't half as lost as he feels. I'm Amy. You can be confused and safe in the same breath; you're managing both rather well from where I'm standing."

She shifts the basket, unhurried, and gives you the ground first.

"Where you are: the Hearthlands — the green vale at the edge of the settled country. Those lights down the valley are Amberford, the market town; that's where I'm up from. The road you woke on runs up to here, the last hill before the wood."

Her eyes go past you, up the dark face of the manor to the one lit window.

"This house is Hearthholt. It's stood dark and shut longer than anyone alive can remember — three generations without smoke from its chimneys, half a ghost story in town. Which is the part that matters tonight, because it isn't dark now."

And then, to the question you actually asked — the one with no good answer in it — she gives you the honest half instead of a pretty whole.

"How you got here I can't tell you, and I'll not pretend to. But I can tell you why I did. That ring in your hand — it lived in a locked drawer in my kitchen. I keep this house's dull affairs, have for years. At dusk the drawer stood open and empty, and I never touched it. A drawer doesn't unlock itself for nothing, so I packed a supper and climbed, to meet whoever it went and found."

A small nod at the iron ring, loose in your fingers.

"That's the question you've been holding but haven't asked. You can ask it."

The two moons stand over the vale. The lit window burns.
