"Tygryth. Focus." I pitched it steady, up through the grate, the way you set a hand on a shoulder. "What does he need? What does it take to heal a dragon?"

The words landed on him like a hand on a shoulder. For a heartbeat the clawing at the grate did not stop — and then it did. I watched an ancient creature do, under a steady voice, exactly what his feral great-grandkin had done under one hours before on the riverbank: he grabbed the task I'd handed him and used it as a handhold to climb out of the pit. The ragged breathing evened. The grief did not leave — it went somewhere, got harnessed, became useful. He pressed his brow once to the cold bars, and when he spoke the wreck in his voice had hardened into something that could work.

"Yes. A task. Give an old soldier a task." A breath. "Listen, then, and I will tell you both the easy part and the part that will take the rest of our lives. The easy part is the iron. Every collar, every lancet, every spigot they have sunk into him comes out, tonight, all of it. That metal is not just bleeding him — it is quenched in his own kind's blood to suppress what he is. While it is in him he cannot heal, cannot hold his own fire, cannot so much as warm himself. Strike it off. The deep taps will bleed when they come free; keep the kin-fire on each wound as you draw it. The flame is his line's, and it will close what it touches. It is medicine, not just light. Then we get him out of this drowned grave before the wider net feels the Stillwater go dark and comes asking why. And then we feed him — our kind lives on magic far more than meat, and he has been bled of it for three centuries, starved down to embers. He must take it back slowly, a little at a time, or it will burn a body too weak to hold it. Iron off, fire on, out of here, fed slow. That is the body." A pause. "The body, I can mend."

His great head turned, down through the oculus, to the vast ashen shape breathing shallow in the blue firelight, and his voice dropped. "The body is the easy part, monk. You heard the dead man's taunt, and the dead man was not lying. He stopped wanting out a hundred years ago. You cannot strike that off with a hammer. You cannot pour it out of a flask. I spent three hundred years in a lighter version of the room he is in — and even I had my rage to keep me company, my hate, something that still wanted. He let go of even that. He went still. That is the deepest chain in this place, and no blade we own will cut it. It takes time. It takes kin — the line gathered close around him, refusing to let him sink back down into the quiet nothing, wanting him back into wanting, day after day, for as long as it takes. A father cannot be the whole of a reason to live; he is too close to the wound. He will need more than me." He looked at me, and at Wren below in the dark water. "It is the reason I will need all of you."

I set the shape of the work to each of them. "Got it. My lady — gather the leftovers outside for a dragon's meal. He needs magic, but meat will be a start. Wren, hold the flame to close the wounds as I draw the iron. Tygryth, you are the only one with the strength to move him and to clear a path — break whatever stands in our way, but try not to bury us in the process. We'll get through this together."

The room became a thing with one purpose. The black-scaled girl went up and out and began hauling the dead down into the dark — she who had torn into her captors on a riverbank that morning now dragging meat to a starved elder of her own blood, and there was a rightness to it she did not name and did not need to. Wren took her place at my shoulder with the kin-fire cradled, ready. And down the spiral ramp, footfalls quickening, came Tygryth — too vast in dragon-shape to take the descent, so he folded, the great dark length collapsing down and down into the dusk-skinned man, smaller, faster, able to run.

He reached the crypt floor, and he stopped.

For the first time in three hundred years he stood before his own blood with no bars between them, and the proud ancient creature simply broke, quietly, all the way. He waded into the black water in his man-shape, small beside the vast ashen length of the chained dragon, and reached up and laid one trembling human hand against the great drained jaw, and his voice when it came was nothing a god was ever written to sound like. "...I lost you the same night I lost everything," he whispered. "Your mother lit the fire so you would always have a way home, and they came before the embers cooled, and they took your sister, and they took you, and they put me in the dark too far away to hear you. Three hundred years I thought they had killed you. I mourned you. And you were here, the whole time, being bled, and I never—" He could not finish. He pressed his brow to his son's jaw, and the rest was just breath.

And the filmed eye — that exhausted patient nothing — moved. Slowly, as if dragging itself up from the bottom of a deep well, it focused on the small weeping shape at his jaw. The ember in it guttered, fought, and caught on something older than a century of stillness: a scent, a shape, the deepest and oldest memory a child keeps. The vast head shifted a fraction into the touch, and the rusted voice cracked out one word, disbelieving, barely a sound at all. "...da?"

Tygryth made a noise like his heart was being unmade and remade at once. "Yes. Yes. I am here. I am late, gods forgive me, but I am here, and I am not leaving you in this place another breath. Hold on to me. Let us get this iron off you."

So we began. The great throat-collar first — the worst of it, where the cruelest taps were sunk. I found the ancient bolt-seam with my hands and my sense both, and struck, and pried, and where the iron was fused beyond my strength Tygryth set his hands beside mine and pulled, a father's three hundred years of grief turned to leverage — and with a shriek of tortured metal the great collar split and came away. It hit the black water with a vast iron crash, three centuries of dragon-iron sinking into the dark where it could never sit on his throat again. The freed taps bled, and Wren was already there, the blue kin-fire flowing over each wound, sealing what it touched, his own line's flame closing his own line's hurts.

And the still one — for the first time in longer than any of us had been alive — took a breath with nothing around his throat. I heard it go all the way down, deep and shuddering and whole, a breath a body had forgotten it was allowed. The ember in his eye steadied. It did not blaze. It just held, and decided, for now, to keep burning. "...lighter," he rasped, wondering, as the weight left his neck. "...it's lighter."

"It will get lighter still," Tygryth said, fierce and wet. "Every piece of it. Tonight."

And the work settled into a rhythm, and the worst of the night was behind us. The black-scaled girl banked the slavers' bodies near him — a grim first meal for a starved god, meat where there should be magic, but a start, and his great head turned toward it with the faintest stirring of an appetite three hundred years could not quite kill. Tygryth lent his strength to each fused tap. Wren followed with the fire. I drew the iron out, piece by piece, from a body that was, slowly, remembering it was a body and not a well. The keeper was ash. The bleeding was stopped. The first collar was off. And in the steady blue kin-fire light, a scattered line gathered close around the blood they had crossed black water to find — a long road of healing ahead of them, and the very first mile of it, against everything, begun.
