Dusk, and a road you have never stood on before.

There is no seam to the crossing — no flash, no falling. One stride ago there was somewhere else, and now there is this: a packed-earth road running the spine of a green valley, hills folding away on either side under a sky going violet at the edges. The air is cool and smells of rain that fell an hour ago on someone else. Two moons are rising, one low and gold, one high and thin as a paring of silver.

You stop. You take stock, because that is what you do.

Your clothes are your own. Your boots are your own, and dry. In your left pocket: a coin you have never seen, heavy, warm as a held hand, stamped with writing that refuses to resolve no matter how steadily you look at it. In your right: an iron key-ring you did not put there, old and honest, five keys of five different sizes, and a worn brass tag with a word you CAN read:

HEARTHHOLT.

And then the world notices you.

It arrives like a window catching lamplight — a pane of violet glass that is not glass, hanging in the air a polite arm's length away.

It names you. Your name, then a number that feels both insulting and fair. Then your skills, one by one, each landing like a stamp in a ledger: the pattern-sense, the steady hands, the appraiser's eye. The pane records you the way a clerk records a delivery — briskly, completely, without opinion.

Then it reaches your talent, and prints one line more, in exactly the voice it printed everything else: no classification. No precedent on record.

It is you who pauses.

You read the line twice. It reads the same both times, and that is somehow the loudest thing on the pane: a ledger has just hit a blank where an entry should be, and the clerk has stamped the blank and moved on, and nothing in all that violet glass finds it worth a second look. You do. You are still looking when the pane folds itself away like a bird tucking a wing.

Across the valley, on a shoulder of hill above a river you can hear but not yet see, stands a house. Manor is the better word — stone below, dark timber above, a slate roof with too many chimneys for one household, windows dark save one. One window, high in the eastern gable, is lit. It was not lit a moment ago. You are certain of that the way you are certain of load-bearing walls.

The key-ring sits in your pocket with the specific weight of an answered question.

The road forks a stone's throw ahead. The left fork runs down the valley floor toward a far-off scatter of town lights, an hour's walk, maybe more, smoke standing up straight from chimneys into the still evening. The right fork climbs — switchbacked, hedged with something blooming white that you cannot name — to the manor's iron gate.

You are alone on the road. The evening is mild. Nothing hunts you. Nothing hurries you. The world, whatever it has decided about you, appears content to let you decide something back.

Behind you the road runs flat and empty into the dusk, and it occurs to you — a fact, filed calmly, the way you file such things — that it does not go back to where you came from. Roads here go where they go. That one goes east.

The gold moon clears the hills. Somewhere below, the river turns over in its bed. The lit window across the valley waits — patient in a way the glass pane never was.
