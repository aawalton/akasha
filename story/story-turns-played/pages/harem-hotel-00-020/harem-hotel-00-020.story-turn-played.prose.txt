You come up out of sleep all at once and whole, as you have not woken since this place took you — no ache, no ghost of the fight in your shoulder. The lamp has burned down to nothing. Aria is a warm weight along your side, awake, watching you surface with the small pleased look of a thing gone as she would have written it. You roll into her, over her, your mouth finding the line of her jaw. "Fuck for luck," you say against her skin, "before the next door?"

She laughs low in her throat, the sound of it moving through you where you are pressed against her. "Luck," she says, tasting the word as if deciding whether to let you have it. Her hand comes up into your hair, taking its time. "I have no luck to give you, you know. It is not a thing I deal in — no one does; the dice fall where they fall, and I would be lying if I told you my hands could tip them. But this, I will take gladly, and for its own sake, and not because it buys you anything at that door. Come here."

And you do. It is not the careful, astonished thing the first night was; you know each other now, the map of it, what she likes and how she likes to be made to wait for it. She sets the pace — slow where she wants you slow, her mouth curved against yours all the while like she is telling you a story and this is only the part before the good part. You lose the door. You lose the floor and the dungeon and the whole climbing weight of the place; there is only the full weight of her over you, the low sounds she does not trouble to hide, the give and grip of her, and her voice still at your ear — "more patience than that; we have all morning" — until neither of you has patience left for anything, and the morning takes you both.

After, you lie in the tangle of the linens, the risen light grey and plain across the room, the two of you gone loose and quiet. She is the first to move — of course she is. She rises and gathers herself back into the shape the day wants, silver and composed, only the smallest disorder left in her hair to admit the morning happened at all. She hands you your shirt. "No luckier than you were," she says, "but better company for the walk." You could not have looked at the far end of the hall last night without something in you flinching from it. This morning, you find you can. You dress. You take up the knife. You go to see what the second floor has grown to stop you.
