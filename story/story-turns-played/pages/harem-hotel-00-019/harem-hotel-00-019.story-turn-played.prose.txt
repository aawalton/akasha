It is stranger than the fight, the quiet that comes after. You stand on the new floor with the knife still in your hand and the wall sealed smooth where a door of dead plaster had been, and the tiredness hits you all at once. "We should stop," you say. The words come out rough. "Both of us. That was — we need a break after that." Aria does not argue. She looks at you a moment, the knowing brightness of her banked low for once, and inclines her head.

But first you look. You have learned this floor's older sibling well enough to know the shape of the place — the hall running to its dark far end, and nearer, where you stand, the doors of rooms. You try them. This is the lived-in end: a suite that is somehow yours, made up as though it had been waiting on you, and past it a smaller room lit silver-grey that must be hers. You go through both as a man goes through his pockets one more time, thorough and without much hope. A bed, a basin, a lamp, linens folded by hands you never saw. Nothing with an edge to it, nothing that answers to weapon or key. The floor keeps its useful things behind its doors, you are starting to see, and pays them out only to those who get through — and here at the top of the climb there is only the ordinary furniture of rest.

So you set the knife on the table by the bed, where the morning will find it, and you let the day come to its end. Whatever the fight took out of you, the rest gives back; you feel it as you lie down, the deep ache going quiet by degrees, the body remembering how to be whole, the floor holding you like solid ground under a man who has finally stopped falling. Aria is there in the low light, near, the warmth of her a fixed point in a place with none of its own. Neither of you reaches for more than this tonight; the fight is too close behind, and the rest of it can keep. But the two of you went into a wall of moving plaster together and came out the other side, and the thread that runs between you lies a little nearer the surface than it did this morning, close enough to feel if you turn toward it. "Sleep," she says, in the old low voice, the one that turns a plain word into a whole hand laid flat on your chest. "The dungeon will still be there in the morning. It is very good at waiting." And at the dark end of the hall, the new door waits with it.
