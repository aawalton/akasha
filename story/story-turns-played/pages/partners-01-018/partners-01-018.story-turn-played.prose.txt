Breakfast gets your full attention — her, and the food, in that order, and both reward it. The eggs are exactly as good as everything else she touches, and you say so with your attention rather than more words; the morning has had its speeches.

Then the plates are empty, and you stand, and insist.

"Absolutely not, I'll—" And she stops, because you mean it. "...Oh. You're serious. You're going to make me SIT here." She sits down slowly, the way one enters a foreign country. "Alan, I don't think I've sat in a kitchen while another soul cleaned it since I was a child — and I'm not certain I managed it gracefully then." A beat, wry, entirely unable to stand down: "The dish-cloth's on the left rail — no, the— yes, that one. And the good pan doesn't soak, it gets wiped warm or it spots—. ...I'm doing it again, aren't I. Sitting and supervising." She visibly makes herself stop, and folds her hands on the table. "There. Look at me. Relaxing. It's harrowing."

You work, and she watches you work, and somewhere in it the watching goes soft.

"...You've a rhythm at that sink, you know. Another thing you remember in your hands and not your head." Easy, unhurried. "Tell me something ordinary while you work — it steadies me the way it steadied you last night. Market's tomorrow; the whole vale comes up the road. You'll want boots that don't mind mud and a story ready for why the window's lit, because forty people will ask it. We can rehearse yours over the washing-up, if you like."

So the washing-up passes in ordinary things — the tending, you notice, running both directions now — until the kitchen is back to ready, every pan wiped warm, and you come and sit down across from her. Ready.

She reads that too, and shifts into her craft — warm, and suddenly businesslike.

"Right. Two things, and the first we've half-said: the larder. I've no answer yet and I'll not counterfeit one — I've parked it, and I'll worry at it till it gives. That one's mine to chase; leave it with me. The second has a clock on it, so we'll do it properly."

And she lays the house before you like an open ledger.

"What Hearthholt costs to keep standing — the honest version, since you'll get it from no one else. It's not a fortune; it's attention, steadily. The north range sheds roof-slates every hard winter — re-laid in spring, or you're bailing the top floor by the next. A dozen flues want sweeping before autumn, or a chimney fire at the first cold snap. Gutters, downspouts, the damp in the cellar wall, the gate-hinges, the drive after the melt. None of it dear alone; all of it certain. I've kept it ticking on modest sums for years — a dark house asks little."

A beat.

"That's the upkeep. Here's what changes now, and why you'll have company by noon: the Compact taxes the vale's holdings. Three generations, Hearthholt paid a pittance — a dormancy rate, the token you set on a ruin nobody lives in. I paid it. But a lit window and a master in residence is another line in their ledger entirely — full assessment, an occupied manor on the best hill in the vale. They'll have seen your smoke, and they'll want it revised."

She stops there — deliberately, hands quiet on the table, the next piece visibly held in reserve.

"That's the shape of the money. There's one more piece — what the Compact will actually DO about it, and it's the piece worth bracing for — but I'll stop and let you ask what you want first. Your house, your questions."
