Sitting was the easy part. Getting up and turning back to the den is the part your body argues with — but you do it, because the room still holds things you came for.

The den does not improve for being looked at straight. Low, wet, close; the reek of old bone sits in it like water in a cellar. Your maul rides dead across your back, its head heavy and burned-out — nothing to draw fire from there, not until you feed it again at a forge you do not have. What you do have is your own well, full to the brim off the rest, and the hooded lantern throwing its small steady circle at your belt. It will have to do.

You work the near ground first — the strip of floor between the stair and the drop — and you work it slow, testing each footing before you trust it. The midden gives up what middens give up. Packs gone to black slime. Boots with the feet still in them. Long bones picked clean and dressed in nothing, so many of them that you stop counting the people and count only what will burn and what will not.

What will not burn, you keep. A smith's eye runs the pile faster than grief can: a double handful of sound metal comes up out of the rot — buckles, a pitted eating-knife, a scatter of good nails, the iron clasp off a pack-frame — and you fold it away in a scrap of hide. Forge-stock, paid for by strangers.

The rest you give to the fire. You reach into your own well and push a low steady heat out through the lantern-flame into the fouled heap until it catches, and the reek turns to char and woodsmoke and climbs away into the dark overhead. As the smoke thins and the murk lifts off the floor, the den shows you the whole of itself at last.

The drop. Wide open now and sheer, a black seam torn clean across the middle of the floor. Everything you came back for is on the far side of it. The thing you killed lies where it fell, lean and grey on the wet rock. Beside it, spilled from the wreck of the seat, the clouded palm-lens catches your lantern and gives back one dull wink of grey; and the slack heap of folded light lies where it dropped when the gold went out of it, grey now and gone flat, like cloth with nothing left inside it.

All of it a hard jump away, across an edge that does not care how rested you are.

You cast your ember-sense out over the gap the way you'd throw a climbing-line — and it snags on something. Not the cold dead weight of the grey thing across the way. Something else: fainter, lower, further out, a live warmth banked down like a coal under ash, somewhere past the drop in the dark you have not burned. It does not move. You cannot name it. You only know the floor is not as empty as one dead body would make it.
