You take the winding-drum core back up off the cold stone and lash it once more to the free end of the chain -- the same rig as before, maul at one end of all that iron, dead-heavy core at the other. But you do not grip it where you gripped it last time. Last time you held the core-end and flung the great maul out wide, and it was a wild and stupid thing, enormous and slow, hauling you a half-step off your center every turn. This time you take the haft up close behind the maul-head itself, the weight of it gathered against your hands, and you send the CORE swinging out instead -- the lighter end, the far end, the weight kept in tight where you can master it.

And it answers your hands. You set your feet and begin to wheel the core-end around you, and where the wide dervish wanted to throw you, this wants to be DRIVEN: the center of it held close, the whirling weight out at the chain's end skating in a tight fast circle that you turn, and check, and turn again. Your shoulders and your hips know this work -- the strength to drive the heavy thing, the quickness to catch it and change its line -- and turn by turn the circle tightens under your control, the core humming around you on its short leash, going where you put it and coming back when you call it. Not the elegant mastery of a thing long practiced; you can feel the rough edges of it still, the catches where your timing lags the weight. But it is CONTROL. Real, usable control. The wild thing has a handle now, and the handle is in your fist.

And there is more in it than your arms. As the core wheels around you, the new sense -- the foreign one, the one that woke on this very platform when you reached into the iron and the force poured through you -- opens quietly alongside the work, and you FEEL the thing inside the drum ride the spin. The sealed tension in it is not dead weight; it leans into the turn, wound and wanting, a stored push you can feel surge and slacken as the circle comes round -- and feeling it, you time against it, let the whip run with the force's own wanting instead of fighting it blind. It steadies the swing. A small thing, a partial thing, but real: the iron is easier to drive when you can feel which way it wishes to go.

You slow the core to a stop at last, breathing hard, the chain going slack and the weight settling, your legs burning pleasantly with the work. You have what you came for -- a flail you can actually wield, and a sense that helps you wield it. But standing there in the quiet with the dead core swinging gently at the chain's end, you cannot shake the feeling that you have only found the shallow edge of something. You have learned to FEEL the force and to ride it, to let it steady what your hands already do. And some deeper part of you, the part that learns things in the body before the mind has words for them, turns toward a thought it cannot yet finish: that to merely feel this force and lean on it is the smallest use of it -- that a man who could DIRECT it, bend it, spend it the way you spend your fire, would do far more here than steady a swing. You do not know how. Not yet. But you know now that there is a how, and that you have just brushed the near wall of it.
