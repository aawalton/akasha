"Yes, yes, always yes. Again, you heard what I didn't say and understood. Co-founder of this house, tender of the web, all of it — yes. And a chance tonight to get to know another who the house has brought to us. Someone we both may come to love. Yes."

She lets out a breath she didn't seem to know she'd been holding, and the composure softens into plain warmth.

"...Yes, yes, always yes. Listen to us." A near-laugh. "Do you know you just did it back to me — 'you heard what I didn't say.' Twice today you've told me I read you well, and both times you were reading me just as close while you said it. That's the thing about a web, Alan: the keeper doesn't stand outside it pulling strings. She's in it — read as much as reading. I forgot that for a moment. You just reminded me."

And then the last thing you said catches up to her, and you watch it arrive.

"And that last thing — 'someone we both may come to love.' Do you feel the size of what you just handed me?" The keeper's wryness, bright for a moment: "An hour ago I signed on to tend a web. Half an hour ago I made it a family with a cornerstone. And now the keeper's allowed to love the people she keeps — the job's grown three times in one afternoon, and God help me, I've liked it more each time." Then quieter — the true thing under it, said plainly, the way you do things now. "But here's the part that actually reached me, so I'll say it plainly. I have loved a great many people from the outside of the room — tending them, minding them, never quite counted among them. The keeper who's never herself kept. And you've just told me that in this house I don't have to love from the doorway. That I'm allowed all the way in — that my heart's a member here, not only my hands." A steady look. "You healed a thing in me just now you couldn't have known was there. That's twice we've done that to each other today — and it isn't even dusk."

She rises then — the steward waking, all that warmth resettling into purpose.

"Right — and now I'll do the least romantic and most important thing a woman can do an hour after a declaration like this one: I'm going to go and cook. We've a guest at dusk, and a house making its first attempt at hosting anyone in three generations, and I'll not have it fumble the introduction." At the doorway she turns, warm, leaving you the hours. "You do as you please till then — help me, wander your house, sleep off the morning. It's all yours, and so am I, and neither's going anywhere. But when that lamp goes in the gate tonight, we meet whoever comes exactly as we are now: a Master and his tender of the web, side by side, table laid, nothing to prove." A small, certain smile. "Let's give the house a night worth the three hundred years it waited for it."

And she's away down the corridor, and within minutes Hearthholt begins to sound like a house with an evening coming: the far-off ring of the good pans, a door standing open to the kitchen garden, water running, something struck up half-hummed and abandoned and struck up again. The light through the tall windows has gone from white to gold and started its long lean toward the hills. Down at the gate, the lamp waits unlit for dusk.

The afternoon is yours.
