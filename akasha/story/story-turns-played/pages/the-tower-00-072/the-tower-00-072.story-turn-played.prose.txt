You don't go in. You stay on the threshold-stone and you make the maul into a longer arm.

The Warden's chain — the heavy length you struck free of the headworks two floors down — you lay one end against the butt of Burning Anger and call the heat up out of yourself to weld it, because the maul's own banked core still runs low from the climb through the dark and won't spare the fire. It costs you a little, drawing the weld-heat off your own kindled ember instead; but the join takes white and clean and cools hard, and now you have a maul on a chain — a head you can swing wide, and throw, and haul back. Routine work. Your hands know it.

Then you put it to use. You set your boots on the stone, keep your weight rocked back over your heels and behind the line — never once letting the work tip you forward across it — and you swing the chained maul around in a circle and hurl it into the gold.

And the gold hands you the lie, plainly, in the weight of the chain. The maul sails through the long laden table and finds nothing — passes through the steaming feast like a stone through smoke, no purchase, no catch, the dishes and the bread and the bright-poured wine parting around an iron head that does not feel them at all. It crashes on through the welcoming host at the table's head, and the host does not flinch, does not turn, does not so much as ripple: it stands with its kind hands open while a war-maul passes clean through where its chest should be, because there is no chest, because it is a painting hung in the air. You haul the chain back and cast again. Again it finds nothing. Again. The feast is light pretending to be supper, and your iron tells you so every time it swings through and feels only cold air.

It takes you many casts — the head skating off the bright lie, sailing through where solid things ought to be — before it finally bites on something that bites back. Something real, low and to the near side, under the gilded suggestion of the floor. You set your back against the chain with your boots still planted on the stone and you drag it out to you, across the threshold-line — and where it crosses into your own true sight the false gold can't follow it, sloughs away, and leaves lying plain on the cold stone at your feet what was always there: bone. Human bone, old and grey, a tangle of some climber who came up these same stairs before you and was welcomed in. The haven had it dressed in gold and called it a chair. You drag out more — a snarl of ribs, a long cracked thighbone — and snagged among them by a lucky catch of the hook, a snapped length of cold iron: a broken blade, pitted, a dead man's sword sheared off at a third of its reach. Real metal. You set it aside.

And while you work, the room answers you — not with a hand, not with a sound, but by trying harder. The welcome WARMS. The gold deepens and sweetens where you've torn the bones out of it, re-pouring itself over the gap, re-laying the feast, re-gilding chairs that were bones that are at your feet now and gone from the floor. The host's open hands lift a little higher, a little more insistent; the whole patient kindness of the place leans toward you over its bare board, as if to say, gently, that there is no need for any of this — that you could simply come in, and sit. The harder you pull the truth out of it, the harder it pulls to bring you in.

You stand on the threshold-stone with the chained maul recovered in your fists, a heap of cold grey bones and a broken blade at your boots, and behind them the haven glows warmer and kinder and more golden than it has yet — lying to you with everything it has. The way down behind you is still open.
