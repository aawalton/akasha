You look at what the pane is holding open, and you already know the answer — you have known it since the hallway, maybe, without the word for it.

Of everything she is, you could take her read on a room, or the tempo she keeps in her two hands — the slowing that has undone you all night. But neither has held you longest. It is the plain force of her: the fact of the whole strange night bending itself around her without her seeming to ask it to. That. You reach for it, and you tell the pane so.

She is watching your face while you do it. She cannot see your letters, but she can read the moment you decide — she told you so, in the dark, not an hour ago. Then her own pane pulls her eyes back to itself, and it is her turn.

You watch her read it. Whatever it lays in front of her, she takes her time with, as she does with everything; and then her gaze lifts off the letters and settles on you, and there is something careful in it, something already choosing.

"I know what I want of you," she says. Low, and sure. "Not the cleverness — I have cleverness of my own, and yours would only crowd mine." Her thumb moves at your jaw. "The other thing. The part of you that held its ground in the hallway and handed me the plain truth, warm, and did not dress it up or take it back when I gave you every opening to. The part that keeps a thing true when it would be easier not to." Her mouth softens. "That. Weave a little of that into me. God knows I could stand to carry some."

She says it to you and to the letters at once, and you feel — not see, feel — the answer take.

For a moment nothing in the room has changed. Her weight is still loose over you, the lamp still low, the silver still bright along her throat. And under all of it now, a thread — you could not say where it runs or how, only that it is there, drawn taut and live between the two of you, and that when you turn your attention along it you can feel the far end of it living in her.

She feels it too. Whatever moves through her, she lets it reach her face and stay there — she does not reshape it into something lovelier, though you can see the old habit want to. "That is going to take some getting used to," she says, unsteady.

Then, past her shoulder, in the dark at the far end of the made room, the letters change — not the ledger-hand now, but something hung like a direction, pale words pointed toward the door you have not opened. The far one. The shut door at the dark end of the hall that has, from the beginning, looked as though it wanted something of you before it would open.

She feels the turn of your attention toward the dark end of the hall, and this time she asks you nothing — only follows your look, and gathers herself. She lifts off you at last and takes up the sapphire silk from the floor, drawing it closed at her shoulder; and by the time it is fastened the loose ease has gone out of her and left the attentiveness underneath, the alertness she wore in the hallway before any of this began. "So. The night gave us this much." The thread; you feel her mean it. "Let us go and see what the morning means to charge for it." And she is moving before you are, down the hall toward the door you have not opened, one hand held back for yours.
