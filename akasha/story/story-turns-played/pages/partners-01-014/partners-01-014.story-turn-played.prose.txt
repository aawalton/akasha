You look into her eyes — blue, steady, doing most of her listening, as ever — and you give her the truth the way the small hours allow it.

"It's remarkable how well you understand me, even when I'm not saying anything with words. I can tell you have a gift of seeing people — it shows in every action you take and every story you tell. I see you." A deep breath; a slow exhale. "As you noticed, I haven't known you long, but I already really like you. I feel safe with you — and somehow I know that matters to me more than just about anything else. I hear what you're saying: not now, not tonight. And what you're not saying: not no, not never. I respect that. But I know myself well enough that I need to go back to my room now, before my body gets any more ideas. I'll see you in the morning?"

For one full beat, the composed woman is simply caught.

"...Oh." Then — honest, because you've earned honest: "You've no idea what you just did, saying that. I spend my whole life being the one who sees — it's the thing I'm for, and it's mostly done in the dark where no one's looking, and I made my peace long ago with not being seen back. And you've known me two days, and you turned it round and handed it to me like it was nothing." She meets your eyes, steady and plainly moved. "It is not nothing. That landed somewhere I don't often let things land. Thank you, Alan. Truly."

A breath, and the wry warmth comes back up under it like the fire under the coals.

"And there it is again — 'somehow I know it matters more than anything.' That's the third true thing you've handed me tonight with no ledger behind it. I'm keeping that one too; I've quite a collection of you started now." A warm, wry softness. "As for the rest — you heard me exactly. Both halves. Not now, and not never. Most people only catch the half that suits them; you caught both and said them back. You keep reading me right, and I keep being glad of it."

She rises with you, and there's a small laugh at your honesty about yourself.

"Go on, then — and thank you for knowing yourself well enough to say it aloud; that's its own kind of gift, rarer than you'd think. Yes — you'll see me in the morning. I'll be the one at the range. Breakfast, then the dull business of roof-slates, and the company we're expecting by noon. The night's over and the world's still here, see."

And then she turns your own register gently back on you — the woman who has answered every asking all night, asking for the first time herself.

"One thing — may I? Before you go."
