And the thing you feel most, holding it, is that yellow is not the wall. Not anymore. At the pauldron, orange had been the absolute roof of you, your whole strength spent just to hold the metal glowing. Now you stand inside a yellow heat with room to breathe, and you can feel the next country up there above it — white, and past white the place where iron stops being a solid thing at all and begins to run — and it is not impossibly far. It is up a slope you can see the top of. Not today, not with what you have in you this morning. But you have grown that much: the ceiling that stopped you cold on floor two is a floor you stand on now.

You ease the channel shut. The yellow dims back down through orange and red and gone, and the bar in your hand is only a bar again, plain and hot and ticking as it cools, telling you nothing it has not already told you. You set it down. You know your own size a little better than you did when you woke.

The experiment told you what you needed to know, and now you put it to work. You clear and scrub a patch of the stone floor by the water's edge until it is as flat and clean as this place will give you — your anvil. You weigh the cold river-stone in your hand — your hammer. The cistern at your side — your quench. And you lay out your stock: the dead iron bar that used to be Burning Anger, the Sentry's steel pauldron, and what loose scrap of plate you can pry and gather. Crude tools, all of it, and you know it. But you do not need fine. You need heavy.

You set your hand to the steel and pour the heat in, and this time you are not testing a ceiling — you are working. The pauldron goes dull-red, cherry, orange, soft enough to fold; you fold it, and beat it, and fold it again around the end of the bar, building mass, the river-stone ringing flat blows against hot metal on cold stone. Scrap goes in glowing and gets hammered home. When a seam needs to truly become one piece you drive the heat into just that line until it runs white and the metals take each other, then plunge it hissing into the cistern to set.

It is ugly. It is lopsided and scorched and nothing a real smith would put his name to. But when you lift it off the stone and heft it, it is exactly the thing you set out to make: a brutal dense head of fused steel at the end of a solid iron haft, heavy enough that it wants to swing once you start it, the head seated so hard onto the bar that you can hammer it against the floor and feel nothing shift. A mace. A blunt, stupid, honest weapon for breaking whatever you decide needs breaking — and your hands knew a little more about how to make it at the end than they did at the start.

You stand in the low light by the water with the thing resting on your shoulder, breathing hard, forge-tired and scorched in a few small places, your old bar and your old armor both gone into this one heavy answer. Cold Revenge still drinks the cold in the cistern at your feet, your other hand free for it if you want it. Above you, still, the unclimbed way up.

Smithing leaves you hollowed in a way that wants sleep, and you give in to it — not the bottomless collapse of before, just a nap, curled by the warm coals with the new mace under your hand. You go down into grey nothing and come back up a while later clearer, the forge-ache eased out of your arms, your focus pooled deep and ready again, the small burns gone tight and quiet. Good enough.

Because there is one more thing you mean to try while the idea is hot in you. The mace is a good weapon and a dead one — plain metal, no fire in it. Every time before, when you have put essence into a thing, you have moved it from somewhere else: cold out of the bar and into the stone, ember out of the coals and into the rivet. A ferry, always, carrying a charge from one vessel to another. This time you do not ferry anything. You take hold of the mace's heavy head, and you reach down into your own banked ember — your supply, the warmth that is simply yours now — and you push it out through your hands and INTO the steel, and you tell it to stay.

It fights you the way the cold stone once did, for a moment — your own essence resisting being left behind, wanting to flow back into you. You hold it there at the threshold, steady, patient, and then it gives. The ember sinks into the grain of the steel and takes, a low banked warmth spreading through the head and settling, staying when you take your hands away. The mace is warm now. It hums faintly against your palm, a held charge waiting to be spent, a channel your flame can run down when you want it to.

You turn it in the low light, the bound heat steady in it, and you understand that you have learned a real thing here — not just how to carry fire between vessels, but how to give it straight from yourself into a thing and make it keep. A dead lump of forged scrap is an ember weapon now, made and charged entirely by your own hand. You set it across your knees. The cistern is quiet, the coals low, and the way up off this floor is still waiting whenever you choose it.

You take up the finished weapon and feel the bound warmth of it steady in your hands, and you know what it is owed. The bar at the core of it carried fire for you from the bottom of this Tower; you spent that bar to make this, and the fire in it now is fire you put there yourself. So you give it the name it already had. Burning Anger. Heavier than it was, blunter, meaner — but the same anger, carried up.

Then you climb back up the stair you bled to hold, Burning Anger resting on your shoulder, to where the great grey corpse of the Sentinel slumps cold across the top of it, a mountain of dead slag wedged between you and the way up. Your tool is ready. It is time to work.

It is not a fight — the thing is dead, and dead it stays — but it is the hardest labor you have done in this place. You set your feet and you swing with everything your forged-deeper frame will give you, and the mace comes down on cold stone with a shock that jars up your arms and rings in your teeth, and a fist of slag breaks loose. Then another. The corpse is enormous and the dead slag is stubborn, grudging every piece, and there is nothing to do but hit it and hit it and hit it — head down, breath sawing, sweat stinging the half-healed burns, the heavy head of Burning Anger biting chips and chunks and slabs out of the carcass blow by blow. It takes a long time. It empties most of what the nap put back in you.

But it gives. Piece by broken piece you haul and kick the rubble clear of the stairmouth until the way up stands open at last — a dark throat of stair climbing on to whatever floor three becomes above here. The mace held: scuffed pale where it met the stone, dinged, but solid in your grip, the forge proven. And in the shattered heart of the wreck, laid bare by your work, sits the thing the Sentinel was built around — a dense dark knot of fused slag the size of two fists, the creature's drained core, cold and inert now, resting in the broken stone within your reach. The path is open, the core lies exposed, and you stand over it winded with the choice of what comes next.

You carry the dead weight of it down — the core, a fist of fused slag and dark crystal, cold all the way through, heavier than a thing that size has any right to be — back down the stair to where your brazier still burns low in its iron basket. You set it down in the coals, right in the heart of the flame, and you crouch there and watch it with more than your eyes.

For a long moment it does nothing. It sits in the fire and takes the heat the way any cold stone takes heat — dully, on the surface, and no deeper. So you reach in with a thread of your own banked ember and give it a push, a little of what you carry poured down into the dead thing to wake it.

And it WAKES. The instant your essence touches it the core stops being a stone in a fire and becomes a mouth. It drinks. Not just the thread you fed it — once it has the taste it reaches past you into the brazier itself and pulls, drawing the loose heat of the flame down into its body and keeping it, and deep in the fused dark the old cracks come up red and steady, filling, banking, a low ember light that was not there a breath before. You know it then the way you know your own pulse: a vessel made to hold fire. Feed it and it fills. It keeps what it takes. It does not give any of it back until something reaches in and draws it out.

You leave the core to its filling — it will keep, banking heat in the coals while you work — and climb back up the stair to the third floor, the mace heavy in your fist, to walk the whole of it slow and certain this time: every corner, every shadow, before you trust it at your back.

Nothing waits for you. The floor is dead and still and yours — no glow, no grind of stone, no breath in it but your own. And walking it slow you read it for what it is in a way you could not while the guardian held the stair: this whole floor was built around the furnace and the thing that kept it, shaped end to end for the making and the breaking of metal, every line of it bent toward the great cold hulk you emptied and the open stair beyond. Whatever the Tower made on this floor, it made with fire.

The way up stands open at the stairmouth, dark and waiting. Below you the core fills in the brazier's coals. The floor is clear, the mace is in your hand, and the next climb is still ahead.

You go back over the third floor a second time, and this time you are not looking for what might kill you — you are looking with a smith's eye, for what the place was made to do and what it might still give you. And it gives you its answer fast: this was a forge, and a good one once. You find the bones of it everywhere now that you know to look — the footings of a great anvil, a rack that held tools, the dark stains of old heat on the stone, shapes in the rubble that were hammers and tongs and gauges before the Tower's long rot got to them.

Got to them and won. The anvil is cracked clean through and half-sunk into the floor, no use to anyone. The hammers are rust-welded to formless lumps; the tongs come apart in your fingers when you lift them, red dust and nothing. Whatever skill built this place, time has eaten the most of it down to stains and stubs.

But not all. Digging through the wreck of the tool-rack you turn up one piece the rot only bit halfway: an iron hammer-head, its haft long gone, the face pitted and rough — but heavy, and when you knock it against the stone it rings instead of crumbling. The core of it is still good. It is no proper forge-hammer, and it is no whole tool yet, missing the haft to swing it by. But it is honest iron with weight behind it, and it is a great deal more than a river-stone in the fist. You knock the worst of the rust off it and you keep it.

You take yourself back down to the brazier by the water and you stop. That is enough — enough forging, enough drinking the dead, enough of the climb for one long day. You bank the fire down to a steady low burn, you set your back to the warm iron of the basket with the water at your side and the dead floor quiet above you, and for the first time since you cannot remember when you let go of all of it and sleep.

It is real sleep, deep and unbroken — nothing comes down the open stair, nothing stirs on the cleared floor, just the small sounds of the coals and the dark. And when you wake you wake whole. The ache is gone out of your bones; the scalds and the burns that the tempering left in you have closed and faded in the night; the deep-down emptiness where your strength and your fire and your wind had all run low is filled again, brimming, every part of you topped off and ready. You feel, for once, like a man at his full and not a man running on the dregs of himself.

The first thing you do, before anything else, is reach for the core. It has sat in the heart of the brazier all night, drinking, and you crouch to it and put your fire-sense against it to ask the one question — is it full? Has it taken all it can hold?

And the answer will not come clear. You reach for the shape of it the way you read iron, the way you read the Sentinel, and this time the sense slides off — the core's glow and the brazier's heat blur together into one warmth and you cannot find the edge between the vessel and the fire that feeds it. What little you do feel reads as appetite: a low, steady pull, the thing still reaching, still drinking, as though it is nowhere near done. You come away from it with nothing certain and only that one muddled impression to go on — that the heart is still hungry, still taking, not yet at whatever its limit is. Whether that read is true or whether your own sense simply failed on it, you cannot say. It sits there in the coals, glowing, and keeps its counsel.

This time you do it slowly. You set the Furnace-heart in your lap and the rivet by your knee and Burning Anger across your thighs, you settle your back against the brazier, and you begin the long careful work of taking yourself apart and building yourself back harder — not in one reckless flood like before, but a steady controlled draw, a thread at a time, exactly as much as your flesh can drink and set without tearing.

You start with the core, and the core surprises you. You had thought it half-done, still hungry — but the moment you open it the fire comes pouring out of it full and fierce and endless, far more than your tired sense had promised you in the dark. It was at its brim all along; your reading of it had simply failed. So much the better. You take it slow and you take it all, pouring its banked heat down into your bones and your sinew and your skin, and where the reckless way scalded you the careful way SETS — the ember going in not as fire but as forging, scouring out the weakness and hardening what is left, permanent, yours.

When the core runs dry you open the rivet and drink that too, and when the rivet is dead you reach into Burning Anger and draw the heat back out of the metal you put it into, unmaking your own binding, until the mace goes cold and plain in your hands and is only a club again. And when the last of the batteries is empty you keep going — into your own held fire, the whole guardian's hoard you have carried banked beneath your skin since the day you drank the Sentinel — and you spend that too, all of it, the bomb you have been pouring itself out as forge-heat for your own flesh. You burn it down to the very floor of yourself, until there is nothing left in any vessel or in you to give, and only then do you stop.
