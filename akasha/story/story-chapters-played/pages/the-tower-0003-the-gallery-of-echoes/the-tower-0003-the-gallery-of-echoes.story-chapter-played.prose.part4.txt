You come back to yourself in a cold room. The brazier has burned down to nothing while you worked; the core is a dead grey stone in your lap, the rivet a spent bead, Burning Anger a plain cold weight across your knees. Every battery you had is empty, and so are you — your fire is gone, all of it, the great banked hoard spent down to ash, and the channels that carried it ache hollow and raw. You are wrung out past anything the climb has done to you yet: your wind short, your focus a guttering candle-end, your skin still smoking faintly where you pushed it to the edge of what flesh can take.

But under the hurt you are not the same man who sat down. You can feel it in the new weight of your own arms, the deeper seat of your own breath, the way your bones sit harder and surer in you — you traded every scrap of fire you owned and a deep measure of your own flesh, and you bought yourself a body forged past what it was. Hardier. Stronger. Made, not given.

The cold mace lies in your lap. The dead batteries lie around you. The way up off this floor stands open at the stairmouth above, and you are emptied to the floor of yourself in front of it.

Exhausted as you are, you do not lie down yet. You drag the last of your fuel together — the whole sad remainder of the stack — build it up in the brazier's basket and coax it alight, and into the new flame you set your three fire-things: the spent Furnace-heart, the dead rivet, the cold mace. To each you give a small seed of fire, the most you can spare from a reserve burned down to nothing, just enough to wake them.

Two of them wake. The core drinks its spark and the rivet drinks its spark and both catch — the brazier's heat flowing in now that there is a seed inside to hold it, their cracks beginning to glow low and dim as they settle in to fill through the night. But the mace does not. You feed it the same spark and it takes the heat the way any cold iron takes heat and gives nothing back — there is no binding left in it to catch and keep, only the plain metal you left when you drew its fire out. A spark cannot rebuild what you unmade; that would want the whole patient work of binding again, not a bedtime ember. You note it, too tired to do more, and let the mace lie cold.

Then, with the fire tended and the two batteries glowing themselves slowly back to life and the last of your wood burning down to feed them, you lie down by the warmth and let go.

You sleep like the dead and wake like the living. Whatever the forging took out of you the long dark gives back — the hollow ache in your channels eased, your wind come back full, the deep weariness lifted — and the body you tempered yesterday wakes rested in its new harder shape: more of you to fill, and filled. You are whole again, and stronger-built than you have ever been.

The brazier has burned down low. The core and the rivet sit in the embers glowing faintly, fuller than you left them, still drinking the last of the heat. The mace lies cold and plain where you set it. Your fuel is gone, spent to the last stick on the night's fire. And above you, past the dead floor, the way up off this level stands open at the stairmouth, waiting.

First, the mace. You crouch by the dying brazier and take up the rivet — warm now, a night's charge banked back into it — and set it against Burning Anger's cold head to do the patient work you skipped last night: not a spark this time but a true binding, drawing the rivet's whole stored fire out in a slow controlled thread and seating it down into the steel, matching ember to iron the way it wants to go. It takes. The charge sinks into the head and HOLDS, the metal warming under your hands, a low heat-glow waking in it again. It is not the brimming thing it was — the rivet had only so much to give, and the rivet is a dead bead now, spent — but Burning Anger is bound once more, warm and willing, a weapon that can carry your fire. You leave the rest of your gear by the cold brazier and take up just the mace and the glowing core.
