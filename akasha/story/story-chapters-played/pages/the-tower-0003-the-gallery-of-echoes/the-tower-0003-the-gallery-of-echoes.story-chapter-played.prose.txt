# The Gallery of Echoes

You go up slow, and you do not charge the top. A body's length from the head of the stairs you drop low and ease the rest of the way on your own caution, until just your eyes clear the lip of the floor above — no more of you than you need to see with, ready to sink back down the instant anything turns your way.

It is a great long hall. After the drowned, close cistern it opens out huge and DRY, a vaulted gallery running away into the dark, the ceiling lost somewhere above you. From the far end comes a dim warm light, reddish, steady — not the cold black you climbed out of; something up there glows. And by that light you can see the floor of the hall, and the floor of the hall is a graveyard of OTHERS. Picked-clean leavings strewn the length of it — a rusted blade, a cracked pack, the pale shapes of bone gone grey with time. Climbers who came this far. Climbers who did not leave. There is precious little to hide behind between here and that glow; it is an open floor, a killing floor.

And at the far end of it, near where the warm light wells up and the way onward must be, there is a SHAPE. Large — larger than the ash-thing you fought at the bottom, large enough that you mark it at once even still and silent as it is. You cannot make out what it is from here, only that it is big, and that it has not yet noticed you; and underneath the seeing, that other sense of yours stirs and confirms it — the warm glow ahead is not just firelight. Something with a real weight of essence sits at the end of that hall. You hold there at the lip a long moment, taking the measure of it, letting none of yourself over the edge — knowing now the shape of what waits, and that it does not yet know yours.

You do not go up. Not yet. The first time you faced a floor of this Tower unready it nearly killed you — the cistern, the cold, the water closing over your head in the shallows — and you have learned the lesson the hard way: prepare first, then climb. So you go back down to the water's edge, to the very shallows that almost drowned you, and you set yourself to build something new before you ever set foot on that killing floor above.

You have two fires and one bar that holds a third. Ember Channel, that pours your heat into iron; Ember Burst, that throws it; and Burning Anger, warm through with the Ember you bound into it. You wonder what happens if you stop using them one at a time. You take the bar in both hands, point it out over the black water, and you channel into it the way you would to arm it — and then, instead of holding, you BURST, shoving the gathered fire down its length and out the end, all three things at once, and you push it all at the water that tried to take you.

Fire answers. Not a neat thing, not the tight burning ball you half-imagined — a WAVE. A great roaring wash of flame tears off the end of the bar and rolls out across the cistern, lighting the drowned room orange to its farthest corner, and where it meets the black water it throws up a hiss and a wall of steam that climbs into the dark. You feel the bar's bound heat go with it, the charge you set into the iron leaping out to lend the throw its weight, spending itself in the doing. It is raw. It is wide. It sprays where a master's would spear — you can feel how loose it is even as it leaves you. But it WENT. Fire left your hand and went where you aimed it, out over the very shallows that nearly drowned you, and burned the cold memory of that water to steam and light.

And behind your eyes the Tower takes note of the new thing you have made — not a borrowed trick but a fusion of your own, rough and Novice and entirely yours. You lower the bar, half-drained and still warm in your grip, your breath coming hard from the focus it cost, and you look at the steam settling back over the dark and know you have a weapon now you did not have an hour ago. It needs work. There is time to give it, before you climb.

The thing you glimpsed at the top of the stairs is large, and a wide soft wash of flame is not what you want to meet it with. You want a spear. So before anything else you take what you made and you go to work on it — and first you take its name back. Lash was wrong; it does not lash, it WASHES. Ember Wave, then. Call a thing what it is.

Then you drill. You point Burning Anger out over the black water and throw the Wave, and watch how loose it spills, and throw it again narrower, and again narrower than that — fighting the flow with everything your focus has, trying to choke the wide wash down into a single tight stream, as fierce and aimed as you can force it. And it COMES, partway. Throw by throw the wash tightens; the fire learns to run in a line instead of a fan; you can feel it growing more yours, more pointable, less a thing you spill and more a thing you aim.

But there is a wall, and you find it. Past a certain tightness it will not go — the last hard edge of it, the narrow searing lance you can picture so clearly, simply will not come, no matter how many times you throw. You can drill yourself to the counterfeit of it, the yard-trained near-miss, and no further. That final control is waiting on something an empty room cannot give you. You throw until you understand that, and then you throw a while longer out of stubbornness, until your focus is wrung dry and the fire guttering off the bar's end is the best you have left in you.

So you stop. You set Burning Anger across your knees, and you sit at the water's edge where the steam of your own fire still hangs, and you close your eyes and breathe. You do nothing and you reach for nothing; you only let the deep well of your focus fill back from the bottom, slow, the way still water finds its level. The cistern is quiet around you. The ache of the drilling eases. The new narrowness you bought settles into your hands and stays there, learned, even if its last edge eluded you.

When you open your eyes again you feel recovered — steady, clear, most of what you spent come back to you. Not the man you were at the bottom of this Tower; something harder and better-armed than that. You have a stream now where this morning you had only a wash, and a weapon in each hand with names, and a fire you can throw. The wall you hit is still there, waiting. But you are readier than you were.

Before you climb into anything with a guardian at the end of it, you want to know what your fire actually DOES — not against straw or memory or open water, but against metal, the stuff that armor and blades and big still shapes are made of. So you take up the Sentry pauldron, the steel one off floor two, and you set your Ember to it and you do not hold back. No channeling into iron to arm it, no throwing it wide — just heat, all of it, every degree you can wring out of yourself poured into one patch of steel to see how hot you can truly make it.

The metal answers. First a sullen warmth under your hand, then a deep red blooming up out of the grey, then brighter, the red climbing toward orange as you bear down and pour and refuse to let it cool. The dark around you fills with the light of it — a hard scorching orange glow throwing your shadow long on the cistern wall, heat rolling off the steel in a wave you have to lean back from. You hold it there, shaking with the effort, and push for more.

And more does not come. That is the thing you learn. You push until your focus screams and the steel sits there glowing a furious orange — hot enough to cook meat, to set cloth alight, to brand — and it will not climb past it. It does not yellow. It does not whiten. It does not soften or sag or run. The wall is real and you have found it: orange-hot is the top of what you are, today, against honest steel. A melt — metal gone to glowing liquid, the way a forge does it — is somewhere far above you, up a road you have not walked yet.

So you ease off, and the glow sinks back down the ladder it climbed — orange to red to a dull sullen ember to nothing, the steel ticking as it cools, kissed and discolored but whole. You set it down. It cost you to learn it, and it was worth the cost: you know your fire now in a way you did not an hour ago. It will scorch and sear and burn anything that lives. It will not yet melt the things that don't. Good to know, before you meet the big still shape upstairs.

You rest first. You learned that lesson at the bottom of the cistern and again on the stair: prepare, then climb. So you sit by the banked brazier on floor two until the ache is gone out of you and the deep well of your focus has filled back to the brim, until you are as ready as you are going to get. Then you stand, and you leave the camp behind — the brazier, the rope, the rivet, the soaking stone, the heat-kissed pauldron, all of it. You take two things and no more: Burning Anger in one hand, Cold Revenge in the other. Heat and cold, a bar apiece, and nothing to weigh you down.

You climb to the mouth of the third floor — and there you STOP. You do not step out onto that long open killing-floor where the bones of better-armed climbers lie picked clean. You stay in the throat of the stair, in the narrow of it, where a big thing can only come at you one width at a time. If it is going to be met, it will be met here, on ground you chose. You set your shoulders against the stone and you get ready to call it to you.

You lift Cold Revenge and you strike it against the doorway stone — once, hard, and the cold iron rings a flat hard note that goes rolling out down the length of that vaulted dark hall and comes back thinned from the far walls. You strike it again. And again, a slow deliberate toll, the sound of something that wants to be heard. Then you go still, both bars ready, and you wait to see what your noise has bought.

For a long breath, nothing. The reddish glow at the far end sits where it sat. And then — it MOVES. The big still shape you marked from the stairhead is not a shape any longer; it shifts, it rises into its own weight, and it turns toward you. It heard. It is coming.

It comes down the hall slow, and the slowness is the first thing you learn — no rush, no charge, a ponderous grinding advance, stone dragging over stone, each step landing with a weight you feel in the floor through your feet. And as it comes into the nearer dark you see it for what it is: a thing of fused rock and cooled slag heaped into the shape of a sentinel, big — bigger than the ash-thing you killed at the bottom, broad and hulking and heavy. Deep cracks run all through its hide, and down in those cracks a sullen reddish-orange light smolders — it is lit from the inside, banked full of heat, cinders sifting from its joints as it walks. The warm glow that fills this whole hall is not a fire on the floor. It is THIS. The glow is the thing itself.

And under your seeing, that other sense of yours opens wide and tells you the second thing: it is FULL. A deep dense well of ember-essence walks toward you on legs of stone — more heat banked in that cracked hide than in your rivet, your bar, anything you have touched. It is slow, and it is a furnace, and it stands between you and the way up. It comes on into the throat of the stair, into the ground you chose, near enough now that the heat of it reaches you — and it is your move.

You do not throw fire. You worked it out the moment you saw it: a thing lit full of its own heat will not much care about yours. So you set Burning Anger down on the stone at your feet, close enough to snatch up again in a heartbeat, and you take Cold Revenge up in both hands, and you wait — you let the furnace come grinding into the throat of the stair, into the narrow you chose, until its banked heat is washing over you and there is no more room for it to come — and then you swing with everything your shoulders have, the cold iron driving for one of those glowing seams in its hide.

It bites — cold meeting that buried fire with a vicious hiss and a spit of cold steam, a shower of slag spalling off where you land it. But the blow stops short of what you wanted. Under the glow the thing is STONE, fused and thick, and the stone turns your strength the way a wall turns a fist; the bar jars in your hands and rings up your arms, and the seam only cracks, shallow, where you meant to split it wide. Brute force is not going to break this open.

But you are not only swinging a bar. Even as your strength fights the stone, your WILL goes in — down through that shallow cracked seam, past the shell, into the hot heart of the thing where all that banked fire lives — and you PULL. You reach for its heat the way you learned to reach for the rivet's, and you drag it up and out and into yourself.

And it comes. A draught of living fire tears up the length of the bar and the seam and floods into you, and where it leaves the creature that one glowing crack guts and DIMS, going sullen and dark as you bleed the heat out of it. It is not much — the seam is shallow, you can only draw what the narrow opening lets through — but it is REAL, and it is the first time you have torn essence from a thing that did not want to give it. The heat floods in past where your body likes to hold it; you feel it scorch you at the edges, the overflow searing your own skin from the inside, and for one bad moment it is nearly more than you can keep. You keep it. You clamp down and you hold, brimming and burned and full of stolen fire — and you understand, all at once, the thing this fight turns on: your bar glanced off the stone, but your will reached the fire underneath. The shell is its armor. The heat is its weakness — and its fuel.

The sentinel feels it. Whatever dull patience carried it grinding down the hall is gone now; the dimmed seam in its hide and the bite of cold have woken it to you as a threat, not a noise. It does not move fast — it cannot, it is too vast and too heavy — but it MOVES: the great slag-heaped bulk hauls around, a limb like a fallen column dragging up off the floor, the inner glow flaring brighter through its cracks as it stokes itself to bring that weight down on the small burning thing in its doorway. You have a heartbeat, maybe two, bought by its slowness — brim-full of its own stolen heat, Cold Revenge gripped in both hands and Burning Anger lying on the stone at your feet, your back to the narrow stair. The blow is coming. What you do in the gap before it lands is yours to choose.
