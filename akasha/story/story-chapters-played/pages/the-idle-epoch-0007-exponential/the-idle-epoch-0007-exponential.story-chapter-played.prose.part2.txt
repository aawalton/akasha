Three vine constrictors. Same species, same level range, same attack pattern. She killed them one at a time, timing each engagement against the internal clock she had been running since she picked up a sword.

Then she walked thirty-one meters away from the nearest construct relay -- outside the Optimization Aura's range -- and found three more.

Same species. Same level. Same attack pattern.

She killed them. The engagements were fine. She was still Mira Vasquez, still a Sword Saint who made monsters look like practice dummies.

But they were slower. By fractions. By the width of a breath between strikes.

She walked back into aura range and killed three more.

Faster.

The difference was roughly eight percent. She could not be sure without Callum's obsessive data tracking, but her body knew. A lifetime of competitive fencing had trained her to feel timing differences measured in hundredths of a second. The aura's +15% idle efficiency was translating into something like +8% active combat performance -- not enough to matter in any single fight, but enough to add up over an eight-hour grinding day. Fewer fractions of seconds wasted between kills. More kills per hour. More XP. More of the one thing she needed and could not get enough of.

She stood in the clearing, sweat cooling on her neck, the canopy filtering alien light across her shoulders, and thought about what that meant.

Mira Vasquez did not idle. Idling was for people who could not fight. Idling was surrender dressed up as efficiency. She had believed that since the first week of the Emergence, when she had watched people set up grinding loops and walk away while she bled for every point of XP, and she had sworn she would never be the person who let a machine do her living for her.

But this was not idling. She was still fighting. Still sweating. Still making decisions that no script could replicate -- reading the vine constrictor's lunge pattern, choosing to sidestep rather than parry because the angle favored a follow-up slash, rotating her hips into the strike with a precision that came from nine years of competitive fencing and not from any System skill.

The aura was not replacing her. It was *filling in the margins*. The tiny gaps between her actions -- the cooldown recovery, the target acquisition, the background processes that even a Sword Saint could not consciously control -- were running smoother. Faster. Cleaner.

She hated it a little, on principle.

She also could not deny what it meant: for the first time in three months, her XP curve had moved. Not by much. But it had *moved*. The wall she had been beating her fists against since Level 23 had developed a crack, and the crack had Callum Voss's name on it.

She sheathed her saber and walked back toward the warehouse, moving faster than she had on the way out.

The Exchange smelled like ozone and grilled fish.

Callum navigated through the mid-morning crowd with his shoulders hunched and Pip flickering on his collar -- the familiar had migrated from his shoulder to his collar two days ago and seemed to prefer the new position, nestled against his neck where it could pulse in rhythm with his heartbeat. He carried a crate of Flux Capacitors balanced against his hip, the product of thirty-six hours of compound-loop-enhanced Refinery output. More capacitors than he had produced in the previous two weeks combined.

He found his usual vendor -- a heavyset man named Grier who bought Flux Capacitors in bulk for construct-smiths across the Foundry District -- and set the crate on the counter.

Grier looked at the crate. Looked at Callum. Looked back at the crate.

"This is double your usual run."

"Supply is up."

"Supply doesn't double in three days." Grier picked up a capacitor and held it to the light. The Flux charge inside pulsed with a steady amber glow -- well-formed, clean, the kind of quality that came from a mature processing construct running at high efficiency. "These are good. Better than your usual, even. Whatever you're doing different, it's working."

Callum said nothing. He had calculated this risk before coming to the Exchange: increased output meant increased visibility. Every Flux Capacitor he sold was a data point. Someone paying attention could map his production curve from sales volume alone.

He was selling anyway. The compound loop required Flux reinvestment, and Flux required gold, and gold required selling what the loop produced. The system was hungry for its own fuel.

"Four hundred gold for the lot," Grier said.

"Four-fifty. Quality premium."

Grier grunted. "Four-twenty."

"Deal."

The gold deposited into his inventory with a soft chime. Callum turned to leave.

The Broker was standing behind him.

They had not been there a moment ago. Callum was certain of that -- he had checked his surroundings when he approached the stall, a habit drilled into him by three months of operating without guild protection in a zone where the unaffiliated were increasingly unwelcome. The Broker had materialized from the crowd the way smoke materializes from a fire: not arriving, exactly, but *becoming present*, as if they had always been there and Callum had only just noticed.

"Good morning," the Broker said.

Their voice was warm. Measured. Pitched at exactly the right volume to be heard over the Exchange crowd without carrying to adjacent stalls. They wore a long coat with too many pockets, leather gloves that gleamed faintly with Flux reinforcement, and a flat cap that looked like it belonged to a different century. Their face was pleasant and unremarkable in the way that a mirror is unremarkable -- it reflected what you expected to see, and you forgot the details the moment you looked away.

Their smile arrived a fraction of a second before Callum finished turning around. As if they had known when he would turn.

"We haven't met," Callum said. It was not entirely true. He had seen the Broker in the Exchange half a dozen times. But they had never spoken.

"We haven't," the Broker agreed. "And normally, I would introduce myself by offering you something you need at a price you can barely afford. It's my standard opening. Very effective." They tilted their head. The smile did not change, but something behind it did -- a sharpening, like a blade being drawn from a sheath that looked ornamental. "Today is different."

Callum waited. He had learned long ago that silence was the cheapest way to gather information.

The Broker seemed to approve of the silence. Their expression shifted by a degree -- not warmer, but more genuine. The smile of someone who had expected to be asked questions and was pleased not to be.

"I'm going to tell you something for free," the Broker said.

The word *free* landed in the space between them like a stone in still water. In six months of post-Emergence economics, Callum had never heard the Broker described as someone who gave anything for free. The Broker traded. The Broker brokered. The Broker extracted value from every interaction with the precision of a surgeon removing exactly as much tissue as the procedure required. *Free* was not in their vocabulary.

Which meant the word itself was the first piece of information, and it was not free at all.

"The Iron Vanguard has assigned a second scout team to the Foundry District," the Broker said.

Callum's heart rate increased by twelve beats per minute. He kept his face still.

"They're not watching you because you're a threat." The Broker's voice did not change inflection. The words came at the same measured pace, the same warm tone, as if they were discussing weather or market prices. "They're watching you because your output numbers doubled in three days, and they can't explain it."

The Exchange crowd moved around them. Vendors called prices. Someone laughed at a stall twenty feet away. The world continued at its usual pace, indifferent to the cold wire of adrenaline threading through Callum's nervous system.

"The Vanguard doesn't like things it can't explain," the Broker said. "Drake Morrison built his guild on the principle that strength is visible, measurable, and earned through active effort. A mid-level Loopweaver -- a class nobody has heard of, running an operation nobody understands -- generating numbers that shouldn't be possible at your level..." The Broker paused. Let the sentence hang, unfinished, like a bridge that ended over open air. "That makes you a variable. And Morrison eliminates variables."

Callum's mind was already running. *Second scout team. The first was the initial report -- "mid-level nobody, weird class, no threat." That was weeks ago, before the Enhancement Construct. Before the compound loop closed. Before the numbers started climbing. The second team means the first team's assessment has been revised. Someone in the Vanguard is tracking my output through Exchange sales data, construct relay signatures, or both. They do not know how I am doing this. They know that I am doing it. That is enough.*

"What's the price?" Callum asked.

The Broker smiled. Not the pleasant, professional smile they had been wearing. A different one -- smaller, sharper, genuinely amused.

"I said it was free."

"Nothing is free."

"Most things aren't. This is." The Broker adjusted one glove, a gesture that looked habitual. "The fact that you'll wonder *why* is worth more than the information."

Callum processed that. It was, he realized, a perfectly optimized transaction. The Broker had given him intelligence that was immediately actionable and potentially survival-critical. The cost was not gold, not Residuum, not information. The cost was uncertainty. A debt of *why* that would sit in his mind, accruing interest, until the Broker chose to collect.

It was, from a systems perspective, elegant. And deeply unsettling.

"You know what I'm building," Callum said. Not a question.

"I know what everyone is building." The Broker's tone was mild. "It's my job to know. What you are building is... more interesting than most."

"Interesting enough to warn me for free."

"Interesting enough that I would prefer you not be shut down before I see what happens next." The Broker held his gaze for exactly two seconds -- long enough to be deliberate, short enough to not be a challenge. "I don't have a side, Mr. Voss. I have a portfolio. And your trajectory has just become the most interesting line on my chart."

They inclined their head -- a gesture that was somehow both gracious and dismissive -- and turned away. Within three steps, they had been absorbed by the crowd. Within five, Callum could not identify them at all. It was as if the Exchange had swallowed them, or as if they had dissolved back into the information they were made of.

Callum stood very still.

Pip pulsed against his neck -- quick, irregular, agitated. The familiar had never reacted to another person's presence before. He filed that away.

He stood in the middle of the Exchange, surrounded by the noise and smell and commerce of a hundred thousand people trying to survive, and he calculated.

*The Iron Vanguard is watching. My output is visible through multiple channels. Drake Morrison is a man who controls through understanding, and he does not understand me. The Broker considers my survival a net positive for their portfolio, which means my destruction would be a net negative, which means there are other parties who would benefit from my destruction, which means the Broker is not the only one watching.*

*The timeline has changed.*

*I thought I had weeks. I may have days.*

He picked up his crate of remaining Flux Capacitors and walked home. He did not hurry. Hurrying attracted attention. But his steps were precise, and his mind was already three moves ahead, restructuring every plan he had around a new constraint: visibility.

The planning session started at sundown and lasted until the warehouse smelled like cold coffee and tension.

Callum had pushed the workbench against the wall and spread his field notebook across the floor, its pages open to hand-drawn diagrams of the Foundry District, the Exchange trade routes, and a timeline that had been revised so many times the original pencil lines were barely visible beneath layers of correction. Jin sat cross-legged with his TI-84 talisman in his lap, running numbers. Mira stood with her back against the support pillar nearest the door, arms crossed, watching the exits even though the exits were not the threat.

"The Broker came to you," Mira said. "In person. Uninvited. With free intelligence."

"Yes."

"That doesn't worry you?"

"The intelligence worries me. The Broker worries me in a different way."

"What way?"

Callum looked at her. "The Broker's business model is information arbitrage. They profit from knowing things before others do and selling at the optimal moment. Giving me this information for free means either it has more value to them unsold than sold -- which implies they are invested in my outcome -- or it is bait designed to alter my behavior in a way that benefits them. Possibly both."

Mira stared at him. "You think like this all the time?"

"Yes."

"That must be exhausting."

"It is efficient."

"That is not the same thing."

Jin cleared his throat. "Can we focus on the part where the Iron Vanguard has two scout teams watching this warehouse?"

The room sobered. Jin pulled up a rough map of the Foundry District he had sketched on his talisman's interface. "If they're monitoring Exchange sales data and construct relay signatures, they already know Callum's output has spiked. The question is what they do with that information."

"Three options," Callum said. He ticked them off on his fingers. "One: they watch and wait. Morrison is methodical. He will want to understand what I am doing before he acts. This is the best case. It buys us time, but not enough -- once they map the compound loop, they will see the exponential trajectory and recognize it as a threat to their resource control."

"Two?" Mira said.

"Two: they tax us harder. The Residuum surcharge on unaffiliated players is already squeezing our margins. If Morrison decides our output justifies a targeted assessment -- a 'security contribution' tied to our production level -- it could drain enough gold to slow the compound loop below the sustainability threshold."

Jin winced. "That would be elegant. He wouldn't even have to confront us. Just turn the economic screws until we choke."

"Three?"

"Three: direct intervention. Morrison sends a team to the warehouse. Not scouts. Enforcers. They shut down the constructs, confiscate materials, and offer us a choice: join the Iron Vanguard or leave the Barrier Zone."

Silence. The Enhancement Construct pulsed in the background, its silver-white thread cycling through the compound loop with a rhythm that felt, to Callum, like a heartbeat he could not afford to let stop.
