# The Cistern

You don't try it up here, not with the armored thing across the water. You go back down — careful on the stair, into the cleared chamber where the ash still lies in its heap and nothing moves. Safe as anywhere gets. You sit against the cold wall and take the cinder out.

It's the System's word that won't leave you alone: essence. Not rock, not ember. Essence — like there's something inside it meant to go somewhere. You close your hand around it, shut your eyes, and try to feel that something, to picture the cinder packed full of banked fire. Then you pull, the way you'd draw a breath, in through your palm and up your arm into your chest.

For a moment, nothing. Then it catches, and it's nothing like a breath. It's a wire dragged through you. Heat floods up your arm faster than you can think — too much — and your whole body locks around it. Your palm scalds. Something behind your eyes wrings tight and grey. You nearly drop it, and don't. You haul what you can into the center of yourself and hold it there until it stops fighting you.

When you open your eyes the cinder is dead. Grey, cold, light in your hand, the orange gone out of it for good. Your palm is burned and your head rings hollow, like you've lost two nights of sleep in one minute. But under the hurt there's something else, low and steady: the cold of the room has an edge you can feel now, and somewhere off through the stone you can sense banked warmth, faint, that you couldn't have found a minute ago. You don't know exactly what you did. You're certain you did something.

You get your feet under you and pick the iron bar back up. Just cold rust in your hands — but you're not the same as you were an hour ago, and you want to know what that means.

You close your eyes. The warmth is there when you reach for it, low in your chest, banked like a coal under ash — the thing you dragged in out of the cinder. You take hold of it the way you'd take a slow breath, and this time you push instead of pull: down your arms, into your hands, out along the cold iron toward the far end of the bar.

It fights you, then it goes. Heat crawls down the rod ahead of your attention. At the tip it gathers and catches — the rusted iron warms, darkens, then wakes to a dull ember-orange, a coal-glow you can feel on your face from a foot away. It isn't fire. It doesn't roar or throw light across the room. It's a low, concentrated heat held at one point of the metal, and holding it there costs you — a steady drain behind your eyes, like keeping your arm out straight with a weight in your hand. You can feel that if you stopped paying attention it would gutter and go cold.

But it's real, it's yours, and you did it on purpose. The bar in your hands is no longer just a heavy length of rust. Its tip burns.

You stop a step below the top of the stair, in the dark, and give the bar everything you've got. The ember-glow at the tip swells — not white-hot, you can't make it white-hot yet, but you push it as far as it will go, until the iron there burns a hard orange and the heat bends the air above it. Then you come up over the lip fast.

The armored thing is where it was, waist-deep, its back half to you, already dragging itself around toward the sound of you — slow, so slow, the fused plate grinding as it tries to bring itself about. It cannot. You read that right: it is a door on a rusted hinge, and you are moving faster than it can turn. You go wide along the platform's edge, around the trailing shoulder, and put yourself square behind it.

The back of its neck is a seam — rotted leather and corroded rings where the helm meets the spine, the one place the plate does not cover. You set your feet and drive the burning tip of the bar into it with both hands and all your weight.

The hot iron goes into the gap, and the thing under the armor is not wet. It is dry — centuries of rot inside the waterlogged shell — and it catches like a struck match. Fire runs up into the joint; the seized vertebrae crack and gout smoke; and the Sentry comes apart at the seams of its own slowness, crashing sideways off the platform's center, plate shrieking, flailing at a body it cannot make obey. Smoke pours from its neck. It is a hand's breadth from finished.

You do not get to enjoy it. The water felt the heat. All around the platform the black surface — which was only ever almost-still — breaks at once, and rises. The wrongness you sensed from the stair has a shape now: a low slick mass of them, leech-things pouring up out of the deep toward the warmth and the thrashing, toward you, where the deep water laps at the platform's edge by your feet. Something cold and many-mouthed closes around your calf and pulls — out, and down.

The leeches do not like the bar.

Where the hot iron passes through the mass, they recoil — a whole section of the swarm flinching back from the heat in the water, and the grip on your calf loosens for half a second. That is all you need. You are built low and your legs are the strongest thing you own; you drive off the platform with both of them, tear the leg free of the cold mouths, and go for the only dry ground you can reach — back the way you came, the broken walkway above the entrance, away from the deep.

You come up out of the water onto cracked stone, streaming, the bar still smoking in your fist. Behind you the swarm surges after — and stops. It heaps itself at the waterline in a low slick wall, mouthing at the edge of the dry stone, and cannot come up onto it. Whatever it is, it belongs to the water. On the walkway you are, for this breath, out of its reach.

Across the flood the Sentry is still burning. It has gone down off the center of its platform and lies half in the shallows, fire working through the seam in its neck, one arm beating at the stone in a slow ruined rhythm. It is not dead. Neither is the thing in the water. But you are out of the deep, on your feet, on dry ground, with both of them held at arm's length — and a moment, just a moment, to choose what you do with it.

You go back down. The descending stair takes you out of the wet cold and the noise of the water, down to the ember chamber on the first floor — cleared, quiet, yours. You let the heat go out of the bar; the glow at the tip dims to nothing and the iron is just iron again, ticking faintly as it cools. You set it down beside you and lie back on the stone.

Then you do the thing you know how to do. You start at your feet and work up — clench, hold, release; calves, thighs, the long muscles of your back, your shoulders, your jaw — tensing each one hard and letting it fall slack, chasing the shake out of your body a piece at a time. The adrenaline has nowhere to go but out. You slow your breath and tie it to your pulse: four beats drawing in, eight beats letting go, the exhale always longer than the inhale, until your heart stops slamming and settles into something you can count without trying.

The cold stone helps. The shake leaves your hands. The wet-dread, the calf-grip, the burning thing in the dark — all of it loosens and drains off into the floor, and what's left is just you, breathing, steady, the way you are when you're all the way down and back in yourself.

One thing does not let go: a deep ache low in your chest and down your arm, banked and dull, where you pulled the fire into yourself a while ago. Rest doesn't touch it. It sits there like a coal under the skin, patient.

After a while you sit up. You feel clear — clearer than you've been since you woke at the bottom of this place. And you turn it over: the slow grinding thing that couldn't make its own turn, and how it came apart once you got behind it and gave it fire; the water that wasn't water, the cold mouths, the way they would not follow you up onto dry stone. You sit with it, and let it become something you know.

You climb back up the spiral stair, and this time you go slow. You keep to the dry stone of the broken walkway, you watch the black water the whole way, and you stop a full stride back from where it laps the edge. No closer. Not again.

Across the flood, on the submerged platform, the armored thing has not moved — because it cannot. It is a ruin. The fire took it: the husk has burned down to a collapsed shell of cracked, smoke-blacked plate, slumped half into the shallows, the dry rot inside gone to char and ash. A thread of smoke still lifts off the neck-seam where you put the bar in. It is finished.

Something in the air registers the fact a half-beat after you do.

And then the rest of the scene resolves under your attention.

The water is the wrong kind of still. When you fled it was boiling with them; now the surface lies almost flat — almost — and that almost is the tell. You watch it long enough to be sure: the slow sag and shift under the skin of it, a mass holding itself just below, settled back into the deep while you were gone and waiting there again. Whatever you cost it by the edge, it has made good. The flood is occupied. The flood is not safe.

Across the water, the far stair — the sealed one — has changed. One of the two seams that held it flush has parted, a finger's width of dark gap opened in the stone. The other still holds. You understand it without being told: the way out wanted both wardens, and you have given it one.

So you stand at the edge on dry stone, clear-headed and whole enough — a dead thing and its armor across the water, a living thing under it, and one seam still closed between you and the way up.

You go back down to the first floor, to the quiet of the cleared chamber, and you sit — not braced, not ready for anything, just comfortable, your back to cool stone. You close your eyes.

The point is there when you reach for it. You don't know how you know that, the way you don't know how you know where your own hand is in the dark, but it's there: a small unspent thing, waiting on a decision. You think of your body — the part of you that has been thinnest all along, the part that bruises and tires and bleeds too easily — and you choose it. Vitality. You set the point there, deliberately, and let it settle.

And you feel it.

Not as a number — as a slow, physical filling-in. Warmth spreads from your middle and down into your limbs, unhurried, like water finding the low places. Your breath goes deeper without your asking it to. The floor feels a little further away, your own weight a little more solid on it, as if someone had quietly added ballast to a boat you'd been riding too high in. The banked ache in your chest is still there — but it sits in more body now, cushioned, less of the whole of you.

It's a real discovery, and you mark it: you can feel this. The change isn't only bookkeeping behind glass — it lands in the flesh, and you are built to notice. Whatever this place makes of you, you will feel it happen.

You don't get up. There are two of the small unspent things left, and you reach for another. This time you choose differently — not the part that endures, but the part that does. Might. The push and the pull, the swing of the bar, the force you can put behind a thing. You set the point there and let it take.

This one feels different from the first.

Where Vitality spread and settled, this gathers. You feel it pull inward to the long muscles of your arms and shoulders and back, a quiet tightening — more rope on the same winch. You open your eyes and pick up the iron bar without thinking about it, and it comes up lighter than it did an hour ago: not by much, but you feel the difference, the way you'd feel a half-pound off a pack you'd carried all day. The next swing you take will land harder than the last.

Two attributes now, two different textures. You're learning the grammar of this place — each one changes you in its own particular way, and you can feel which is which.

The last one. You take it without hesitation now — you've found the rhythm of this — and you give it to Finesse: the quickness, the third corner of the body's triangle. Endurance, power, and now speed. You set the point, and feel the unspent weight of choice go to nothing. There are none left. You close your eyes for it.

This one is the quickest of the three to arrive, and the most alive.

It doesn't spread like Vitality or gather like Might — it sharpens. You feel it in your hands first, a fine readiness in the fingers, then in the soles of your feet, the small constant adjustments of balance gone suddenly cheaper, more exact. When you shift your weight the floor answers faster. You have the sense, sitting still, that you could move — and that the moving would be cleaner than it was, a half-beat quicker onto a mark, a little surer on wet stone. The body's three threads, and you've thickened each: what holds, what hits, what moves.

That's all of them. The unspent points are gone, spent into flesh you can feel the shape of now. Whatever you've become, you did it with your eyes closed, on your own floor, in your own time — and you know exactly what you spent.

You climb back to the flooded floor, and you keep to the dry stone of the walkway, well back from the edge. You bring the heat up in the bar — it's easier now, a thing your hands know, the tip swelling to that hard orange glow — and then you crouch at the lip of the water and lower the burning iron into the black.

The flood answers in two ways at once.

The first is steam: the cold takes your heat instantly, a hard hiss and a gout of vapor off the surface, and you feel the glow dying in the metal almost before it's under — a breath, two, and the iron is only warm, then cold. The water drinks every bit of the fire you spent on it. Heat does not last here. The flood is its enemy.

The second is the swarm. The warmth pulls them — you watch the dark mass shift under the surface and slide toward the hot point, drawn the way they were drawn to the burning Sentry — and then, as the glowing tip comes among them, they flinch. A knot of them peels back from the iron, recoiling, a pocket of clear black water opening around the heat for as long as the heat lasts. Drawn to it, and afraid of it, in the same motion.

You sit back on your heels and turn it over, and it comes together clean.
