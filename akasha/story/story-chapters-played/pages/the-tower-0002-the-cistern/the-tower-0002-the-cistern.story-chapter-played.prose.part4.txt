The threshold floor pays you best. It is the driest ground in this place, stone above the water's reach, and the wreck of your first fight is still strewn across it: the cooled grey drift of the Ashling's ash, and around it the brittle leavings of whatever the thing had nested in, dry as old paper. You gather what your arms will hold — splintered lengths of long-dead wood, fibrous husk-matter that crackles when you flex it, a double handful of dry kindling that will catch at the first spark. Good fuel. The kind that wants to burn.

Coming back up through the cistern you do worse, and you knew you would — it is a drowned room and near everything in it is wet through. But the high edges, above where the water ever climbed, give you a few lengths of grey driftwood, damp on the skin but sound at the core; they will burn well enough once the dry stuff has made a bed of coals to lay them on. You add them to the armful.

You carry the whole of it back up and set it down beside the brazier-stand — dry wood and husk-fiber and damp driftwood stacked together within reach of the flint striker. An armful of things that will burn, gathered where you left them, waiting now beside the one thing in this Tower that can hold a real and lasting fire.

You set your hand to the laid fuel and call up your ember — not into iron this time but out, a deliberate spark of projected heat pressed into the dry kindling — and it catches at once. The husk-fiber crackles, the splintered wood takes, and within a minute you have what this place has not given you before: a real fire, low and orange and lasting, building its own bed of coals. The damp driftwood, laid at the edge, begins to steam and then to burn. You feed it, and it holds.

Into the heart of it you set the fire-purged rivet — the little heat-clean seed of iron from the Sentry's char — and then you sit back on your heels, open the sense the cinder woke in you, and watch the flame the way you have learned to watch essence: not with your eyes but with that other attention, turned to read what moves beneath the heat.

And you get your answer, clear as you have ever read anything. The flame itself is almost nothing. It throws off the faintest haze of ember essence, yes — a thin smoke of it rising with the real smoke — but it disperses as fast as it climbs, formless, gone, far too thin to gather and nothing at all to grow on. The fire is not a well. It does not pour the way the Ashling's core poured. Burn wood from now until the Tower falls and you would draw not a drop you could use. That door, at least, is closed.

But the rivet is another matter. Where the flame's essence scatters and is lost, the rivet DRINKS — you can feel it plainly, the diffuse heat pulling INTO that small iron seed and gathering there, thickening, the warmth in its core growing denser and surer breath by breath. It is not making essence. It is concentrating it: soaking the fire's scattered heat into a thing of its own element and holding it. Charging, slow but unmistakable. And the principle of it settles in your mind with the quiet click of a thing understood — raw fire throws essence away; a seed of the same element draws it in. The rivet sits in the coals, glowing, slowly filling, and you watch it fill.

Before you settle in to wait, you take the cold-bitten bar — the one you made, still holding its loose knot of the swarm's cold — and carry it to the platform's edge and lower it back under the dark surface, and leave it there to lie. If a heat-seed drinks from a fire, you reason, then perhaps a cold-bitten thing will drink from the water that birthed its essence. You leave it soaking and turn back to the warmth.

Then, with the rivet charging in the coals and nothing to do but wait, you turn an experiment on yourself. You drop and do pushups — slow, honest, down and up — until your arms fold under you and will not push again. You roll over into squats, driving up off those strong legs until they buckle and spill you onto the stone. You take up the rusted iron bar and swing it, forehand and back, the whole honest weight of it, until your shoulders burn through and your grip lets go and the bar drops ringing at your feet.

And when you lie there after, chest heaving, every muscle wrung out and shaking, you reach inside for any sign that it MEANT something — and find none. No warmth of advancement, no quiet click of a thing leveling over. The Tower does not stir for it; the System does not so much as glance your way. Whatever raises a man in this place, it is not this — not reps counted alone in an empty room, not effort for its own sake. Your body did the work, and your body will only be sorer for it; that is all it bought. Strength here is taken from the Tower, it seems, not built in the yard. Worth knowing.

By the time you can sit up again, you feel it: the rivet is full. Through the sense the cinder left in you, the little seed of iron in the coals has gone from drinking to brimming — it will hold no more. So you reach for it, not with your hand but with the other reach, and this time you do take the essence into yourself. But you remember the cinder that nearly cored you, and you do not take it all. You take HALF. You draw the heat up out of the rivet slow and measured, feeling for the exact middle of it, and there you STOP, and let the rest sink back.

It is nothing like that first draw. The cinder was alien, and it fought, and it burned going in; this is your own element, heat to your heat, and it comes home. It flows into you warm and right, and where it settles your Ember runs deeper for it — fuller, surer, the banked fire behind your ribs grown by a real measure. It costs you, yes: a sting under the breastbone as it integrates, a steady pull on your focus to hold the draw exactly to the half. But nothing like the wound the cinder tore. You stop clean at the middle, and the rivet, half-drained, sits glowing in the coals, already beginning to drink again.

And at the far edge of your attention, down in the dark water, the cold-bitten bar answers the very same law in its own key — you can feel it faintly feeding, pulling the thin cold of the cistern into the knot you tied, the loose binding firming a little as it drinks. The fire charges the heat-seed; the water charges the cold one. The principle holds on both poles. Without quite meaning to, you have dug yourself two slow wells in this Tower — one of heat, one of cold — and set one of each to filling.

You sit with the fire and wait, your sense turned down to the dark water, until you feel the cold-bitten bar go full — the knot you tied in it brimming with the cistern's cold, holding no more. So you reach down and draw it up, streaming, into your hands, and you take the river-stone in the other, and you try to do to the stone what the Tower did to the bar: move the cold across, and make it stay.

It does not come easy. The heat is your element and it answers your hands like a hound to its name — but this is the opposite of heat, cold and still and contrary, and pushing it is like writing with the wrong hand. You feel for the middle of the bar's charge and pull half of it loose, and it slips and drags the whole way, fighting your grip, twice nearly scattering before you can press it into the stone. You bear down — your focus burning off fast, far faster than the rivet ever cost you — and you force the cold into the river-stone and tie it there.

It holds. Barely. When you open your sense to check it, the cold is IN the stone — but the binding is a clumsy, loose thing next to even the rough knot in the bar, and you can feel it already straining to seep back out. A beginner's work done left-handed. Still: it is bound, and it is a second cold thing of your making. You lower both of them — the bar to refill, the crude-bound stone to soak and be fed — back under the dark surface, and sit back, your head light from the cost of it.

With the cold work done and both stones soaking, you turn the rest of your patience on the rivet, and run it like a wheel. Let it fill in the coals; reach in with the other reach and draw half its heat into yourself; let it fill again. This is your own element and it is everything the cold was not — each half-draw comes home warm and easy and RIGHT, no fight in it at all, and you feed the brazier from your armful to keep the coals hot and go again.

And somewhere in the turning of it, the Tower teaches you the edge of the thing. The first pulls deepen you for real — you feel the banked fire behind your ribs grow surer with each one. But then it slows. Then it STOPS. The flame is too thin a well, and your body, it seems, will only hold so much heat drawn from so weak a source; past a point the rivet fills and you draw and nothing in you answers, the deepening simply spent. You can run the wheel all you like — it will not pay past where it has already paid.

So you ease off, and let the rivet sit charged in the coals. The stack of fuel by the stand has burned down low while you worked; the flame is steady but the armful is nearly spent. You are deeper in your fire than you were this morning — a real measure, honestly taken — and you have learned, again, the same lesson this Tower keeps teaching: there are no free wells here, and nothing pours forever.

You keep the fire fed and let the rivet drink itself full again in the coals — banking a little more fuel onto the stack from what lies within reach to hold the flame. And when the rivet brims, you take up your rusted iron bar in one hand and reach for the rivet's heat with the other, and this time you do not pull it into yourself, nor into a contrary thing like the stone. You pull it into the iron.

And it is EASY. After the cold stone fought you left-handed every inch of the way, this is like pouring water downhill — Ember into iron, your own element into the metal your own hand already knows, no contrariness in it anywhere. You draw half the rivet's charge up and lay it into the bar in one smooth, unhurried motion, and feel it settle and take hold and STAY: a low banked warmth living in the iron now, steady, persistent, yours. Not a flicker you have to hold up by force, the way you hold an ember in your hand — a charge that sits in the metal on its own and waits. The bar is warm in your grip and stays warm. And somewhere behind your eyes the Tower marks the clean ease of it, the doing-it-right without strain, and moves you up a step for it.

With the bar charged and warm at your side, you let yourself stop. You bank the brazier down to a low steady glow, enough to keep the dark and the cold at the edges, and you lie down on the driest stone you can find near the warmth, and you make yourself breathe slow, and you let go.

You have earned it. The second floor took a great deal out of you — the drowned cistern, the long essence-work, the cold that fought you and the heat that came home, the training that bought you nothing but ache. And now, by the small honest fire you made with your own hands in a place that gives nothing freely, you sleep. Real sleep. The body knits itself back together the way bodies do; the deep wells of your focus fill again from the bottom up; the wrung-out muscle eases and refills. It is hard ground and there is no food in you and it is not a perfect rest — but it is rest, the first true rest this Tower has let you take, and you wake from it whole again, or near enough, the fire still glowing low beside you and the long day's cost paid back.
