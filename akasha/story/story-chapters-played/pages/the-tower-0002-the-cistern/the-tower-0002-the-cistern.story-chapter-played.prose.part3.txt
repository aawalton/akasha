You are scraped hollow again — the big burst took nearly everything you had to give, and the well in your chest rings empty when you reach for it. But you are dry, and standing, and the thing in the water is gone instead of you.

Across the flood, the far wall finishes what it started. The second seam in the sealed stair grinds apart, the last finger's width of stone drawing back, and cool air spills down from the dark above the way it did once before. The way out wanted both wardens. You have given it both.

You stop at the mouth of the far stair — the corroded spiral that climbs up and out, sealed until a few minutes ago — and you lower yourself to sit at its foot, and for once you turn your attention inward instead of out. You take stock of the body you have been spending so hard.

You go looking for thirst. It is there: a dryness at the back of your throat, the faint tack of a mouth gone too long without water. But it is a whisper, not the parched, thick-tongued thirst that everything you have done down here — the sweating, the burning, however many sleeps it has been — should have built. The irony is not lost on you: there is water in every direction, a whole black flood of it, and what you can read of it off the air says foul, mineral, the kind you would regret. Your body is not begging for it anyway.

You go looking for hunger and find the same thing. A low hollow under the ribs, the abstract idea of an appetite — none of the gnawing a body should be screaming after this long. You try to count backward, how many fights, how many times you have slept, and the count slides out of your hands; time in here has no edges to hold.

By any honest reckoning you should be wrecked with thirst and hunger both. You are not. Something here is running the meter slow — letting you spend and spend without quite handing you the bill. You do not know what that means. You only know it is not normal, and that you caught it.

The sac is where it fell, at the lip of the dry stone near where the swarm came apart — a dark, glistening pouch the size of a fist, skinned in something that might be membrane and might be the leeches' own hide, swollen with the fluid inside it. You pick it up. It is heavier than it looks, and the only warmth in it is your own, off your hand; the thing itself is cold.

You turn it over slowly and make yourself read it the way you read everything down here — patiently, letting the details come. The fluid inside shifts like thin tar, sticky where a little has wept through a split in the skin; it clings to your fingers and, after a moment, stings, a mild creeping caustic burn — not enough to hurt, enough to respect. The sac holds its shape. You could throw this: it would carry, and it would break, and whatever it splashed would not enjoy the burn. Raw material too, for the kind of craft you do not have yet — a usefulness in it you can feel without being able to name.

And then, under all of that, the other thing. The same wrongness you felt the first time you held the cinder — the sense of something IN it, banked and waiting, meant to go somewhere. An essence. You reach for it the way you learned to reach for the ember, and your sense closes on nothing it recognizes. Where the cinder answered with heat, this answers with cold: a still, deep, patient cold, the swarm's own element, the exact opposite pole of the fire you already carry. Your analysis gets there where your senses will not — this is a seed, the way the cinder was a seed. You could draw it, the way you drew the ember. It would be nothing like an easy fit with what is already in you.

You work the sac's neck closed and stow it — into a pocket, against your hip, somewhere it will ride without splitting — and set the question of what it is aside for now. Then you turn back across the flood, toward the slumped dark shape on the half-drowned platform, to see what the Sentry left behind.

The water lets you come now. You wade the cold shallows to the husk without anything rising to meet you, and crouch over what the fire made of it. The armored shell is a ruin — plate cracked, the dry-rot inside burned to char and ash, the whole of it collapsed in where the heat went through the neck. But not all of it failed. One piece rode above the fire: a shoulder guard, the curved plate that covered where the neck met the body — scorched but whole, the straps charred, the metal sound. You work it free of the wreck. It has real weight and a real shape; it would sit over a shoulder and turn a blow the bare cloth you are wearing would not. The first armor this place has offered you. You do not put it on — you just have it, in your hands, considered.

You almost stop there. But something small keeps tugging at the edge of the sense you have grown for heat — a thread of warmth in the char that should not still be warm, this long after the fire. You sift the ash where the neck-seam burned, and your fingers close on it: a single rivet of iron, no bigger than a thumb-joint, that the fire did not blacken but PURGED — burned clean and bright, and faintly warm to the touch hours on. You would have walked past it with your eyes; your eyes are not what found it. And when you hold it the way you have learned to hold these things, it answers — the same banked something the cinder had, an essence waiting to be drawn. But where the sac at your hip runs cold against you, this runs WITH the grain: heat, the element already in you. Whatever drawing it would do, it would not be reaching for your opposite. It would be reaching for more of your own.

You carry the pauldron and the warm rivet back across to the foot of the up-stair and set them down beside the sac of ichor — a small cache of maybe-useful things, gathered where you will pass them on the way up. Then you go back over the whole drowned room slowly, deliberately, the way you have learned you have to, because your eyes skate past things unless you make them stop.

It pays out. Half in the water near the entrance lip lies the toppled brazier-stand you had barely registered before: its iron basket still cradles a fistful of dry-ish char, and bolted to its rim is a flint-scrape striker — a fire could be coaxed here, with patience, a real and lasting flame and not just the ember you carry in your chest. From a ring in the wall hangs a coil of rope, four meters of it, rotted and frayed and not to be trusted with your full weight — but rope is rope, and you lift it down and add it to your cache. The walkway slabs themselves, you note, are cracked loose along the ring; one could be pried up for a heavy throw, or braced as a crude shield-board, if it came to that.

And when you crouch at the platform's edge and look down through water gone finally still, you can see the bottom for the first time — and something on it. Under the shelf where the Sentry stood, half-buried in silt, the dull straight line of a second iron bar, twin to the one in your hand, and beside it a smooth pale river-stone the size of an egg. Both are down in the deep, under the cold water that drowned you once already. Reachable — but only by going back in.

The corroded bracket-spikes in the wall you test and abandon; they crumble at a pull, good for nothing. The rest of the room, you have now read to its corners.

You right the toppled brazier-stand, tip the water off it, and carry it — awkward, heavier than it looks, the char sliding in its basket — over to the foot of the up-stair, and set it with the rope and the rest of your cache, up on the dry where the damp will not reach the char. A fire, if you ever need one, banked and waiting to be struck.

Then you go back to the platform's edge, and make yourself do the thing your body least wants. You leave the cache behind, strip the loose weight off you, take three long breaths to load your blood, and go in.

The cold is a fist. It closes on you the instant you are under, driving the air half out of you — but you are ready for it this time, and you do not fight it; you kick. Your legs are the strongest thing you own and they drive you down clean, through the black, one hand out ahead until it finds the lip of the submerged shelf. You feel along the silt beneath it — grit, the soft give of settled muck — and then iron: the straight cold length of the second bar, just where your eyes told you it would be. You close your fist on it, sweep your hand once more, and find the stone too, smooth and round, and take that as well. Then you plant your feet on the shelf and kick UP, and break the surface into the merely-cold air with both prizes in one hand and your breath whooping back into you.

You haul out onto the dry, streaming and shuddering, the cold gone deep into you and your legs burning from the climb — but you went into the water that drowned you, on your own terms this time, and you came back out with what you went for.

You sit with the three points a while before you spend them. The number that pulls at you is the seven — VITALITY, the one single digit left on a sheet of teens, an eyesore, and you have felt already how a point there deepens the body's wells at a stroke. One more would round it, make you a little harder to drown the next time the water closes over you.

But you turn it over, and set it down. The body is not where you are strongest, and not where there is most still to find — you have climbed this place on what your mind does that the rest of you cannot, and there is more in that than you have yet learned to reach. So you spend inward.

The first point you set into your INTELLECT, slow, watching for what answers. It is not loud. It is a widening — the room's facts arranging themselves a half-step faster, the channel-sense you carry running clearer and deeper, more of it to draw on before you touch the bottom. You hold still, and feel the floor of your Focus drop further down beneath you.

The second you set into your PERCEPTION, and wait again.

This one you feel at the edges. The drip and shift of the drowned room come in a little sharper, the dark a little less total, the space between a thing happening and you answering it drawn narrower. It is not the watchfulness you have always gone without — your eyes still skate past what they will — but the raw take of your senses opens a notch wider, and you are quicker off the mark for it.

The last you set into your WILL, and pay it the same close attention.

It lands as steadiness. Something under you firms — the part that holds when fear or cold or another's pressure leans on it, the part that nearly broke beneath the water and held anyway. Your Focus deepens again from this side too, the same well fed from a second spring, and around it a guard you can feel without quite being able to name.

You settle onto the landing at the head of your little cache — the rope and the brazier-stand and the spare bar within arm's reach — and put your back flat against the cold stone, and close your eyes, and go looking inward for the bottom of yourself.

It is a long way down to it. The bursts under the water and the heat that finally ended the Glut emptied your Focus nearly to the floor; what is left is a guttering thing, a candle-end. But you have learned the shape of this now — the slow patient breath, the attention turned off the room and onto the banked warmth behind your ribs — and you hold it, and wait, and do not grasp.

It comes back the way water finds its level: not in a rush, but steadily, the well filling from somewhere deeper than you can see, minute on minute, until the candle-end is a lamp and the lamp is a steady fire and the cold dry floor of you is gone under a depth you can lean your whole weight on again. Thought comes clear and quick. The ember answers the moment you brush it, eager and full.

Your body is another matter, and you feel it plainly as you sit — the bruise-deep ache of the dive still in your legs, the hollow lightness of a body that has not eaten and not truly rested. The quiet behind your eyes does not reach those. But your Focus is back, brim to brim, and that is the thing you came down onto this stone to find.

You gather your materials to you on the dry stone — the sac of swarm-ichor, slack and cold; the spare iron bar you fished from the deep; the smooth river-stone — and you sit with them in your lap, your full-filled Focus humming behind your ribs, and you set out to find whether this place will let you MAKE.

You reach into the sac the way you have learned to reach — not with your hand but with the sense the Ashling's cinder woke in you — and you find the essence in it: that cold caustic thread you have touched before and refused to take into yourself. You do not take it now either. Instead you try a thing you have never tried. You close your will around it and pull it OUT of the sac, up and free, and hold it — a writhing handful of cold that wants nothing to do with you — and you turn it toward the iron bar, and push.

It fights you the whole way. Your grip was shaped on fire, and this is the other thing, the opposite pole; handling it is like trying to carry water in fingers made for coals — it slides, it bites back, it will not pour where you aim. You hold it by main force of reasoning, feeling out the shape of a technique even as you use it, and the holding PULLS: your Focus pouring out of the deep well you just filled, to keep the cold thing in transit between the sac and the iron. And then, all at once, it crosses. The essence sinks into the bar and CATCHES — and the iron goes cold in your hands, truly cold, a caustic sting waking in the metal that was not there a breath ago.

You have done it. And you can feel, even as you sit panting over it, exactly what you have done: the binding is loose, crude, a knot tied by someone who has only just learned there are knots — it will not hold forever, will bleed off as the bar is used, and you have not the skill yet to set it deep. But it HELD. The sac in your lap has gone empty and slack, its essence spent into the iron. The river-stone lies by your knee, untouched, gathered and waiting. And the thing you came down here to learn, you have learned: this place will let you make. Essence can be drawn out of one thing and bound into another. The door is open.
