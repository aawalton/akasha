The heat is fragile here — you can't carry a hot blade into this water and keep it. But the swarm came to the warmth and shrank from the flame, both, and that is a handle. You don't have to kill the thing in the water, where it is strong and your fire dies. You could bait it — dangle heat where you want it to go — and drive it: pull the mass toward a lure and herd it the one direction it cannot survive, up and out, onto the dry stone where it loses cohesion and you do not. The thing that would not follow you onto the walkway could be made to come there.

Not yet. But you know the shape of the kill now. You let the bar cool in your hand, and watch the water settle.

You bring the heat up again, and this time you don't stop at the tip. You push it down the whole top half of the bar — more iron to hold the fire, more of it to burn through before the cold can win. It costs you: the drain behind your eyes opens wider, Focus running out faster to keep that much metal lit. The upper length of the rod wakes to a long ember glow, brighter and broader than the tip alone ever was.

Then you step down off the dry stone into the flood.

The cold bites to the shin at once — and the water answers your heat the way you hoped. Out in the dark the mass stirs and turns toward you, drawn to the warmth at your feet, sliding in close the way it slid toward the burning Sentry. You let the nearest of them come, and when the black water humps up against your legs you drive the glowing bar straight down into the thick of it.

It works the way it worked before, which is to say barely. The hot iron sears through them — a knot of the swarm cooks and comes apart, a worse wound than last time, real — but the rest is water, and you cannot stab water. The mass folds around the bar and closes again behind it. You've hurt it. You haven't stopped it.

And it is so much faster than you.

Before you can set for a second strike the whole near surface of the flood rises against you at once. They find your legs, your waist — cold mouths through your soaked clothes, dozens, then more, a weight that isn't trying to bite so much as to hold, and then to pull. The cold of it saps into you everywhere they touch, taking the strength out of your legs faster than any single wound, and the mass hauls — out, away from the lip, toward the deep.

You go for the retreat you promised yourself: back, now, up onto the dry stone. Your foot finds the underside of a slick slab and skids off it. The pull and the bad footing meet at the worst instant — your leg goes out from under you and the flood takes the space where you were standing. You come down hard into it, the cold closing over your chest, the bar still in your fist but the glow at its top already guttering out, drowned. They are on you and around you and under you, and the floor of the cistern falls away beneath your feet into black.

You are in the water. You did not get out.

There is no plan left in it. There's just the thing in your chest — the banked coal you've spent so carefully, a thread at a time — and the cold mouths dragging you down, and the black closing over your head. So you stop being careful. You reach all the way to the bottom of that heat, take hold of all of it at once, and shove it OUT. Not down the bar. Not anywhere clever. Just out, through your skin, in every direction at once, into everything touching you.

It tears out of you. For half a heartbeat the water against your body stops being water — it's a sheath of steam and ember-light, a hard bloom of heat going off against every leech clamped to you at point-blank, faster than the cold can drink it. They cannot take that. The whole near mass convulses and flings itself away from you, recoiling as one, and the grip — the dozens of cold mouths holding you under — lets go all at once. A pocket of clear, roiling, bubbling black opens around you where a breath ago there was only swarm.

And it's gone. All of it. Where the heat lived there's a hollow now — you poured the coal out to the dregs, and the place in your chest is scoured and ringing, a struck bell with nothing left to give. The discharge cost you on the way out, too: your skin sings where the burst passed through it, that inward scald you know from the cinder, and your arms have gone heavy and far-off.

But you're free, and the water above you is brighter than the water below. You get your legs under you — your legs, the strongest thing you own, and they answer — and drive up and back toward the lip you came off of, the cleared pocket buying you the half-second the swarm needs to re-gather. Your hand finds the cracked edge of the walkway. You haul, your chest scrapes stone, and then you're out, streaming, face down on the dry slabs, the bar somehow still in your fist.

Behind you the flood heaves and re-knits itself, the mass pouring back together where you blew it apart, mounding at the waterline — and stopping there, balked, unable to climb onto the dry. You did not kill it. You got out. You're on the stone, soaked and shaking and burned hollow, nothing left in your chest to light — and you know now, in your body, exactly what the water costs.

You make it back down the spiral stair on legs that don't entirely belong to you, and the cleared first floor takes you in the way it has before — quiet, dry, nothing in it that wants anything from you. You sit, and then you just stop, for a moment, and feel the fact of it: you are alive. You were under the black with the cold mouths on you and the floor gone from beneath your feet, and you are here, breathing, dripping on warm stone. It lands as something close to gratitude, plain and physical.

Then you do the work you know. Feet first, up through the legs and back and shoulders — clench, hold, let go — wringing the last of the shake out of yourself. Four beats in, eight beats out, the exhale always longer, until your pulse comes down off its ledge and your body believes, finally, that the danger is behind you. The wet-dread and the leech-cold and the bell-empty hollow where your heat used to be all loosen their grip, one strand at a time.

And then, because you have nothing left, you let yourself lie down on the warm stone and sleep.

It isn't restful the way a bed is restful — it's the heavy, bottomless sleep of someone who has spent everything, dreamless, more like being switched off than lying down. You don't know how long it lasts. There's no day here to measure it against.

When you come up out of it the difference is in your whole body. Your legs are yours again. The bone-deep tiredness has burned off, and the hollow in your chest where the ember-heat lives has refilled itself — slowly, on its own, while you were under — so that when you reach for it now, it answers, banked and ready. Most of the hurt has closed; the bruises and the cold-sapped weakness of the fight have faded down to nothing. Only the old ache stays, the deep one low in your chest where you first pulled the fire in. That one hasn't moved at all. It sits under everything else, patient, the way it always has. You sit up on your own floor, clear and whole and quiet — and ready to climb again.

But not yet. Before you climb, you want to own the thing that saved you. In the water it tore out of you on its own, all at once, because you were drowning and had no say in it. You'd rather not have to be drowning to do it again.

So you stand off from the heap of broken masonry along the wall, set your feet, and reach for the heat in your chest — not to push it down the bar this time, but to throw it outward, the way it went in the flood. The first time, nothing: the heat just sits, or leaks the familiar way, down toward your hands and the iron. Your body knows how to channel. It doesn't know how to burst. The panic had a key your calm doesn't.

You stop, breathe, and think about what actually happened under the water — not the fear, the mechanism. It wasn't aimed. It was everything, let go at once, through the skin. So you try it not as a push but as a release: you gather a measured part of the heat — only a part, you've learned that much, you are not hollowing yourself out in a dry room — and you let that part GO, outward, all at once.

It works. A short, hard pulse of heat leaves you in a ring, the air shivering with it, and it crosses the few feet to the rubble and breaks against the stone — a crack of sudden heat, dust jumping off the masonry, a dark scorch blooming where the wave lands. Far smaller than the thing in the flood. But it is the same thing, made small and made on purpose, and that is the part that matters. You can meter it. You don't have to drown to use it.

But it's slower now, and there's less of it; the part you cooked does not come back, and what's left pools low at the bottom of its own reach, churning, shy of the lip where the fire caught it. From here, with no cold mouths on your leg to hurry your thinking, you can finally see the whole shape of it. You don't have to go in. You never had to go in. For the first time on this floor, the water is the thing that's losing.

You set your feet on the dry stone and reach all the way down to the bottom of the heat in your chest — not the metered pull of the drills, but near everything you can hold and still stand — and you let it GO, the largest ring you have ever thrown.

Whatever held it together is no longer holding.
