Mari's laugh got there before Aria's did. We were still at the table, the candles low, the world waiting under us. "A *beautiful* new day," she repeated, savoring it, chin coming up off her hand. "He wakes up at the bottom of a grave with the dawn somewhere overhead, and his first thought is *let's rob it.* Cousin, I take back every doubt. He's one of us."

"He is," Aria agreed, warm — and the balcony thinned all the way out, the candlelight going blue, the stone coming up cold around me. "Then let's spend it well. You're going down into the pockets of the dead, monk — so mind whose pockets they are."

The crypt took me back. The kin-fire had burned all night without asking anyone's leave, and by its light the drowned stone showed itself honestly for the first time — not a battlefield anymore, just a place where people had been put down carefully, long ago, and then very carelessly disturbed. My found blood slept in a knot near the fire, the son in the middle of them, iron gone from him but the shape of it still pressed into how he lay, curled around the mending. I let them sleep. This work was quieter with fewer questions in it.

Mari was there in the blue dark with me — the wet-haired girl again, the one who'd walked herself from a cage to *aimed* in a single night — and she fell in at my shoulder without being asked.

The Warden's alcove gave first. Whatever the Warden of the Well had been before the well, it had been a keeper of records — and keepers never stop. Under the collapsed lectern, wrapped in oilcloth that had outlived three floods: a ring of black iron keys, seven of them, each stamped with a sigil I didn't know and one I did — the same mark that had been scorched into the crossing where I'd refused the bargain. She crouched beside me, hair dripping, and touched that one with a claw, once, very lightly. "That door's still shut somewhere," she said. "And now we're the ones holding its teeth."

The dead themselves I robbed gently, and they paid me gently back. Grave-goods, the honest kind: a flask of oil that burned without smoke when I tested a drop on the kin-fire; a coil of silk cord thin as a lie and strong enough to hold her full weight when she swung on it, delighted, from the broken vault rib; a handful of old coin worth more as metal than money. On the sad dragon's hoard-ledge — the hole's lonely heart, where I'd half-expected treasure and found mostly sorrow — one true thing: a plate of cold-etched bronze, palm-sized, that bit my fingers with chill the moment I lifted it. A map. Not of anywhere we'd walked. Ridgelines like knives, a coastline gone white, and at its center a ring of peaks around an absence — a place the etcher had refused, or feared, to draw. The plate was cold because it remembered where it came from. The frozen corner of the world had just put its calling card in my pack.

And for the son — because I looked, because of course I looked — the crypt gave up its last kindness: a niche behind the alcove, and in it a physician's roll older than the flood. Bone needles, dried herbs gone to dust, useless — but folded at its heart, a copper tag on a leather cord, stamped with a name. His name. The one the iron had been trying to file off him. I stood there a long moment holding three hundred years of somebody's proof that he had been a person before he was a punishment.

She watched me pocket it next to the stone she knew I kept. She didn't say anything. She bumped her shoulder against mine, once, hard, the way you'd shove a door closed against weather, and went back to swinging her silk cord like a girl who absolutely had not just been caught being moved.

And then the blue went gold at the edges and we were at the table again, just for a breath — Mari in her chair, wings lazy, pointing at Aria over her cup. "If you make me do the *...da?* thing again with a copper name-tag, I'm flipping this table."

"No promises," Aria said, and meant it, and turned the candlelight back toward me. "So. Take the count, monk: seven black keys and one of them belongs to a door you already refused once. Smokeless oil, good cord, dead men's coin. A cold bronze map that aches toward the deep ice. And a name, for when he wakes and needs one. The dawn's up top, the stair is long, and your people will be stirring soon — they'll be slow, and hungry, and the son will need carrying the first miles whether his pride likes it or not."

She set the die down, cool and unrolled, and gave me the knowing smile across the low candles.

"You're richer than you were when the sun rose. Now spend the rest of the day. Where do you lead them — up into the dawn and which way after: toward food and walls to mend in, or does that cold little map already have its hook in you?"
