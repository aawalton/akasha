The Crystal Salt Desert announced itself in sound first.

Not the sound of wind — there was no wind on Floor 20, no organic movement in the architecture, nothing living in the air. What the floor produced instead was a low, continuous frequency that came from the formations themselves: the tall crystal spires catching whatever frequency the Tower maintained in its deep bones and translating it into something audible at the edge of hearing. Not a hum exactly. More like the sound a glass makes when you've run a wet finger along its rim — sustained, precise, the particular register of something vibrating in its natural frequency.

Solenne felt it in the Conductor before she heard it with her ears.

The signal through the Conductor was different from the Ash Plain. Not better or worse — different, the way a room with stone walls sounds different from a room with wood. The Aspects were all present and at their working register, each one reading cleanly through the medium of the crystal floor: Steadfast solid and patient, Clearvoiced picking up detail faster than the other floors, Brightspirit — the particular quality of Nadia — at the open-present register she'd been running since Floor 19. The crystal amplified certain frequencies. She filed it for later use.

Formation three. Tomás had called it and the party was in it: Dayo on point with the anchor-quality she brought to everything, Tomás and Nadia on the flanks at range, Vesper and Miriam in the protected center, Solenne at the back where the Conductor read could take in all six signals simultaneously.

The floor ahead was wide open salt flat, the surface alternating between packed crystal granulate (solid, no noise, footstep pattern close to stone) and loose granulate (not solid, fine as sand, the sound of movement different and relevant). Between the packed sections, formations rose at varying heights: some at knee level, catching light at the exact angle to create shadow pools with sharp edges; some at chest height, creating a visual obstruction that forced the eye to work; some tall enough to be called spires, twenty feet and more, the crystal surfaces splitting light into angles that didn't exist in outdoor geometry. The light came from inside them, as it had at the door — not from above, not from a source she could identify, but generated by the formations themselves, the mineral structure conducting something into luminescence.

Tomás moved slightly forward on the right flank.

He had said: "The entry geometry is different from Floors 13 through 18." He had not said more than that, and she had not asked. She understood what she was seeing now, thirty feet into the floor: the crystal formations created false-depth corridors. Parallel crystal faces produced a mirror effect — a passage that looked clear at twenty-five feet was actually two surfaces reflecting each other, and the endpoint was a reflection rather than a reality. The passage was shorter, or longer, or around a corner that didn't appear to exist. The floor did not honor sight lines.

She filed this with the Resonant Stalker density divergence and did not say it aloud. The party would have the floor in front of them and the floor would explain itself faster than any briefing.

---

The Saltback came out of the packed flat twenty feet ahead with no visual warning.

What Tomás had described as the tell: a quarter-second of the surface doing something wrong. She saw it a fraction of a second after Tomás did — the particular shift in the flat granulate, the surface tension changing as something moved beneath it. Dayo was already adjusting, her weight distribution shifting from the forward-lean of patrol into the anchor posture she'd been building for twenty floors, the stance that could hold a direct impact without transferring it.

The Saltback burst.

Surface-ambush type: it had been below the packed flat with the stillness of something that could wait, and it came up all at once — the crystalline hide surface-bright, each plate angled, the motion not the fluid emergence of an organic thing but the mechanical burst of something that had been coiled and released. Dayo took the impact on full armor, the force distributed the way she'd been distributing force since Floor 1, and held.

The hide. Tomás had noted it in the brief and Solenne had filed it: crystalline hide, the surface of each plate a smooth angled face. Pattern Break would scatter against the plate surfaces rather than disrupting the creature's Mana structure. She read the Brightspirit signal through the Conductor — Nadia already adjusting her angle, hunting for the gap in the plate geometry, the seam between plates where the hide didn't fully cover. The seam would be there. Every surface-ambush creature had a seam, a functional gap the hide couldn't close without losing mobility.

Vesper's barrier geometry went up fast — not the full-containment architecture she'd built for the Ashcrown boss, but the minimal-construct version: two faces closing the creature's retreat angle, the geometry tight and economic. Not lethal. The Fire Immunity architecture that had made direct damage irrelevant against Drifters didn't apply here, but the Saltback's hide made direct Mana-construct damage inefficient, and Vesper understood efficient before she understood anything else.

Miriam held her MP. Tomás moved.

He moved at a specific angle — not the straightforward flanking position, but something that accounted for the false-depth geometry of the corridor behind the Saltback. If the creature retreated toward what looked like open passage, Tomás's angle would be positioned to cut off the actual exit rather than the apparent one. She had not told him to do this. He had been working the false-depth geometry in his model since they stepped through the door.

Nadia found the seam on the right-side neck plate.

The Pattern Break landed in the gap — the disruption traveling through the exposed Mana-weave at the seam, the crystalline plating not a factor when the skill was aimed at what the plating couldn't cover. The Saltback's burst-pattern faltered. Dayo moved into the falter with the particular decisive quality she brought to gaps in opponent timing: not pushing, not overcommitting, just moving into the space the falter created with exactly the force required to end it.

Twenty-two seconds.

The body was on the packed flat. The crystalline hide caught the ambient light in the same way the formations did — the plates still conducting luminescence even separated from the creature. Vesper was already crouching, her hands moving with the quick-assessment quality she brought to useful materials. She did not ask. She extracted two plates cleanly, the plate geometry intact, and added them to the raw materials section of the carry stack without comment.

"It looks different when it's not trying to kill you," Nadia said.

She was looking at the plates in Vesper's hands — the way the light worked through the crystalline surface, the angles.

"Yes," Vesper said.

She was already back on her feet.

---

The second encounter came from the false-depth.

They had moved through two corridors on the packed flat — the formations at chest height on either side, the sight lines reading clearly ahead — when Tomás stopped and held a fist up.

The party stopped.

He was looking at the corridor ahead. The opening was clear: twenty feet to what appeared to be open flat on the far side, straight passage, no obstructions. She read his expression. The working face — the one he wore when his model had produced something he hadn't expected and he was revising it.

Then she saw what he'd seen.

The shadow pattern was wrong. The light inside the formations should have produced a shadow at a specific angle given the corridor geometry — the tall spire to the left casting a shadow on the flat at a predictable angle. The shadow was there, but it was shorter than the geometry required. The corridor was shorter than it appeared. The passage was a reflection.

She counted the actual distance.

Twelve feet, not twenty. The Saltback was on the other side of the reflection, which put it at approximately eight feet from Tomás's current position. Burst range.

Tomás put his weight onto his back foot without a word.

Dayo was already rotating — the Steadfast signal through the Conductor shifting from patrol to anchor with the smoothness of twenty floors of building exactly this response, the stance adjustment so practiced it read as stillness from the outside. She covered the distance to Tomás's position in three steps and put herself between Tomás and the passage end.

The Saltback burst.

It was eight feet, not twenty. The impact was harder than the first encounter — less distance for the creature to lose velocity, more of its burst force arriving all at once. Dayo held. Her HP registered a number Solenne had not seen in several floors: not critical, but a real cost. The impact had been harder than the floor's first encounter by a meaningful margin.

Vesper's geometry was already going up on the flanks. Nadia was adjusting for the seam — the neck-plate seam, the same gap she'd found in the first encounter, but the angle was different here in the corridor geometry and she was hunting it rather than seeing it immediately.

Miriam put a hand on Dayo's shoulder.

One hand, contact, the Warmhearted signal through the Conductor shifting into the active-use register rather than the passive monitoring she'd been running since they entered. Not a full heal — not the significant MP investment of Mend — but the targeted micro-repair that Solenne had been watching Miriam develop since Floor 14, the precision application that addressed the specific structural stress rather than overall HP. Dayo's HP registered a correction upward. Miriam's MP registered a cost.

Nadia found the seam.

The Pattern Break landed clean. Twenty-eight seconds.

Nobody spoke for a moment. Vesper extracted two more plates from the hide — quicker this time, the process already familiar — and the carry stack received them.

"The corridor geometry is going to keep doing that," Tomás said.

Not a question. Not an apology. A data point released into the shared space.

"I know," Dayo said.

She was already checking the next corridor's shadow geometry — not waiting for Tomás to run the model, running her own version of it: where the light source should be, where the shadow should fall, where the discrepancy was. She had been watching him do this for twenty floors. She had some of the model now. Not all of it. Enough.

Solenne read the Clearvoiced signal through the Conductor: present, open, picking up something at range. She turned her attention toward the open flat ahead.

The Resonant Stalkers were at 60 meters.

She had known they would be there — the intel had said they would be there, Tomás's advance pass had placed them there, Brennan's confirmation had confirmed it. Knowing and seeing were different. There were six of them, spread across the open salt flat in a spacing that was not a pack pattern and not a random distribution: each one positioned at the edge of the corridor's exit with the particular attentiveness of something that had been waiting.

They were not moving toward the party.

They were pacing. Each one moving along its 60-meter radius with the measured quality of something that was tracking a signal and maintaining a precise relationship to it. Not circling — pacing, back and forth along an arc, the arc perfectly calibrated to the bond-aura tracking range. They knew exactly where the party was. They were choosing not to close.

The Conductor picked them up in a way she had not expected.

Not an Aspect signal — they didn't carry Harmony Aspects, they weren't Awakened humans, they were Tower creatures. But the bond-aura tracking mechanic meant they were responding to bond-aura, and bond-aura was Resonance, and Resonance was what the Conductor was built to read. What she received from the direction of the Stalkers was faint and consistent and not nothing: a frequency she had no category for yet. Not hostile. Not neutral. Something attentive.

She filed it.

"They're holding at 60," she said. Not quietly — at normal volume, the information released into the shared space the way Tomás released information. "Six. Spread. They're not closing."

"Yet," Tomás said.

---

The rest node was visible from the flat.

Tomás had known where it was — had scouted it on the advance pass, had included its location in the floor schematic — but she had not understood what he meant by "crystal hollow" until she could see it from the packed flat. Three spires had grown together at their tops, the crystal faces fusing where they met, creating a vaulted interior accessible through a narrow entrance on the south face. The interior would be larger than the entrance suggested. The entrance itself was just wide enough for one person at a time.

The Stalkers paced at 60 meters.

The party made the rest node in one clean movement — Dayo first through the entrance, Tomás behind, the formation collapsing into single-file and then expanding again on the inside. The Stalkers did not close. They held their radius, pacing, and she could feel the attentive frequency in the Conductor even inside the crystal hollow.

The hollow was what Tomás's schematic had said: larger inside, the vaulted ceiling of fused spires creating a space with room for all six people without the cramped quality of a corridor rest node. The walls were crystalline throughout — ambient light from inside the crystal faces, consistent illumination without a source. Cooler than the flat. The floor of the hollow was dry packed salt, unmarked. Along the inner curve of the three spires, the Tower had placed what the rest nodes always contained: supply refresh, recovery access.

And a fire-bowl.

She had not seen a fire-bowl since Floor 12. The Tower placed them in rest nodes on specific floors — not all of them, not reliably, but sometimes, and when it did she had come to understand it as something the Tower was offering rather than something it had generated automatically. A fire-bowl was for sitting around. It was not tactical. It said: you have time and this is what time is for.

Dayo looked at it.

She was not the type to express reaction without function, but there was a brief thing on her face — the recognition of something she hadn't known she wanted until she saw it.
