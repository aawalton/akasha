"Highly concentrated mana," I repeated. "What does that mean?"

Marcus hadn't moved from his place. His healed arm rested across his stomach. His eyes were fixed on me with concern.

"I'm not the best person to explain this," he said. "I never studied mana theory. Never had the education for it."

He sat up slowly, bracing himself with his good arm.

"But I know enough to tell you that what you're doing shouldn't be happening."

His gaze moved to my mouth. To the faint trails of blue vapor that curled from my lips with each exhale.

"Mana Breath is known even among regular soldiers," Marcus continued. "It's not some hidden art. There are books. Cheap ones. Badly printed, sold under the table at market stalls and in back alleys. The kind of thing a desperate man buys when he's been passed over for promotion one too many times and starts thinking there might be a shortcut."

He rubbed the back of his neck.

"When you're at the bottom. When you've been told your whole life that you'll never form a Circle, never amount to anything, never be more than a body between the enemy and the people who matter. Some men break. Some drink. And some buy a ten-copper pamphlet from a street vendor and spend their nights trying to breathe mana like they did in the past."

"Do they succeed?" I asked.

"Some." Marcus shrugged. "Most don't. They breathe wrong, strain something internal, give up after a week of headaches and nosebleeds."

He paused.

"But the lucky ones are the ones who fail."

I frowned. "Why?"

Marcus leaned forward. His elbows rested on his knees.

"Mana Breath is a substitute for Circles," he said. "Not a complement. A substitute. The two systems are incompatible. Once your body learns to channel mana through breathing, through the lungs, through the tissue itself, the pathways that Circles would normally form along are permanently closed."

He held up one hand, fingers spread.

"Imagine a river. Water flows from the mountains to the sea along a specific course. Circles are like canals dug alongside that river. Controlled. Structured. They redirect the water into channels that a mage can use."

His fingers closed into a fist.

"Mana Breath is like flooding the valley. The water goes everywhere. Through the soil. Through the roots. Through every crack in the earth. It saturates everything, but it doesn't follow channels. It can't. The flood has already carved its own paths, and those paths are incompatible with canals."

He opened his fist again.

"Once you've flooded the valley, you can never dig the canals."

I stared at him.

"You're saying I can never form Circles."

"I'm saying Mana Breath prevents their formation entirely," Marcus said. "Permanently. No Circles. No external projection. No spell matrices."

His eyes held mine.

"It also makes learning some techniques harder, some impossible. Even archaic ones. Even the old methods that predate the Circle system. Mana Breath reshapes the internal pathways so thoroughly that alternative channeling methods have nothing clean to build on."

The cell was quiet.

I sat with the information and turned it over.

The one technique I'd managed to learn, the single method I'd discovered through a hundred deaths and endless repetition, had locked every other door.

My luck was staggering in its consistency.

"Only—" Marcus started, then stopped.

I looked at him.

His brow was creased. The concern hadn't faded. If anything, it had deepened.

"I've never seen anyone use Mana Breath to the point where the mana becomes visible," he said quietly. "Not in person. Not in any account I've read or heard. The technique is supposed to produce trace enhancement. Marginal improvements to physical performance. The kind of thing that gives a regular soldier an edge in a fight."

He gestured toward my mouth.

"That is not trace enhancement."

The blue vapor curled lazily from between my lips. I could feel it. The cool-mint burn in my lungs, denser now than it had been in the arena.

"Whatever you're doing," Marcus said, "it's something else. Something I've never encountered. The density alone, it must be unique."

He shook his head.

"No wonder the monks noticed."

I didn't know what to say.

I didn't know how I'd done it. Didn't know if I could do it again. The arena had been desperation and fury and a hundred loops of accumulated rage channeled through lungs.

Whether that was reproducible or a one-time eruption, I had no way to tell.

My silence seemed to answer the question Marcus hadn't asked.

"You don't know how you did it," he said.

"No."

He nodded slowly. As if he'd expected that.

Gerret had been listening from his makeshift bed, both ears swiveled forward.

His amber eyes were wide.

"Wait," Gerret said. He pushed himself upright, wincing as the movement pulled at his chest wound. "Wait, wait, wait."

He pointed at me with one furred hand.

"You can use mana? Actually use it? Channel it through your body?"

"Apparently."

"That's amazing!" The word burst out of him. His tail, which I hadn't noticed before because he kept it tucked beneath his robe, thumped against the walls. "Do you understand what that means? If you can channel mana, maybe there's a way to learn magic. Something beyond just—"

"He can't."

Marcus's voice cut through Gerret's enthusiasm like a blade through cloth.

The lupine boy's ears drooped.

"If Kaspar has learned Mana Breath," Marcus said, not unkindly, "he will never be capable of forming Circles. Magic, real magic, can't be done without Circles. Nothing that leaves his body in any structured form."

He looked at Gerret.

Gerret's jaw worked. The excitement drained from his face, leaving behind a complicated expression.

Lyra shifted in her corner.

Her wings rustled softly against the stone.

"It's still better than most of us," Lyra said.

"Mutants can barely learn to sense mana at all," she continued. "Our bodies were changed by it. Altered so deeply that the normal pathways don't exist. Most of us can't feel mana moving through us any more than a fish can feel the water it swims in."

She flexed one wing slightly.

"These grew because mana rewrote my body before I was born. It saturated everything. Changed the bones and muscles. Changed the way my lungs work and my heart beats."

Her jaw tightened.

"But it didn't leave me anything I could use."

She looked at me.

"You can feel it. You can use it. Don't complain about closed doors when some of us never had a door to begin with."

I held her gaze for a moment, then nodded.

She was right.

I had nine cores. I had a technique. I had something, however limited, however primitive, however dismissed by every monk and scholar and Academy textbook in existence.

It wasn't magic.

But it wasn't nothing.

Before anyone could speak again, a sound cut through the cell.

Metal on stone. The scrape and clatter of trays being set down. A smell followed, warm and rich.

Food.

Two soldiers appeared at the bars. They slid four plates through the gap at the bottom of the door without ceremony, then retreated down the corridor.

I pulled my plate toward me and looked down.

Better than yesterday.

Not dramatically. The bread was the same coarse grain, the same dense texture. But there was more of it. A thicker slice, accompanied by a wedge of hard cheese and a strip of dried meat that actually looked like it had come from an animal rather than a boot.

A small bowl of porridge sat beside the bread.

The improvement was marginal. A step above rather than a leap toward comfort.

But after the arena the warmth of that porridge against my palms felt divine.

We ate in silence.

The quiet wasn't uncomfortable. Spoons scraped against bowls. Bread tore with soft sounds.

Between bites, fragments of conversation surfaced.

"The residential quarter near the bay," Marcus said, gesturing vaguely with his bread. "That's where most of the city live. Shipbuilders. Dockworkers. The people who keep the port running."

"Is the port important?" I asked.

"It's everything. Silberwald's capital sits at the mouth of the largest natural harbor on the continent. Half the kingdom's trade flows through it. Grain from the eastern provinces. Timber from the north. Mana Ore from the mountains."

He chewed. Swallowed.

"And military supplies. Everything headed for the southern front gets routed through the port if it's too heavy for overland transport."

"What about the Academy?" I pressed. "How does that work?"

Marcus shrugged. "Above my pay grade. Literally. The Academy is a world unto itself. Its own laws, hierarchy and budget. The Crown funds it, but the Headmaster runs it, and what happens behind those walls stays behind those walls."

Gerret snorted. "Unless you're a noble kid who washes out. Then the whole kingdom hears about it."

Lyra spoke between bites of cheese, her voice muffled.

"The lower city is worse than the upper. The mutant quarters are near the docks. Packed together. No mana-glow in the streets. The guard patrols stop two blocks before the district line."

She said it without bitterness.

"Is that where you're from?" I asked.

"No." Her jaw tightened. "I'm from a village. Three weeks' travel east. But I've heard stories."

She didn't elaborate.

Gerret yawned, his jaw stretching wide enough to display every one of his too-many teeth. He moved gingerly back to his bed and lowered himself.

Lyra retreated to her corner without a word.

The rest lasted less than two hours.

The cell door opened.

Two soldiers stood in the gap. Their eyes found me.

"Slave One Hundred Forty-Seven. Afternoon bout. On your feet."

I was already standing.

Marcus looked up.

"Be careful," he said. "The afternoons are different."

Gerret's ears swiveled toward me.

"Good luck," he said. Then, quieter: "Watch for weapons you don't recognize. They let the veterans pick first."

Lyra didn't speak.

From her corner, she met my eyes for a single moment. A nod. Small and direct.

I walked out of the cell.

The soldiers flanked me. One ahead, one behind. We climbed the stairs. Followed the corridors. Passed through the stone passages .

Yet it was different.

The building trembled.

Not from structural weakness. From sound. The vibration that traveled through the stone. Thousands of feet stamping against stone seating in unison. Thousands of voices raised in a roar.

I emerged into the staging area beneath the arena.

This was different from the morning.

The weapons weren't scattered across the sand for competitors to snatch mid-sprint. Instead, they were arranged on racks along the wall. Dozens of them. Real weapons this time.

Steel, not wood.

Swords of every length and configuration. Short gladius-style blades. Longswords. Bastard swords. Curved sabers. Heavy cleavers.

Spears. Halberds. Maces. Flails.

Shields of every size, from bucklers no larger than a dinner plate to full tower shields that would cover a man from chin to knee.

A trident.

I walked along the rack slowly. My fingers trailed across pommels and grips.

The hand-and-a-half sword sat near the center of the display.

Its blade was longer than the short sword I'd carried in Grezheim. The grip was wrapped in leather that had been worn smooth by previous hands. The guard extended in a simple crossbar.

It was nothing special. No runes, enchantments or artisan's mark.

But when I lifted it, the weight settled into my palms. Heavier than the short blade. Lighter than the decorative sword used on the dungeon.

I could use both hands or one.

I took it.

The gate rose.

The arena swallowed me.

The noise hit first.

The morning session had drawn hundreds. The afternoon brought thousands.

Every tier was packed. The monks occupied their usual section in the lower tiers, but they were islands in a sea of faces that stretched to the highest rows. Commoners. Soldiers. Merchants. Laborers who had left their workshops and their stalls to fill the upper galleries.

Nobles filled the middle tiers.

Above them all, on the high platform, a single figure.

One prince.

The one in the black uniform. The silver cross on his chest.

His posture was relaxed. One arm draped over the carved stone armrest. His chin slightly raised. His eyes moved across the sand below with the attention.

The other seat was empty.

I stepped onto the sand.

The roar swelled. Then faltered.

I could feel the crowd's assessment landing on me. The messy dark hair. The thin frame. The robe filthy and torn. My appearance marked me as anything other than a veteran fighter.

The disapproval was almost instant.

Across the arena, the far gate ground open.

My opponent emerged.

He was older than me. Broader. His arms were thick. His head was shaved, the stubble dark against a scalp marked with old wounds.

He carried a trident.

The crowd recognized him. The roar that greeted his appearance was visceral. Feet stomped. Voices chanted something I couldn't make out.

He raised one fist to the crowd. Turning in a slow circle, drinking the noise, feeding on it. His smile was wide and practiced.

A monk's voice boomed from the lower tier.

"AFTERNOON BOUT! INDIVIDUAL COMBAT!"

The crowd's roar intensified.

"SLAVE ONE HUNDRED FORTY-SEVEN!"

Silence.

A scattering of noise. Curious murmurs. A few jeers.

"VERSUS SLAVE THIRTY-ONE! TWENTY-THREE VICTORIES! THE TRIDENT!"

The arena erupted.

The Trident soaked it in. He planted the butt of his trident against the sand, crossed his arms over the shaft, and grinned at me from across forty paces of open ground.

The grin said everything.

He had already won. In his mind. In the crowd's mind. The outcome was decided before the first strike.

I planted my feet.

The hand-and-a-half sword hung at my side, point down. My right hand gripped the leather. My left hand rested near the pommel.

I breathed.

The mint-burn bloomed in my chest. The mana flowed into my lungs, pooled, and began its slow radiation outward through my body.

"BEGIN!"

The Trident charged.

He crossed the sand with long, pounding strides.

I didn't move.

His approach was aggressive. Designed to close distance fast, to ram the weapon into the space I occupied before I could establish a defense. The weapon's reach was its greatest advantage. If he kept me at the end of those prongs, my sword couldn't touch him.

He committed to a lunging thrust.

The trident drove forward, all three prongs aimed at the center of my chest.

I stepped off.

One step. Left foot crossing right, body rotating, the trident's prongs sliding past my ribs.

His momentum carried him forward. Two steps past where I'd been standing.

I didn't counter.

I watched.

He recovered. Planted the shaft. Spun the trident sweeping in a wide horizontal arc aimed at my midsection.

I leaned back. The prongs passed beneath my chin.

Another thrust. Faster this time. Lower, aimed at my hip.

I parried with the flat of the sword, redirecting the shaft sideways. The trident's weight made it unwieldy in close, and the deflection cost me nothing.

He pulled back. Reset. Came again.

I read him the way I'd learned to read the swordsman.

Through instinct and repetition.

The Trident was different. His weapon was different. His style was different.

But the principles were the same.

His shoulders rotated before the trident moved. His leading foot set the angle of attack. When his grip shifted on the shaft, widening or narrowing, it predicted whether the next strike would be a thrust or a sweep.

He had strength. Real strength. The kind that came from real combat.

Twenty. Thirty? Fifty. He may have fifty fights. Something around that. Still, just this morning, I had more than a hundred.

Even if my face doesn’t show it, I have more experience. No matter how small the difference may be.

His attacks were broad. Committed to arcs that covered maximum space but sacrificed control. The trident was a weapon that rewarded aggression and punished subtlety.

Overwhelm.

Intimidate.

Drive forward until the opponent broke.

Against someone else, it would have worked.

Against someone that doesn't fear death. Not that much.

His next combination came. A feint high, a sweep low, a thrust to follow.

I slipped the feint. Jumped the sweep. The thrust I caught on the crossguard of my sword and redirected past my shoulder.

The opening appeared.

His right side exposed, his leading arm extended too far, his weight committed forward.

I could have killed him.

The thrust was there. A clean kill that would have ended the fight in a single heartbeat.

The crowd was screaming for it.

Their voices merged into a single, continuous roar. They wanted blood. They wanted the spectacle they'd paid for.

I didn't give it to them.

Instead of thrusting, I rotated the sword in my grip.

The flat of the blade came around in a horizontal arc that caught the Trident across the temple.

His head snapped sideways. His knees buckled. The weapon's shaft slipped in his grip. He stumbled three steps to the right, his eyes glazing, his mouth slack.

He didn't go down.

I closed the distance before he could recover.

The pommel of my sword drove into his jaw. A short, brutal strike that used the weapon's weight as a bludgeon. The impact snapped his head back.

His guard dropped.

I hit him again. The crossguard this time, raking across his forearm.

He swayed.

His eyes rolled white. Face-first into the sand.

He didn't move.

The arena went quiet.

The quiet lasted three heartbeats.

Then the booing started.

It began in the lower tiers.

"COWARD!"

The word erupted from a dozen throats at once.

"WHAT KIND OF FIGHT WAS THAT?"

"BOOOOOO!"

Tier by tier, section by section, until the entire arena were enraged. Cups were thrown. Scraps of food arced through the air.

The nobles maintained a studied silence. Their expressions ranged from bored to mildly offended, as if I'd served them a meal that was insulting.

The monks wrote.

I stood over the unconscious body of my opponent and looked up at the crowd.

Thousands of mouths open, screaming their disappointment at someone who wouldn't bleed for their entertainment.

I raised both arms.

Spread them wide. Palms up. The sword dangling from my right hand, its blade clean, not a drop of blood on the steel.

I held the pose for three full seconds.

The booing doubled in volume.

I grinned.

The staging corridor was quiet after the arena's noise.

No soldier clapped me on the shoulder. No monk appeared to offer congratulations the way one had after the morning session.

I walked between my escorts in silence. Their faces were blank.

The absence of acknowledgment was its own message.

In the morning, I had won and been praised. Invited to the afternoon bout. Told that success would benefit my cell.

Now I had won again, and the corridor was empty.

Winning wasn't enough.

The cell door opened. I stepped through.

Three faces turned toward me.

Marcus was the first to read the answer in my expression.

"You won," he said. Not a question.

"I won."

Gerret's tail thumped against his pallet. Both ears stood straight.

"How?" he demanded. "Who was it? Was it one of the big ones? Did you use the mana thing again? Tell me everything."

"The Trident," I said. "Twenty-three wins."

Gerret's eyes went wide. "The Trident? That guy's a monster. How—"

"He's strong," I said. "But he fights like a charging bull. Straight lines. Big swings. I waited for him to overcommit, then hit him with the flat of the blade until he stopped standing."

Lyra's amber eyes found mine from her corner.

"You didn't cut him," she said.

It wasn't a question.

"No."

Something shifted in her expression. The hard edges softened by a fraction.

She didn't say anything else.

The four of us sat in the cell and let the victory settle. It wasn't much. A single bout won. A ranking marginally improved. Better rations, maybe. A slightly longer rest between fights.

Gerret talked for a while. Rapid-fire questions about the fight that I answered in fragments between stretches of silence. Marcus commented about tactical observations. Lyra listened.

The mana-glow dimmed as evening fell.

We settled into our beds.

Sleep came easier than it should have.

I closed my eyes.

The dark was absolute.

I stood in it.

A smile appeared.

It materialized from the nothing.

A skeleton's face, with a wide grin.

Two points of light appeared above the grin.

Golden coins.

They sat in the hollows where eyes should have been.

The grin widened.

Beneath me, beneath the nothing, something moved.

I looked down.

A river.

The water was black. It moved without sound.

But I could feel it.

I couldn't see where it came from.

I couldn't see where it went.

The skeletal grin spoke.

"One hundred and thirteen."

The voice had no source.

"One hundred and thirteen times you died to cross this day."

A pause. The grin didn't move.

"To cross the margins of a single river in your destiny."

The gold coins tilted. Regarding me. Measuring.

"How many of those deaths were necessary?"

I didn't answer.

"How many times did your pride keep you from leaving the others behind?"

How many times could I have survived if I'd fought only for myself? How many loops could I have skipped if I'd let Gerret fall and focused on my own opponent? How many deaths were purchased not by necessity but by the stubborn, irrational refusal to walk past someone who was bleeding?

The grin waited.

I didn't answer.

"You could have left them," the voice said. "On the first day. On the tenth. On the fiftieth."

The gold coins burned brighter.

"But you couldn't bear it."

A pause.

"Not because you are kind. Not because you are noble. Not because the strong protect the weak."

The grin stretched wider. Too wide.

"Because your pride would not allow it."

Pride.

"You would rather die a hundred times than admit you cannot save everyone. You would rather bleed and break and reset and bleed again than accept the limitation. Than look at a girl with wings and a boy with ears and say: I am not enough."

"Pride is the sin that wears virtue's face," the voice said. "It tells you that your suffering is meaningful. That your sacrifice is noble. That the pain you choose is different from the pain inflicted upon you."

The gold coins dimmed.

"It is not."

The face disappeared.

"Pride, young Kaspar. This is your sin."
