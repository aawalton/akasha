
# Czech Citizenship Paths (May 2026)

Czechia operates under Act No. 186/2013 Coll. (effective 1 Jan 2014), which fully permits dual/multiple citizenship (no renunciation required). Standard naturalization demands ~10 years total residence (5 temporary + 5 permanent), a Czech B1 language exam, and a Czech Life & Institutions civics test. The declaration paths under §31 (descendants of Czechs/Czechoslovaks who lost or never had citizenship) and §32 (former CSFR citizens) — expanded in 2019 and again in June 2024 to cover up to the fourth generation — are the strongest diaspora-friendly routes in the EU.

## Birth on Territory (Jus Soli) — Effectively Closed

- Czechia is strictly jus sanguinis. Birth on Czech soil alone confers nothing.
- Narrow anti-statelessness exception (§5): child born in CZ to two stateless parents where at least one has 90+ day authorized stay.
- Foundlings (§7) acquire citizenship if found in CZ and parentage unknown (age threshold ~3 years).
- §38: stateless child born in CZ and entrusted to foster care acquires citizenship by final court decision.
- Timeline: at birth or by court order. Backlog: not applicable.

## Descent (Jus Sanguinis) at Birth — Automatic

- Child of at least one Czech-citizen parent acquires Czech citizenship at birth, regardless of birthplace.
- No registration deadline post-2014 (the old "Article 17" automatic-loss-on-foreign-naturalization rule was repealed).
- Verification: apply for a Certificate of Czech Citizenship at the consulate; processing typically 6–18 months.

## Restored Citizenship by Declaration — §31 (Descendants)

- Category: declaration (not discretionary naturalization). Right-based, not application-based.
- Eligibility: descendants (child / grandchild / great-grandchild / **great-great-grandchild** post-June 2024) of a former Czech or Czechoslovak citizen who lost or never acquired Czech citizenship. Originally covered emigrants who lost citizenship 1949–1989 under communist-era denaturalization; the 2019 amendment extended to broader emigrant categories; the **27 June 2024 amendment extended to the 4th generation** and re-opened the window for persons born 1949–1969 abroad to a Czechoslovak parent (and their descendants) who missed the brief 2014 window.
- Requirements: documentary proof of ancestor's Czech/Czechoslovak citizenship (birth/baptism records, naturalization records of the country of emigration showing prior CS citizenship, military or domovský list records). **No language exam. No residency. No civics test. No clean-record requirement beyond standard.** No application fee at the discretionary scale.
- Filed at any Czech embassy/consulate or directly with the Ministry of Interior.
- Timeline: declaration is constitutive on acceptance. Document gathering is the bottleneck — typically 6–24 months. Processing of the declaration itself: 3–12 months after filing.
- Dual citizenship: explicitly permitted.

## Restored Citizenship by Declaration — §32 (Former CSFR Citizens)

- §32(1): persons who as of 31 Dec 1992 were citizens of the Czech and Slovak Federative Republic but became neither Czech nor Slovak citizens at the split, **and have never since acquired either**, may declare Czech citizenship.
- §32(2): **direct descendants** of such persons (any generation) may also declare, provided they have never been Czech or Slovak citizens.
- No language, residency, or civics requirements.
- Narrow category in practice — most ex-Czechoslovaks ended up as Czech or Slovak citizens automatically in 1993.

## Naturalization by Residency (Standard) — §11–§14

- **Residency**: 5 years of permanent residence (`trvalý pobyt`). Permanent residence itself requires 5 years of prior continuous lawful temporary/long-term residence for non-EU nationals, or 5 years of continuous residence for EU nationals. **Total: ~10 years.** EU citizens may apply for permanent residence after 2 years if married to / family member of a Czech permanent resident.
- **Physical presence**: actually present in CZ for at least half of the 5-year permanent-residence window; absences capped at ~2 months/year (6 months for serious reasons).
- **Language**: Czech B1 exam (ALTE-certified, administered by Charles University / cestina-pro-cizince.cz). Waived for under-15, over-65, applicants with physical/mental disability, or those who completed ≥3 years of Czech-medium schooling.
- **Civics**: Czech Life and Institutions exam (constitutional system, geography, history, culture). Same waivers as language.
- **Clean record**: no convictions in CZ in the last 5 years, no convictions in any country in the last 10 years (for intentional crimes).
- **Tax / social security**: documented earnings and tax payment for the 3 years preceding application; no significant unjustified burden on the welfare system in the same window.
- **Integration**: shared-residence proof, employment / business / family ties.
- **Fee**: CZK 2,000 (adults), CZK 500 (reduced cases / minors).
- **Timeline**: statutory 180 days from filing with the Ministry of Interior. **In practice 12–24 months** end-to-end (regional office forwards within 30 days; Ministry review is the bottleneck). No formal "backlog" figure published by MV ČR, but field reports through 2024–2026 consistently cite ~18 months.
- **Total elapsed**: ~10–12 years from arrival.

## Naturalization by Marriage — No Special Facilitated Path

- Czechia does **not** shorten the residency clock for spouses of Czech citizens. The same 5-permanent-residence and ~10-year total applies.
- The only marriage-related facilitation is in evidence: if married ≥5 years to a Czech citizen, living in shared household, and holding permanent residence, the integration / income proofs are more lenient and the financial-burden threshold is relaxed.
- Spouses do **not** get a fast-track to permanent residence either — they must still satisfy the 5-year (or 2-year EU-family) temporary-residence prerequisite.
- Net effect: marriage offers documentary leniency, not a shorter timeline.

## Investment / CBI — None

- Czechia has **no citizenship-by-investment program** and never has.
- The "Long-Term Residence Permit for the Purpose of Investment" exists but is residency-only and does not shortcut the standard 10-year naturalization clock — see [../residency/czechia.md](../residency/czechia.book-chapter.md).
- No realistic shortcut for investors.

## Distinguished Contribution — §15

- Discretionary grant for permanent residents whose Czech citizenship would yield significant benefit in science, education, culture, sport; or for humanitarian / international-commitment / state-interest grounds.
- Waives the 5-year permanent-residence requirement and may waive language/civics. Permanent residence itself is still required.
- Rare in practice — used for Olympic athletes, defectors, refugee scientists, etc. No public quota; decided by the Ministry of Interior with cabinet review for high-profile cases.

## Service-Based — Limited

- No general military or government-service shortcut.
- §15-style discretionary grants have been used for individuals who served Czech state interests abroad (intelligence, diplomatic support), but this is ad hoc, not a defined path.

## Refugees and Stateless Persons — §14

- Recognized refugees and stateless persons may apply for naturalization after **3 years of permanent residence** (vs. the standard 5).
- Permanent residence itself requires ~4 years of asylum + 1 year of PR-eligibility (the refugee-PR clock starts earlier than the standard third-country clock).
- Language/civics still required, but financial-burden and integration tests are softened.
- Total elapsed: ~7–8 years from asylum grant.

## Slovak Nationals — Limited Facilitation, Mostly Historical

- The Czech-Slovak split (1 Jan 1993) automatically allocated citizenship based on republic-level citizenship in the federation. The §18 transitional window for Slovak citizens to acquire Czech citizenship by declaration closed in 1999.
- Today, Slovak citizens follow the **standard EU-national naturalization track** (5 years of permanent residence; permanent residence after 5 years of continuous residence, or 2 years if Czech family member).
- The historical language-exam waiver for Slovaks **no longer applies**. (Practical impact: minor; Slovak speakers pass Czech B1 trivially.)
- §32 (above) covers the tiny residual population of persons who ended up neither Czech nor Slovak at the split.

## Re-acquisition of Lost Citizenship

- Pre-2014 Czech citizens who lost citizenship under the old Article 17 (automatic loss on foreign naturalization) may **re-acquire by declaration under §31** — no application, no language, no residency. This was the original purpose of §31 before the 2019/2024 expansions.
- Persons who voluntarily renounced may re-acquire by application (standard naturalization with potential §15 facilitation if previously Czech).

## Comparison Summary

Ranked fastest-to-citizenship for a typical applicant:

| Rank | Path | Total time | Likelihood |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | §31 declaration (Czech ancestor lost citizenship; up to 4th gen) | 6–24 months (mostly document gathering) | High if ancestor verifiable |
| 2 | §32 declaration (ex-CSFR or descendant, never Czech/Slovak) | 6–12 months | Narrow eligibility |
| 3 | Re-acquisition by declaration (lost under old Art. 17) | 6–12 months | Self-evident if you previously held |
| 4 | §15 distinguished contribution | 1–3 years after PR | Very rare |
| 5 | Refugee/stateless naturalization | ~7–8 years | Conditional on protection status |
| 6 | Standard naturalization | ~10–12 years | Achievable but long |
| 7 | Marriage (no acceleration; same 10-year track) | ~10–12 years | Achievable; documentary easier |
| 8 | Investment-residency → naturalization | ~10–12 years | Capital-heavy, no acceleration |
| — | CBI | N/A | Does not exist |
| — | Jus soli | N/A | Effectively closed |

For Future-Alan: if any Czech or Czechoslovak ancestry exists in the family tree back to ~1900 (great-great-grandparent), **§31 is by far the strongest play** — no language, no residency, dual citizenship explicit, and the June 2024 amendment maximally expanded eligibility. Standard naturalization is otherwise a 10–12-year slog with a hard B1 language gate.

## Recent Changes (2023–2026)

- **27 June 2024**: amendment to Act 186/2013 expanded §31 to the **4th generation** of direct descendants and re-opened the path for persons born 1949–1969 abroad to a Czechoslovak parent (and their descendants) who missed the brief 2014 declaration window. Single biggest expansion since 2014.
- **2019 amendment**: prior expansion of §31, allowing children/grandchildren of former Czechs/Czechoslovaks to declare without renouncing other citizenships.
- **2014 (in force from 1 Jan)**: Act 186/2013 replaced the 1993 Act; fully permitted dual citizenship; eliminated automatic loss on foreign naturalization.
- **2025–2026**: ongoing draft amendments under discussion to tighten standard naturalization (extending the no-criminal-record window, possibly raising language to B2 for some categories) — not yet passed as of May 2026.
