
## Overview

Switzerland is uniquely **three-tiered**: every naturalization requires approval at the **federal**, **cantonal**, and **communal** level. Each tier has its own residence clock, language threshold, integration test, fees, and (in some communes) a town-meeting vote. Federal law sets the floor; cantons and communes add their own requirements on top. Dual citizenship has been freely permitted since 1 January 1992.

## Citizenship at birth (jus sanguinis)

### Swiss parent
Acquired automatically at birth from a Swiss parent regardless of birthplace. For children of unmarried parents where the father is Swiss, citizenship attaches once paternity is legally established (treated as acquired at birth). Switzerland does **not** recognize jus soli — birth on Swiss soil grants no citizenship.

### Anti-statelessness jus soli
A child born stateless in Switzerland may apply for **facilitated naturalization** (not automatic). They must have resided in Switzerland for at least 5 years, including the year before the application. This is the only "soil"-based path.

## Ordinary naturalization

The standard adult path. All three tiers must be satisfied **concurrently** within one overall residency window — cantonal and communal time runs *inside* the federal 10-year period, not on top.

### Federal layer
- **10 years** of lawful residence in Switzerland, including 3 of the last 5 years before application.
- **Years 8 to 18 count double** (so a child raised in Switzerland from age 8 can reach the 10-year mark with 6 actual years), but a minimum of 6 years actual physical residence is required.
- Time on B or C permits counts in full; F (temporary admission) counts half; N (asylum) and L (short-term) do not count.
- **C permit (settlement permit)** held at the time of application.
- **Language**: A2 written + B1 oral in a Swiss national language (German, French, Italian, or Romansh — the relevant one for the canton).
- **Integration**: respect for the Swiss legal order and constitutional values, no threat to internal/external security, participation in economic life or education, encouragement of family integration.
- No serious criminal record, no current social-assistance dependency (or repayment in last 3 years), debt enforcement record reviewed.

### Cantonal layer
- Each canton sets its own **2 to 5 years** in-canton residence requirement (often immediately before application).
- Many cantons require their own knowledge test on cantonal civics, history, and geography.
- Some cantons set language thresholds above the federal floor (e.g., B1 written).
- A cantonal naturalization commission interview is standard.

### Communal layer
- Communes typically require **2 to 5 years** of residence in the commune (often **3 years** as a common baseline), again running inside the federal window.
- The commune runs the local civics knowledge component and an integration interview.
- In **some small communes** (notably in Appenzell Innerrhoden and rural cantons), the decision is still taken by the citizens' assembly or municipal council — applicants can be **voted on by their neighbors**. A negative communal vote blocks the application even when federal and cantonal criteria are met.

### Timeline
After all residence clocks are met, the three-layer process itself typically takes **12 to 24 months**, with significant variance — some cantons publish 6-month decisions, others have multi-year backlogs.

## Facilitated naturalization (federal-only)

Decided by the State Secretariat for Migration (SEM); cantons and communes are consulted but do not have approval authority. Faster and cheaper than ordinary naturalization. Typical processing **12 to 18 months**.

### Spouse of a Swiss citizen
- Resident in Switzerland: **5 years** total Swiss residence + **3 years** marriage + the year before application in Switzerland.
- Resident abroad: **6 years** marriage to the Swiss spouse + close ties to Switzerland (regular visits, language, family contact).
- Language and integration requirements apply.

### Third-generation foreigners (since 2018)
For applicants born and raised in Switzerland whose grandparent generation already established residence:
- Born in Switzerland, holds a C permit, completed at least 5 years compulsory schooling in Switzerland.
- Must apply **before age 25**.
- At least one grandparent was born in Switzerland or acquired a right of residence here.
- At least one parent held a C permit, lived in Switzerland 10+ years, and attended compulsory schooling in Switzerland.

### Children of one Swiss parent
A foreign minor with one Swiss parent who did not transmit citizenship automatically (e.g., historical edge cases, unmarried parents pre-paternity establishment) can apply via facilitated naturalization.

### Stateless minors
A stateless child resident in Switzerland 5 years (including the year before application) may apply.

## Reinstatement / restoration

Article 27 of the Swiss Citizenship Act (SCA). For people who lost Swiss citizenship via forfeiture, release, or — historically — marriage to a foreigner.

- **Within 10 years of loss**: reinstatement application from anywhere.
- **After 10 years**: requires 3 years of Swiss residence before application.
- **Pre-1992 women** who lost citizenship by marrying a foreigner (until 1952 automatic; 1952-1992 if they failed to opt-in within 10 years) and **their descendants** can apply for restoration. Approximately 85,200 women were affected; a 2024-2025 parliamentary push has been debating broader descendant restoration but no expanded statute has passed as of May 2026.

## Distinguished service / discretionary

There is no broad "merit" or "investor" naturalization in Switzerland. Federal law allows naturalization without the standard residency in extremely narrow cases (e.g., long-serving Swiss state employees abroad). This is not a practical path for outsiders — there is no Swiss equivalent of the Austrian Article 10(6) extraordinary-merit grant.

## Dual citizenship

**Fully permitted** since 1 January 1992. Switzerland imposes no obligation to renounce prior nationality. The US, EU member states, and most jurisdictions permit dual nationality with Switzerland — verify the rules of the other state. Naturalizing as Swiss does not trigger any Swiss-side loss of other citizenships, and prior Swiss citizens who later naturalized elsewhere do not lose Swiss nationality.

## Recent changes (2024-2026)

- Federal residence quotas, language thresholds, and the three-tier structure unchanged.
- Continued cantonal harmonization of integration criteria following the 2018 Citizenship Act revision.
- 2024-2025 parliamentary discussion of extended restoration rights for descendants of women who lost citizenship pre-1992 — not yet enacted.
- December 2025 reporting confirms cantons continue to diverge significantly on **how** integration is demonstrated (written tests, interviews, community-service expectations).
