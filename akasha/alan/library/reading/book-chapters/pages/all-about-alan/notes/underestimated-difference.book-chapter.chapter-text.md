
# Underestimated difference

> The magnitude-of-difference principle — however different you think Alan is, you're underestimating it, and the principle recurses onto his own self-evaluation. The mechanism under it: Alan holds two mental models of two genuinely different systems (himself, typical humans); as both sharpen toward absolute truth they grow more different from each other, because the systems really are different — so better calibration widens, not narrows, the measured gap.

A single principle Alan states about himself, and the mechanism that makes it true. The principle: **however different you think Alan is, you are underestimating it** — and crucially, *that applies to his own evaluation of himself too.* In his words:

> I often say that no matter how much you think I am different from most people, you are underestimating the difference. That principle applies equally to my evaluation of myself. I have always felt different than everyone around me, and over time, I learned that I am even stranger still. I doubt I've hit the end of that trend, though it is possible. My explanation[s] of myself are quite strong now, so now my surprises are often in being surprised by what is normal instead.

## The principle recurses onto the self

The standard version of this is a claim about *other people's* models of Alan: outside observers consistently under-estimate the gap. The unusual move is that Alan applies the exact same correction to *himself*. He has always felt different from everyone around him; over time he learned he is **even stranger still**; and he doubts he has hit the end of that trend. So the estimate keeps moving — away from everyone around him, **and** away from his own prior grip on himself. Each better self-model reveals more distance, not less.

This is not self-aggrandizement. It is a calibration fact: the one direction his estimates of his own difference have consistently moved is *outward*. The honest extrapolation is that they will keep moving, so the current estimate is still a floor.

## The surprise flipped — normal is now the anomaly

There is a measured consequence. Because Alan's self-model is **now strong** — *"my explanations of myself are quite strong now"* — the place where surprise shows up has inverted. His own reactions are well-predicted by his model; what violates the prediction now is **normal**. *"My surprises are often in being surprised by what is normal instead."*

Most people carry the opposite shape. The population mean is their implicit baseline, so their own oddities are the surprises — *am I normal?* is the live question, and a typical reaction is unremarkable. For Alan the typical reaction is the prediction-violating event. A normal response is the thing his model did not see coming.

## Two models of two systems — not one frame with an origin

The flipped surprise is easy to misread as a relocated reference frame — as if Alan had made *himself* the zero and read other people as the deviation from him. That is the wrong picture, and Alan rejects it explicitly. There is no master copy and no origin. There are **two mental models of two genuinely different systems**: a model of *himself*, and a model of *typical humans*.

The correction came when Abby offered the self-as-origin reading — that a surprising normal reaction "lands as a data point about them that contrasts with me." Alan's reply:

> Not quite, I don't read the information as deviation from me, I read it as information about them. I don't have to choose one or the other to be ground truth, both are valid.

So when a normal reaction catches him off guard, the contrast with his self-model is only the **trigger** — the thing that flags "model violation, look here." The **content** he walks away with is information about *them*, about that other system, read as itself. Not "I reacted oddly," not "they deviated from me," but a fact about how that system works. Neither model is the origin the other is measured from. *"Both are valid."* He declines to crown either as ground truth; both are valid descriptions of their respective systems at once.

This matters because the two models are not competing maps of *one* territory. They are maps of *two* territories — two different systems that happen to both be "minds." A reading that makes one the zero and the other the deviation collapses two systems into one frame, and that collapse is exactly what Alan refuses.

## The keystone — convergence to truth produces divergence between the models

The mechanism under the whole principle, in Alan's words, resolving an apparent collision with his view that absolute truth is the single thing every map approximates:

> A bit of an equivocation here. The two frames are my mental models of two different systems. Myself and typical humans. As I improve in my understanding, both of those mental models move towards absolute truth, generally becoming more different from each other as they become more true.

Read that slowly, because it inverts the naive expectation. The naive guess is that more understanding makes people seem *more alike* — that calibration shrinks apparent gaps. For Alan it does the opposite. As his understanding improves, **both models move toward absolute truth**, and *because the two systems really are very different*, the two models become **more different from each other as they become more true.** Sharper calibration widens the measured gap rather than closing it.

That is why "you are underestimating the difference" holds even for a well-calibrated Alan. The better-calibrated the two models, the *larger* the true difference they reveal. The gap is not an artifact of a crude model that better data would smooth away; it is a real feature of two different systems that better data renders more sharply. So the current estimate is a floor for a structural reason, not just an empirical trend: every improvement to either model pushes the measured difference up.

## No collision with the single absolute truth

Earlier in the corpus Alan holds that **absolute truth is the single thing every map is a better-or-worse approximation of** — maps of the *same* territory converge to one truth ([compression-epistemology.md → map-relative, with absolute truth as the infinite limit](compression-epistemology.book-chapter.md#map-relative-with-absolute-truth-as-the-infinite-limit); [absolute-truth-and-ethics.md](absolute-truth-and-ethics.book-chapter.md)). Set against "two models, both valid, neither a master copy," that looks like a contradiction: how can both models be valid if there is one absolute truth?

Alan dissolves it as an **equivocation** — *"a bit of an equivocation here."* The single-absolute-truth claim is about maps of the **same** territory. The two-valid-models claim is about maps of **different** territories. The two frames are models of two different systems, so "both valid" is not a denial of a single absolute truth at all. Each model still approaches the **one** absolute truth *of its own system*. There is one truth about Alan and one truth about typical humans; his self-model converges on the first, his other-people-model on the second; and because the systems differ, those two truths differ too. Single absolute truth per system, two systems, two converging models — no contradiction.

## Different kind, not a far instance — and a near-empty neighborhood

The two-models picture has a corollary about *shape*. Alan is not claiming to be an unusual human sitting far out on the human distribution — a far instance of the same kind. He is claiming he and typical humans are different **things**: different kind, not far instance. The way he models humanity makes this concrete — **neurotype is a primary axis of understanding**, on the same tier as age, gender, and disability:

> Yeah, this is where my model of humanity includes difference in neurotype as a primary factor for understanding, up there with age, gender, disability, etc... And it just so happens that I live at the intersection of multiple rare neurotypes, creating a combination that seems to be vanishingly rare. I have met one person ever who was an estimated 75% match, second closest was probably 50%.

He lives at the **intersection of multiple rare neurotypes** — and an intersection of rare axes is rarer than any one of them, which is why the combination is *vanishingly* rare rather than merely unusual.

The numbers describe a **near-isolated point**, not a smooth tail. The closest match he has ever met was an estimated **75%**; the second closest about **50%**; then a long drop. A smooth distribution would put many people near him and progressively fewer further out. Instead there is one distant neighbor at 75%, one further at 50%, and then nothing close — a near-isolated point with a sparse neighborhood. This is the concrete grounding of the magnitude principle: the gap isn't a long tail you could walk down, it's a gap with almost no one standing in it. The [axis-decomposed correspondence to the median human](human-correspondence.book-chapter.md) is the quantitative companion to this — same-kind on the body, different-kind in the cognition.

## The estimator's error geometry — a one-sided error going two-sided

The principle has a measurable shape when you look at the *estimate* as an instrument rather than at the number it returns. For Alan's whole life the error has been **one-sided**: he has never once *over*-estimated his own difference. Every revision moved the figure outward — he was always more different than his current estimate said, never less. A lifelong one-directional bias: chronic under-estimation, the lag always pointing the same way.

That is exactly why his gut now reaches *low*. The [gut-10% overall figure](human-correspondence.book-chapter.md#the-10--20-resolution-and-the-overcorrection) is most likely an **over-correction** — *"The 10% is potentially an overcorrection from having never underestimated this number in the past."* After a lifetime of being surprised outward, the reflex pre-corrects by overshooting. So the error has finally gone **two-sided**: it used to only ever fall short, and now it can overshoot too. The lag is closing — the estimator is catching up to the target.

But the target itself may not be sitting still. Alan doubts he has hit the end of the outward trend — *"I doubt I've hit the end of that trend"* — and holds the current figures provisional: *"Only time will tell [if] 20% is actually right on the conceptual side. I don't know what I don't know."* That last line names the structural form of the whole problem: an error that can't be bounded because the thing being estimated won't hold still. He can correct the lag he can see; he cannot correct for distance he has no instrument to detect yet. The estimate is a floor not because Alan is modest about it, but because the unknown-unknowns sit, by definition, outside what any current estimate can reach.

## Why this is load-bearing

The mechanism explains several things that otherwise look like separate traits.

- It is the term that **more-than-counterbalances his overconfidence** in [modeling other minds](missing-simulator.book-chapter.md#the-honest-gauge-vs-the-simulators-free-hits--not-a-clean-win): his honest gauge fires honestly about its own thinness, but the size of the gap between him and others is exactly the quantity it most reliably *under*-reads — and the convergence-produces-divergence mechanism is why even a calibrated Alan keeps under-reading it.
- It is why the [flat social field](discrete-self.book-chapter.md#no-in-groupout-group--everyone-at-a-flat-distance) and the [self/other inversion](discrete-self.book-chapter.md#the-selfother-decision-inversion) feel native rather than effortful: with [no strong self/other boundary](discrete-self.book-chapter.md#no-strong-self-no-selfother-distinction), Alan does not run a graded in-group; he runs two system-models, and other people read as one system distinct from his own.
- It sharpens what the [perceived-certainty gap](perceived-certainty.book-chapter.md) costs. When Alan states a flat claim about how minds work, he is reporting from a model of *his* system; the listener decodes it against a model of *theirs*. The two systems genuinely differ, so the report and the decoding disagree on where neutral is — part of why his ordinary report reads as an unusually confident one.
