
# Skin-on-skin prevention

> Skin-on-skin prevention as a system — tactile baseline management against input-doubling cost. Any fabric layer suffices as a barrier; tight clothing, loose clothing, body pillows, and posture choices all qualify. Distinct mechanism from tight-clothing's two-fold mechanism.

A coherent system across body contact: prevent skin from touching skin, by inserting a barrier or shifting position. Promoted into its own file from cycle 5 captures alongside [lotion regimen](lotion-regimen.book-chapter.md); the two together form the tactile baseline management class.

## Mechanism: input doubling

Skin touching skin registers on both sides at once. When the inside of one arm rests against the torso, both the arm-skin and the torso-skin receive the contact input — the tactile signal is delivered twice. On a [nervous system already running at high per-input cost](nervous-system-sensitivity.book-chapter.md#input-doubling-on-skin-on-skin-contact), this doubling is multiplicative load.

Alan finds his own skin touching his own skin irritating for this reason. The irritation isn't aesthetic and isn't psychological; it's a measurable tactile signal counted twice, on a system that pays a high cost per input. The mechanism root lives in [nervous-system-sensitivity.md](nervous-system-sensitivity.book-chapter.md).

## Prevention via barriers

Any fabric layer between two skin surfaces breaks the contact and removes the input-doubling.

- **Clothing-as-barrier.** Sleeves between arm and torso. Shirts between forearm and chest. Pants between knees. Socks between feet.
- **Body pillows.** Between knees during sleep. Between arm and torso during side-lying.
- **Posture choices.** Avoid hand-clasping. Avoid sitting positions that bring legs together at the inner knee. Shift a limb off another limb when contact lands.

The shared mechanism is the same: a barrier between the two skin surfaces, or a position that doesn't bring them into contact.

## Loose works as well as tight

The barrier function is independent of the tight-vs-loose distinction. Any fabric layer between the two skin surfaces suffices; the barrier doesn't have to grip. Loose pants prevent knees-together skin contact just as well as tight pants. A loose shirt prevents forearm-against-chest contact just as well as a tight one.

This is the cycle-5 correction to an earlier hunch that skin-on-skin prevention was a third mechanism of [tight clothing](tight-clothing.book-chapter.md). It isn't. Tight clothing's two-fold mechanism (proprioceptive grounding + touch-stimuli cost reduction) is preserved as-is; skin-on-skin prevention runs on a separate mechanism (input-doubling avoidance) and a separate tool family (any barrier, plus posture, plus body pillows). The two systems overlap in tools — tight clothing happens to provide a barrier as a side effect — but they are not the same system.

The implication for the [loose-break under low Mana](tight-clothing.book-chapter.md#loose-break-scope-and-trigger) move: swapping pants and shirt to loose does not lose the skin-on-skin prevention. Loose covers it. What's traded is the grounding-and-saturation function of the tight kit, not the barrier function.

## Tactile baseline management as a system class

Skin-on-skin prevention sits in a system class — **tactile baseline management** — alongside [lotion regimen](lotion-regimen.book-chapter.md). Both are *defensive maintenance* patterns against tactile cost sources:

- **Skin-on-skin prevention** defends against input-doubling cost (the multiplicative load from skin touching skin).
- **Lotion regimen** defends against skin-state cost (the self-generated signal from dry skin — see [lotion-regimen.md → Self-generated cost from the un-lotioned baseline](lotion-regimen.book-chapter.md#self-generated-cost-from-the-un-lotioned-baseline)).

Both run a default-maintained-state shape: maintain a protected baseline (a barrier present, a lotion layer present) continuously, respond to disruption by restoring the baseline.
